This miracle molecule promises youth, muscle and longevity. But what's the catch? Inside India's peptide party moment

First came collagen. Then niacinamide. Then PDRN. Now, peptides are the new buzzword in beauty and wellness.

From creams and serums to injectable treatments, these short chains of amino acids are being promoted for everything from skin ageing and collagen production to muscle recovery, weight loss and longevity.

But behind the peptide hype is a growing concern: injectable compounds that are not approved for human use are increasingly being sold online and promoted on social media, despite limited human evidence and questions around product quality and safety.

Search for “peptides” online and you’ll find retailers offering vials of compounds with names that sound more like exoplanets than wellness products — GHK-Cu, CJC-1295, BPC-157 and Thymosin Beta-4. Biohackers, fitness enthusiasts and wellness influencers are turning to them in the hope of improving performance, speeding recovery or optimising health.

And the trend is spilling offline. In Bengaluru, a planned “Peptide Party” in Koramangala, billed as India’s first, was scheduled for August 29. The invite-only event, promoted by Upsurge Labs LLP, promised “peptide tastings”, music, food and networking. It was called off after the Karnataka Food Safety and Drug Administration sought details on the peptide preparations. Organisers said no injectable or prescription material was planned and that they were “cooperating fully” with the regulator.

So, what exactly are peptides — and how much of the hype is backed by science?

What experts say

The biggest concern, doctors say, is that the popularity of injectable peptides is running ahead of the evidence. Human data on several compounds being promoted online remains limited, particularly on their long-term safety.

Dr P Narendra, Consultant Dermatologist at SPARSH Hospital, RR Nagar, Bengaluru, said consumers should not assume injectable peptides are safe simply because they are marketed as regenerative or anti-ageing products.

"From a dermatological standpoint, my advice would be to exercise caution when it comes to assuming that peptides administered by injections cannot be dangerous merely due to being called regenerative and anti-aging drugs," he told Moneycontrol.

"At present, there is no proof based on clinical human trials as to how long-term use of GHK-Cu, BPC-157 and TB-500 peptides affect the health of kidneys and urine production in people," Narendra said.

"Moreover, the safety of peptides can depend on many factors such as doses, formulations and the quality of products used. In addition, unlicensed and poorly compounded drugs are associated with extra risks as well," he added.

"Thus, people interested in using peptides should not administer themselves injections following the information on the Internet," Narendra said.

Dr DM Mahajan, Senior Consultant- Dermatology at Indraprastha Apollo Hospital, Delhi, also pointed to the lack of long-term human data.

"There is limited human data on the long-term safety of injectable peptides such as GHK-Cu, BPC-157 and TB-500," he told Moneycontrol.

"We do not yet have enough evidence to say how repeated or prolonged use may affect kidney function or urinary health," Mahajan said.

"Anyone considering these injections should discuss them with a qualified doctor rather than assuming that they are safe because they are marketed as peptides," he added.

For topical peptides used in creams and serums, the picture is somewhat different. Doctors say some formulations show promise, although results vary by peptide and formulation.

"The results obtained for topical peptides are encouraging, but need to be taken with a grain of salt," Narendra said.

"Some peptides have shown the ability to affect collagen formation pathways and thus can help with skin conditions such as hydration and texture and fine lines formation. However, all peptides do not show the same results," he said.

"Even though some studies have found a positive effect of peptides on wrinkle formation, more clinical trials are required," Narendra added.

"From the point of view of a dermatologist, topical peptides can be used as an additional component, but not as an alternative to such proven ways of fighting ageing as sunscreen use," he said.

Mahajan said there was also little evidence that using several peptide products together delivers better results.

"There is no good evidence that using several peptide products together gives significantly better results," he told Moneycontrol.

"In skincare, using a few well-chosen products consistently is usually more useful than layering multiple products with similar ingredients," he added.

The appeal of injectables, however, partly comes from the belief that they can produce faster or stronger effects.

"Injectable peptides have become more attractive for consumers due to the belief that injections are capable of producing more significant or quicker effects compared to topical skincare products," Narendra said.

"But that belief may not be based on any scientific facts," he added.

"Systemic administration of the peptide, as in the case with injectable products, implies the existence of an additional risk factor of the unknown nature," Narendra said.

"Moreover, there is no sufficient information about the safety and efficacy of BPC-157, GHK-Cu, and TB-500 in humans," he added.

Dr Bhanu Mishra, Consultant - Nephrology at Fortis Hospital, Shalimar Bagh, said evidence for non-approved injectable peptides was particularly limited compared with approved peptide-based medicines.

"In comparison with approved peptide injectables (GLP-1 receptor agonists, e.g., Ozempic, Wegovy and insulin), there is much less evidence available on safety, dosages and long-term effects of the non-approved injectable peptides, including GHK-Cu, BPC-157 and TB-500," he told Moneycontrol.

"Currently, there are no clinical studies that would confirm or deny that administration of any of these injectable peptides may be associated with improvement or impairment of kidney functioning in healthy individuals," Mishra said.

"Nonetheless, lack of evidence does not necessarily mean safety," he added.

"In particular, human studies are extremely scarce in case of BPC-157 and GHK-Cu," Mishra said.

He also cautioned against treating animal research on TB-500 as proof of safety in humans.

"TB-500 is an analog of thymosin beta-4, which is studied in animal experiments for possible use in kidney injury treatment models; interestingly, some of these studies focus on the potential protective effects, but this does not mean that TB-500 injection is clinically proven safe and/or helpful for kidney in humans," he said.

Mishra also flagged product quality as a concern with unapproved peptides.

"There is also risk of contamination and mislabeling of these unapproved peptides," he said.

"The main problem is their quality. Improperly made or unapproved medications can have wrong dosing levels, impurities, degradation products, and contaminants, causing allergic reactions and infections," Mishra added.

For consumers, the key distinction is between peptide products with established evidence and experimental or unapproved compounds being marketed as wellness shortcuts.

Dr (Col) Vijay Dutta, Director, Internal Medicine &amp; Respiratory Services, ISIC Multispeciality Hospital, told Moneycontrol: "People are so young and injecting peptides is so popular these days, and it's concerning because lots of people are buying them from the internet and using them without a doctor's guidance."

"The message is clear: Don't try to use injectable peptides as a health quick fix. Before you take any injected product, find out what it is, why you are taking it, if it is approved for this use, and if it is under the supervision of a medical professional. Social media should not replace solid medical treatment."

(Supreme Court of India) Uncommunicated Adverse ACR Entries Can Be Considered for Compulsory Retirement: Supreme Court Summary: The Supreme Court has held that uncommunicated adverse entries and below-benchmark gradings in Annual Confidential Reports (ACRs) may be considered while deciding whether a government employee should be compulsorily retired. An order of compulsory retirement cannot be quashed merely because such entries were not communicated to the employee. In Chatur Singh Dhruw v. State of Chhattisgarh & Ors., 2026 INSC 1205, the Court upheld the compulsory retirement of two employees of the State’s Legal Metrology Department. The judgment dated 6 November 2026 was delivered by Justice Manmohan, sitting with Justice Shree Chandrashekhar. Background of the Case The appellants, Chatur Singh Dhruw and Manharan Lal Sahu, were initially appointed as Peons (Class IV) in 1989 and 1993, respectively. They were subsequently promoted to Assistant Grade III in 2012 and 2013, and their services were regularised through orders dated 19 June 2015. Under a government circular dated 25 April 2017, departments were directed to review employees who had completed 20 years of qualifying service or attained 50 years of age. A Screening Committee examined 14 eligible clerical employees and recommended compulsory retirement of five, including the appellants. Retirement orders were issued on 9 October 2017. Both the Single Judge and Division Bench of the Chhattisgarh High Court declined to interfere. Employees Challenged Uncommunicated Entries and Selective Treatment The appellants contended that the authorities had applied the retirement criteria selectively and had failed to examine their entire service records. They argued that the orders were punitive and stigmatic, having been founded on adverse assessments of their conduct and efficiency. According to them, compulsory retirement had been used to bypass disciplinary proceedings. They further submitted that uncommunicated ACR entries could not be relied upon and that their promotions and subsequent regularisation had diminished the relevance of earlier adverse material. Compulsory Retirement Must Rest on Relevant Material The Supreme Court reiterated that compulsory retirement under the applicable service rules is neither punitive nor stigmatic. It rests on the competent authority’s assessment that continued retention of an employee would no longer serve public interest. However, that assessment cannot proceed on conjecture or whim. It must be supported by objective and relevant material, including service records, performance evaluations and integrity assessments. Judicial review examines whether relevant material existed, whether the authority considered it and whether the conclusion was reasonably possible. Courts do not reassess the evidence as an appellate authority. Interference remains permissible where the decision suffers from absence of evidence, non-application of mind, manifest arbitrariness, perversity or mala fides. Retention of Another Employee Does Not Establish Discrimination The Court held that an employee cannot successfully challenge compulsory retirement merely because another person with a comparable service record was retained. Assessment of an employee’s utility, integrity, efficiency and suitability is an individual exercise. Article 14 does not recognise negative equality, and an omission or illegality benefiting one employee cannot justify extending the same treatment to another. On the facts, the Court found that the Committee had applied uniform criteria to all 14 employees. Nine employees were retained, while five were recommended for retirement. Entire Service Records Were Examined The Court rejected the contention that the assessment was confined to recent years. The Committee first evaluated the appellants’ entire service records against the minimum benchmark equivalent to Grade B. It then examined their ACRs for 2013–2017 to identify declining performance or repeated low gradings. The recent reports were therefore a corroborative component of the overall assessment, rather than its sole foundation. Chatur Singh received an A grading in 2013, followed by C gradings for four consecutive years. Manharan Lal Sahu’s reports similarly reflected repeated C gradings and unsatisfactory performance. Promotion and Regularisation Do Not Erase Subsequent Adverse Entries The Court clarified that the principle concerning adverse entries losing significance after promotion relates to entries preceding the promotion. It does not neutralise adverse assessments recorded after promotion, concerning performance in the higher post. Further, regularisation does not amount to a merit-based promotion. It confers substantive status in the existing post and does not itself endorse efficiency or future suitability. Uncommunicated ACRs Can Support Retirement Assessment Relying on Baikuntha Nath Das v. Chief District Medical Officer, Baripada, the Court held that consideration of uncommunicated adverse remarks does not, by itself, invalidate compulsory retirement. The appellants’ reliance on Dev Dutt and Sukhdev Singh was misplaced because those decisions concerned promotion, career progression and communication of entries affecting promotional prospects. Supreme Court Dismisses Appeals The retirement orders contained no allegation of misconduct or finding of culpability. Internal assessments did not make them stigmatic, and no departmental inquiry was pending or contemplated. Finding the exercise supported by relevant material and uniformly applied standards, the Supreme Court dismissed both appeals and affirmed the High Court’s judgments, without costs. REASONING SCOPE OF JUDICIAL REVIEW IN MATTERS OF COMPULSORY RETIREMENT 23. In order to determine the controversy arising in the present appeals, it is necessary, at the outset, to delineate the scope and contours of judicial review in matters concerning compulsory retirement. 24. It is now trite law that an order of compulsory retirement is neither punitive nor stigmatic in character. Such an order does not cast any aspersion upon the conduct of the employee nor does it imply any finding of misconduct 1. Consequently, the rule of audi alteram partem, as well as the protection afforded under Article 311(2) of the Constitution, have no application to an order of compulsory retirement made in accordance with the relevant service rules. 25. The decision to compulsorily retire a Government servant is founded upon the subjective satisfaction of the competent authority that the employee’s continued retention in service is no longer conducive to public interest. The ultimate assessment as to whether an officer has become ‘dead wood’ or whether his continuance would be detrimental to administrative efficiency falls within the domain of the executive. However, such satisfaction cannot be found upon mere conjecture or whim. It must rest upon objective and relevant material available on record. This Court in Nand Kumar Verma vs. State of Jharkhand & Ors., (2012) 3 SCC 580 has held that ‘It is also well settled that the formation of opinion for compulsory retirement is based on the subjective satisfaction of the concerned authority but such satisfaction must be based on a valid material. It is permissible for the Courts to ascertain whether a valid material exists or otherwise, on which the subjective satisfaction of the administrative authority is based’. 26. Further, where the Government itself prescribes norms or criteria governing the exercise of the power, judicial review extends only to examining whether such norms or criteria have been duly observed 2. 27. It follows, therefore, that an order of compulsory retirement must be supported by relevant material such as Annual Confidential Reports, integrity assessments, performance evaluations and other service-record entries bearing upon the employee’s utility and suitability for continued retention. Judicial review in such cases does not entail a re-appreciation or re-weighing of the material by the Court as though it were sitting in appeal over the administrative decision. The Court is concerned only with whether relevant material existed, whether such material was taken into consideration by the competent authority, and whether the conclusion drawn therefrom is one which a reasonable person could have arrived at. So long as these requirements are satisfied, the Court will not substitute its view for that of the authority entrusted with the decision-making process. THE POWER OF COMPULSORY RETIREMENT IS WIDE AND NON- RETIREMENT OF A SIMILARLY SITUATED EMPLOYEE DOES NOT, BY ITSELF, FURNISH A VALID GROUND OF CHALLENGE 28. Equally well settled is the principle that judicial interference with an order of compulsory retirement is warranted only where the decision is shown to be based on no evidence, suffers from non-application of mind, is manifestly arbitrary or perverse or is vitiated by mala fides 3. 29. An employee cannot successfully assail an order of compulsory retirement merely by contending that another officer possessing a comparable service records was not subjected to similar action. The evaluation of an employee’s utility, integrity, efficiency and overall service profile is essentially an individualized exercise undertaken by the competent authority on the basis of the materials pertaining to that particular employee. The fact that action was taken against one employee while another similarly situated employee was left untouched does not, by itself, render the action unconstitutional or discriminatory. The doctrine of equality enshrined in Article 14 of the Constitution embodies a positive concept and does not countenance the claim of negative equality. This Court has consistently held that an illegality or omission committed in favour of one person cannot furnish a valid basis for extending the same benefits to another. If a wrong has been committed in an earlier case, it cannot be relied upon to perpetuate a similar wrong in subsequent cases 4. 30. Consequently, the power to compulsorily retire a Government servant under the applicable service rules is of wide amplitude and is conditional principally by the requirement that the authority must form its opinion bona fide and upon relevant material. The mere fact that another officer, who may appear similarly placed, was not compulsorily retired cannot, by itself, constitute a valid defence to an otherwise lawful order of compulsory retirement. IN ANY EVENT PARAMETERS UNIFORMLY APPLIED 31. Having examined the materials placed on record, this Court finds that the Circular dated 25th April 2017 prescribes a comprehensive mechanism for reviewing the cases of Government servants who have either attained the age of 50 (fifty) years or completed 20 (twenty) years of qualifying service. The object of the Circular is to identify those employees whose continued retention in service would not subserve public interest. The Circular mandates that such review shall be undertaken on the basis of uniform and objective criteria, including honesty and integrity, physical capacity, reputation, efficiency and the overall service record of the employee, particularly the pattern and gradings reflected in the Annual Confidential Reports. 32. This Court further finds that a Screening Committee was constituted in accordance with the aforesaid Circular, with due representation from both the Administrative Department and the Controlling Authority. The Committee adopted a uniform, objective and transparent methodology for assessing the suitability of all 14 (fourteen) employees for continued retention in service upon attaining the age of 50 (fifty) years. 33. Upon completion of the assessment process, the Screening Committee concluded that 5 (five) employees belonging to the clerical cadre did not satisfy the minimum standards prescribed under the Circular dated 25th April 2017. Insofar as the Appellants are concerned, their overall service-record assessment was found to be below the benchmark equivalent to Grade ‘B’, while their Confidential Reports for the period 2013-2017 disclosed a pattern of consistently low performance, declining efficiency or other indicators adverse to their continued retention in service, as duly recorded in the proceedings of the Committee. 34. A perusal of the year-wise tabulation of the gradings assigned to all 14 (fourteen) employees, as reflected in the affidavit filed by the Principal Secretary, Department of Respondent No.1-State of Chhattisgarh, read together with the Minutes of the Screening Committee, demonstrates that none of the 9 (nine) employees who were retained in service exhibited the combination of adverse factors found in the cases of the Appellants, namely, (i) an overall service-record assessment falling below the benchmark of Grade ‘B’ (ii) sustained gradings of ‘C’ during the period from 2013 to 2017 without any discernible improvement and (iii) adverse remarks pertaining to efficiency and performance. Significantly, the Committee, applying the same standards and methodology, recommended compulsory retirement not only of the Appellants but also of 3 (three) other employees similarly found to have fallen below the prescribed benchmark. 35. In the considered opinion of this Court, the recommendations made by the Screening Committee were founded upon an objective evaluation of the utility, efficiency and suitability of the employees concerned, with a view to maintaining administrative standards and ensuring efficiency in public service. The exercise was not intended to punish or penalise the Appellants. The materials on record disclose that all eligible employees, including the Appellants, were subjected to the same process of review and evaluation, without any deviation from or relaxation of or departure from the parameters prescribed under the Circular. 36. Consequently, this Court is satisfied that the parameters and standards applied while considering the cases of the Appellants for compulsory retirement were uniformly applied to all similarly situated employees and that no element of discriminatory treatment or selective application of criteria has been established. FULL SERVICE-RECORD PROFILE OF THE APPELLANTS WAS EXAMINED 37. The records further reveal that in the case of every employee, including the Appellants, the Committee initially undertook a comprehensive evaluation of the entire service records in order to determine whether the employee satisfied the minimum benchmark equivalent to Grade ‘B’, as contemplated under the Circular. Thereafter, as an ancillary and corroborative exercise, the Committee examined the Confidential Reports pertaining to the immediately preceding 5 (five) years, namely, the period from 2013 to 2017, with a view to ascertaining whether there existed any discernible pattern of declining performance, repeated low gradings or deterioration in efficiency. These Confidential Reports were not treated as the sole basis of the evaluation rather, they constituted one component of the broader assessment undertaken on the basis of the employee’s complete service profile. In any event, this Court finds that the emphasis placed upon the Confidential Reports of the preceding 5 (five) years was fully consistent with Para ‘D’ of the Circular dated 25th April 2017, which provides as under:- “D. The overall evaluation of the records of the entire service period be less than “Good” B grade. On the basis of evaluation, it should also be seen whether the work of the last 5 years of the government servant is not decreasing.” (emphasis supplied) 38. It was only after the aforesaid parameters had been applied uniformly and comprehensively to all eligible employees that the Screening Committee identified 5 (five) employees, including the present Appellants, whose overall service-record assessment fell below the prescribed benchmark and whose recent Confidential Reports disclosed patterns indicative of unsatisfactory performance, declining efficiency or lack of utility in public service. The remaining 9 (nine) employees, having satisfied the prescribed standards, were found fit for continued retention in service. PROMOTION DOES NOT EFFACE SUBSEQUENT ADVERSE ENTRIES 39. This Court is unable to accept the contention that the promotion of the Appellants rendered the subsequent adverse material irrelevant for the purposes of compulsory retirement. The principle that adverse entries lose their significance upon promotion of an employee to a higher post is confined to those entries which pre-date the promotion and are deemed to have been overlooked by the employer while conferring such promotion. That principle cannot be extended to obliterate or neutralise entries recorded after promotion, which relate to the employee’s performance in the promoted post itself. 40. In the present case, the Appellant-Chatur Singh Dhruv, was promoted from Class-IV cadre to the post of Assistant Grade-III on 14th March 2013, whereas the Appellant-Manharan Lal Sahu, was promoted from the post of Peon to Assistant Grade-III on 03rd October 2012. It is, therefore, evident that both Appellants had already assumed the promoted post prior to the recording of the Annual Confidential Reports for the years 2013 to 2017, which constituted an important component of the materials considered by the Screening Committee while undertaking the review exercise. 41. The record further reveals that Chatur Singh Dhruv was awarded a grading of ‘A’ in the year 2013 but was thereafter consistently graded ‘C’ for four consecutive years. Similarly, the Annual Confidential Reports of Manharan Lal Sahu for the period 2013-2017 disclose repeated ‘C’ gradings accompanied by remarks indicative of unsatisfactory performance. These entries, having been recorded after the Appellants had entered the promoted post, were directly relevant to an assessment of their efficiency, utility and suitability for continued retention in service. 42. Consequently, this Court is of the considered view that the subsequent Annual Confidential Reports of both Appellants reflected declined performance and unsatisfactory discharge of duties in the promoted post and cannot be rendered inconsequential merely by reason of their earlier promotions. On the contrary, the material on record indicates that the Appellants failed to maintain the requisite standards expected of incumbents occupying the promoted post. 43. Equally untenable is the contention founded upon the regularisation of the Appellants. Regularisation of service in a post already held by an employee does not amount to promotion based on merit or comparative assessment. It merely confers substantive status upon the employee in the existing post and cannot, by itself, be construed as an endorsement of the employee’s efficiency or future suitability. This Court is, therefore, of the view that neither the promotions granted to the Appellants nor their subsequent regularisation had the effect of effacing or diminishing the relevance of the below-benchmark gradings and adverse assessments recorded thereafter. THE IMPUGNED ORDERS ARE NEITHER PUNITIVE NOR STIGMATIC 44. A plain reading of the impugned orders dated 09th October 2017 discloses that they merely record that the Appellants having completed the requisite period of service were being retired in the public interest with the approval of the State Government in exercise of the power conferred under Fundamental Rule 56 and Rule 42(1)(b) of the Chhattisgarh Civil Services (Pension) Rules, 1976. 45. The orders do not contain any allegation of misconduct, any finding of culpability or any expression capable of casting a stigma upon the Appellants. The language employed therein is confined to the statutory basis of the action and is wholly consistent with the settled legal character of an order of compulsory retirement. Since both orders are identically worded in all material particulars, the order pertaining to one of the Appellants, namely, Chatur Singh Dhruv, is reproduced hereunder:- “OFFICE OF CONTROLLER, LEGAL METROLOGY, CHHATISGARH Indrawati Bhawan, Block II, 3rd Floor, Naya Raipur Email ID: [email protected] No.2141/Vi.Ma/Stha/2017 Naya Raipur, Dt. 9.10.2017 ORDER To, Sh. Chatur Singh Dhruv Assistant Grade-03 Office of Inspector Legal Metrology Mahasamund (CGS) Since, you have completed 20 Years of service as on 08.09.2009, therefore, in exercise of powers conferred by Rule 2 (A) of Chhatisgarh Original Rule 56 and Rule 42 (1) (B) of Chhatisgarh Civil Services (Pension) Rules, 1976, therefore, in the public interest and with the approval of the State Government you are hereby retired with immediate effect from 09.10.2017 without giving you notice. Sd/-illegible Dy. Controller Legal Metrology, Chhattisgarh, Naya Raipur End. No…./Vi.Ma/Stah. 2017, Naya Raipur Date: 09.10.2017” 46. This Court is also unable to accept the contention that the observations recorded by the Screening Committee in its internal proceedings or MoM impart a stigmatic character to the impugned orders. It is well settled that while examining the validity of an order of compulsory retirement, the Court must primarily have regard to the order itself. Internal deliberations, assessments and reasons forming part of the decision-making process are intended to assist the competent authority in arriving at its subjective satisfaction and do not, by themselves, render the consequential order punitive or stigmatic. In the present cases, the minutes merely record the Committee’s evaluation of the service records, efficiency, integrity and utility of the employees concerned and cannot be construed as findings of misconduct or culpability. THE COMMITTEE’S ASSESSMENT DID NOT INVOLVE ANY ADJUDICATION OF MISCONDUCT 47. As noted hereinabove, the review undertaken by the Screening Committee in September 2017 was a purely administrative exercise conducted in public interest in accordance with Fundamental Rule 56, Rule 42 of the Chhattisgarh Civil Services (Pension) Rules, 1976 and the Circular dated 25th April 2017. The exercise neither involved an adjudication of misconduct nor partook the character of a disciplinary proceeding. No stigma was attached to the Appellants and the recommendations were founded exclusively upon considerations of overall suitability, efficiency, integrity and service record, which are germane to determining whether a Government servant ought to be retained in public service. Significantly, no departmental inquiry was either pending or contemplated against either of the Appellants at the relevant time. The submission that the impugned action was adopted as a device to circumvent disciplinary proceedings is, therefore, wholly unsupported by the record and deserves to be rejected. UNCOMMUNICATED ADVERSE ENTRIES OR BELOW-BENCHMARK GRADINGS MAY BE TAKEN INTO ACCOUNT FOR THE PURPOSES OF COMPULSORY RETIREMENT 48. The submission advanced on behalf of the Appellants that adverse entries and below-benchmark gradings recorded in their Annual Confidential Reports could not have been relied upon since such entries had not been communicated to them is equally devoid of merit. The issue is no longer res integra. In Baikuntha Nath Das (supra), this Court authoritatively held that an order of compulsory retirement is not liable to be interfered with merely because the competent authority, while arriving at its opinion, took into consideration uncommunicated adverse remarks. The Court expressly observed that ‘an order of compulsory retirement is not liable to be quashed by a Court merely on the showing that while passing it, uncommunicated adverse remarks were also taken into consideration. That circumstance by itself cannot be a basis of interference…’. The contention of the Appellants, therefore, runs contrary to the settled legal position governing compulsory retirement. 49. This Court is of the considered view that the reliance placed by the Appellants upon the decisions of this Court in Dev Dutt (supra) and Sukhdev Singh (supra) is misplaced. Those decisions were rendered in the context of promotion, career progression and the requirement of communicating entries that may adversely affect an employee’s promotional prospects. The principles enunciated therein operate in a distinct field and cannot be mechanically extended to cases involving compulsory retirement, which are governed by a separate and well- settled jurisprudence. Accordingly, the said judgments offer no assistance to the Appellants. CONCLUSION 50. For all the aforesaid reasons, this Court is satisfied that the process culminating in the compulsory retirement of the Appellants was undertaken in accordance with the applicable statutory provisions and the Circular dated 25th April 2017 and that the prescribed parameters were uniformly applied. Further, the decision of the competent authority was founded upon relevant and cogent materials and that no case of arbitrariness, mala fides, perversity or violation of any constitutional or statutory guarantee has been made out warranting interference by this Court. 51. Consequently, the impugned judgments dated 12th April 2023 are affirmed and the Appeals stand dismissed. There shall be no order as to costs. Pending application(s), if any, shall stand disposed of.

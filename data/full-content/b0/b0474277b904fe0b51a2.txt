(Supreme Court of India) Summary: The Supreme Court of India dismissed the Special Leave Petition filed by the Revenue against Unitech Reliable Projects Pvt. Ltd., after condoning the delay. The short Supreme Court order does not provide independent reasons on the merits. Consequently, the Delhi High Court’s decision setting aside the penalty under Section 271(1)(c) of the Income Tax Act, 1961, remained undisturbed. The High Court had held that penalty proceedings could not be sustained where the Assessing Officer failed to specify whether the charge concerned concealment of income or furnishing inaccurate particulars of income. The dispute related to Assessment Year 2012-13. The assessee had filed its return declaring a loss of Rs. 12,83,96,466. During assessment, the Assessing Officer questioned the substantial increase in real estate project expenses. The assessee explained that it had followed the percentage completion method in earlier years and that expenses increased upon completion of the project. The Assessing Officer also questioned a component of Rs. 35,34,96,000 included in the claimed project expenditure of Rs. 78,40,58,041. The assessee subsequently surrendered the returned loss. Separately, the Assessing Officer added Rs. 3,02,436 under Section 41(1) in respect of trade payables outstanding for more than three years, treating the liabilities as having ceased or been remitted. These adjustments led to the imposition of a penalty of Rs. 4,36,41,961 by order dated 23 September 2015. The Commissioner of Income Tax (Appeals) upheld the penalty. However, the Income Tax Appellate Tribunal set it aside because the notice dated 14 March 2015, issued under Section 274 read with Section 271(1)(c), did not identify the particular statutory charge. The Tribunal relied on CIT v. SSA’s Emerald Meadows and considered the principles governing defective penalty notices. The Revenue challenged the Tribunal’s order before the Delhi High Court in ITA 291/2023. The Delhi High Court observed that even the assessment order initiating penalty proceedings referred collectively to concealment of income and furnishing inaccurate particulars without identifying the specific limb applicable to the assessee. Relying on SSA’s Emerald Meadows, Manjunatha Cotton and Ginning Factory, PCIT v. Sahara India Life Insurance Company Ltd. and PCIT v. Minu Bakshi, the Court emphasised that penalty proceedings entail civil consequences and require the Assessing Officer to apply his mind and communicate the nature of the alleged default clearly. The High Court held that the failure to identify the applicable charge was a material defect. It declined to interfere with the Tribunal’s order, finding that no substantial question of law arose. The Supreme Court subsequently dismissed the Revenue’s Special Leave Petition through a brief order stating that the delay was condoned and the petition dismissed. The dismissal left the High Court’s decision undisturbed, without adding any separate substantive reasoning or expressly declaring a new legal principle. Cases Discussed CIT v.

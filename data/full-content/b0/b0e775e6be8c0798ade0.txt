Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); Jindal India Power Limited Vs DCIT (Delhi High Court) Background of the dispute and assessment proceedings: The Income Tax Appellate Tribunal, Delhi Bench ‘A’, comprising Shri Mahavir Singh, Vice President, and Shri M. Balaganesh, Accountant Member, examined whether the waiver of ₹2,767.86 crore representing the principal component of outstanding borrowings could be taxed as income from other sources under Section 56(2)(x) of the Income-tax Act, 1961. The assessee challenged the assessment order dated 29 March 2024, passed under Section 143(3), and the appellate order dated 16 March 2026, passed by the National Faceless Appeal Centre. Although three grounds had been raised, grounds 1 and 2 were not pressed during the hearing. Accordingly, the Tribunal confined its examination to ground 3, concerning the taxability of the principal amount waived by the consortium of lenders. The central controversy was whether the extinguishment of an existing financial liability under a negotiated debt-resolution arrangement could legally constitute receipt of money without consideration. The Assessing Officer had treated the principal waiver as a taxable monetary receipt, whereas the assessee maintained that the transaction represented a reduction of a capital liability and did not involve any actual receipt of money during the relevant previous year. Financial difficulties, borrowings and classification as NPA: Jindal India Power Limited was engaged in generating thermal power and was a substantially owned subsidiary of Jindal India Powertech Limited. The company had established a thermal power project comprising two units of 600 MW each in Odisha. The project was financed through approximately ₹5,500 crore of debt obtained from a consortium of 16 banks and financial institutions, together with approximately ₹1,500 crore raised through equity and preference share allotments. The borrowings had been obtained during financial year 2014-15 for establishing the power-generation infrastructure. Although the assessee initially serviced its debt obligations regularly, persistent operational losses arising from low capacity utilisation of the power plants adversely affected its financial position. Consequently, the company defaulted on its repayment obligations, and its loan account was classified as a non-performing asset with effect from 28 December 2016. The accumulated debt included both outstanding principal and substantial unpaid interest. With the objective of recovering a commercially viable portion of their dues and facilitating revival of the power project, the lenders negotiated a comprehensive resolution arrangement with the assessee. These circumstances were significant because the waiver did not arise from a gratuitous transfer or voluntary enrichment of the borrower but formed part of a commercially negotiated settlement intended to address financial distress and recover outstanding banking exposure. Master Resolution Agreement and commercial consideration: The assessee and the consortium of lenders entered into a Master Resolution Agreement dated 29 May 2021, providing for a one-time settlement of the outstanding financing facilities. Before finalising the arrangement, the lenders had undertaken a Swiss challenge process by inviting competing bids for assignment of the outstanding debt. However, no competing bidder submitted a proposal, following which the lenders proceeded with the assessee’s resolution proposal. Under the agreement, the borrower was required to pay ₹2,450 crore as the resolution amount, while the parent entity was required to transfer 10% of the assessee’s equity shares to the lenders on a fully diluted basis. The arrangement also required replacement of non-fund-based banking facilities within the prescribed period and compliance with several other contractual obligations. The agreement contained detailed provisions relating to repayment schedules, security interests, interest obligations, withdrawal or suspension of pending recovery proceedings, maintenance of a trust and retention account, and consequences of default. Importantly, the settlement was conditional, and the lenders retained contractual rights in the event of non-compliance. The Tribunal considered these obligations material in determining whether the waiver had been granted without consideration. It concluded that the lenders were receiving substantial financial and contractual benefits in exchange for accepting the negotiated reduction of outstanding debt. Accordingly, the transaction could not be characterised as an unconditional waiver unsupported by consideration. Composition of outstanding liabilities and disputed addition: The Tribunal examined the financial details of the settlement and recorded that the total outstanding liability amounted to ₹9,431,49,07,871. This comprised principal outstanding of ₹5,217,85,93,088 and unpaid interest of ₹4,213,63,14,783. Against these liabilities, the agreed settlement amount was ₹2,450,00,00,000. Consequently, the aggregate amount recognised as waived was ₹6,981,49,07,871, comprising a principal waiver of ₹2,767,85,93,088 and an interest waiver of ₹4,213,63,14,783. The assessee credited the entire waiver to its profit and loss account as an exceptional item for the financial year ending 31 March 2022. However, while computing taxable income, it excluded the aggregate amount on the ground that the waiver was not chargeable to tax under the applicable statutory provisions. The Assessing Officer examined Section 28(iv) and Section 41(1) and ruled out their applicability to the transaction. Nevertheless, he proceeded to add ₹2,767,85,93,088 under Section 56(2)(x), treating the principal component of the waiver as money received without consideration. The Commissioner (Appeals) upheld this approach. The Tribunal therefore had to determine whether a loan waiver could satisfy the independent statutory conditions prescribed for taxation under Section 56(2)(x). Section 56(2)(x) requires actual receipt of money: The Tribunal closely examined the language of Section 56(2)(x)(a), which applies where a person receives a sum of money exceeding the prescribed threshold without consideration during the relevant previous year. It held that the provision requires an identifiable receipt of money and cannot be extended merely because an assessee obtains an economic benefit through extinguishment of a liability. In the present case, the borrowed funds had been received in earlier financial years and had been utilised for acquiring capital assets. The Master Resolution Agreement did not provide for any fresh disbursement or payment of money by the lenders to the assessee during financial year 2021-22. Instead, it relieved the borrower from the obligation to repay a portion of the existing debt, subject to fulfilment of the settlement conditions. The Tribunal emphasised the legal distinction between receiving money and being discharged from an obligation to pay money. The waiver of a liability may improve the financial position of a borrower, but that consequence does not automatically convert the transaction into receipt of a sum of money within the meaning of Section 56(2)(x). Since the statutory requirement of actual receipt was absent, the charging provision could not be invoked. This conclusion was independently reinforced by the fact that the settlement involved substantial consideration flowing to the lenders. Judicial precedents and distinction between capital and trading liabilities: The Tribunal considered several judicial decisions addressing the taxation of loan waivers and the interpretation of provisions governing business income and income from other sources. Particular reliance was placed on Jai Pal Gaba Vs ITO, in which the Chandigarh Bench examined a bank settlement and held that waiver of a loan under a genuine commercial arrangement could not automatically be treated as a receipt without consideration. The Tribunal also considered the coordinate-bench decision in ACIT Vs Infinite Buildcon Pvt. Ltd., which addressed the requirement of actual receipt for applying Section 56(2)(x). The Revenue relied upon CIT Vs Mahindra & Mahindra Ltd., reported in 404 ITR 1, and CIT Vs T.V. Sundaram Iyengar & Sons Ltd., reported in 222 ITR 344. However, the Tribunal distinguished the statutory questions involved in those decisions. It observed that Mahindra & Mahindra concerned Sections 28(iv) and 41(1), whereas the present dispute specifically involved Section 56(2)(x). The Tribunal applied the principle explained in CIT Vs Sun Engineering Works (P.) Ltd. that judicial observations must be understood in the context of the statutory questions actually considered and decided. It also noted the amendment to Section 28(iv), effective from 1 April 2024, which extended that provision to specified benefits in cash or kind. The Tribunal did not treat this subsequent amendment as applicable to assessment year 2022-23 or as altering the requirements of Section 56(2)(x). Final findings and deletion of ₹2,767.86 crore addition: After considering the Master Resolution Agreement, the nature of the original borrowings, the financial statements, the assessment findings, the submissions of both parties and the relevant judicial authorities, the Tribunal concluded that the disputed principal waiver was not taxable under Section 56(2)(x). It found that the loan had originally been obtained for acquiring capital assets and that the waiver consequently related to a capital liability. More importantly, the Tribunal held that the settlement did not involve actual receipt of money by the assessee during the relevant previous year. The statutory requirement of receiving a sum of money was therefore not fulfilled. The Tribunal further held that the waiver was supported by commercial consideration, including payment of ₹2,450 crore, transfer of 10% equity shares and performance of other contractual obligations. The arrangement was a genuine debt-resolution transaction rather than a gratuitous transfer. Accordingly, the essential conditions for invoking Section 56(2)(x) were absent. The Tribunal deleted the addition of ₹2,767,85,93,088 and allowed ground 3 of the assessee’s appeal. Since grounds 1 and 2 had not been pressed, the appeal was partly allowed. The decision establishes, on the facts of the case, that the extinguishment of an existing capital loan liability under a commercially negotiated settlement cannot be treated as money received without consideration merely because the borrower obtains financial relief. Cases Discussed 1. CIT Vs Mahindra and Mahindra Ltd. (Supreme Court); (2018) 404 ITR 1 (SC). The Assessing Officer relied heavily on this judgment while examining the taxability of the loan waiver. The Tribunal distinguished the decision, observing that the Supreme Court had considered Sections 28(iv) and 41(1) of the Income-tax Act, 1961, whereas the present controversy concerned Section 56(2)(x). The Tribunal held that observations made in the context of different statutory provisions could not be applied mechanically to treat the waiver of an existing loan liability as an actual receipt of money. 2. ACIT Vs Infinite Buildcon Pvt. Ltd. (ITAT Mumbai); ITA No. 2241/Mum/2021; 20/09/2022; AY 2018-19. The Tribunal expressly relied upon this coordinate-bench decision. The Mumbai Bench had held that waiver of a loan received in an earlier assessment year could not be equated with actual receipt of money during the year of waiver. Consequently, Section 56(2)(x) was inapplicable. The Delhi Bench followed this reasoning while deleting the addition arising from the waiver of loan principal. 3. Jai Pal Gaba Vs ITO (ITAT Chandigarh); (2019) 108 taxmann.com 494. The Tribunal reproduced and considered this decision concerning the taxability of loan waivers under Sections 28(iv), 41(1) and 56(2)(vi). The precedent was relevant to the distinction between a capital receipt, remission of a trading liability and a receipt without consideration. 4. CIT Vs T.V. Sundaram Iyengar & Sons Ltd. (Supreme Court); (1996) 222 ITR 344 (SC). The Revenue relied on this judgment to support taxation of the amount waived. The Tribunal examined the distinction between amounts received during the ordinary course of business that subsequently acquire the character of income and the waiver of a capital loan under a commercial debt-restructuring arrangement. It did not accept the Revenue’s reliance as sufficient to sustain the addition under Section 56(2)(x). 5. Chandrakant H. Shah Vs ITO (ITAT Mumbai); (2010) 124 ITD 177. The Tribunal referred to this decision concerning the interpretation of consideration under Section 56(2)(v). The Mumbai Bench had held that granting an interest-free unsecured loan could not automatically be characterised as a transaction without consideration. The Delhi Bench also noted that the decision had been approved by the Bombay High Court in Income Tax Appeal No. 3154 of 2009, decided on 06/09/2010. 6. ACIT Vs Subodh Menon (ITAT Mumbai); (2019) 175 ITD 449. This decision was discussed in the context of Section 56(2)(vii). The Mumbai Bench had recognised that a bona fide commercial transaction undertaken to satisfy banking covenants should not automatically attract provisions designed to address transfers without consideration. The Delhi Bench considered this reasoning while examining the commercial character of the assessee’s debt restructuring. 7. K.P. Varghese Vs ITO (Supreme Court); (1981) 131 ITR 597 (SC). The judgment was referred to within the discussion of ACIT Vs Subodh Menon. It concerned the relevance of legislative purpose and the circumstances in which anti-avoidance provisions should be interpreted so as not to encompass genuine transactions outside their intended scope. 8. CIT Vs Sun Engineering Works (P.) Ltd. (Supreme Court); (1992) 198 ITR 297 (SC). The Tribunal relied upon this decision for the principle that judicial observations must be understood in the context of the questions considered and decided by the Court. Applying this principle, it held that the Supreme Court’s observations in Mahindra and Mahindra could not be detached from their statutory context and used to justify an addition under Section 56(2)(x). 9. Premal P. Pandya Vs DCIT (ITAT Ahmedabad); [2026] 186 taxmann.com 640; AY 2022-23. This decision appeared in the detailed arguments reproduced in the order. It concerned the scope of Section 56(2)(x) and the contention that the provision was confined to bogus or disguised gifts. It was referred to in support of a broader interpretation of the statutory charge. 10. CIT Vs G.R. Karthikeyan (Supreme Court); (1993) 201 ITR 866 (SC). This precedent appeared in the discussion concerning the inclusive definition of income under Section 2(24) of the Income-tax Act. It was cited for the proposition that the statutory definition of income is broad and must be interpreted according to its language. 11. Union of India Vs A. Sanyasi Rao (Supreme Court); (1996) 219 ITR 330 (SC). This judgment was referred to alongside G.R. Karthikeyan in the discussion of the scope of the statutory definition of income and the operation of charging provisions. 12. I. Chatterji Vs CGT (Bombay High Court); (1990) 185 ITR 610. This judgment appeared in the discussion reproduced from Chandrakant H. Shah concerning the meaning of consideration. It distinguished consideration recognised under general contract law from consideration measurable in money or money’s worth for the purposes of taxation.

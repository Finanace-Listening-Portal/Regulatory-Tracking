Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); Indira Vs Y V Srinivas (Karnataka High Court) Summary: The Karnataka High Court allowed Indira’s criminal revision petition and set aside her conviction under Section 138 of the Negotiable Instruments Act in a cheque dishonour case involving an alleged cash loan of ₹21 lakh. The Court found that the accused had rebutted the statutory presumptions under Sections 118 and 139, while the complainant failed to establish his financial capacity or the alleged loan after that rebuttal. The complainant, Y V Srinivas, alleged that he advanced ₹21 lakh in cash in November 2020 for the education of Indira’s children and received a cheque dated 10 March 2022. The cheque was returned with the endorsement ‘Bank Branch Block’. The trial court convicted Indira and imposed a fine of ₹23,89,800, and the appellate court affirmed the conviction. The High Court examined the complainant’s admission that he had no documentary proof of the loan, charged no interest, obtained no security, and did not know basic details of the accused’s family. Although he claimed a monthly income of ₹72,000, he produced no supporting financial documents or income-tax returns to show that he possessed ₹21 lakh at the relevant time. Indira relied on a bank letter dated 8 May 2019 identifying six misplaced cheques, including the disputed cheque, and documents concerning property sales and her daughter’s education loan. The Court also noted that the cheque was drawn on Vijaya Bank, whose cheques had ceased to be valid by the time of its presentation following the bank’s merger with Bank of Baroda. It considered the discrepancies in defence testimony insufficient to defeat the overall rebuttal. Applying the limited principles governing criminal revision, the High Court held that the courts below had focused on peripheral inconsistencies while overlooking material evidence and had wrongly applied the statutory presumptions. It therefore set aside both judgments, acquitted Indira of the Section 138 offence, and directed refund of the deposited amount on proper identification. This is the revision petition filed under Sections 397 and 401 of the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973 (in short, ‘Cr.P.C.’), praying for setting aside the judgment of conviction and order of sentence dated 02.09.2024 passed in CC.No.53220/2022 on the file of XXXIV Additional Chief Judicial Magistrate, Mayo Hall Unit, Bengaluru (ACJM-34) (in short, ‘the trial Court’), confirmed in Criminal Appeal No.25337/2024 on the file of LXXIII Additional City Civil and Sessions Judge, Mayohall Unit, Bengaluru (CCH-74) (in short, ‘the appellate Court’) and to acquit the accused. 2. The parties would be referred with their ranks, as they were before the Tribunal for the sake of convenience and clarity. 3. The complainant has filed the private complaint under Section 200 Cr.P.C., before the trial Court alleging the offence under Section 138 of the Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881 (in short ‘NI Act’) against the accused. 4. The case of the complainant in nutshell is that the accused is known to the complainant through his brother-in-law-Mahesh since 5 years and both were in good terms. The accused approached the complainant through Mahesh during October-2020 and requested the complainant to lend a sum of `21,00,000/- for the purpose of education of the children of the accused. Considering the friendship and long-term association of the parties, the complainant advanced `21,00,000/- by way of cash in the month of November-2020. The accused promised to repay the said loan within one year but did not repay it. After repeated requests and demands by the complainant, the accused has issued the subject cheque bearing No.719461 dated 10.03.2022 for a sum of `21,00,000/- drawn on Vijaya Bank (Now Bank of Baroda), Vidyanagar Branch, Mandya. When the complainant presented the said cheque for encashment, it was returned with endorsement ‘Bank Branch Block’ on 11.03.2022. Hence, the complainant got issued legal notice dated 26.03.2022 through his counsel through RPAD, which was served on the accused as per the postal track consignment report. Even after its service, the accused has not repaid the cheque amount. Hence, he filed the complaint on 13.05.2022 for appropriate reliefs. 5. After service of summons, the accused appeared through her counsel. She pleaded not guilty of the offence when accusation leveled against her was read over and explained to her. Afterwards, her statement under Section 313 Cr.P.C., was recorded. Subsequently, she has cross-examined the complainant; the accused has let in her evidence and also examined two witnesses and produced some documents. 6. After hearing arguments, the trial Court came to the conclusion that, the accused has committed the offence punishable under Section 138 of NI Act and convicted her to pay fine of `23,89,800/-; in default of payment of fine, to undergo simple imprisonment for 6 months and out of the fine amount `23,84,800/- shall be payable to the complainant as compensation and remaining `5,000/- shall be remitted to the State. 7. The learned counsel for the respondent places reliance on the judgments of the Hon’ble Apex Court in Amit Kapoor v/s Ramesh Chander and Another1 and Malkeet Singh Gill v/s State of Chhattisgarh2 and judgment of Co-ordinate bench of this Court in the case of K.Kuppuraj v/s J Thrilokamurthy3 14. Having heard arguments of both sides, verifying the revision petition papers, the trial Court and the appellate Court records, the point that arises for consideration is: “Whether the revision petitioner establishes perversity or grossly erroneousness in the judgment of the trial Court and appellate Court or the judgment of the trial Court and appellate Court is glaringly unreasonable or wholly unreliable or untenable and the judgment of the trial Court and appellate Court considered immaterial or irrelevant material or no material or non-consideration of any relevant material or judicial discretion has been exercised arbitrarily or capriciously ?” 15. The finding of this Court on the above point is in Affirmative for the following: REASONS 16. The revisional jurisdiction of this Court is very limited. In this regard, the Hon’ble Apex Court and the coordinate bench of this court in following cases held as follows: 17. The Hon’ble Apex Court in Amit Kapoor’s case cited supra at paragraph Nos.11 and 12 held as follows: “11. Another very significant caution that the courts have to observe is that it cannot examine the facts, evidence and materials on record to determine whether there is sufficient material on the basis of which the case would end in a conviction; the court is concerned primarily with the allegations taken as a whole whether they will constitute an offence and, if so, is it an abuse of the process of court leading to injustice. 12. It is neither necessary nor is the court called upon to hold a full-fledged enquiry or to appreciate evidence collected by the investigating agencies to find out whether it is a case of acquittal or conviction.” 18. The Hon’ble Apex Court in Malkeet Singh’s case cited supra at paragraph Nos.10 and 11 held as follows: “10. Before adverting to the merits of the contentions, at the outset, it is apt to mention that there are concurrent findings of conviction arrived at by two courts after detailed appreciation of the material and evidence brought on record. The High Court in criminal revision against conviction is not supposed to exercise the jurisdiction alike to the appellate court and the scope of interference in revision is extremely narrow. Section 397 of the Criminal Procedure Code (in short “CrPC”) vests jurisdiction for the purpose of satisfying itself or himself as to the correctness, legality or propriety of any finding, sentence or order, recorded or passed, and as to the regularity of any proceedings of such inferior court. The object of the provision is to set right a patent defect or an error of jurisdiction or law. There has to be well-founded error which is to be determined on the merits of individual case. It is also well settled that while considering the same, the Revisional Court does not dwell at length upon the facts and evidence of the case to reverse those findings. 11. This Court in Manju Ram Kalitu v. State of Assam, while dealing with the scope of reappreciation of evidence by higher Court in criminal revision. observed in paras 9, 10 and 11 of the judgment as under: (SCC pp. 333-34) “9. So far as Issue 1 is concerned i.e. as to whether the appellant b got married with Smt Ranju Sarma, is a pure question of fact. All the three courts below have given concurrent finding regarding the factum of marriage and its validity. It has been held to be a valid marriage. It is a settled legal proposition that if the courts below have recorded the finding of fact, the question of reappreciation of evidence by the third court does not arise unless it is found to be totally perverse. The higher court does not sit as a regular court of appeal. Its function is to ensure that law is being properly administered. Such a court cannot embark upon fruitless task of determining the issues by reappreciating the evidence. 10. This Court would not ordinarily interfere with the concurrent findings on pure questions of fact and review the evidence again unless there are exceptional circumstances justifying the departure from the d normal practice. ‘9…. The position may undoubtedly be different if the inference is one of law from [the] facts admitted and proved or where the finding of fact is materially affected by violation of any rule of law or procedure.’ (Vide Sriniwas Ram Kumar v. Mahabir Prasad, SCC p. 139, para 9) 11. Thus, it is evident from the above that this Court being the fourth court should not interfere with the exercise of discretion by the courts below as the said courts have exercised their discretion in good faith giving due weight to relevant material and without being swayed by any irrelevant material. Even if two views are possible on the question of fact, we, being the fourth court, should not interfere even though we may exercise discretion differently had the case come before us initially. In view of the above, we are not inclined to interfere with the finding of fact so far as the issue of bigamy is concerned nor the quantum of punishment on this count is required to be interfered with.” 19. The Co-ordinate bench of this Court in K.Kuppuraj’s case cited supra at paragraph No.5 held as follows: “5. This Court in exercise of power under Section 397 of Cr.P.C. cannot re-appreciate the evidence and arrive at a different conclusion, even if different view is possible from the evidence. The jurisdiction under Section 397 of Cr.P.C. could be exercised only when the decision under challenge is found grossly erroneous, non-compliance with the provisions of law; finding of fact affecting the decision is not based on evidence; non-consideration of the material evidence and that the lower court has exercised the discretion arbitrarily or perversely and acted in excess of its jurisdiction or abused its power resulting in failure of justice. No such errors or illegalities are found in the impugned order.” 20. In the case of Kuntegowda v/s Thurubaiah in Criminal Appeal No.3654/2026, (Arising out of Special Leave Petition (Criminal) No.2247 of 2024 dated 04.08.2026 reported in 2026 SCC OnLine SC 1485, the Hon’ble Apex Court at paragraph Nos.7.1. to 7.3 has discussed the revisional jurisdiction of this Court, which reads as follows: “7.1 This Court, in the State of Maharashtra vs. Jagmohan Singh Kuldip Singh Anand, (2004) 7 SCC 659 observed that the High Court, in exercise of its revisional jurisdiction, cannot embark upon an in-depth roving re-examination of the oral evidence and medical evidence and come to a conclusion contrary to the consistent one reached by two courts below. In the facts of the present case, in the impugned judgment, the High Court gravely erred in upsetting the concurrent findings of conviction of the trial court and the Appellate Court by substituting its own conclusions and reasoning on the merits of the case and thereby erred in setting aside the well-reasoned and correct judgement and orders of the trial and appellate courts. 7.2 Upon perusal of the impugned judgment and order dated 06.10.2023, it is apparent that the High Court went into great detail into each of the testimonies, documents and merits of the case which could have been avoided especially when the subject matter had come under its revisional jurisdiction. Instead, acting as an appellate Court, the High Court deemed it fit to go into the merits of the case, something which is generally impermissible unless a glaring contradiction is apparent on the face of record. In State of Kerala vs. Puttumana Illath Jathavedan Namboodiri, (1999) 2 SCC 452, while considering the scope of the revisional jurisdiction of the High Court this Court has laid down the following: “5…. In its revisional jurisdiction, the High Court can call for and examine the record of any proceedings for the purpose of satisfying itself as to the correctness, legality or propriety of any finding, sentence or order. In other words, the jurisdiction is one of supervisory jurisdiction exercised by the High Court for correcting miscarriage of justice. But the said revisional power cannot be equated with the power of an appellate court nor can it be treated even as a second appellate jurisdiction. Ordinarily, therefore, it would not be appropriate for the High Court to reappreciate the evidence and come to its own conclusion on the same when the evidence has already been appreciated by the Magistrate as well as the Sessions Judge in appeal, unless any glaring feature is brought to the notice of the High Court which would otherwise tantamount to gross miscarriage of justice. On scrutinizing the impugned judgment of the High Court from the aforesaid standpoint, we have no hesitation to come to the conclusion that the High Court exceeded its jurisdiction in interfering with the conviction of the Respondent by reappreciating the oral evidence….” 7.3. The contours for exercise of revisional jurisdiction has been well settled by the judicial dicta of this Court wherein time and again it has been observed that the High Court shall not interfere with the orders of the lower court unless: i. The order or finding of the lower court is perverse, grossly erroneous, glaringly unreasonable or wholly unreliable or untenable in law. ii. The lower court has passed the impugned order after considering an immaterial or irrelevant material or no material at all. iii. There is a non-consideration of any relevant material or the judicial discretion has been exercised arbitrarily or capriciously.” 21. On careful perusal of the above said citations, and the principles noted in those citations, this court can interfere with the judgment of the trial Court confirmed by appellate Court only if they are perverse or grossly erroneous, or grossly unreasonable, or wholly unreliable, or wholly untenable, or the trial Court considered an immaterial or irrelevant, material or no material or not at all relevant material or judicial discretion has been exercised arbitrarily and capriciously. 22. The alleged offence in this case is the offence under Section 138 of NI Act; to attract the offence under Section 138 of NI Act, the complainant has to satisfy the following ingredients: (i) that there is legally enforceable debt; a. that the cheque was drawn from the account of bank for the discharge in whole or in part of any debt or other liability; b. that the cheque has been presented to the bank within a period of six months from the date on which it is drawn or within the period of its validity, whichever is earlier; c. that cheque so issued has been returned due to insufficiency of funds; d. the payee or the holder in due course of the cheque makes a demand for the payment of the said amount of money by giving a notice in writing to the drawer of the cheque, within 15 days of the receipt of information by him from the bank regarding the return of the cheque as unpaid; e. the drawer of such cheque fails to make payment of the said amount of money to the payee or the holder in due course of the cheque within 15 days of the receipt of the said notice. 23. If the aforementioned ingredients are satisfied, then there shall be a presumption under Section 139 of the N.I. Act. Always, the cheque is a bill of exchange and thus there shall be a presumption under Section 118 of the NI Act. There is also statutory presumption under Section 139 of NI Act that there exists legally recoverable debt and the cheque is issued for recovery of legally recoverable debt either wholly or in part. But the presumptions available under Sections 118 and 139 of the NI Act are rebuttable presumptions. 24. With this background, the facts of the present case are to be analysed and to verify whether the trial Court and the appellate Court have not examined the records produced by either party or erroneously appreciated the evidence. 25. It is the contention of the complainant that the accused has borrowed a sum of `21,00,000/- from the complainant on 20.11.2020 and to repay the said amount, the accused has issued the subject cheque dated 10.03.2022 in favour of the complainant; when it is presented for encashment, it was returned dishonored with an endorsement ‘Bank Branch Block’ dated 11.03.2022. There are judgments of Hon’ble Apex Court that even though the cheque is dishonoured due to ‘bank branch block’ the offence under Section 138 would be attracted. 26. When once the complainant is able to establish this clause No.(iii) to (vi) then, automatically the statutory presumption under Section 139 of NI Act would be attracted in favour of the complainant that, there exists a legally enforceable debt and the cheque is issued for the discharge in whole or in part of any other liability. 27. There is specific suggestion to the complainant regarding his financial capacity. He was also cross-examined about his income and other details which reveal that, the accused is disputing the financial capacity of the complainant. 61. The accused has not put a mere suggestion denying the financial capacity of the complainant, but she has elicited something from the cross-examination of the complainant regarding his income and he was not an income tax payee etc., as discussed above and further, this huge lending of `21,00,000/- only through cash; Under these circumstances, it was incumbent upon the complainant to produce some evidence to show his financial capacity which he did not do. 62. The accused has taken two defenses. One is financial capacity of the complainant which is discussed as above. Another is she lost the cheque and it was misused by the complainant. To substantiate it, she examined her husband and her neighbour as DWs.2 and 3. DWs.2 and 3 have taken a little bit different version from the accused that the cheque in question was stolen by one Mahesh from the house of the accused but that is not the contention of the accused. 63. The learned counsel for the complainant vehemently would submit that one Mahesh introduced the accused to the complainant and said Mahesh is his brother-in-law. In this regard, the accused denied the suggestion that she knows this Mahesh. However, her husband in his evidence has deposed that one Mahesh was tenant under him. It is not suggested to the accused that said Mahesh is tenant under her husband but only suggested to her that one Mahesh is known to her, which she denies. Under these circumstances, it cannot be said that, the accused knows Mahesh and she is not deposing truth on this point. Mahesh might be the tenant under her husband and not under her to say that she is having personal knowledge about the said tenant. Admittedly, this accused is a housewife and not having any avocation. Her educational qualification is not questioned in the cross-examination. 64. Another point stressed by the learned counsel for the complainant is that similar 4-5 cases are registered against the accused wherein also she has taken the similar defence. 65. In this regard, in the cross-examination, the accused has admitted that Kanyakumari, Srinidhi and Mahesh have filed cheque dishonour cases against her. However, she has deposed that all those three persons are strangers to her and she does not know how they got the cheque leaves. She also admitted that Mahesh has filed a suit against her and obtained decree against her but the said decree was challenged in an appeal. 66. According to the accused, she has not lodged any complaint to the police regarding loss of her cheques but her husband has deposed that he has lodged the complaint; anyway the complaint details are not produced. 67. Only because there are some discrepancies in the evidence of the accused and her witnesses regarding how she lost the cheques, nothing more was elicited in their cross-examination to disbelieve their version. 68. As discussed above, the initial burden is on the accused to rebut the presumptions under Sections 118 and 139 of NI Act. 69. The above discussion reveals that the accused has rebutted those presumptions. To summarize it, lending of `21,00,000/- cash to an unknown person; no document is taken at the time of lending; further, the complainant is getting a meager salary of `72,000/- per month and no material is produced to show that he had `21,00,000/- at the time of lending. The complainant does not know anything about family members of the accused, which substantiates the contention of accused that complainant is stranger to her. By eliciting these facts, the accused has rebutted the presumptions in favour of the complainant. However, after such rebuttal, the complainant has not produced any evidence to prove his contention. 70. It is to be noted here that only in the case of complete wrong appreciation of evidence or without examining the materials available on record, the trial Court and appellate Court have passed the judgments; then and then only the revisional Court can interfere with it. 71. The above discussion reveals that the trial Court and the appellate Court have given importance only to the cross-examination of DWs.1 to 3 regarding how they have misplaced the cheques or whether cheques were stolen and DWs.2 and 3 have not identified the signature of the accused in her deposition and other documents like vakalathnama. But the intrinsic fact that, the complainant has lent money of `21,00,000/- without any document; kept quiet for a period of 15 months and his income was only `72,000/- per month are completely ignored by the trial Court and the appellate Court. Thus, the judgment of the trial Court and the appellate Court are grossly erroneous and wrong appreciation of evidence and further, the reasoning given by the trial Court and the appellate Court is wholly unreliable and untenable based on the irrelevant material and wrong appreciation of presumptions under Sections 118 and 139 of NI Act. Hence, it requires interference. 72. Accordingly, this Court passes the following: ORDER 73. The revision petition is allowed. 74. The accused is acquitted for the offence punishable under Section 138 of NI Act. 76. The amount in deposit shall be refunded to the accused on proper identification.

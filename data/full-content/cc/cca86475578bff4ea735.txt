Summary: The Indian Premier League (IPL) operates through distinct commercial supplies, including match admission, sponsorship, media and digital rights, franchise licences, stadium advertising, player services, merchandise and hospitality. This article examines how the GST 2.0 rate changes affect each stream and why classification, contractual terms and the identity of the supplier and recipient remain decisive. IPL admission attracts the 40% rate, while many commercial services continue at 18%, subject to the applicable notifications and the true nature of the transaction. Bundled hospitality packages raise composite- and mixed-supply questions under Section 8 of the CGST Act; merely splitting an invoice does not necessarily create independent supplies. The Punjab Appellate Authority for Advance Ruling in KPH Dream Cricket distinguished complimentary tickets without consideration from supplies to related or distinct persons under Schedule I, making the source article’s broader claim that every free ticket is taxable incorrect. Sponsorship reverse-charge liability also depends on the supplier’s legal status following the January 2025 amendment. The discussion extends to overseas media licences, advertising services provided to foreign clients, domestic and overseas player engagements, endorsements, venue catering and input tax credit. Businesses must check place-of-supply rules, registration, valuation, reverse-charge conditions, documentary support and restrictions on credit before settling GST positions. The accompanying matrices summarise the source’s proposed treatment of IPL revenue streams, but transaction-specific classification and rate verification remain necessary, particularly for hospitality, goods and cross-border arrangements. Chasing Runs, Counting Revenue – GST 2.0 & IPL Tax Scorecard The Indian Premier League is far more than a cricket tournament not only in India but across the globe setting a benchmark is this prestigious sporting event in cricket. It is a well strategically layered commercial ecosystem of franchise licenses, broadcast rights, gate receipts, sponsorships, stadium advertising, player contracts, endorsements, merchandise and hospitality. Each layer is a separate taxable affiliation, and GST does not treat the IPL as one indivisible transaction. The decisive questions for every stream are the same: what is supplied, who supplies it, who receives it, what is the consideration, where is the place of supply, is the recipient registered, and does any exemption, composition scheme, reverse-charge notification or input tax credit (ITC) restriction apply. So many questions require focused answers to avoid protracted litigation in future. This article is an effort to walks through those questions for each stakeholder viz., the Board of Control for Cricket in India (BCCI) and the IPL, franchises, broadcasters, sponsors, advertisers, ticketing platforms, venue operators, players, agents and brands. It also reflects the post-GST 2.0 rate structure and process reforms in which the headline change for the IPL is the move of match admission from 28% to the new 40% slab making it a premium luxury Supply. 2. The foundational principle in GST law here is not whether the transaction is “IPL – related”. Under GST everything is Supply until proven otherwise. It is whether the transaction is a supply of goods or services under Section 7 of the Central Goods and Services Tax (CGST) Act, 2017 and the Integrated GST (IGST) Act, 2017. Once a supply is established, the next questions are taxability, classification, rate, place of supply, valuation, ITC eligibility and fixing the exact liability. 3. The Central Board of Indirect Taxes and Customs (CBIC), in Circular No. 177/09/2022-TRU, while clarifying the taxability of Supply of various services, reiterated the foundational position that all goods and services are taxable unless specifically exempted or declared to be neither a supply of goods nor a supply of services under Schedule III of the CGST Act 2017. The circular’s logic carries directly to sport too and inter-alia includes a franchise, broadcaster, sponsor, advertiser, venue operator, player or agent who cannot assume exemption merely because the activity is connected with cricket. Commercial sports events are fully taxable unless a statutory exemption explicitly applies. The practical consequence is that IPL contracts should be structured with clearly separated consideration for each service supplied. A single omnibus “IPL rights” invoice will invite avoidable disputes over classification, valuation, ITC and place of supply, for sure. 4. After the GST 2.0 rate rationalization has been enacted with the recommendation of the GST Council, India’s historical four-slab structure of 5%, 12%, 18% and 28% has been shrunk into a simplified structure as a 0% rate for essentials, 5% for merit goods, 18% as the standard rate for most goods and services, and a dedicated 40% de-merit or “sin” slab on luxury supplies. The 12% and 28% slabs have been dismantled. 5. There are three consequences that follow for the IPL, and accordingly, Admission cost moves up. IPL match ticketing is not an exempted commercial sporting activity and has been reclassified from 28% into the 40% luxury/de-merit slab. Most commercial services stay put. The existing GST rate of taxes on Franchise fees, media rights, sponsorship, advertising and player services remain at 18%. Merchandise averages. Items/activities previously taxed at 12% have been consolidated into the 5% or 18% brackets as the case may be. The GST Council has thereby explicitly drawn a stark line between recognised, federation-organised sport and private franchise entertainment. The IPL categorization has been made very clear that it sits on the commercial side of that line. 6. There are so many associated activities bundled in the conduct of an IPL event leading to classification of Composite or Mixed supplies theory. We need to understand why unbundling it is not a legal shield. The widening gap between the 40% admission rate and the 18% standard rate raises the stakes of bundling. Under Section 8 of the CGST Act, a composite or mixed supply is taxed according to its principal supply or in the case of a mixed supply, at the highest rate among the components. Let us try to understand it with an example. Consider a hospitality suite in the cricket stadium, that combines match tickets (40% GST rate), corporate catering (18% GST rate) and branded merchandise (5% or 18% GST rate as the case may be). If the contract is billed as a single undifferentiated package, there is a real risk that it is treated as a mixed supply under Section 8(b), of the CGST Act 2017, drawing the peak 40% rate across the entire contract amount. Whereas, itemizing stadium seating, fine dining, intellectual property licensing and promotional player appearances into separately priced line items makes it the most effective protection available to franchises, hospitality managers and agencies. Divide and charge may be effective tax management. Admission to an IPL match is treated as admission to an entertainment event, specifically a sporting event. Before GST 2.0, the rate schedule prescribed 28% GST for such admission and CBIC Circular No. 164/20/2021-GST under para 10.2, confirmed that entry 34(iiia) applies to admission to entertainment events or amusement facilities, including sporting events “such as Indian Premier League and the like”. That remains the clearest IPL specific CBIC authority on ticket taxation and it settled that a paid IPL ticket is taxable as an admission service. Therefore, under GST 2.0, the rate for admission to IPL matches (SAC 999692) has risen from 28% to 40%, with effect from September 22, 2025, and levied as 20% CGST plus 20% SGST, or 40% IGST. IPL tickets are now taxed on par with casinos, online money gaming and luxury motor vehicles, so to say. Divergence from recognised sporting events. Sporting events organized by recognised national or international federations, such as bilateral BCCI Tests and ODIs or Olympic qualifiers, are exempt where the ticket is priced up to ₹500 and are taxed at 18% where the ticket exceeds ₹500 on full amount. The IPL, as a commercial franchise property is outside that category, attracts the full 40% GST ad-velorem. Premium seating and bundles. The 40% rate is not confined to a basic stadium seat. Any VIP packages, hospitality boxes and bundled experiences must be unbundled if they are to be charged under respective rates. The admission component bears the admission rate, while catering, merchandise, transport, accommodation and other separately identifiable services are taxed under their own provisions unless they are supplied together and charged on aggregate value taking them as a Composite supply of admission to sporting events. Ticketing platforms. The franchise, BCCI or ticketing entity making the supply must account for the output tax. Where tickets are sold online, the platform may act as an e-commerce operator or agent, and the contractual arrangement determines whether it collects tax on behalf of the supplier, issues invoices, or is itself liable under Section 9(5) of the CGST Act, 2017 as a aggregator. Under Section 52, it must also collect tax at source (TCS) where mandated. Here to, the Payment gateways and invoicing systems must be calibrated for the 40% levy. The effective price impact. Moving from 28% to 40% after GST 2.0, raises the tax-inclusive price of a ticket by roughly 9.4%. All Franchises using dynamic pricing algorithms must decide whether to absorb part of the increase or risk softer occupancy at non-marquee fixtures. All these will be but business decisions. Complimentary IPL Tickets: Supply and ITC Treatment 8. A particularly important IPL specific query is the ruling of the Punjab Appellate Authority for Advance Ruling (AAAR) in the case of KPH Dream Cricket Pvt. Ltd., Order No. 01/AAAR/CGST/KPH/2022. The specific Question was Is the distribution of complimentary IPL tickets a “supply” under the CGST Act, 2017? The Punjab AAAR distinguished complimentary tickets issued without consideration from tickets supplied to related or distinct persons. It held that free complimentary tickets are not a supply merely because they promote business; Schedule I may apply where the recipient is a related or distinct person. The AAAR also distinguished input tax credit consequences for taxable and non-taxable complimentary distributions. This appellate ruling must not be confused with the earlier Punjab AAR view. Deemed supply: not automatic for all complimentary tickets; assess consideration and Schedule I. Valuation: where a taxable supply exists, determine value under Section 15 and applicable valuation rules; the 40% admission rate applies only if the supply falls within that entry. Input tax credit: the AAAR denied ITC for complimentary tickets outside taxable supply, while recognising eligibility where the distribution qualifies as a taxable Schedule I supply, subject to the Act. Documentation. Franchises should record the recipient, purpose, number of tickets, face value and commercial rationale to arrive at the exact liability as well as ineligible ITC as a blocked credit. The sensible response for franchise marketing teams is to fold ticket allocations into the contractual consideration of sponsorship agreements, rather than distributing free passes at discretion. Sponsorship Services and the Reverse Charge 9. Sponsorship of IPL matches, teams, tournaments, awards, replays, tosses, boundary ropes, digital inventory or jerseys is a taxable sponsorship service at 18% (SAC 998399). The sponsor receives visibility, association with the IPL or franchise brand and often specified marketing rights. The liabilities in respect of Sponsorship services had changed with effect from 16th January 2025. The current rules are tabulated as below: – Service Provider Service Recipient Applicable Mechanism Taxpayer / Liable Party Non-Body Corporate (e.g., individual, trust, unregistered entity) Body Corporate or Partnership Firm RCM (Reverse Charge) Service Recipient pays GST Body Corporate (e.g., Pvt Ltd, Ltd, LLP) Any Recipient (Body Corporate or Non-Body Corporate) FCM (Forward Charge) Service Supplier pays GST Non-Body Corporate Non-Body Corporate (e.g., individual, prop firm) FCM (Forward Charge) Service Supplier pays GST The exemption does not reach the IPL. Again, Entry 53 of Notification No. 12/2017-Central Tax (Rate) exempts sponsorship of sporting events only when organised by specified bodies like national sports federations, affiliated sports bodies, the Association of Indian Universities, the School Games Federation of India, specified disability-sports bodies, the Central Civil Services Cultural and Sports Board, the Indian Olympic Association for national games, and Panchayat Yuva Krida Aur Khel Abhiyan (PYKKA). The IPL falls within none of these categories, so all IPL sponsorships are taxable. Reverse charge applies. Under Entry 4 of Notification No. 13/2017-Central Tax (Rate), where sponsorship services are supplied by a non-body corporate to a body corporate or partnership firm, the liability to pay 18% GST shifts to the recipient. The corporate sponsor discharges tax through its electronic cash ledger and then avails ITC, while the BCCI or franchise issues a bill of supply or commercial invoice indicating that tax is payable under reverse charge (RCM). Delayed cash-ledger payments or ITC mismatches can attract interest and departmental scrutiny. In other situations it is under Forward Charge mechanism as usual. Sponsors should insist that agreements separate the sponsorship fee from advertising inventory, hospitality, tickets, merchandise, player-access rights, content production and reimbursements. A bundled “sponsorship package” may contain several supplies with different GST characters/rates and being a mixed supply will attract the highest rate of tax from among the Supplies. Broadcast and Digital Media Rights 10. The grant of television, satellite, cable, radio, OTT, streaming, highlights, clips, archive and sub-licensing rights is a taxable service. The supplier, generally BCCI/IPL or the rights holder, grants the licensee a right to broadcast, exhibit, reproduce, distribute or exploit IPL content. The classification is a licensing service for the right to use intellectual property (SAC 997332), taxable at 18% under forward charge. The Karnataka Authority for Advance Ruling has examined the classification of licensing services for the right to broadcast and show original films, sound recordings, radio and television programmes, in the case of M/s. J.B. Exhibitors (Order No. KAR ADRG 30/2023 dated September 15, 2023). Although that ruling concerns media licensing generally and not the IPL specifically, it supports the principle that granting a right to broadcast or exhibit content is a licensing service rather than a sale of goods. Domestic licensees. BCCI/IPL charges IGST at 18% on inter-State supplies, or CGST and SGST/UTGST at 9% each where supplier and recipient are in the same State or Union Territory. Broadcasters claim ITC subject to Section 16 of the CGST Act and use it against downstream advertising and subscription revenues. Overseas licensees. Rights granted to overseas platforms qualify as zero-rated exports under Section 2(6) of the IGST Act, provided the recipient is outside India, the place of supply is outside India and proceeds are realised in convertible foreign exchange. Exporters operate under a Letter of Undertaking (LUT) and may claim refunds. If the recipient or place of supply is in India, IGST at 18% applies as if it is a domestic transaction. Contract drafting. A media-rights agreement should name the licensee, territory, platform, term, exclusivity and sublicensing rights. It may contain separable supplies, such as live broadcast, clips and highlights, sponsorship integration, advertising inventory, production and technology services. Therefore, the service of distributing rights to exhibit a film (from a distributor to an exhibitor) falls under SAC 999614 (Motion picture, videotape, and television program distribution services) rather than SAC 997332 (Licensing services for the right to broadcast and show original films, sound recordings, radio, and television programmes). The supply of Services are classified under SAC 999614 attract an 18% GST rate and other debates for classifying them under different SAC like 999614 have been laid to rest. Franchise Fees and Revenue Sharing with BCCI 11. The annual fee paid by a franchise to BCCI is also a taxable supply. The consideration is for a bundle of rights including participation in the league, use of the franchise identity, access to central revenues and the right to operate a team. It is classified as licensing of commercial or intellectual-property rights (SAC 9973/9983) and taxed at 18%, because it is neither admission to an event nor a sponsorship. The franchise can claim full ITC under Section 16 provided it holds a valid tax invoice and the supplier has paid the tax, while satisfying other conditions. Direct tax versus indirect tax. In Knight Riders Sports Pvt. Ltd., the income-tax authorities (the Income Tax Appellate Tribunal) held that the annual franchise fee paid to BCCI was allowable as revenue expenditure, since it conferred only a limited annual participation right and not an enduring capital asset. That conclusion answers whether the expense is deductible for income tax. It does not alter GST. The fee remains a taxable supply at 18% on the consideration. Labels are not conclusive. BCCI disclosures show IPL payments divided into heads such as franchise payments, central licensing amounts and net central revenue (NCR) shares. These labels/head of accounts do not determine GST treatment. Each payment must be examined for its true nature as generally participation right, license fee, revenue share, sponsorship allocation, media-rights are treated as consideration under GST and attract GST liabilities. Stadium and On-Field Advertising 12. Perimeter LED boards, boundary cushions, static stadium boards, ground signage, jersey branding, naming rights, dugout branding and in-broadcast placements are advertising services (SAC 998361) or licences of commercial rights, taxable at 18% under forward charge. The franchise or BCCI entity granting the right is the supplier, and the advertiser can claim ITC where the advertising is used in its taxable business and invoice and payment conditions are met. CBIC Circular No. 230/24/2024-GST clarifies that an Indian advertising agency providing a comprehensive advertising solution to a foreign client on a principal-to-principal basis is not an intermediary merely because it procures Indian media space. The place of supply ordinarily follows Section 13(2), subject to the actual contractual facts and export conditions. A genuine facilitation-only arrangement must be assessed separately under the intermediary provisions. The distinction between agent and principal therefore matters enormously. An agency that buys and resells inventory, creates content or invoices in its own name looks like a principal; one that only arranges space for a foreign principal looks like an intermediary. Contracts and invoicing must match commercial reality. Player Auctions and Remuneration 13. The auction is not itself a taxable event; GST follows the underlying supply. A payment under an IPL contract is consideration for the player’s sporting, performance and related services (SAC 9996/999651), taxable at 18%. Auction price, signing bonus, retention amount, match fee, performance bonus and reimbursements must each be examined. A genuine reimbursement of actual expenses, properly documented, will need separate treatment as they may be exclusions from the Transaction Value under Section 15 (3) of CGST Act 2017 especially after radical changes in post supply discounts. Domestic players. Players are treated as independent contractors. Once an Indian player’s aggregate turnover exceeds ₹20 lakh, registration is required and 18% GST is charged under forward charge on auction contracts, match fees and retention payments. Overseas players. Payments to overseas players for matches played in India constitute an import of services. Under Notification No. 10/2017-Integrated Tax (Rate) and Section 5(3) of the IGST Act, 2017 the franchise must self-assess and pay 18% IGST under reverse charge and then claim input tax credit acting on behalf of the foreign players. Agents. An agent’s commission for securing or managing a player contract is an intermediary or agency service taxable at 18%. Where the agent is non-resident and the place of supply is India, the franchise bears reverse-charge liability. A common misconception is that all player payments automatically attract GST. The correct position turns on whether the payment is consideration for a service, the residence of supplier and recipient, GST registration, and whether a reverse-charge notification applies. Income-tax on player payments is a separate obligation and does not discharge GST per-se. Brand Endorsements by Players 14. A player’s endorsement of a franchise, sponsor or product is a taxable service (SAC 998365/9983), whether classified as endorsement, advertising or promotion, or as a licence to use the player’s name, image, likeness and persona. The rate is 18%. A GST-registered domestic player charges GST under forward charge. Where the player is non-resident, or the service falls within a reverse-charge category, the Indian brand pays IGST under RCM provided the place of supply is India, which is subsequently eligible for ITC subject to the obligations under Section 16 & 17(5) of the CGST Act 2017. 15. In order to have absolute clarity on the GST liabilities, endorsement agreements should separately state the base fee, performance bonus, image-rights, licence fee, appearance fee, social-media deliverables, exclusivity, territory, usage period and expense reimbursement. Each element may have a different GST character even when documented as a single deal and separating them is commercially useful tool for effective Tax liability management. Ancillary Supplies: Merchandise, Food and Hospitality 16. Apart from all above, the following supply in relation to IPL shall also attract GST liability, generally as Supply of Goods. Merchandise Merchandise. With the 12% slab abolished, replica jerseys, apparel, caps and accessories are taxed at 5% for apparel meeting threshold specifications, or at 18% for premium merchandise and headgear. The change also removes inverted-duty problems for peripheral merchandise. Food and Beverages Food and beverages. Stand-alone kiosks and concourse counter sales operate at 5% GST without ITC. In-lounge hospitality catering and VIP buffets are outdoor catering taxable at 18% with full ITC. Whether a supply is a restaurant service or a sale of goods depends on the facts like where it is served and how it is served. IPL GST Rate Matrix After GST 2.0 17. Every person associated with IPL eco system, should assess his tax liability in a most diligent manner and if liable either Forward charge or Reverse Charge, should ensure that registration, invoicing, e-invoicing and return filing reflect the real supply chain. The supplier must issue a tax invoice with the prescribed particulars, charge the correct tax and report the supply in the relevant return. E-invoicing credentials are mandatory above the prescribed turnover threshold of ₹5 crore, and their absence will draw departmental scrutiny and probable consequent litigations. 19. Under Section 16 of the CGST Act, 2017 ITC is available only where the input or input service is used in the course or furtherance of business, the tax has actually been paid to the Government, the recipient holds a valid tax invoice and the other statutory conditions are met. Blocked credits, including those on gifts and free samples such as complimentary tickets, are denied. Businesses entities should maintain separate accounting for promotional tickets, hospitality, employee entertainment and sponsor-hosting expenses. 20. Cross-border arrangements demand special care and attention as there will be several participants in this effort from India and abroad. The place-of-supply rules in Sections 12 and 13 of the IGST Act,2017 read with Circular No. 230/24/2024-GST, determine whether IGST is payable in India, whether a supply is zero-rated, or whether an Indian intermediary must discharge tax or otherwise. The Reverse-charge payments, whether on sponsorship or overseas players, must be discharged only through the electronic cash ledger on time, because late payment attracts interest. Conclusion: IPL GST Compliance Priorities 21. GST 2.0 is a pivotal realignment of the fiscal treatment of India’s premier commercial sports event. By reducing the four-tier structure into a streamlined one and placing franchise sport in the 40% slab, the GST Council has separated public-interest athletic competition from private entertainment sporting activities. Accordingly, Four lessons stand out for every IPL stakeholder. Gate economics have changed. The rise from 28% to 40% adds about 9.4% to the final price of a ticket. Franchises must price carefully and ensure that ticketing systems handle the 40% forward charge cleanly. Promotional ticketing requires separate GST and ITC analysis. The Punjab AAAR did not treat all free tickets as taxable; supplies to related or distinct persons may fall under Schedule I. Unbundling is legal protection. The gap between 40% and 18% magnifies mixed-supply risk under Section 8(b). Seating, catering, IP licensing and player appearances should appear as separate, independently priced line items, never in an omnibus invoice. Reverse-charge rigor is non-negotiable. Multi-crore sponsorship deals and overseas player contracts remain governed by RCM under Notification No. 13/2017-Central Tax (Rate) and Notification No. 10/2017-Integrated Tax (Rate). ITC leakage, delayed cash payments or missing e-invoicing set-ups will trigger interest and scrutiny. This apart other enduring principles remain that, IPL sponsorship cannot shelter under the sporting-event exemption; player auction value is not itself the taxable event, because GST follows the underlying service or license; income-tax treatment, as in Knight Riders decision mentioned above, is separate from GST; and cross-border advertising ultimately turns on the intermediary-versus-principal distinction. 22. GST 2.0 simplifies classification and eases peripheral merchandise, but for the IPL’s ecosystem it demands vigilant legal review, unbundled documentation and constant audit readiness. In this league, the best tax strategy is the one drafted into the comprehensive contract incorporating all the activities, roles and responsibilities before the first ball is bowled, else compliance obligations may go for a toss!!!!! 23. The IPL may be decided by sixes and super overs, but GST liabilities are decided by invoices, classifications and compliance discipline. A taxpayer who keeps proper books, unbundles supplies, pays taxes on time and claims credits correctly is simply rotating the strike and keeping the scoreboard ticking. Those who ignore the rules, however, risk being caught behind by audit, stumped by reverse-charge provisions, run out on documentation gaps and eventually hit for a penalty hat-trick. 24. In the league of GST 2.0, compliance is the safest shot in the book and the only strategy that guarantees a place in the winners’ circle. Success in GST will belong not merely to those who score the most runs, but to those who maintain the cleanest scorecard. Its because in this innings, the Tax authorities are ever-watchful third umpire, and every stakeholder must ensure that when the compliance replay appears on the big screen, the verdict remains a confident ‘NOT OUT’.

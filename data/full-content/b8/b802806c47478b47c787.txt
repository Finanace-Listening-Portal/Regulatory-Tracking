(ITAT Mumbai) Summary: The Income Tax Appellate Tribunal (ITAT), Mumbai Bench “A”, dismissed the Revenue’s appeals against Lupin Investment Pvt. Ltd. for Assessment Years (AYs) 2009-10 and 2010-11, upholding the Commissioner of Income Tax (Appeals) [CIT(A)] decision quashing reassessment proceedings initiated over share application money received from group companies. The Tribunal held that reopening an assessment on an issue specifically examined during the original scrutiny proceedings, without any fresh tangible material, amounted to an impermissible change of opinion. The reassessment had been triggered by observations made in appellate proceedings for AY 2012-13 concerning the appropriate year for taxing share application money. However, those observations did not constitute new material relating to the assessment years under consideration. The Tribunal also noted that the CIT(A)’s independent finding regarding non-compliance with the reassessment procedure under section 148A of the Income-tax Act, 1961, had not been specifically challenged by the Revenue. Consequently, the reassessment proceedings were held invalid, while the substantive additions under section 68 and consequential issues relating to interest and penalty were not separately adjudicated. The assessee, Lupin Investment Pvt. Ltd., was the successor to Lupin Properties Ltd., which was engaged in mall management, property leasing and investment activities. Lupin Properties Ltd. was amalgamated with Lupin Investment Pvt. Ltd. with effect from 1 October 2016 pursuant to an order of the National Company Law Tribunal. The proceedings for the earlier assessment years were accordingly pursued by the successor company. For AY 2010-11, the predecessor company had filed its return on 30 September 2010 declaring total income of ₹58,81,491 under the normal provisions of the Act, besides reporting book profit under section 115JB. The return was initially processed under section 143(1), following which the case was selected for scrutiny. The original assessment under section 143(3) was completed on 31 March 2013, determining total income at ₹59,38,890 after an addition of ₹82,000 towards notional interest. The subsequent dispute arose when the Department sought to reopen the completed assessments to examine share application money received from related entities. The reopening originated from observations or directions contained in the CIT(A)’s appellate order for AY 2012-13. In those proceedings, an issue arose regarding the assessment year in which share application money should be considered for taxation. The appellate observation indicated that the relevant amount ought to have been examined in the year of receipt rather than in the year of allotment of shares. Acting upon this observation, the Assessing Officer initiated reassessment proceedings for earlier assessment years. The record refers to an initial notice under section 148 dated 16 March 2021, which, according to the assessee, was issued in the name of a nonexistent entity and subsequently dropped. Another notice was issued on 10 April 2021. The assessee challenged the validity of this fresh notice, contending that it had been issued without complying with the procedure introduced under section 148A. The assessee also questioned whether an observation concerning a different assessment year could independently justify reopening assessments that had already undergone scrutiny. During the reassessment proceedings for AY 2010-11, the Assessing Officer examined share application money of ₹10.75 crore received from group concerns, including Lupin Marketing Pvt. Ltd. and Visiomed India Pvt. Ltd. The assessee maintained that the identity and creditworthiness of the investors and the genuineness of the transactions were established through Permanent Account Numbers, audited financial statements, confirmations and bank statements. It explained that the transactions were conducted through normal banking channels and that the investors’ funds were primarily derived from dividend income received from Lupin Ltd. The assessee also furnished valuation workings and contended that the provisions governing taxation of excessive share premium under section 56(2)(viib), and the subsequently amended requirements under section 68, were not applicable to the relevant assessment year. The Assessing Officer rejected these explanations, questioned the basis of the share valuation and treated the entire ₹10.75 crore as unexplained cash credit under section 68. The reassessment order dated 29 September 2021 determined total income at ₹11,34,38,892. Before the CIT(A), the assessee challenged both the jurisdiction assumed for reopening and the substantive addition. It argued that the share application money had already been examined during the original assessment proceedings under section 143(3). According to the assessee, the original Assessing Officer had sought shareholder particulars, examined supporting evidence, obtained confirmations and conducted further enquiries before accepting the transactions. Therefore, the later reassessment was not based on any newly discovered transaction or previously unavailable information. Instead, it represented an attempt to reconsider the same material and arrive at a different conclusion. The assessee also alleged borrowed satisfaction, arguing that the Assessing Officer had relied upon appellate observations concerning AY 2012-13 without independently establishing escapement of income in the relevant years. Additional objections concerned the applicability of section 148A, the requirement of full and true disclosure for reopening beyond four years, limitation under sections 149 and 150, and procedural requirements relating to Document Identification Numbers and digital signatures. The CIT(A) accepted the principal jurisdictional objections and held the reassessment proceedings invalid. The appellate authority found that the original assessment had involved specific examination of share application money and that the assessee had furnished relevant primary evidence. The subsequent reopening was founded upon observations made in proceedings for another assessment year rather than fresh tangible material relating to the years being reopened. Relying upon the Supreme Court’s decision in CIT v. Kelvinator of India Ltd. (320 ITR 561), the CIT(A) held that the reassessment power under section 147 could not be exercised merely to review an earlier assessment. The appellate authority also referred to CIT v. Usha International Ltd. (348 ITR 485) for the principle that an issue specifically queried and answered during scrutiny may be regarded as examined even where the assessment order does not contain an elaborate discussion. The CIT(A) further relied upon judicial principles concerning independent application of mind, disclosure of material facts and procedural safeguards in reassessment proceedings. On the substantive addition, the CIT(A) separately examined the evidence furnished in support of the share application money. It noted that the investor companies were identifiable group concerns assessed to income tax, that the transactions had passed through banking channels, and that the source of funds had been explained with reference to dividend income received from a listed company. The CIT(A) observed that the Assessing Officer had not brought adverse material establishing that the confirmations, financial statements or banking records were false or fabricated. It also considered the assessee’s argument that section 56(2)(viib), introduced with effect from AY 2013-14, was not applicable to the relevant assessment year. Referring to decisions including CIT v. Lovely Exports Pvt. Ltd. (216 CTR 195), CIT v. Gagandeep Infrastructure Pvt. Ltd. (394 ITR 680) and CIT v. Orissa Corporation Pvt. Ltd. (159 ITR 78), the CIT(A) concluded that the assessee had discharged its evidentiary burden under section 68. It accordingly deleted the addition of ₹10.75 crore. These findings formed part of the first appellate order, although the Tribunal ultimately disposed of the Revenue’s appeals on jurisdictional grounds without independently deciding the merits of the addition. Before the Tribunal, the Revenue contended that the reopening was supported by tangible material arising from the appellate proceedings for AY 2012-13. It argued that the CIT(A) had incorrectly characterised the reassessment as a change of opinion and had improperly interfered with the additions made by the Assessing Officer. In the appeal for AY 2009-10, the Revenue’s grounds also challenged the treatment of alleged DIN and digital-signature defects, the adoption of base income of ₹5.39 crore, the deletion of an addition of ₹8.98 crore under section 68, interest under section 234B and matters concerning penalty under section 271(1)(c). The assessee supported the CIT(A)’s order and maintained that the Department was attempting to reopen issues already investigated and accepted during the original assessments. The assessee filed substantial paper books containing supporting documents and correspondence from the earlier scrutiny proceedings. The Tribunal examined the original assessment records and found that the assessee’s case involved much more than the passive disclosure of information. The original Assessing Officer had issued a notice under section 142(1) dated 21 July 2011 specifically requiring details of shareholders and share application money. In response, the assessee furnished a statement identifying the investors and the amounts received. The statement included share application money from Rahas Investments Pvt. Ltd., Lupin Marketing Pvt. Ltd., Lupin Investments Pvt. Ltd. and Visiomed (India) Pvt. Ltd., with aggregate amounts of ₹29,27,42,750 shown as at 31 March 2009, compared with ₹66,61,588 as at 31 March 2008. The Tribunal regarded this documentary record as significant because it established that the disputed subject had been expressly brought before the original Assessing Officer for examination. The enquiry extended beyond the initial shareholder statement. The assessee had furnished details of shareholders and beneficial owners, including their addresses, PANs and shareholding percentages. It had also submitted particulars of share application money outstanding as at 31 March 2009, together with the names and amounts attributable to individual applicants. The record contained confirmations of accounts, acknowledgements of income-tax returns and bank statements. In the case of Visiomed (India) Pvt. Ltd., a confirmation dated 7 November 2011 was available in the paper book. The Assessing Officer had additionally examined an apparent discrepancy between the share application money and the company’s authorised share capital. In response, the assessee furnished reconciliations dated 28 and 29 November 2011 explaining the position and the return of excess share application money. Further enquiries were undertaken under section 133(6), and the corresponding notice and reply were also produced before the Tribunal. These records demonstrated an actual process of enquiry, verification and consideration during the original assessment. The Tribunal emphasised the distinction between mere disclosure of information and conscious examination of an issue by the Assessing Officer. Where an assessee simply places documents on record without any enquiry, the question whether an opinion was formed may require separate consideration. However, in the present case, the original Assessing Officer had specifically requested information, examined the supporting documents, pursued further enquiries, sought reconciliation of an apparent discrepancy and ultimately accepted the transactions. The absence of a detailed discussion of each document in the original assessment order did not erase the examination established by the contemporaneous record. The Tribunal held that a completed scrutiny assessment could not be reopened merely because the Department subsequently wished to adopt a different view of the same material. Applying CIT v. Kelvinator of India Ltd., the Tribunal reiterated that the doctrine of change of opinion operates as an inbuilt safeguard against arbitrary reassessment. The power under section 147 is not equivalent to a power of review. Reopening requires tangible material bearing a live connection with the belief that income chargeable to tax has escaped assessment. In this case, the Tribunal found no new document, subsequent discovery or independent information concerning the share application money that had emerged after the original assessment. The observation made in the appellate proceedings for AY 2012-13 concerned the appropriate year for considering the receipt of share application money. It did not introduce fresh material into the assessment records for AYs 2009-10 and 2010-11. Since the relevant transactions and supporting evidence had already been examined, reopening the same issue amounted to reconsideration of an earlier assessment rather than a valid exercise of reassessment jurisdiction. A further decisive aspect concerned the procedure prescribed under section 148A. The assessee argued that the first reassessment notice issued in March 2021 had been dropped because it was addressed to an incorrect or nonexistent entity, and that the subsequent notice issued in April 2021 required compliance with the amended reassessment procedure. The assessee relied upon Union of India v. Ashish Agarwal (444 ITR 1) in support of this contention. The Tribunal observed that the CIT(A) had recorded a specific finding that the reassessment was invalid because the prescribed procedure under section 148A had not been followed before issuance of the notice under section 148. Importantly, the Revenue had not specifically challenged that finding in its grounds of appeal. The Tribunal held that an independent finding of the first appellate authority, which was sufficient by itself to invalidate the reassessment and remained unchallenged, could not be disregarded while seeking restoration of the reassessment order. This provided an additional ground for dismissing the Revenue’s appeal. Having upheld the invalidation of reassessment jurisdiction, the Tribunal declined to adjudicate the substantive grounds concerning the additions under section 68. It explained that once the assumption of jurisdiction to reopen an assessment failed, the reassessment order could not provide a sustainable foundation for the additions made therein. Consequently, the Revenue’s objections relating to the deletion of share application money additions did not survive for separate consideration. The grounds concerning interest under section 234B and penalty proceedings under section 271(1)(c) were similarly treated as consequential or academic. The assessee’s cross-objections raising alternative legal and factual grounds also became academic because it had already succeeded on the principal jurisdictional issue. Accordingly, the Tribunal’s decision should not be interpreted as an independent final determination of every substantive question concerning the genuineness of the share application money or the valuation of shares. For AY 2010-11, the Tribunal noted that the Revenue’s appeal and the assessee’s cross-objection raised substantially the same issues as those considered for AY 2009-10. The reopening for both years arose from the same appellate observations concerning AY 2012-13 and suffered from the same defects relating to change of opinion, borrowed satisfaction and non-compliance with section 148A. The Tribunal therefore applied its reasoning for AY 2009-10 to AY 2010-11 and dismissed the Revenue’s appeal for that year as well. A separate appeal, ITA No. 5952/MUM/2026, had been filed inadvertently as a duplicate for AY 2010-11 in the name of Privilege Health Care Services Pvt. Ltd. At the request of the Departmental Representative, and without objection from the assessee, that appeal was dismissed as withdrawn. In its consolidated order pronounced on 25 September 2026, the Mumbai ITAT dismissed the Revenue’s substantive appeals for AYs 2009-10 and 2010-11, dismissed the duplicate appeal as withdrawn and disposed of the assessee’s cross-objections as academic. The decision establishes that reassessment cannot be sustained merely by relying upon observations from proceedings relating to another assessment year when the relevant transactions have already been specifically examined during the original scrutiny assessment and no fresh tangible material is available. It also illustrates the importance of maintaining documentary evidence of enquiries conducted during assessment proceedings, including notices, replies, confirmations, bank records and reconciliations. Equally significant is the procedural consequence of failing to challenge an independent adverse finding recorded by the first appellate authority. The relief granted in this case rests on the invalidity of the reassessment proceedings, rather than a separate adjudication by the Tribunal of the underlying share application money additions.

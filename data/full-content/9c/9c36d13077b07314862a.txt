Summary: The Supreme Court of India, in G.R. Infra Projects Limited Ratlam v. State of Madhya Pradesh, decided on 19 August 2026, and Tata Steel Limited v. Union of India, decided on 25 August 2026, examined the conditions necessary for invoking Section 74 of the CGST Act, 2017. Both judgments reinforce that tax discrepancies, ITC mismatches, audit objections and alleged short payments do not automatically establish fraud, wilful misstatement or suppression of facts with intent to evade tax. The proper officer must independently examine the relevant material and disclose the foundational facts supporting such allegations in the show cause notice itself. In G.R. Infra Projects, the Supreme Court quashed a Section 74 notice containing general allegations of fraud without identifying the underlying circumstances and rejected the Department’s attempt to cure those defects through a counter-affidavit. In Tata Steel, the Court held that an audit objection could not substitute independent statutory satisfaction and that protective demands could not be issued merely to preserve limitation without legislative authority. The Court quashed the impugned notice and consequential order while permitting fresh proceedings under Section 74, subject to the specified conditions and timeline. The decisions distinguish ordinary tax determination under Section 73 from fraud-based proceedings under Section 74 and clarify the importance of limitation, proper notice drafting, independent application of mind and procedural safeguards. Although Section 74A governs tax determination for FY 2024–25 onward, the requirement that serious allegations must rest on identifiable facts remains relevant. These judgments provide important guidance for GST investigations, adjudication, statutory appeals and writ proceedings. Introduction The power to recover tax is an essential component of any fiscal legislation. However, the authority to recover tax and the authority to invoke allegations of fraud, wilful misstatement or suppression of facts are legally distinct. The Central Goods and Services Tax Act, 2017 recognises this distinction through separate statutory mechanisms for ordinary tax defaults and defaults involving fraudulent or deliberately evasive conduct. Sections 73 and 74 of the CGST Act, as applicable to financial years up to 2023–24, establish different frameworks for determining unpaid or short-paid tax, erroneous refunds and wrongly availed or utilised input tax credit. While Section 73 addresses cases not involving fraud, wilful misstatement or suppression of facts with intent to evade tax, Section 74 deals with cases involving these additional culpable ingredients. The distinction is not merely procedural. Invocation of Section 74 carries serious consequences, including an extended limitation period and enhanced penal exposure. Consequently, the conditions for invoking Section 74 must be strictly examined. In practice, disputes frequently arise where the GST Department invokes Section 74 on the basis of differences between GSTR-2A/2B and GSTR-3B, audit objections, discrepancies in books of account, supplier-related investigations or alleged short payment of tax. The central question is whether such discrepancies, without further supporting evidence, can justify the allegation of fraud or suppression. Two important judgments delivered by the Supreme Court in August 2026 have addressed this issue directly: M/s G.R. Infra Projects Limited Ratlam v. State of Madhya Pradesh & Others, Civil Appeal No. 11277 of 2026, decided on 19 August 2026. M/s Tata Steel Limited v. Union of India & Others, 2026 INSC 920, Civil Appeal No. 12020 of 2026, decided on 25 August 2026. Both judgments were delivered by a Bench comprising Hon’ble Justice J.B. Pardiwala and Hon’ble Justice K. Vinod Chandran. The Supreme Court has clarified that merely reproducing the expressions “fraud”, “wilful misstatement” or “suppression of facts” in a show cause notice does not satisfy the statutory requirements of Section 74. The proper officer must possess a factual basis for invoking the provision, and the essential allegations must be disclosed in the notice itself. These decisions have significant implications for GST investigations, audit-based demands, adjudication proceedings, appellate remedies and the interpretation of extended limitation. 2. Statutory Framework: Sections 73 and 74 2.1 Section 73 — Ordinary Tax Determination Section 73, for financial years governed by the earlier statutory framework, provides for determination of tax not paid, short-paid, erroneously refunded or ITC wrongly availed or utilised for reasons other than fraud, wilful misstatement or suppression of facts with intent to evade tax. Under the original statutory framework, Section 73(10) prescribed a period of three years for passing the order, computed with reference to the prescribed statutory event, while Section 73(2) required the notice to be issued at least three months before the applicable deadline. The actual limitation date must be determined after considering the relevant financial year, amendments, notifications, statutory extensions and applicable judicial orders. 2.2 Section 74 — Fraud-Based Tax Determination Section 74 applies where the specified tax default occurs by reason of fraud, wilful misstatement or suppression of facts to evade tax. The provision is distinguishable from Section 73 in three important respects. First, it requires the existence of additional statutory ingredients relating to culpable conduct. Second, it provides an extended limitation framework, with the order ordinarily required within five years under Section 74(10), subject to applicable law. Third, it carries enhanced penal consequences. Therefore, the invocation of Section 74 requires more than proof of an ordinary tax discrepancy. 2.3 Essential Ingredients of Section 74 The following elements must be examined: Existence of unpaid or short-paid tax, erroneous refund or wrongly availed/utilised ITC. Identification of the relevant transactions and statutory contraventions. Factual circumstances constituting fraud, wilful misstatement or suppression. Nexus between the alleged culpable conduct and the tax default. Compliance with the applicable limitation and adjudication requirements. The Supreme Court’s August 2026 judgments primarily concern the requirement that the factual foundation for invoking the fraud provision must be apparent from the show cause notice. 3. First Judgment: G.R. Infra Projects Limited Ratlam v. State of Madhya Pradesh Court: Supreme Court of India Date: 19 August 2026 Citation: Civil Appeal No. 11277 of 2026, arising from SLP(C) No. 33594 of 2025 Bench: J.B. Pardiwala and K. Vinod Chandran, JJ. 3.1 Facts of the Case The dispute concerned a show cause notice dated 13 June 2025 relating to FY 2018–19. The Department had conducted investigations, issued summons, sought documents and recorded statements of persons associated with the assessee. A draft notice-cum-investigation report was prepared, followed by further communications and issuance of the impugned notice under Section 74. The assessee challenged the notice on the ground that the ordinary limitation period under Section 73 had expired and that the Department had failed to disclose the factual basis necessary to invoke Section 74. The High Court had upheld the notice, resulting in the matter reaching the Supreme Court. 3.2 Arguments of the Assessee The assessee submitted that Section 74 could be invoked only where the notice disclosed allegations establishing fraud, wilful misstatement or suppression of facts. The mere use of those statutory expressions was insufficient. It was further submitted that the Department could not rely upon Section 74 merely to overcome the expiry of limitation under Section 73. 3.3 Arguments of the Revenue The State contended that the proceedings involved allegations of fraud and suppression. It sought to rely upon its counter-affidavit before the Supreme Court to explain the factual basis of those allegations. 3.4 Supreme Court’s Findings The Supreme Court rejected the attempt to supplement the notice through a counter-affidavit. The Court held that the validity of a statutory notice must be determined from the notice itself. The necessary conditions cannot be supplied subsequently through pleadings filed in court. Upon examining the notice, the Court found that it contained only a general allegation of fraud or concealment without explaining how fraud had been inferred or concealment detected. The Court observed that the use of alternative expressions without identifying the underlying facts demonstrated the absence of a sufficiently reasoned basis for invoking Section 74. It was necessary for the notice to disclose the acts or circumstances leading the assessing officer to conclude that the assessee had employed a fraudulent or deliberately evasive device. 3.5 Decision The Supreme Court set aside the High Court’s order and the impugned show cause notice. It directed the State not to pursue further proceedings pursuant to the quashed notice. 3.6 Ratio Decidendi The essential factual allegations supporting fraud, wilful misstatement or suppression must appear in the show cause notice itself. The Department cannot cure their absence through a subsequent counter-affidavit or by mechanically reproducing the statutory terminology. 3.7 Significance The decision reinforces three principles. First, Section 74 cannot be invoked without a specific factual foundation. Second, the Department cannot retrospectively improve a defective notice through subsequent pleadings. Third, expiry of the ordinary limitation period does not itself justify invocation of the extended limitation provision. 4. Second Judgment: Tata Steel Limited v. Union of India Court: Supreme Court of India Date: 25 August 2026 Neutral Citation: 2026 INSC 920 Civil Appeal: No. 12020 of 2026, arising from SLP(C) No. 16859 of 2026 Bench: J.B. Pardiwala and K. Vinod Chandran, JJ. 4.1 Facts of the Case The dispute related to FY 2018–19, 2019–20 and 2020–21. The proceedings originated from an objection raised by the Comptroller and Auditor General of India concerning alleged ITC mismatches and short payment of tax. The Department sought explanations and documents from the assessee. However, the assessing officer had not accepted the audit objection as conclusively establishing the alleged liability. The matter was placed in the departmental call book, indicating that proceedings were being kept in abeyance. A notice dated 13 June 2025 was subsequently issued under Section 74. Thereafter, a communication dated 1 July 2025 sought to revive the proceedings and proposed a protective demand because the statutory limitation period was approaching. The assessee challenged the proceedings before the Supreme Court. 4.2 Arguments of the Assessee The assessee contended that the Department had failed to establish any material indicating fraud, wilful misstatement or suppression. An audit objection or ITC mismatch, without additional evidence, could not justify invocation of Section 74. It was also submitted that the GST legislation did not authorise protective assessments merely to preserve limitation. 4.3 Arguments of the Revenue The Revenue submitted that proceedings had commenced before the expiry of the ordinary limitation period and that the available material disclosed suppression and wilful misrepresentation. Reliance was also placed on the former Explanation 2 to Section 74 concerning suppression of facts. 4.4 Independent Satisfaction of the Proper Officer The Supreme Court held that proceedings under Sections 73 and 74 must be founded upon the satisfaction of the assessing officer. An audit objection does not automatically establish statutory liability. In a Section 74 proceeding, the officer must go further and examine whether fraud, wilful misstatement or suppression led to the alleged mismatch or short payment. The Court noted that the Department itself had questioned the audit objection and had placed the matter in the call book. These circumstances were relevant in examining whether the assessing officer had independently formed the requisite satisfaction. 4.5 Mere ITC Mismatch Does Not Establish Fraud The Supreme Court found that the notice did not identify foundational facts demonstrating a deliberate device to evade tax or avail excess ITC. The general statement concerning availment of ITC without documentary evidence and suppression of facts was insufficient. The Court emphasised that merely employing statutory expressions does not demonstrate the application of mind necessary to invoke the extended limitation period. This is one of the most significant aspects of the judgment. An ITC mismatch may warrant verification and further investigation. However, the existence of a mismatch does not automatically establish the additional culpable ingredients required under Section 74. 4.6 Limitation under Section 73 The Supreme Court examined the statutory limitation framework under Section 73, including the relevant annual-return due dates and the effect of the Supreme Court’s COVID-era limitation orders. The Court distinguished the deadline for issuing a notice under Section 73(2) from the deadline for passing an order under Section 73(10). It rejected the Revenue’s contention that the earlier commencement of proceedings, by itself, preserved jurisdiction after the applicable limitation had expired. On the computation adopted in the judgment, the relevant Section 73 limitation had expired on 28 February 2025. The notice dated 13 June 2025 was therefore outside the ordinary limitation period. 4.7 Protective Assessment Not Permitted The Court also considered the Department’s attempt to issue a protective demand to safeguard limitation. It held that the GST statutory framework did not provide for such a protective assessment mechanism. The Department could not substitute administrative expediency for the statutory conditions governing adjudication. 4.8 Effect of Omission of Explanation 2 The Revenue relied upon the former Explanation 2 to Section 74. The Supreme Court rejected its applicability in the circumstances, noting its omission with effect from 1 November 2024. This aspect requires careful consideration in other proceedings because the applicable statutory text, transitional provisions and date of initiation must be examined separately. 4.9 Final Decision The Supreme Court set aside the impugned show cause notice and consequential Order-in-Original dated 26 December 2025. However, the Court expressly left open the possibility of the Department initiating appropriate fresh proceedings under Section 74, if supported by the requisite foundational facts, with an order to be passed before 28 February 2027. Accordingly, the judgment did not permanently extinguish every possible claim concerning the transactions. 4.10 Ratio Decidendi An audit objection or ITC mismatch does not automatically justify invocation of Section 74. The assessing officer must independently form the necessary satisfaction, and the show cause notice must disclose the factual circumstances supporting fraud, wilful misstatement or suppression. The judgment also establishes that limitation cannot be preserved through an unauthorised protective demand. 5. Comparative Analysis of Both Judgments Issue G.R. Judicial Principles Emerging from the Two Decisions Principle I — Section 73 and Section 74 Are Substantively Distinct The distinction between Sections 73 and 74 is not limited to the length of the limitation period. Section 74 requires additional culpable circumstances, and those circumstances must form part of the Department’s case from the beginning. Principle II — Fraud Cannot Be Presumed from a Tax Discrepancy A difference in returns, alleged excess ITC, audit observation or short payment of tax does not, without more, establish fraud or deliberate suppression. Such discrepancies may justify investigation, but the extended limitation and enhanced penalty require satisfaction of the statutory conditions. Principle III — Foundational Facts Must Appear in the SCN The notice must explain the factual circumstances supporting the allegation. A general statement that the assessee committed fraud or suppressed facts is not sufficient if the notice does not disclose the basis of that conclusion. Principle IV — Independent Application of Mind Is Mandatory The proper officer cannot mechanically adopt an audit objection, investigation report or another authority’s conclusions. The officer must independently examine the material and form the satisfaction required under the applicable provision. Principle V — A Defective SCN Cannot Be Repaired Through a Counter-Affidavit The legal validity of a notice must be determined on the basis of the notice and the requirements applicable when it was issued. Subsequent explanations in litigation cannot replace missing statutory allegations. Principle VI — Limitation Cannot Be Circumvented The extended limitation under Section 74 is available only when the statutory ingredients justifying its invocation are present. The Department cannot employ the provision solely because proceedings under Section 73 have become time-barred. Principle VII — Protective Demand Requires Statutory Authority Administrative concerns about impending limitation do not confer a power to issue a protective demand where the GST legislation does not provide for it. Principle VIII — Consequences of Quashing Depend on the Judgment and Statute Quashing a defective notice does not necessarily mean that all possible future proceedings are prohibited. The availability of fresh proceedings depends upon limitation, the applicable statutory framework and the precise relief granted by the Court. 7. Relationship with Earlier Supreme Court Jurisprudence The decisions are consistent with earlier judicial principles concerning extended limitation and suppression under indirect tax legislation. In Pushpam Pharmaceuticals Co. v. Commissioner of Central Excise, 1995 Supp (3) SCC 462, the Supreme Court examined suppression in the context of extended limitation. In Cosmic Dye Chemical v. Commissioner of Central Excise, (1995) 6 SCC 117, the Court considered the culpability associated with wilful misstatement and suppression. In Continental Foundation Joint Venture v. Commissioner of Central Excise, (2007) 10 SCC 337, the Court distinguished deliberate suppression from an ordinary omission. Similarly, Uniworth Textiles Ltd. v. Commissioner of Central Excise, (2013) 9 SCC 753, emphasised that mere non-payment does not automatically establish suppression with intent to evade. The significance of the August 2026 judgments lies in the direct application of comparable principles to Section 74 of the CGST Act. 8. Practical Implications for GST Adjudication and Appeals The judgments provide important guidance for both taxpayers and departmental authorities. 8.1 For Taxpayers A taxpayer receiving a notice under Section 74 should examine whether the notice: Identifies the specific transactions giving rise to the proposed demand. Explains the factual basis of the alleged fraud, wilful misstatement or suppression. Establishes the connection between the alleged conduct and the tax default. Relies upon disclosed evidence rather than unsupported assumptions. Has been issued within the applicable statutory limitation framework. A notice containing only an aggregate ITC difference or a general allegation of suppression may warrant a specific preliminary jurisdictional objection. 8.2 For GST Audit Proceedings Audit observations are relevant inputs for tax administration but are not conclusive determinations of liability. Where an audit objection concerns ITC differences, the proper officer should examine invoices, reconciliations, statutory returns and other relevant records before determining whether a tax default exists and whether it involves the additional ingredients required under Section 74. 8.3 For Appeals under Section 107 An assessee challenging a Section 74 order may separately raise grounds concerning: Invalid invocation of the fraud provision. Absence of foundational facts in the SCN. Lack of independent satisfaction. Expiry of ordinary or extended limitation. Findings in the order travelling beyond the SCN. Failure to consider reconciliations and evidence. Unsustainable enhanced penalty. These grounds should be supported by the exact language of the SCN, the chronology and the adjudication record. 8.4 For Writ Proceedings Where the notice itself reveals the absence of jurisdictional conditions, the judgments may support consideration of a challenge under Article 226. However, the availability of the statutory appellate remedy, the stage of proceedings and the recognised exceptions to the alternative-remedy rule must be examined before approaching the High Court. 9. Effect on Section 74A for FY 2024–25 Onward The Finance (No. 2) Act, 2024 introduced Section 74A as the determination framework for FY 2024–25 onward. The earlier separate demand mechanisms under Sections 73 and 74 continue to be relevant for periods up to FY 2023–24. Section 74A has a different structure, including a common determination framework and differentiated consequences where fraud, wilful misstatement or suppression is involved. Therefore, the limitation conclusions reached under the former Sections 73 and 74 cannot automatically be applied to Section 74A. Nevertheless, the broader judicial principle that serious allegations must be supported by specific facts remains relevant when determining whether the enhanced fraud-related consequences under Section 74A are attracted. 10. Concluding Remarks The Supreme Court’s judgments in G.R. Infra Projects Limited Ratlam v. State of Madhya Pradesh and Tata Steel Limited v. Union of India represent important developments in the interpretation of Section 74 of the CGST Act, 2017. The decisions reaffirm that the power to invoke extended limitation and enhanced penal consequences is conditional upon the existence of the statutory ingredients prescribed by Parliament. A tax discrepancy may justify investigation, verification and, where legally permissible, ordinary tax determination. It does not automatically establish fraud, wilful misstatement or suppression of facts with intent to evade tax. The most significant contribution of these judgments is the emphasis placed upon the contents of the show cause notice itself. The Supreme Court has made clear that the Department must identify the foundational facts supporting the allegation of culpable conduct. The statutory expressions cannot be employed mechanically, nor can their absence be cured through subsequent explanations filed during litigation. The judgment in Tata Steel further clarifies that audit objections do not dispense with the assessing officer’s obligation to independently examine the material and form the requisite satisfaction. It also reinforces the principle that administrative concerns about limitation cannot justify a protective demand without statutory authority. At the same time, these judgments should not be interpreted as granting immunity from tax recovery wherever an initial notice is defective. A properly founded proceeding may remain permissible where the statute, limitation and terms of the judicial order allow it. For taxpayers, the decisions strengthen the grounds available to challenge notices based solely upon discrepancies or unsupported allegations. For departmental authorities, they provide a clear standard for legally sustainable investigation, notice drafting and adjudication. The central message emerging from both decisions is that the existence of a tax discrepancy and the existence of tax fraud are separate legal questions. Section 74 can be invoked only when the Department establishes a factual foundation for the latter, and that foundation must be disclosed in the show cause notice. The judgments therefore reinforce an essential principle of fiscal jurisprudence: while tax administration must be effective, the exercise of statutory power must remain within the conditions, procedures and limitations prescribed by law.

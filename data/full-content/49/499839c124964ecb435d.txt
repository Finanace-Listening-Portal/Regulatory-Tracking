(ITAT Mumbai) The Income Tax Appellate Tribunal (ITAT), Mumbai Bench, in DCIT Vs Mahindra Homes Pvt. Ltd., ITA No. 1212/Mum/2025, decided on 15 September 2025, examined the Revenue’s appeal against the order of the Commissioner of Income Tax (Appeals) for Assessment Year 2015-16, involving a transfer pricing adjustment of ₹28,94,33,680 on interest paid on Compulsorily Convertible Debentures (CCDs). The assessee, a joint venture between Mahindra Lifespace Developers Ltd. and SCM Real Estate Singapore Pvt. Ltd., was engaged in residential real estate development and had issued CCDs to its Singapore-based associated enterprise carrying interest at 17.65% per annum. The assessee benchmarked the interest using the Comparable Uncontrolled Price (CUP) method against seven debt instruments identified from BSE, NSE and NSDL databases, with coupon rates ranging from 16.5% to 20% and a median rate of 18%. The Transfer Pricing Officer rejected this analysis and adopted an arm’s length interest rate of approximately 8.61% using Bloomberg loan comparables, resulting in the disputed adjustment. The CIT(A) deleted the adjustment by relying on Tribunal decisions in the assessee’s own cases for earlier assessment years. However, the Tribunal observed that CCDs are hybrid financial instruments containing an embedded equity conversion feature, which distinguishes them from ordinary non-convertible debentures and term loans. It held that the assessee’s benchmarking failed to account for equity conversion rights, subordination, security and other relevant contractual differences, while the TPO’s comparables involved secured or floating-rate loans from unrelated sectors such as oil and gas and infrastructure. Since neither analysis satisfied the requirements of Rule 10B(2) and Rule 10B(3) of the Income-tax Rules, 1962, the Tribunal set aside the CIT(A)’s decision and restored the CCD interest benchmarking issue to the AO/TPO for fresh examination, directing appropriate adjustments for material differences in risk, convertibility, security and contractual terms. The Tribunal further considered the Revenue’s objections concerning the deletion of disallowances relating to legal and professional expenses, advertisement and sales promotion expenditure, and commission and brokerage expenses incurred in connection with the assessee’s real estate business. The Assessing Officer had treated certain expenditure as capital in nature or attributable to construction activities, whereas the CIT(A) had allowed relief by treating the relevant expenditure as revenue expenditure. Following the Tribunal’s directions in the assessee’s own cases for Assessment Years 2016-17 and 2017-18, the Bench held that the correct treatment of such expenses depended on their actual connection with the real estate projects. It clarified that expenditure directly attributable to pre-construction or construction activities should be capitalised as part of inventory or work-in-progress, while general administrative and selling expenses should be allowed as revenue expenditure in the year of incurrence, subject to verification. The Tribunal therefore set aside the CIT(A)’s findings on these issues and directed the Assessing Officer to examine the nature and purpose of each category of expenditure before deciding its allowability in accordance with law. Accordingly, the Revenue’s appeal was allowed for statistical purposes, with both the ₹28.94 crore CCD interest transfer pricing dispute and the expenditure classification issues remanded for fresh adjudication. The Tribunal did not finally uphold the transfer pricing addition or conclusively determine the deductibility of the remanded expenses. Cases Discussed Mahindra Homes Pvt. Ltd.

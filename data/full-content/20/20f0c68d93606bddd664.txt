Morgan Chase Bank Vs ADIT (ITAT Mumbai) Summary : The Mumbai ‘I’ Bench of the Income Tax Appellate Tribunal, comprising Judicial Member Amit Shukla and Accountant Member Rakesh Kumar Lodha, decided the cross appeals of J.P. Morgan Chase Bank N.A. (formerly The Chase Manhattan Bank) and the Income Tax Department for assessment year 2002-03. The appeals arose from the CIT(A)’s order dated 30 March 2006 against an assessment under section 143(3) of the Income-tax Act, 1961. The United States-incorporated bank operated through a Mumbai branch admittedly constituting its permanent establishment (PE) under the India-USA DTAA. It had revised its returned income from ₹28,90,00,100 to ₹28,10,32,880. The dispute covered expatriate salary, inter-office service profits, transfer-pricing benchmarking, centralised expenditure, valuation of banking securities and interest on repurchase transactions. The Indian branch provided correspondent banking services, cheque-processing services and investor services in support of the bank’s overseas operations. The correspondent banking functions chiefly involved marketing, liaison, relationship management and customer coordination for foreign-currency accounts, letters of credit, export collections and remittances handled overseas. Cheque processing involved scanning, recording and transmitting cheque information, while investor services comprised settlement assistance and securities and cash reconciliation. The bank characterised these functions as routine or limited-risk support services and used the transactional net margin method (TNMM), with operating profit to operating cost as the profit-level indicator. The Transfer Pricing Officer (TPO) questioned comparable companies, the use of financial data from earlier years, depreciation adjustments and allocation of indirect, corporate, Head Office and processing-hub expenses. A distinct treaty dispute arose because the Mumbai branch recorded ₹4,02,63,333 as correspondent banking revenue and ₹5,35,06,676 as investor-services revenue, aggregating to ₹9,37,70,009. Against these amounts it recorded corresponding expenditure of ₹3,66,03,030 and ₹4,86,42,433, respectively, totalling ₹8,52,45,463. The excess of revenue over expenditure amounted to ₹36,60,303 for correspondent banking and ₹48,64,243 for investor services, or ₹85,24,546 in all. Although that excess appeared in the branch’s accounts, the bank excluded it from taxable income, invoking the reciprocal provisions governing charges between establishments of the same enterprise under Article 7(3) of the India-USA DTAA. The Revenue regarded it as profit attributable to Indian functions under Article 7(2). The Tribunal distinguished Article 7(2)‘s distinct-and-independent-enterprise attribution hypothesis from the specific treatment of intra-enterprise charges in Article 7(3). On a conjoint reading of the two clauses, it held that the identified ₹85,24,546 excess from specified inter-office service charges was not includible in profits attributable to the Indian PE in the circumstances of the case. It rejected the CIT(A)’s interpretation that the reciprocal restriction operated only if an actual disallowance under the preceding limb had first arisen. The bank’s separate net interest income of ₹40,38,331 from placements with overseas branches, already offered to tax, was distinguishable because Article 7(3) contains an express exception for banking-enterprise interest. Ground 8 was allowed, with directions against double exclusion or overlapping transfer-pricing taxation. In correspondent banking, the bank’s transfer-pricing study showed a 14.16% operating margin against a six-company weighted-average comparable margin of 14.77%. The TPO excluded Kinetic Trust Ltd. and PNR Capital Services Ltd., relied on relevant-year data for four retained companies and calculated an arithmetic mean of 42.73%. The CIT(A) sustained the exclusion of the first two companies but directed averaging of arithmetic means using three financial years, in part because earlier-year margins differed substantially from the relevant-year results. The Tribunal admitted the bank’s additional challenge to the retention of Pioneer Investcorp Ltd., Centrum Finance Ltd. and Integrated Enterprises (India) Ltd., applying the principle in National Thermal Power Co. Ltd. v. CIT that an additional legal ground relevant to correct assessment may be examined. On functional analysis, the Tribunal directed exclusion of Pioneer Investcorp Ltd., whose merchant-banking and underwriting activities carried materially different functions and risks; Centrum Finance Ltd., which undertook diversified financing, investment banking, leasing and syndication activities without sufficiently reliable segmental information; and Integrated Enterprises (India) Ltd., principally engaged in depository, share registry, brokerage and capital-market activities. Their general classification within financial services did not make their entity-level margins comparable with routine correspondent banking marketing and liaison services. The Tribunal required recomputation on an appropriately comparable functional base, with the exercise confined to the conclusions and directions in its order rather than an unrestricted fresh benchmarking exercise. The Tribunal held that Rule 10B(4), as applicable to the relevant year, gives primacy to the financial data of the year in which the international transaction occurred, namely FY 2001-02. Earlier two-year data may be considered under the proviso only where it reveals particular facts capable of influencing the determination of the transfer price. The variation between a 12.23% mean derived from earlier-year information and the TPO’s 42.73% relevant-year mean did not, without identifying such circumstances, justify an automatic three-year average. Nor did the unavailability of the latest accounts when the original study was prepared justify ignoring them once available during assessment. The Tribunal reversed the CIT(A)’s averaging direction, restored relevant-year data subject to the revised comparable set and allowed the Revenue’s third ground. For cheque-processing services, the bank had reported an operating margin of 14.71% against its comparables’ mean of 14.36%. The TPO removed Nucleus Netsoft & GIS India Ltd., a business engaged in specialised web-mapping and location-based services, rejected the claimed depreciation adjustment and adopted a higher margin after modifying financial information and costs. The Tribunal sustained exclusion of Nucleus because reliable segmental information had not established comparability with routine cheque-processing functions; database nomenclature alone could not decide the matter. It nevertheless held that a depreciation adjustment may be considered under Rule 10B(3) if financial particulars already on record permit reasonably accurate quantification of a material effect on operating margins. An unsupported or merely estimated adjustment was impermissible. Investor services involved trade settlement, transaction processing and reconciliation functions performed for overseas banking establishments. The bank reported an operating margin of 12.08%, while the TPO applied a revised processing-services approach, enhanced the cost base and determined an arm’s length value of ₹7,26,94,045 against the recorded ₹5,35,06,676, giving rise to an adjustment of ₹1,91,87,369. Although the CIT(A) broadly extended his cheque-processing directions to this segment, the Tribunal emphasised that each international transaction must be benchmarked according to its own functions, assets and risks. Its findings on functionally suitable comparables, permissible depreciation adjustments and correctly allocated costs were to be applied to the investor-services recomputation without treating the two segments as automatically identical. The cost-allocation dispute concerned indirect support expenses and a common expenditure pool comprising corporate items of ₹1,66,75,944, Head Office expenditure of ₹1,48,07,863 and hub expenditure of ₹3,11,81,346. The TPO used salary-and-bonus expenditure ratios to allocate common amounts among service segments. The Tribunal held that a cost incurred for the Indian banking business does not necessarily form part of the operating cost of every international service transaction. Severance costs, vacated-premises rent, equipment relocation, public-relations retainers and directors’ fees required category-wise scrutiny of their actual nexus with the services. Allocation by employee-cost ratio might suit personnel-driven expenditure but could not be applied indiscriminately. It directed correction of duplicated indirect costs and avoidance of double treatment of separately disallowed expenditure. The Head Office expenditure claim of ₹1,48,07,863 represented an allocation of central managerial and administrative costs, calculated by the bank at five per cent of adjusted total income under section 44C. The TPO fixed its arm’s length price at nil because the allocation and benefits were insufficiently evidenced; the CIT(A) upheld this approach, noting among other matters that a PwC certificate concerned a different accounting period and included expenditure whose connection with Indian operations was uncertain. The Tribunal clarified that section 44C is a ceiling on qualifying Head Office expenditure, not an automatic deduction, but equally that an absence of separate payment or booking in branch accounts does not itself justify a nil value. It restored the issue for limited verification of the actual expenses, relevant period, Indian nexus, reasonable allocation and treaty/statutory conditions. The separate hub claim of ₹3,11,81,346 related to centralised operational and technology services furnished through facilities at Hong Kong and Bournemouth. The TPO had likewise determined its arm’s length price at nil, questioning supporting evidence and attribution to the Indian branch. The Tribunal directed limited verification in light of service arrangements, RBI approvals, contemporaneous records and relevant findings in the bank’s own case for AY 1999-2000. It held that a failure to raise intra-bank invoices or make physical remittances did not, by itself, prove that centralised services had not been rendered. At the same time, an agreement or approval did not conclusively establish that every allocated cost was arm’s length. Any allowable amount had to be supported and fairly allocated, with section 44C applicable only if its statutory definition was met. As regards salary of ₹99,89,796 paid overseas to Mr. Dominic Price, the Indian branch’s Chief Executive Officer following the November 2001 merger, the Assessing Officer and CIT(A) had rejected the deduction because the amount was not paid or debited by the Mumbai branch, the employment contract was unavailable and the revised claim exceeded the ₹23,67,301 originally returned. The Tribunal relied on the merger history, RBI approval dated 24 January 2002, Form 16, audited statements and evidence of the executive’s management of Indian operations. It held that payment by the Head Office and the absence of a branch-book debit did not defeat section 37(1) deductibility where the functions were exclusively Indian. Section 44C was not attracted merely because payment originated abroad, and the entire ₹99,89,796 deduction was allowed. In the Revenue’s remaining grounds, the Tribunal upheld deduction of ₹7,79,516 representing diminution in the value of securities held as banking stock-in-trade. Consistent valuation at cost or market value, whichever was lower, was supported by United Commercial Bank v. CIT and CIT v. Bank of Baroda; the Revenue identified no defect merely by disputing favourable earlier appellate orders. It also upheld deletion of ₹1,06,58,000 interest on repurchase transactions in securities: the Assessing Officer had wrongly treated that disclosed repo interest as a notional loss on unmatured forward-foreign-exchange contracts, whereas those contracts had separately produced gains of ₹7,26,66,000. The Revenue’s first two grounds were dismissed and its data-year ground allowed. The bank’s treaty-profit and CEO-salary grounds succeeded outright; service-pricing and central-expenditure grounds were partly allowed for statistical purposes, with remand confined to specified verifications. Both appeals were accordingly partly allowed. Cases Discussed CIT v. Emirates Commercial Bank Ltd., (2003) 262 ITR 55 (Bombay High Court) — invoked in relation to expenditure incurred outside India for Indian banking operations and distinguished below on evidentiary grounds. Kedarnath Jute Manufacturing Co. Ltd. v. CIT, (1971) 82 ITR 363 (Supreme Court) — accounting treatment does not by itself determine deductibility; United Commercial Bank v. CIT, (1999) 240 ITR 355 (Supreme Court) — cited for the recognised cost-or-market-value, whichever lower, method of valuing stock-in-trade. CIT v. Bank of Baroda (Bombay High Court) — cited on valuation of banking securities held as stock-in-trade.

Public sector bank stocks showed significant negative abnormal returns in some cases, while private sector lenders experienced mixed reactions, including initial gains followed by losses. Advt The study found that fraud announcements did not produce uniform or consistently significant cumulative losses across all four cases examined. However, public sector banks showed more immediate negative reactions in some instances, while private sector banks sometimes initially benefited from investors shifting funds away from the affected lender before experiencing adverse reactions later.The paper, Stock market reaction to fraud outbreaks in the Indian banking sector, by Mahesh Dahal and Joy Das, examined fraud cases involving IDBI Bank, Punjab National Bank (PNB), Bank of India and Canara Bank. Using Bombay Stock Exchange data, the researchers measured abnormal stock returns around the announcements and assessed the spillover effects on other public and private sector banks.The study examined stock price movements over a 41 trading day window, covering 10 days before and 30 days after each announcement, and used regression analysis to identify factors associated with stock price stability.The findings varied significantly across the four cases. In the IDBI Bank case, dated April 26, 2018, the lender did not record statistically significant abnormal returns on the announcement day or during the pre and post announcement periods. Public sector bank stocks, however, recorded significant negative abnormal returns on several days before the announcement, suggesting that information or rumours may have circulated before the formal disclosure. Private sector banks experienced both significant positive and negative returns during the period. Advt The casesIn the PNB case, dated May 18, 2018, the bank recorded a significant negative abnormal return two days before the announcement, followed by significant positive returns on the first and second trading days after it. Public sector and private sector peers also showed mixed reactions, with positive and negative returns at different points during the event window.The Bank of India case, dated January 21, 2020, produced no statistically significant cumulative abnormal returns across the event windows examined for the affected bank, public sector peers or private sector banks. Individual daily returns nevertheless fluctuated, indicating that the absence of a significant cumulative effect did not mean the market remained unaffected throughout the period. Advt The Canara Bank case, dated December 18, 2020, showed a clearer immediate impact. The lender recorded a cumulative abnormal return of minus 9% over the announcement day and the following trading day, statistically significant at the 1% level. Private sector banks recorded a positive cumulative abnormal return over the first three days, significant at the 10% level, while cumulative returns for public sector peers were not statistically significant over the periods examined.The results suggest that investors may initially shift investments from an affected bank to its peers, but that reaction can change as more information becomes available. The researchers found that private sector peers sometimes recorded initial positive abnormal returns before subsequent declines. Public sector peers also showed mixed reactions, rather than a uniform decline across all cases. Advt The regression analysis found that bank age and total trading turnover were positively associated with stock price stability during fraud-related events. Age was statistically significant at the 1% level, while turnover was significant at the 5% level. Firm value, number of shares, number of trades, delivery quantity and the Covid-19 variable were not statistically significant in the model. The findings therefore point to the role of age and trading turnover, rather than establishing that bank size independently improves resilience.The researchers concluded that the evidence partly supported the hypothesis that fraud announcements generate negative abnormal returns for affected banks, particularly public sector lenders. They found conditional support for spillover effects, with the direction and timing of market reactions varying across ownership groups and cases.The study said its findings were limited by the four fraud cases selected and its focus on short and medium term market reactions. The results cannot therefore be generalised to all Indian banks or fraud announcements.

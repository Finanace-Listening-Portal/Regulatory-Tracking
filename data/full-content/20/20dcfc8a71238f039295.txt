Where jewellery seized during a search continues to remain with the Department, the taxpayer may face further proceedings to secure its return even after obtaining substantial relief from the appellate authorities. The Karnataka High Court considered this situation in Narayan Vedavathi v. Deputy Commissioner of Income Tax and connected petitions, W.P. No. 26501 of 2025 and connected matters, decided on 4 November 2026, Neutral Citation: 2026:KHC:61322. The Court permitted applications for release of articles unnecessary for enforcement under section 132B, while preserving the statutory time available for giving effect to appellate orders. Background of the Dispute The petitions arose from searches conducted under section 132 of the Income-tax Act, 1961, during 23–25 June 2022. Gold, silver and diamond jewellery belonging to different family members had been seized. The petitioners sought release of these articles. Certain petitions also challenged CBDT Instruction F.No.299/06/2023 dated 16 October 2023, particularly its alleged requirement concerning adjustment of explained jewellery against liabilities and restrictions on release against security. One petition additionally questioned an assessment order and corrigendum. During the pendency of the proceedings, the petitioners obtained substantial relief from the CIT(A) and the Income Tax Appellate Tribunal. Their counsel therefore confined the immediate request to securing a decision on release of the seized articles. Petitioners’ Grievance: Relief Without Return of Jewellery The petitioners maintained that no liability would remain if the appellate orders were given effect to. Their grievance was that the Department was unwilling to decide on release until it determined whether further appeals should be filed. They requested a definite timeline for passing the appeal-effect orders. Alternatively, they sought permission to approach the jurisdictional Principal Commissioner for release, coupled with a direction requiring a timely decision. The assertion that no liability would survive was, however, the petitioners’ submission. The High Court did not itself determine the final outstanding demand. Revenue’s Stand and the Court’s Approach The Revenue submitted that return of the articles would depend upon the Assessing Officer’s appeal-effect decision and the operation of section 132B. Nevertheless, it accepted that the Principal Commissioner could consider an application for release as an interim arrangement, having regard to the appellate relief and any proposed further challenge. The Court declined to order immediate release before the Assessing Officer passed the necessary appeal-effect orders. It also held that the statutory period available to the Assessing Officer must remain available and could not be curtailed through the requested direction. Thus, the Court recognised that appellate relief and its consequential implementation were distinct stages relevant to the release request. Articles Unnecessary for Recovery Require Separate Consideration The significant aspect of the judgment lies in the Court’s treatment of articles that may no longer be required for enforcement. The Court observed that the Revenue’s possible grievance might concern only part of the relief granted. If that position justified release of gold, silver or diamonds unnecessary for enforcement under section 132B, a fair decision had to be taken promptly. The Principal Commissioner was therefore required to consider both the relief already granted and the extent of the Revenue’s grievance. The existence of a possible further challenge did not prevent consideration of a suitably confined release request. This provides a practical route where the surviving dispute may justify retention of only some articles, rather than continued retention of everything seized. What the High Court Directed The petitions were disposed of with liberty to apply to the Principal Commissioner of Income Tax, Central, Bengaluru, for release of seized gold, silver and diamonds that might not be required for enforcement, considering the appellate relief and the extent to which the Revenue was not aggrieved. Where such applications were filed within one week of receipt of a certified copy, the Principal Commissioner had to consider them and communicate the decision within two months from receipt of that certified copy. The petitioners could also place the certified order before the jurisdictional Assessing Officer for deciding the appeal-effect matter according to law. Crucially, this was a direction for consideration and communication of a decision, not an unconditional direction to release all jewellery within two months. Challenge to CBDT Instruction Remained Undecided The judgment should not be understood as invalidating the CBDT Instruction dated 16 October 2023 or approving the alternative securities proposed by the petitioners. The Court expressly preserved the grounds urged because none of those grounds had been considered. Accordingly, the constitutional and statutory challenges remained undecided. Author’s Comments The decision offers a useful remedy against administrative indecision after substantial appellate success. A release application should clearly connect the appellate relief with the demand that may survive and identify the articles whose retention is unnecessary. Its value lies in requiring focused consideration of the actual recovery requirement. At the same time, taxpayers must distinguish between a right to timely consideration and an adjudicated entitlement to immediate release. Accurate presentation of that distinction is essential when relying upon this Karnataka High Court ruling. With these categorical opinions by this Court, the only question is whether the petitions could be disposed of reserving liberty to the petitioners to file an application with the jurisdictional Principal Commissioner of Income Tax [the third respondent] to seek release of the seized gold, silver, and diamonds relevant to each of these petitioners. 6. This Court discerns a willingness to consider such application if the petitioners choose to make such application. Therefore, there must be liberty in this regard to the petitioner, and this Court must observe that the consideration must be in the light of the relief that both the CIT [Appeals] and the Tribunal have granted to the petitioners and the Revenue’s grievance that could be. This Court also observes that this would be essential because if the HC-KAR NC: 2026:KHC:61322 Revenue’s grievance is only confined to certain relief and this justifies release of the seized gold, silver, and diamonds which is not required for enforcement under Section 132B of the IT Act, there must be a just decision in this regard at the earliest. Hence, the following: ORDER [A] The petitions stand disposed of reserving liberty to the petitioners to file applications with the third respondent requesting for release of the seized gold, silver, and diamonds that may not be required for enforcement in the light of the relief that they have been admitted to by the CIT [Appeals] and the Tribunal and to the extent that the Revenue is not aggrieved. [B] If the petitioners make such an application, within a week from the date of receipt of a HC-KAR NC: 2026:KHC:61322 certified copy of this order, the third respondent shall consider the application in the light of this Courts’ observations and communicate the same to the petitioners within two [2] months from the date of receipt of a certified copy of this order. [C] The petitioners are also reserved with liberty to file a certified copy of this order with the jurisdictional Assessing Officer who has to decide on the “giving effect to” as is contemplated in law. [D] It is needless to observe that with the afore disposal the petitioner will not be impeded on any ground urged as none of the grounds is considered.

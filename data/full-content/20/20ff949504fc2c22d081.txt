Summary: A Tax Residency Certificate (TRC) is a key document for individuals and companies seeking relief under the India–UAE Double Taxation Avoidance Agreement, but it is not an automatic tax exemption. The UAE Federal Tax Authority issues certificates for specified periods and purposes. Applicants must distinguish UAE domestic tax-residence tests from the separate treaty definition, particularly the treaty’s physical-presence requirement for individuals. A UAE visa, Emirates ID or company trade licence alone does not establish treaty entitlement. For companies, the location of management and control, business operations and commercial substance can be important. Indian payers must also examine the nature of income, beneficial ownership, permanent-establishment exposure and applicable anti-abuse provisions before granting treaty treatment. The supporting package typically includes a valid TRC, the Indian prescribed information form, contractual evidence and appropriate declarations. For tax years under the Income-tax Act, 2025, Form 41 replaces the earlier Form 10F framework. Certificate validity, the period of residence and the payment date should be reconciled before withholding tax is determined. Newly incorporated companies should check the UAE authority’s eligibility and timing requirements. Indian residents receiving UAE income may similarly require an Indian certificate of residence. The most practical approach is to collect complete, consistent documentation before the transaction rather than seek a refund after excessive withholding. Its practical significance is easy to understand. If a Dubai-based consultant receives income from India, an Indian company pays dividends to a UAE shareholder, or a UAE business earns interest or royalties from India, the Indian payer must decide whether the payment qualifies for treaty treatment. A valid UAE TRC helps establish that the recipient claims residence in the UAE for the relevant period. However, a TRC is frequently misunderstood. It is evidence of tax residence, not an automatic exemption from tax. It does not independently determine the nature of income, eliminate withholding obligations, prove beneficial ownership or confirm that a UAE company has no permanent establishment in India. For Indian entrepreneurs and professionals living in Dubai, the real value of a TRC depends on obtaining the correct certificate, for the correct period, and using it with the other documents required for the transaction. What is a Tax Residency Certificate? A Tax Residency Certificate is an official document issued by the tax authority of a country confirming that the applicant satisfies the applicable tax-residence requirements for a specified period. In the UAE, the certificate is issued by the Federal Tax Authority. It may be requested for the application of a double taxation agreement or for another qualifying purpose. In India, an eligible Indian resident may obtain a certificate of residence from the Income Tax Department when it is required to claim treaty benefits in another country. The certificate ordinarily identifies matters such as: Name and legal status of the applicant; Country of tax residence; Relevant period; Tax identification details; Registered or residential address; and Issuing tax authority. The document allows the payer and tax authorities in the other country to verify that the person seeking treaty relief has obtained formal residence confirmation from the competent authority of the claimed country of residence. Why is a TRC important in India–UAE transactions? The India–UAE DTAA distributes taxing rights between the two countries. Depending on the type of income and the underlying facts, it may restrict the tax that India can impose on income paid to an eligible UAE resident. A TRC may become relevant when a UAE resident receives income from India in the form of: Dividends; Interest; Royalties; Business or consultancy fees; Capital gains; Rent or other property-related income; Employment remuneration; Directors’ fees; or Income from professional services. Without satisfactory residence documentation, an Indian payer may be reluctant to apply a beneficial treaty provision. It may instead deduct tax according to Indian domestic law, leaving the UAE recipient to pursue a refund or other remedy later. Providing the documentation before payment is therefore usually more efficient than attempting to correct excessive withholding after the transaction has been completed. UAE domestic residence and treaty residence are not identical One of the most important points in an India–UAE case is the distinction between residence under UAE domestic law and residence under the India–UAE treaty. Under the UAE domestic framework, an individual may qualify as a tax resident through different tests. These may consider: Presence in the UAE for at least 183 days during the relevant consecutive 12-month period; Presence for at least 90 days together with specified connections to the UAE; or The location of the individual’s usual residence and centre of financial and personal interests. Satisfying a UAE domestic test may support an application for a TRC. It does not necessarily mean that the India–UAE treaty’s definition has also been satisfied. Article 4 of the India–UAE DTAA contains a treaty-specific definition. For an individual claiming UAE residence under that treaty, presence in the UAE for an aggregate period of at least 183 days in the relevant calendar year is particularly important. This distinction can have a material effect. For example, an Indian entrepreneur may hold a Dubai residence visa, maintain an apartment in the UAE and spend 120 days there. The person might qualify under a UAE domestic residence test if the additional prescribed conditions are met. Nevertheless, that does not automatically establish UAE residence under the India–UAE DTAA if its separate 183-day requirement is not satisfied. A certificate issued for a domestic purpose should therefore not be treated as interchangeable with a certificate required specifically for claiming benefits under the India–UAE treaty. A UAE residence visa is not a TRC A residence visa permits an individual to reside in the UAE under the immigration framework. An Emirates ID serves as an official identity document. Neither document, by itself, is a Tax Residency Certificate. The three documents perform different functions: A residence visa establishes immigration permission; An Emirates ID establishes identity and resident status for administrative purposes; and A TRC provides formal evidence of tax residence for the stated period and purpose. An Indian citizen may possess a long-term UAE residence visa while continuing to spend most of the year in India. The person’s tax residence must still be determined under the applicable domestic laws and treaty. Business owners should avoid describing themselves as UAE tax residents merely because they hold a Golden Visa, employment visa, investor visa or Emirates ID. Applying for a UAE TRC as an individual An individual seeking a UAE TRC must submit an application through the Federal Tax Authority’s designated electronic platform. The evidence required depends on the applicant’s circumstances and the purpose for which the certificate is requested. The supporting documents may include: Passport; Emirates ID, where applicable; Official immigration entry-and-exit report; Proof of accommodation in the UAE; Employment contract or salary certificate; Evidence of business activity or professional income; UAE bank statements; Evidence of a permanent home; Information concerning financial and personal interests; and Documents required under the relevant tax treaty. The official immigration report is especially important because treaty eligibility may depend on the number of days physically spent in the UAE. Travel days should be calculated from official records rather than memory, flight tickets or calendar entries alone. Frequent travel between Dubai and India can make informal calculations unreliable. An individual should also confirm that the certificate period corresponds with the year for which treaty relief is being claimed. A certificate for one period should not be assumed to prove residence for an earlier or later year. Applying for a UAE TRC as a company A UAE company may require a TRC when it earns income from India or another treaty jurisdiction. The application normally requires more than a trade license and incorporation certificate. Depending on the circumstances, the company may need to provide: Valid UAE trade license; Certificate of incorporation; Memorandum or constitutional documents; UAE Corporate Tax registration number, where available; Office lease or evidence of business premises; Financial statements; Identification and authority documents of the signatory; Details of directors and senior management; Evidence of operational activity; and Information showing where management and control are exercised. Under the India–UAE treaty, a UAE company’s residence position requires careful attention to incorporation as well as management and control in the UAE. Consider a Dubai company that has a UAE trade license but no employees, management team or operational office in the Emirates. Its Indian shareholder approves contracts, controls online banking and makes every strategic decision from India. A UAE incorporation certificate or TRC application cannot erase those operating facts. The company may face questions concerning: Its qualification as a UAE treaty resident; Place of effective management under Indian law; Existence of an Indian permanent establishment; Commercial substance; Beneficial ownership; and Application of treaty anti-abuse provisions. The strongest TRC application is one supported by genuine and continuing UAE business activity. Eligibility timing for newly incorporated UAE companies A newly established company should not assume that it can obtain a UAE TRC immediately after incorporation. Under the current Federal Tax Authority service conditions, a juridical person generally must have been established for at least 12 months before it becomes eligible for the certificate. The permitted application timing also depends on the selected period. This can create a practical issue for a new Dubai company entering into an Indian contract during its first year. If the Indian customer requires a valid TRC before applying treaty treatment, the new company may not yet be able to provide one. The parties should consider this before finalising the commercial price, withholding clause and payment schedule. The agreement should address: Which party bears withholding tax; Whether the consideration is grossed up; Documents required before payment; Consequences of delayed certificate availability; and Procedure for obtaining a refund, if applicable. Tax documentation should form part of transaction planning rather than becoming an afterthought when the first invoice is due. Certificate period and transaction timing A UAE TRC is issued for a selected tax period or another qualifying period of up to 12 months. It is not issued indefinitely and does not ordinarily certify future residence. Period matching is particularly important in India–UAE transactions because the Indian and UAE reporting periods may not always align. The business should compare: Period stated on the TRC; Indian tax year in which the income arises; Date of invoice; Date of payment or credit; Date on which withholding is triggered; and Period used for the relevant UAE residence analysis. If a certificate expires during a long-term contract, an updated TRC may be required for subsequent payments. Indian customers often request fresh documents each year, even where the contractual relationship remains unchanged. How a UAE resident should use a TRC in India Obtaining the certificate is only the first step. The UAE recipient must provide the appropriate documentation to the Indian payer or other relevant party in time for the withholding decision. A practical document package may include: Valid UAE TRC issued for the treaty purpose; Current Indian prescribed residence-information form; Tax identification particulars; Permanent Account Number, where held or required; Declaration regarding beneficial ownership; No-permanent-establishment declaration, where relevant; Copy of the governing contract; Invoice and description of services; Supporting note on the applicable treaty article; and Any declaration requested by the Indian payer. For tax years governed by the Income-tax Act, 2025, the prescribed additional information is furnished through Form 41 under the applicable rules. Older periods and guidance under the Income-tax Act, 1961 may refer to Form 10F. The correct form must be selected according to the law governing the relevant tax year. Businesses should not keep using an old template merely because it was accepted for an earlier payment. Practical example: Dubai consultancy receiving fees from India Assume a Dubai consulting company prepares a market-entry report for an Indian customer. The work is performed by its employees from its UAE office, and the company has no office, dependent agent or service presence in India. Before making payment, the Indian customer asks for treaty documents. The Dubai company provides: UAE TRC covering the relevant period; Current prescribed Indian form; No-permanent-establishment declaration; Beneficial-ownership confirmation; Service agreement; Invoice; and Explanation of where the services were performed. These documents allow the Indian customer to evaluate the payment properly. However, the TRC does not itself answer whether the amount constitutes business profits, royalty or another class of income. The rights granted under the contract, the nature of the deliverables and the company’s presence in India must still be examined. If the Dubai team transfers intellectual-property rights or employees perform extensive work from the customer’s premises in India, the result may be different. Residence documentation and income classification are connected, but they are not the same question. Practical example: UAE shareholder receiving an Indian dividend Suppose a Dubai company owns shares in an Indian business and receives a dividend. The UAE company may seek the benefit of the dividend article in the India–UAE DTAA. The Indian company should examine whether the recipient: Qualifies as a UAE treaty resident; Holds a valid TRC for the relevant period; Has furnished the prescribed Indian information; Is the beneficial owner of the dividend; Has a permanent establishment to which the holding is effectively connected; and Satisfies applicable anti-abuse requirements. If the UAE entity is merely required to pass the dividend to another party and exercises no real control over the income, possession of a TRC may not resolve the beneficial-ownership concern. What a TRC does not prove A TRC is important, but its legal role should not be overstated. It does not, by itself, establish: That a payment is exempt from Indian tax; The correct withholding rate; The classification of income; Beneficial ownership; Absence of a permanent establishment; Commercial substance; Arm’s-length pricing; Compliance with FEMA; Eligibility for foreign tax credit; Absence of an Indian place-of-effective-management risk; or Protection from treaty anti-abuse provisions. These matters require independent examination. A UAE company may hold a valid TRC and still create a permanent establishment through employees or agents in India. Similarly, a UAE individual may possess a TRC but remain exposed to Indian tax on income that India is entitled to tax under the treaty. The certificate opens the door to treaty analysis; it does not determine the final outcome. Commercial substance remains relevant The India–UAE treaty, as modified by applicable international anti-abuse provisions, cannot safely be used through an entity established mainly to secure a tax advantage without genuine commercial reasons. Authorities may examine whether a UAE company has: Competent personnel; Suitable premises; Independent decision-making; Financial capacity; Control over assets and income; Responsibility for commercial risks; Proper accounting records; Local operational expenditure; and Activities consistent with its trade license and contracts. The mere existence of a Dubai address, bank account and TRC may not be sufficient where the company’s real business continues to be managed and performed from India. Indian residents requiring a TRC for use in the UAE The documentation flow also works in the opposite direction. An Indian resident earning income from the UAE may need an Indian TRC to claim benefits available under the India–UAE DTAA. For tax years governed by the Income-tax Act, 2025, the resident may apply electronically in Form 42. The Indian tax authority issues the residence certificate in Form 43 after examining the application. The applicant should ensure that: Indian residence is supportable for the relevant year; The application period matches the UAE income period; Income and treaty details are accurate; The certificate is obtained before the UAE payer’s deadline; and Any separate UAE procedural requirements are completed. For earlier periods governed by the Income-tax Act, 1961, the corresponding older forms and procedures may continue to apply. The applicable tax year, rather than the date on which the question arises, determines which legislation and forms govern the matter. Common TRC mistakes The practical problems surrounding TRCs are usually caused by timing or documentation rather than the basic treaty principle. Common mistakes include: Treating a UAE visa as proof of treaty residence; Applying for a domestic-purpose certificate instead of a treaty-purpose TRC; Selecting the wrong treaty country; Using a certificate that does not cover the payment period; Applying before the eligibility conditions are satisfied; Miscalculating UAE physical-presence days; Ignoring the treaty’s separate residence definition; Submitting the old Indian form for a period governed by the new law; Providing documents only after tax has been deducted; Assuming that the TRC automatically determines the treaty rate; Failing to establish beneficial ownership; Giving a no-permanent-establishment declaration that contradicts actual activity; Claiming UAE management while decisions are made from India; Using inconsistent names or addresses across the license, TRC, invoice and contract; and Allowing the certificate to expire during a continuing payment arrangement. A practical pre-payment checklist Before a UAE resident requests treaty treatment from an Indian payer, the following questions should be answered: 1. Does the recipient satisfy the India–UAE treaty’s residence definition? 2. Has the correct UAE certificate been obtained specifically for treaty purposes? 3. Does the certificate cover the relevant payment and tax period? 4. Has the current Indian prescribed form been completed? 5. What is the correct character of the income under the treaty? 6. Is the UAE recipient the beneficial owner? 7. Does the recipient have any office, employee, agent or project presence in India? 8. Is the income connected with an Indian permanent establishment? 9. Are the contract, invoice and declarations factually consistent? 10. Have applicable withholding, PAN and filing requirements been examined? 11. Does the UAE entity have sufficient commercial substance? 12. Has the documentation been given to the Indian payer before payment or credit? Completing this review before invoicing can prevent delayed payments, contractual disagreements and unnecessary withholding. Conclusion A Tax Residency Certificate is a central document in India–UAE tax planning, but it should be viewed as evidence rather than a complete tax solution. For individuals in Dubai, physical presence must be documented carefully, and the difference between UAE domestic residence and India–UAE treaty residence must be understood. For companies, incorporation and a trade license are not enough; management, control and commercial operations should genuinely support the claimed UAE position. The certificate should also match the relevant period and be accompanied by the current Indian form and transaction-specific declarations. Classification of income, permanent establishment, beneficial ownership, withholding tax and anti-abuse provisions must be considered separately. The most effective approach is to obtain and review the TRC package before the Indian payer processes the transaction. Correct documentation delivered at the right time is far more useful than a certificate produced only after excessive tax has already been withheld.

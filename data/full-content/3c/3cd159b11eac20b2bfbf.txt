Registry Objections Cannot Become Barriers to Access to Justice: The Madras High Court, Madurai Bench, in Selvaraj Vs Koodankulam Nuclear Power Plant India Limited & connected matters, delivered an important judgment concerning the powers of civil court registries and the permissible scope of scrutiny before a plaint is registered as a civil suit. Justice N. Seshasayee examined five civil revision petitions filed under Article 227 of the Constitution of India, involving complaints that plaints were repeatedly returned on objections concerning their legal merits or remained pending for considerable periods without registration. The Court identified three recurring problems: first, registries were raising objections that properly belonged to judicial adjudication rather than preliminary scrutiny; secondly, plaints were returned repeatedly, with fresh objections being introduced after earlier defects had been rectified; and thirdly, some plaints remained neither numbered nor returned for weeks or months. The Court observed that these practices caused serious inconvenience to litigants, undermined procedural consistency and obstructed the fundamental right of access to justice. Rather than merely deciding the individual petitions, the Court considered it necessary to examine the wider institutional problem and formulate uniform principles governing the registration of civil suits. It emphasised that the judiciary’s responsibility is not confined to deciding disputes after their institution but also extends to ensuring that litigants are not arbitrarily prevented from entering the judicial system. 2. Land Acquisition Dispute and Repeated Return of Plaint in CRP(MD) No. 915 of 2020: The first petition arose from a dispute involving approximately 2.44 acres of land in Survey No. 218/2A, together with a borewell and buildings, situated near the Koodankulam nuclear power project. The petitioner asserted that although adjoining lands had been acquired for the project, the disputed property had not formed part of the acquisition. According to the plaint, the acquired portion was assigned Survey No. 218/2B, while the unacquired portion remained Survey No. 218/2A. Nevertheless, the patta issued in favour of the project authorities allegedly included the unacquired land. The petitioner had previously instituted O.S. No. 89 of 2015 seeking declaration of title and had filed an application to correct an error concerning the survey number. During the pendency of those proceedings, he alleged that he was forcibly dispossessed and that a compound wall was constructed over the disputed property. He therefore presented a suit under Section 6 of the Specific Relief Act, 1963, seeking recovery of possession. The trial court initially returned the plaint on questions concerning land acquisition, the pending declaration suit and the maintainability of proceedings against the project authorities. After the petitioner answered those objections, the plaint was returned again, this time raising an additional objection under Section 6(2)(b) concerning suits against the Government. The High Court found that the trial court had misunderstood the nature of the claim, failed to appreciate the distinction between a possessory remedy and a title-based action, and incorrectly treated a government company as equivalent to the Government for the statutory restriction. These errors demonstrated the danger of prematurely examining the merits before registering a plaint. 3. Delayed Registration, Cheque Disputes, Commercial Injunctions and Property Title Litigation: The remaining four petitions illustrated different forms of procedural obstruction. In CRP(MD) No. 943 of 2020, the petitioner complained that a plaint presented on 31 August 2020 had remained neither registered nor returned for approximately two months. In CRP(MD) No. 991 of 2020, the plaintiff had already instituted O.S. No. 201 of 2019 for recovery of money based on a promissory note and acknowledgement of debt. He subsequently presented another suit seeking a declaration that six cheques issued by him were unsupported by consideration, together with an injunction restraining their encashment. The trial court returned the plaint after invoking Section 41(b) of the Specific Relief Act and questioning the maintainability of the proposed relief. In CRP(MD) No. 967 of 2020, a plaintiff sought an injunction restraining defendants from recovering amounts arising from commercial transactions otherwise than through due process of law. The plaint was repeatedly returned, initially for formal defects and subsequently on objections under Section 41(d) of the Specific Relief Act and the supposed unavailability of injunctions against persons in their personal capacity. In CRP(MD) No. 330 of 2020, the plaintiff claimed title to three cents of land allegedly allotted to his grandfather in a family partition. He challenged a subsequent sale deed concerning the larger property and alleged fraudulent acquisition of patta. The trial court rejected the unnumbered plaint through a detailed order, reasoning that the challenge to the 1971 sale deed was barred by limitation because the plaintiff had not specified when he discovered the alleged fraud. The High Court considered these proceedings collectively because they exposed the tendency of trial courts to decide disputed questions of law and fact before the suits had even been registered. 4. Constitutional Right of Access to Justice and the Inherent Right to Institute Civil Proceedings: The High Court approached the controversy through the maxim ubi jus ibi remedium, meaning that where the law recognises a right, it ordinarily provides a remedy for its violation. It explained that Section 9 of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908, reflects this principle by recognising the jurisdiction of civil courts to try suits of a civil nature unless their cognisance is expressly or impliedly barred. Referring to Ganga Bai Vs Vijay Kumar, (1974) 2 SCC 393, the Court observed that every person possesses an inherent right to bring a civil suit unless prohibited by statute. The existence of a weak or ultimately unsuccessful claim does not, by itself, authorise a registry to prevent its presentation. The Court also relied upon Anita Kushwaha Vs Pushap Sudan, (2016) 8 SCC 509, in which the Supreme Court recognised access to justice as a facet of the right to life under Article 21 of the Constitution. An effective adjudicatory mechanism must be fair, objective and accessible, and the procedure governing access must itself satisfy the requirements of justice. The High Court distinguished between the litigant’s substantive right to seek judicial redress and the court’s responsibility to determine the dispute. A civil court cannot discharge its adjudicatory responsibility unless the litigant is first permitted to enter the judicial process. Consequently, scrutiny of a plaint must be conducted as an exercise in procedural compliance rather than as an opportunity to determine whether the plaintiff deserves relief. The Court warned that procedural barriers created without statutory authority may defeat the very rights that the judicial system exists to protect. 5. Registration of Plaint Is a Ministerial Function Distinct From Judicial Adjudication: A central feature of the judgment was its detailed examination of the difference between administrative or ministerial acts and judicial acts. The Court explained that judicial adjudication ordinarily involves application of judicial mind to questions of law, examination of facts, consideration of evidence and determination of substantive rights or liabilities. By contrast, scrutiny of a plaint for registration primarily involves verifying compliance with the procedural requirements prescribed by law. The judgment referred to several authorities concerning the nature of judicial power, including Regina (John M’Evoy) Vs Dublin Corporation, Royal Aquarium and Summer and Winter Garden Society Ltd. Vs Parkinson, Attorney General of Gambia Vs Pierre Sarr N’Jie and R.M. Seshadri Vs Second Additional Income-Tax Officer. It also considered Indian decisions treating the numbering of proceedings as a ministerial function, including P. Surendran Vs State by Inspector of Police, K. Venkatesan Vs E. Hemantharaj, G.V. Vanitha Vs K. Dhanasekaran, M. Ramachandran Vs M. Palani and M. Rajendran Vs Government of India. The Court held that preliminary scrutiny should not require elaborate factual investigation or assessment of the plaintiff’s entitlement to relief. The registry cannot assume the position of a defendant by raising potential defences, disputing allegations, demanding proof of contested facts or compelling changes to the nature of the relief sought. Such questions belong to the judicial process and must be considered at the legally appropriate stage. 6. Presentation of Plaint, Institution of Suit and Statutory Requirements Under CPC: The High Court examined Order IV Rule 1 CPC, which provides for institution of suits through presentation of plaints complying with the requirements of Orders VI and VII. It explained that the law distinguishes between the physical presentation of a plaint and its formal registration as a suit. Relying upon Olympic Cards Limited Vs Standard Chartered Bank, (2013) 1 LW 385, the Court noted that presentation ordinarily results in assignment of a diary or filing number, whereas registration and entry in the register of suits complete the institutional process. A plaint returned for rectification of defects remains a returned plaint and not a registered suit. The date of presentation may nevertheless remain relevant for limitation purposes, subject to the applicable law. The Court recognised that the registry may examine whether the plaint contains the particulars required by Order VII, whether procedural requirements have been complied with and whether the prescribed documents and other filing requirements are satisfied. However, the existence of these powers does not permit the registry to supplement the Code with additional requirements based on the preferences of individual judicial officers. The Court emphasised that the procedural framework must be applied uniformly across the district judiciary. It also referred to the principle that procedural law exists to facilitate justice, observing through P. Suresh Vs R. Rangasamy that procedure must not become an obstacle to enforcement of substantive rights. The distinction between presentation and registration therefore provides no justification for converting the preliminary stage into a substantive enquiry into the merits of the proposed suit. 7. Order VII Rules 10 and 11 CPC: Permissible Rejection Cannot Become a Mini-Trial: The Court analysed the statutory distinction between return of plaint under Order VII Rule 10 CPC and rejection of plaint under Order VII Rule 11 CPC. Order VII Rule 10 deals with returning a plaint for presentation before the court in which the suit should have been instituted, while Rule 11 permits rejection on specified grounds, including failure to disclose a cause of action, undervaluation not corrected within the permitted time, insufficient court fee not remedied, and a suit appearing from the plaint itself to be barred by law. Relying upon Madiraju Venkata Ramana Raju Vs Peddireddigari Ramachandra Reddy, (2018) 14 SCC 1, the High Court acknowledged that a plaint may be rejected even before formal registration if the statutory grounds are established. However, the Court emphasised that this power must be exercised judicially and cannot justify a roving enquiry into the truth of the plaintiff’s allegations. The scrutiny must ordinarily proceed on the statements contained in the plaint itself, without demanding additional evidence or evaluating possible defences. Where limitation is apparent from the plaint, the court may examine the statutory bar; however, where the plaintiff pleads facts supporting an exemption from limitation under Order VII Rule 6, the matter may require consideration after registration. The judgment therefore did not prohibit all threshold examination of maintainability. Instead, it distinguished legitimate scrutiny for statutory and institutional defects from an impermissible examination of contested factual and legal questions. The Court made clear that a judicial power to reject a plaint cannot be converted into an unrestricted administrative power to refuse its numbering. 8. Court Fees, Suit Valuation and Limits on Preliminary Scrutiny: The High Court separately examined disputes concerning valuation of suits and payment of court fees, observing that these questions frequently resulted in repeated returns of plaints. It considered Order VII Rule 11(b) and (c) CPC, which provide for rejection where undervaluation or insufficient stamping is not corrected within the time allowed by the court. The judgment also examined Sections 10 and 12 of the Tamil Nadu Court Fees and Suits Valuation Act, 1955. Under Section 12(1), the court must consider the proper court fee before registration on the basis of the plaint allegations, accompanying materials and valuation statement. Nevertheless, the High Court explained that this responsibility does not authorise an elaborate investigation into disputed facts or the collection of additional evidence at the preliminary stage. Where the plaint itself provides objective material demonstrating an incorrect valuation, the court may act in accordance with law. However, disputes subsequently raised by defendants concerning valuation may be considered under Section 12(2), including by framing an appropriate preliminary issue. The Court referred to S.RM. AR. S. SP. Sathappa Chettiar Vs S. RM. AR. RM. Ramanathan Chettiar, AIR 1958 SC 245, Tara Devi Vs Sri Thakur Radha Krishna Maharaj, (1987) 4 SCC 69, and Commercial Aviation and Travel Co. Vs Vimla Pannalal, (1988) 3 SCC 423. It also considered K. Natarajan Vs P.K. Rajasekaran, 2003 3 LW 803, concerning scrutiny of plaints and deficiencies in court fees. The Court’s approach sought to reconcile the statutory duty to verify court fees with the equally important obligation not to obstruct registration through unnecessary enquiries. 9. Detailed Checklist Prescribed for Registry Scrutiny Before Numbering Suits: A significant part of the judgment was the comprehensive checklist contained in paragraph 31, identifying matters that may legitimately be examined during preliminary scrutiny and matters that must remain outside its scope. The Court permitted verification of the cause title, form of pleading, necessary particulars of parties, representative capacity where applicable, territorial and pecuniary jurisdiction, specified amounts in money suits, property descriptions, statutory filing requirements and other formal matters contemplated by the Code. However, it prohibited examination of the sufficiency or adequacy of substantive pleadings, demands for proof of allegations, testing of the correctness of the plaintiff’s factual assertions and adjudication of disputed merits. Concerning cause of action, the registry could examine whether the plaint disclosed a legally recognisable or enforceable right on its plain reading, but could not insist upon proof or undertake an extensive examination of the claim. Concerning jurisdiction, any statutory exclusion of civil court jurisdiction had to be clearly established and strictly understood. Regarding limitation, a plaint could be scrutinised where the bar was apparent on its face, but the registry could not demand additional evidence or clarificatory material to decide disputed limitation questions. The Court also recognised that a plaintiff’s choice of relief cannot be arbitrarily rewritten by the registry. The checklist was intended to establish consistent standards across the district judiciary, replacing varying individual practices with a common procedural framework. Its central principle was that preliminary scrutiny must identify statutory and formal defects without anticipating the judicial determination of the suit. 10. Repeated Return of Plaint Prohibited and Hearing Mandatory Before Rejection: The High Court strongly criticised the practice of repeatedly returning plaints on different grounds after litigants had already complied with earlier objections. Referring to S. Parameswari Vs Denis Lourdusamy, (2011) 5 CTC 742, it observed that the impropriety of repeated returns had already been recognised, yet the practice continued in the district judiciary. The Court also considered Muthuganesah Vs Thillaimani, 2016-2-LW 340, concerning the appropriate procedure when a plaintiff disputes the necessity of complying with objections raised by the court. In paragraph 32.1, the High Court directed that whenever rejection of a plaint under Order VII Rule 11 is contemplated even before registration, the matter must be posted before the open court and the plaintiff or counsel must be heard. The opportunity of hearing was treated as an essential element of procedural fairness, not a formality dependent upon a specific request by the litigant. In paragraph 32.2, the Court further directed that no court should return a plaint more than once for curing permissible defects. If objections remain disputed or the plaintiff refuses to comply with a requirement considered mandatory, the appropriate course is judicial consideration and an order according to law, rather than successive administrative returns. These directions sought to prevent indefinite delays, arbitrary introduction of fresh objections and the denial of an effective remedy against decisions concerning registration. The Court emphasised that procedural fairness must operate from the earliest stage of civil litigation. 11. Three-Working-Day Deadline and Supervisory Responsibility of the High Court: Recognising that delay in registering a plaint can itself cause serious prejudice, the High Court prescribed a specific timeline in paragraph 32.3. It directed that a decision concerning numbering of a presented plaint, in accordance with the framework laid down in paragraph 31, must be taken within three working days, excluding the date of presentation and intervening holidays. The direction addressed the recurring complaint that plaints remained pending for weeks or months without any decision, leaving litigants uncertain whether their proceedings would be registered or returned. The Court also stressed the need for administrative measures to secure compliance with the prescribed standards and observed that the State Judicial Academy could devote attention to improving procedural understanding within the district judiciary. In paragraph 33, it indicated that the administrative implementation of the declared parameters should be considered by the High Court. In paragraph 34, the Court further observed that civil revision petitions challenging procedural errors reflect the quality of procedural administration in subordinate courts. An effective district judiciary, equipped with a proper understanding of procedural law, would reduce avoidable litigation and unnecessary supervisory intervention. The judgment therefore combined legal clarification with institutional reform, seeking to make registration of civil suits prompt, predictable and consistent while preserving the judicial power to reject plaints on legitimate statutory grounds. 12. Final Findings on All Five Petitions and Legal Significance of the Judgment: Applying the principles developed in its judgment, the Madras High Court concluded that the concerned trial courts had improperly extended preliminary scrutiny into matters requiring judicial consideration. In CRP(MD) No. 915 of 2020, the trial court had wrongly treated a possessory suit under Section 6 of the Specific Relief Act as a challenge to land acquisition, failed to appreciate the distinction between possession and title, and incorrectly equated a government company with the Government for the statutory restriction. In CRP(MD) No. 991 of 2020, the Court found that the objection under Section 41(b) of the Specific Relief Act had been misconceived in the context of the plaintiff’s declaration and injunction claims concerning cheques. In CRP(MD) No. 967 of 2020, the trial court had wrongly questioned the maintainability of a prohibitory injunction intended to prevent recovery of commercial debts otherwise than through lawful means. In CRP(MD) No. 330 of 2020, the trial court had prematurely examined limitation and the alleged fraudulent sale deed, thereby undertaking an adjudicatory exercise at the pre-registration stage. The High Court accordingly allowed CRP(MD) Nos. 915, 967, 991 and 330 of 2020, set aside the challenged orders and directed the respective trial courts to number the plaints and proceed with the suits according to law. In CRP(MD) No. 943 of 2020, the plaint had already been registered during the proceedings, and therefore the petition was closed without costs. The Registry was directed to return the respective original plaints to the petitioners in the four allowed revisions. The judgment establishes that although courts retain their statutory powers to scrutinise and reject defective plaints, they must not conduct mini-trials, insist upon proof of disputed allegations, repeatedly return plaints on fresh objections or indefinitely delay registration. Its broader significance lies in protecting access to justice under Articles 21 and 227 of the Constitution and ensuring that procedural law remains an instrument for administering justice rather than an obstacle to its delivery.

Vs DCIT (Supreme Court of India) Case Summary: The Supreme Court of India declined to interfere with the judgment of the Madras High Court in the case of Gangotri Textiles Ltd. Vs DCIT, wherein the High Court had upheld the levy of penalty under Section 271(1)(c) of the Income-tax Act, 1961 for non-disclosure of substantial capital gains arising from the sale of lands and windmills during Assessment Year 2012-13. The assessee, a listed company, had filed its return of income on 26.09.2012 without declaring capital gains arising from the sale of two landed properties situated at Kalapatti and Dharapuram and the sale of windmills. During scrutiny assessment under Section 143(3), the Assessing Officer discovered that the assessee had omitted long-term capital gains of ₹1,37,31,142 arising from the sale of lands and short-term capital gains of ₹21,59,90,469 arising from the sale of windmills for a consideration of ₹21,60,00,000, after deducting the opening written-down value of ₹9,531. These transactions had not been reported under the capital gains schedule of the return, where the assessee had declared ‘Nil’. After the discrepancies were detected during assessment proceedings, the assessee submitted a letter dated 03.03.2015 admitting that the capital gains had been omitted due to oversight and furnished a revised computation of income. The Assessing Officer included the undisclosed capital gains in the assessed income and separately initiated penalty proceedings under Section 274 read with Section 271(1)(c). The assessee claimed that the omission was inadvertent, that the relevant transactions had been disclosed in its published annual report and that the company was experiencing severe financial difficulties following the sale of its assets by bankers. These explanations were rejected, and the Assessing Officer imposed the minimum penalty of 100% of the tax sought to be evaded by order dated 25.09.2015. The Commissioner of Income Tax (Appeals) and the Income Tax Appellate Tribunal subsequently upheld the penalty, leading the assessee to approach the Madras High Court under Section 260A of the Act. Before the Madras High Court, the assessee challenged the penalty proceedings primarily on the ground that the show-cause notice dated 12.03.2015 issued under Section 274 read with Section 271(1)(c) was defective, vague and indicative of non-application of mind by the Assessing Officer. According to the assessee, the notice referred to both concealment of particulars of income and furnishing of inaccurate particulars of income without clearly specifying the precise statutory charge. It was argued that these were two distinct defaults and that the Assessing Officer was required to identify the relevant limb before initiating penalty proceedings. The assessee relied upon several judicial precedents, including CIT Vs Manjunatha Cotton and Ginning Factory, (2013) 359 ITR 565 (Karnataka High Court), CIT Vs SSA’s Emerald Meadows, and CIT Vs Samson Perinchery, which were cited in support of the requirement that a penalty notice must communicate the specific allegation against the taxpayer. The assessee also relied upon decisions concerning the necessity of recording satisfaction and the distinction between concealment of income and furnishing inaccurate particulars. The Revenue opposed the challenge, pointing out that the assessee had never objected to the alleged defect before the Assessing Officer, Commissioner (Appeals) or Tribunal. The High Court examined the issue with reference to its earlier decision in Sundaram Finance Ltd. Vs DCIT, (2018) 93 taxmann.com 250 (Madras), and held that the objection concerning the notice, in the circumstances of the present case, involved a mixed question of fact and law rather than an exclusively legal issue. The assessee had responded to the notice on 08.04.2015 by expressly denying both concealment and furnishing of inaccurate particulars, thereby demonstrating its understanding of the allegations. The Court considered that no prejudice had been established and declined to permit the assessee to raise the alleged defect for the first time at the High Court stage. Thus, the High Court rejected the procedural challenge on the particular facts and conduct of the assessee, rather than laying down that every defective penalty notice is necessarily valid. The distinction between the statutory charges remains important, as also reflected in the judicial discussion of penalty proceedings involving inconsistent charges in the notice and assessment record. On the substantive question of concealment and bona fide conduct, the High Court examined the chronology of the assessment proceedings and found that the assessee’s explanation of an inadvertent mistake was not supported by its conduct. The original return filed on 26.09.2012 contained no disclosure of the sale of lands or windmills under the head ‘Capital Gains’. Although notice under Section 143(2) had been issued on 13.08.2013 and subsequent information was sought under Section 142(1), the assessee did not disclose the relevant transactions in its reply dated 22.09.2014. Only on 03.03.2015, following scrutiny and detection of the transactions through information obtained by the Department, did the assessee submit a computation incorporating the omitted capital gains. The Court noted that the assessee had taken approximately 19 months to respond substantively to the scrutiny notice concerning the omitted transactions and had not filed a revised return of income. The company attempted to submit a revised statement of income on 01.03.2017, nearly two years after completion of the assessment, but the High Court held that this subsequent action could not improve its case or absolve it of penalty liability. The assessee relied particularly upon Price Waterhouse Coopers (P.) Ltd. Vs CIT (Supreme Court), (2012) 348 ITR 306, wherein a genuinely inadvertent and bona fide error was held insufficient to justify penalty. However, the High Court distinguished the principle on facts, holding that the conduct of Gangotri Textiles did not establish a genuine bona fide mistake. The Court emphasised that disclosure in an annual report prepared for corporate reporting purposes was not equivalent to disclosure in the income-tax return, especially when the annual report itself had not been furnished to the Income Tax Department. It further observed that the capital gains had been reported as ‘Nil’ in the computation prepared by the Chartered Accountant and signed by the Managing Director. The information concerning the transactions had emerged through the Department’s Annual Information Return mechanism rather than through voluntary disclosure in the original return. Consequently, the High Court concluded that the belated explanation of oversight lacked credibility and that the case involved concealment of income as well as furnishing of inaccurate particulars. The facts were therefore materially different from cases involving demonstrably bona fide computational errors corrected during assessment proceedings. The High Court also rejected the assessee’s contention that the penalty order was unreasoned and that the Assessing Officer had failed to record the requisite satisfaction for initiating proceedings under Section 271(1)(c). After examining the penalty order dated 25.09.2015, the Court found that the Assessing Officer had considered the explanation of inadvertence, the financial condition of the company and the judicial authorities relied upon by the assessee before concluding that the explanation lacked bona fides. In particular, the Court referred to MAK Data (P.) Ltd. Vs CIT, (2013) 358 ITR 593 (Supreme Court), for the principle that a disclosure made during assessment proceedings does not automatically release an assessee from penalty liability. The same principle concerning the limits of voluntary disclosure is discussed in the TaxGuru report on voluntary disclosure and liability to penalty. The assessee’s argument that the penalty should not survive because its returned income and assessed income continued to reflect substantial losses was also rejected. The assessee had contended that the recomputation of capital gains merely reduced its reported loss from approximately ₹33 crore to ₹11 crore and did not produce positive taxable income. The High Court relied upon CIT Vs Gold Coin Health Food Pvt. Ltd., (2008) 304 ITR 308 (Supreme Court), which recognised that the provisions governing penalty for concealment can apply even where additions result in the reduction of a returned loss and the final assessed income remains a loss. The Court also referred to Union of India Vs Dharmendra Textile Processors, (2008) 306 ITR 277 (Supreme Court), in the context of the civil character of penalty liability, and considered the Revenue’s reliance on Jivanlal and Sons, where attributing an incorrect claim to professional advice had not justified cancellation of penalty. These decisions reinforced the Court’s conclusion that neither financial distress nor the absence of positive assessed income automatically established bona fides or precluded the imposition of penalty. The Court ultimately found the penalty order to be a reasoned determination supported by the factual record. In its concluding findings, the Madras High Court held that the assessee had failed to establish any ground for interference with the concurrent findings of the Assessing Officer, Commissioner of Income Tax (Appeals) and Income Tax Appellate Tribunal. The substantial questions of law relating to the validity of the penalty notice, alleged complete disclosure through financial statements and the claimed debatable nature of capital gains reporting were answered against the assessee. The Court held that the belated challenge to the show-cause notice could not succeed in the absence of demonstrated prejudice, that the disclosure made in the annual report did not establish compliance with the income-tax reporting requirements and that the subsequent admission of previously omitted capital gains did not establish a bona fide mistake. Accordingly, the tax case appeal was dismissed, the penalty under Section 271(1)(c) was sustained, and the connected miscellaneous petitions were closed. The assessee thereafter approached the Supreme Court by way of special leave petitions. After hearing senior counsel for the petitioner, the Supreme Court stated that, in the facts and circumstances of the case, it was not inclined to interfere with the impugned judgment and order of the High Court in exercise of its powers under Article 136 of the Constitution of India. The special leave petitions were therefore dismissed and the pending applications disposed of. The Supreme Court did not deliver a separate reasoned adjudication of the substantive penalty issues. Its order left the Madras High Court judgment undisturbed, but the non-speaking dismissal of special leave should not be treated as an independent declaration of law on every issue considered by the High Court. The combined proceedings illustrate the importance of accurate reporting of capital gains in the income-tax return, the evidentiary significance of the taxpayer’s conduct during scrutiny assessment, the need to substantiate an alleged bona fide mistake and the legal distinction between a genuine procedural defect causing prejudice and an objection raised belatedly without demonstrating such prejudice. The limits of applying this decision to other defective-notice disputes have also been considered in subsequent proceedings, including the ITAT Chennai decision distinguishing Gangotri Textiles in a defective penalty notice dispute.

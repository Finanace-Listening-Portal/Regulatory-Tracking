Articles 11 min read IRDAI proposes sweeping insurance distribution overhaul; cuts EoM, caps commissions, targets mis-selling The Insurance Regulatory and Development Authority of India (IRDAI) has proposed a five-year glide path to bring life insurers’ EoM to 12.5% and general insurers’ to 20%, while commission limits will be linked to product complexity, distribution channel and selling effort. IRDAI said the reforms seek to create a customer-centric, competitive, efficient and transparent distribution ecosystem. Advt The regulator's diagnosis is particularly sharp: insurance distribution costs have risen considerably even as the expansion in the number of insured people has remained limited. IRDAI said the 2023 EoM reforms, which gave insurers greater flexibility at the entity level, have not delivered the expected improvement in affordability, insurance penetration and customer value. Distribution costs have instead returned to historically high levels.Distributor payouts growing much faster than businessOne of the strongest data points in the consultation paper relates to the life insurance corporate agency channel. In a representative sample covering about 92% of premium procured through corporate agents, new business premium increased 28% between FY23 and FY25, while total distributor remuneration jumped 125%.IRDAI said distributor remuneration now accounts for nearly 27% of first-year premium, with rewards and incentives adding another 30%-60% over base commission. The regulator said this makes base commission alone an incomplete measure of the actual cost of distribution.The paper also highlights wide variations in effective distributor payouts. Effective payouts on ULIPs range from around 5% to nearly 40%, while other savings products attract effective commissions of 29%-60% of first-year premium, exceeding 65% in some cases.The NBFC corporate agency channel has nearly tripled since FY23, with effective payouts averaging around 42% of new business premium. About 93% of this business comprises single-premium Group Credit Life products sold at the point of loan origination, where the customer has limited opportunity to compare products. Effective payouts on Group Credit Life have risen to around 45%, according to IRDAI. Advt IRDAI also points to a persistency mismatch. The 61st-month persistency rate for life insurance is only 48%, while the online channel has a 61st-month persistency rate of 71%. The regulator said this indicates that consciously and independently made purchases tend to show stronger continuation.EoM to be brought down to 12.5% for life, 20% for general insuranceAt the centre of the proposed reforms is a new EoM framework.For life insurers, IRDAI has proposed bringing EoM down to 15% of premium within two years and 12.5% within five years, with annual reductions beginning FY2027-28. Insurers that were already below the benchmark in FY25 would have to bring EoM down to 10% within five years, which IRDAI identifies as the sector's long-term goal. Advt For general insurers, the proposed glide path is 25% of premium within two years and 20% within five years, again beginning FY2027-28. Insurers already below the benchmark would also have to move to 20% within five years.The computation itself would also change. IRDAI proposes calculating EoM as gross expenses against Gross Direct Premium Income (GDPI) in India rather than Gross Written Premium. Gross commissions would continue to be included, while inward reinsurance would effectively be removed from the calculation. The regulator said this would eliminate the anomaly of double-counting premium in the sector. Advt The regulator is also proposing to cut its own regulatory fee on insurers from 0.05% to 0.04% of premium, subject to a maximum of ₹20 crore.IRDAI said the lower EoM framework is intended to expand the risk pool available in general insurance and improve returns for policyholders in life savings products.The paper also flags wide differences in operating expenses beyond commissions. In FY26, these ranged from 6.1%-27.7% of premium for life insurers and 6.8%-28.8% of GDPI for general insurers, according to IRDAI.Commission regime to shift from uniform caps to effort and complexityIRDAI is proposing a fundamental change in how distributor commissions are determined.Rather than a uniform approach, commission limits would factor in the insurance segment, line of business, distribution channel, product complexity and effort required to sell and service the product. The regulator also proposes higher commission limits for business sourced from underserved geographies.The proposed additional commission allowance is 10% of the applicable limit for business sourced from towns and cities with populations below 10 lakh, excluding the smaller-town category, and 20% for business sourced from towns with populations below 50,000 and all rural areas.The commission framework also distinguishes between open and closed distribution architectures. The consultation paper proposes higher commission opportunities for distributors operating within closed architecture, where they have restrictions on the insurers or products they can distribute, while open-architecture distributors would operate under opportunity-based commission levels.IRDAI also proposes that all forms of payments to distributors, including direct or indirect, monetary or non-monetary payments, be considered for regulatory purposes, making it harder to circumvent commission limits through rewards or other structures.For life insurance, the proposed commission grid would, for example, allow Distribution Entities a maximum first-year commission of 5%-20% on individual non-linked/linked products depending on premium-payment term, while agents would have a 6.25%-25% range. For products with a 10-year-and-above premium payment term, the proposed first-year ceiling is 20% for Distribution Entities and 25% for agents.The framework also seeks to increase the emphasis on renewal-linked remuneration. For eligible life products, renewal commission can rise progressively from the sixth policy year based on persistency, subject to a maximum of 7%, according to the proposed grid.Motor insurance commissions face particular scrutinyMotor insurance is singled out as a major area of concern.IRDAI said average motor insurance commission is around 24%, with a range of 13%-50%. OEM brokers and Motor Insurance Service Providers (MISPs), which account for about 30% of combined new and old vehicle business, receive an average commission of 24%, with the maximum reaching 31%.During FY25, OEM brokers and MISPs generated around ₹29,000 crore of premium and received almost ₹7,050 crore in commissions.For new vehicles, IRDAI said OEM brokers command average commissions of 27%, while MISPs receive as much as 38%, despite third-party insurance being mandatory for registration and the regulator's assessment that selling third-party and even own-damage/comprehensive cover for a new vehicle involves relatively low effort.Banks and NBFCs financing vehicle purchases and insisting on bundled insurance receive around 16% commission, according to the paper.The regulator is therefore proposing to reduce layers in motor distribution, encourage digital/direct purchase and impose much lower commission limits for mandatory third-party insurance and other easy-to-sell motor products.Banks, NBFCs face tighter rules on insurance salesThe consultation paper takes aim at the practice of selling insurance alongside loans and other financial products.IRDAI proposes to prohibit compulsory bundling of insurance with credit or loans, while allowing specific package offers where there is a demonstrable benefit to the customer.The proposal would allow certain combinations, such as group term insurance for depositors or borrowers, insurance bundled with credit cards without additional customer charges, and certain loan-plus-insurance arrangements where safeguards and informed consent are provided.For a loan where insurance is offered as an additional security, the customer would have to be told the interest rate with and without the insurance, and the customer would retain the ability to make an informed choice.The regulator is also proposing a direct attack on sales incentives that can encourage mis-selling. All forms of remuneration, direct or indirect, monetary or non-monetary, would be included in the regulatory definition of commission.For bank and NBFC employees selling insurance, IRDAI proposes prohibiting volume-linked or reward-linked incentives. The paper specifically refers to foreign and domestic trips, luxury gifts, milestone bonuses and contest rewards as examples of incentives that can create conflicts with customer suitability.Mis-selling could follow the individual salespersonThe proposed framework would introduce individual-level accountability for mis-selling.IRDAI proposes tagging the functional identity of specified persons, salespersons, PoSPs, agents or associates to the policies they sell. Information about mis-selling incidents would then be placed in the public domain as part of the person's performance record.The regulator says this would create accountability, act as a deterrent, alert future recruiters and allow prospective customers to consider a salesperson's mis-selling history.Mis-selling would also trigger commission claw-back by insurers.The consultation paper gives a detailed list of practices that could amount to mis-selling, including presenting a regular-premium product as single premium, selling regular-premium policies without explaining the consequences of discontinuing premium payments, selling term insurance to people outside their working age or without dependants, selling regular-premium policies to customers without steady income, and selling ULIPs to risk-averse or older customers without explaining the risks and charges.It also identifies selling insurance as a fixed-income deposit or high-return investment, inducing customers to surrender an existing policy in favour of a new one through misleading return promises, and selling high-value single-premium products with low liquidity and inadequate insurance coverage as examples of problematic practices.Three-category distribution architectureThe regulator is also proposing to replace the existing fragmented intermediary structure with three broad categories:Insurance Distribution Entities (IDEs)Insurance Distribution Persons (IDPs)Market Infrastructure Institutions (MIIs)The guiding principle is “same structure, same functions, same norms.”Entities with the same structure would have the same scope of business, obligations and regulatory framework. IRDAI proposes simpler registration, significantly lower entry and capital requirements and reduced regulatory fees.The proposed architecture would also allow distributors greater flexibility to undertake non-insurance financial and non-financial activities. Hospitals could distribute health insurance after registering as IDEs, subject to safeguards and lower commission limits, while non-dealer automobile garages could sell motor insurance by becoming associates of insurers.Insurers themselves could also distribute products of a group insurance company in a non-competing segment through branch offices, subject to safeguards and lower commissions.The intention is also to open insurance distribution to entrepreneurs in smaller towns and rural areas while creating additional income opportunities for IDPs.Cost audits and more public disclosureIRDAI wants greater visibility into the actual economics of distribution.The regulator proposes mandatory cost audits for insurers covering all expenses, including payouts and non-monetary incentives to intermediaries. IDEs with insurance-related revenue above ₹100 crore would also be required to undergo cost audits covering receipts and expenses.Further, IDEs with insurance-related revenue above ₹50 crore would have to publicly disclose key accounting parameters, including revenue, expenses, related-party payments and profit after tax, on their websites.Insurers and large distributors would also have to disclose their commission policies and structures in a simple and accessible manner. For policies with a sum insured above ₹50 crore, the commission would have to be disclosed on the policy itself.The regulator also proposes publicly available information on the performance of insurers and distributors, including mis-selling, surrender and persistency indicators, as well as tracking of dark patterns.Bima Sugam, new digital infrastructure to change how insurance is boughtIRDAI is proposing Market Infrastructure Institutions as fully digital, pull-based alternatives to conventional insurance distribution.Bima Sugam is identified as one such MII. The regulator expects it to become operational with a wide range of life and general insurance products in the next 4-6 months.The paper also envisages additional MIIs promoted by groups of insurers. Such platforms are proposed to be not-for-profit and would have to provide a neutral, cost-effective marketplace where insurers compete on product features, pricing, performance, claims settlement and grievance redressal.At least 11 insurers would need to come together to establish an MII, with no insurer holding significant beneficial ownership. Once fully operational, each shareholder insurer would hold less than 10% shareholding or voting rights.The proposed Public Insurance Registry (PIR) would operate as regulator-owned digital public infrastructure, intended to support comparison, portability, transparency and operational efficiency.The broader objective is to move from the traditional model where “insurance is sold” to a complementary model where “insurance is also purchased”, with customers able to compare products, prices, insurers and distributors more easily.The bigger problem IRDAI is trying to fixThe consultation paper's underlying argument is that India's insurance sector has grown in premium terms without a corresponding expansion in the number of people covered.IRDAI notes that the two largest life insurers account for 65% of the market, while the bottom 15 insurers together account for only around 5%. In general insurance, the top 10 insurers account for 68% of the market.In retail health insurance, 10 states account for 80%-82% of policies, with Maharashtra alone accounting for around 20%. In life insurance, 66%-70% of retail new business is concentrated in 10 states.The regulator also says the cost of doing business remains high. For life insurers excluding the two largest players, CoDB stands at 22%, with individual insurers ranging from 18% to 39%. For general insurers excluding the largest insurer, CoDB is 29%, with individual insurers ranging from 20% to 48%.IRDAI's conclusion is that the existing architecture has tended to reward premium collection more than consumer value, market expansion and operating efficiency. The proposed second-generation reforms are therefore designed to make distribution cheaper, more competitive and more transparent while redirecting incentives towards policyholder outcomes.IRDAI has invited comments from policyholders, insurers, distributors, agents and other stakeholders. Feedback can be submitted through the consultation portal, the prescribed Excel template or email, with the deadline set for October 25, 2026. accountability, act as a deterrent, alert future recruiters and allow prospective customers to consider a salesperson\u0027s mis-selling history.\u003Cbr\u003E\u003Cbr\u003EMis-selling would also

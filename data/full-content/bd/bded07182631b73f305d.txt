Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); Janaksinh Jayvantsinh Sarvaiya Vs ITO (ITAT Ahmedabad) Summary: The Income Tax Appellate Tribunal, Ahmedabad “A” Bench, partly allowed Janaksinh Jayvantsinh Sarvaiya’s appeal for Assessment Year 2010–11 against the CIT(A), NFAC order dated 27 October 2025. The dispute arose from an Anti-Corruption Bureau (ACB) search on 9 April 2009, during which cash and other assets were found. While the Tribunal rejected the challenges to reassessment, it deleted the addition of ₹67,37,500 as unexplained cash, the additions challenged in relation to jewellery and silver articles, and a separate ₹5 lakh addition for cheque receipts from land purchasers. On jurisdiction, the assessee alleged absence of valid prior approval and argued that assets seized by the ACB could be assessed only through sections 132A and 153A, rather than reassessment under section 147. The Department produced the approval letter dated 10 March 2017 issued by PCIT-4, Vadodara, leading to dismissal of the approval ground. The Tribunal distinguished the assessee’s reliance on ACIT v. Tungabhadra Minerals (P.) Ltd., observing that the Assessing Officer had received information about discovered cash and assets and formed an opinion that income had escaped assessment. It referred to Yogendrakumar Gupta v. ITO and PCIT v. Abhisar Buildwell (P.) Ltd. No Income Tax Department search under section 132 or requisition under section 132A was shown; consequently, section 153A was not attracted merely because the ACB had conducted a search. The pending dispute over the legality of the ACB action did not invalidate the reassessment. On the cash addition, ₹67,60,500 had been found, of which ₹23,000 was accepted as explained. The assessee attributed the balance principally to advances from the sale of agricultural land at Mota Surka, Sihor, Bhavnagar, together with agricultural and commission income. His explanation referred to ₹28 lakh and ₹30 lakh received under a Banakhat and two subsequent sale deeds executed on 10 November 2009. He also relied on confirmations concerning execution and notarisation of the agreement, purchasers’ statements, historical agricultural-income particulars for himself and his wife, and observations of the Sessions Court concerning the investigation. The Assessing Officer rejected the explanation because of stamp-paper date discrepancies and concerns arising from the ACB inquiry. The CIT(A) additionally referred to the unregistered agreement, the purchasers’ ability to establish sources, alleged signature discrepancies and Suraj Lamps & Industries Pvt. Ltd. v. State of Haryana. The Tribunal held that the absence of registration of the agreement to sell lost significance in view of the subsequent sale deeds covering the same land and buyers. Whether title had passed under the earlier agreement was not decisive for identifying the seller’s source of cash. One purchaser’s statement that his uncle handled family finances, read with the uncle’s admission of the purchase and payments, credibly supported the transaction. The sale deeds recorded consideration of ₹26,70,000 and ₹41,30,156. The Banakhat, eventual sale deeds and statements recorded during the ACB inquiry adequately explained the cash; the ₹67,37,500 addition under section 69A was therefore deleted. The jewellery dispute included two gold biscuits valued at ₹2,80,000, gold ornaments and silver utensils. Jewellery worth ₹9,54,269 belonging to the daughter had been returned. Of 478.83 grams attributed to the assessee and his wife, 304.58 grams belonged to the wife and 174.25 grams to the assessee; the latter was valued at ₹2,33,500, with six kilograms of silver utensils separately valued at ₹1,49,500. The CIT(A) declined relief under CBDT Instruction No. 1916 dated 11 May 1994 because the search was by the ACB. The Tribunal disagreed: the Instruction reflected customary accumulation of jewellery over time and the practical difficulty of retaining purchase bills. Its limits—500 grams per married woman, 250 grams per unmarried woman and 100 grams per male family member—could guide assessment of reasonable family holdings. Considering the assessee’s family circumstances, it directed deletion of the addition on this account and allowed grounds 10–12. The separate ₹5 lakh addition represented two cheques of ₹2,50,000 each deposited on 21 November 2009. Their receipt and source from the land purchasers were not doubted; the CIT(A) had sustained the addition because the underlying sale agreement had been treated as sham. Having accepted the land transaction as genuine in light of the registered sale deeds, the Tribunal found no basis to assess these receipts as unexplained money under section 69A. It deleted the addition and partly allowed the appeal overall, with the reassessment challenges remaining dismissed. Cases Discussed ACIT v. Tungabhadra Minerals (P.) The grounds of appeal are taken up issue-wise and disposed of accordingly. 4. Regarding Ground Nos. 1, 2 and 3 validity of proceeding u/s 147 of the Act: 5. In Ground of appeal No. 1 the assessee has challenged the validity of the reassessment order on the ground that valid and mandatory prior approval of the prescribed authority was not obtained. In the course of hearing, the Ld. Sr. DR filed the approval letter dated 10-03-2017 passed by PCIT-4, Vadodara. This ground of appeal is dismissed. 6. In Grounds of appeal Nos. 2 & 3 the assessee has challenged the proceedings u/s 147 on the grounds; (i) that where assets were seized by the Anti-corruption Bureau, the only option available with the Assessing Officer was to initiate proceedings u/s 153A r.w.s. 132A and not u/s 147 of the Act; (ii) the authorities below erred in drawing adverse inference from the search and seizure by the ACB despite the fact that the legality and validity of the search and consequential proceedings were sub-judice before the competent court. 7. The Ld. A.R. drew our attention to the reasons recorded by the A.O. for reopening, filed in the paper book. The Ld. A.R. also relied on the decision of the Supreme Court in ACIT vs. Tungabhadra Minerals (P.) Ltd.: (2025) 179 taxmann.com 255 (SC). In the case before the Hon’ble Supreme Court, the A.O. had reopened assessment on the basis of some observations of Justice M.B. Shah Commission’s Report regarding under- invoicing of exports, and on a decision of the Supreme Court declaring activities of mining in Goa after 22-11-2007 as illegal as the mining leases expired on that date. There, the Supreme Court held, were insufficient for reopening in the absence of any independent material. 8. In the case before us, the A.O. had received information about discovery of unaccounted cash and assets and formed an opinion that the undisclosed income of the assessee had escaped assessment. On this basis the process of reassessment was initiated. In our view, therefore, decision of the Hon’ble Supreme Court in Tungabhadra Minerals (P.) Ltd. (supra) does not help the assessee’s position. 9. The issue of reopening based on a report of the investigation wing was examined by the Hon’ble Gujarat High Court in the case of Yogendrakumar Gupta vs. ITO: (2014) 366 ITR 186. The Hon’ble High Court, while upholding the reopening, held as under: “20. From a combined review of the judgments of this Court, it follows that an Income tax Officer acquires jurisdiction to reopen assessment under Section 147(a) read with Section 148 of the Income Tax 1961 only if on the basis of specific, reliable and relevant information coming to his possession subsequently, he has reasons which he must record, to believe that by reason of omission or failure on the part of the assessee to make a true and full disclosure of all material facts necessary for his assessment during the concluded assessment proceedings, any part of his income, profit or gains chargeable to income tax has escaped assessment. He may start reassessment proceedings either because some fresh facts come to light which where not previously disclosed or some information with regard to the facts previously disclosed comes into his possession which tends to expose the untruthfulness of those facts. In such situations, it is not a case of mere change of opinion or the drawing of a different inference from the same facts as were earlier available but acting on fresh information. Since, the belief is that of the Income tax Officer, the sufficiency of reasons for forming the belief, is not for the Court to judge but it is open to an assessee to establish that there in fact existed no belief or that the belief was not at all a bona fide one or was based on vague, irrelevant and nonspecific information. To that limited extent, the Court may look into the conclusion arrived at by the Income tax Officer and examine whether there was any material available on the record from which the requisite belief could be formed by the Income tax Officer and further whether that material had any rational connection or a live link for the formation of the requisite belief. It would be immaterial whether the Income tax Officer at the time of making the original assessment could or, could not have found by further enquiry or investigation, whether the transaction was genuine or not, if one the basis of subsequent information, the Income tax Officer arrives at a conclusion, after satisfying the twin conditions prescribed in Section 147(a) of the Act, that the assessee had not made a full and true disclosure of the material facts at the time of original assessment and therefore income chargeable to tax had escaped assessment. The High Courts which have interpreted Burlop Dealer’s case (Supra) as laying down law to the contrary fell in error and did not appreciate the import of that judgment correctly. 21. We are not persuaded to accept the argument of Mr. Sharma that the question regarding truthfulness or falsehood of the transactions reflected in the return can only be examined during the original assessment proceedings and not at any stage subsequent thereto. The argument is too broad and general in nature and does violence to the plain phraseology of Sections 147(a) and 148 of the Act and is against the settled law by this Court. We have to look to the purpose and intent of the provisions. One of the purposes of Section 147, appears to us to be, to ensure that a party cannot get away by wilfully making a false or untrue statement at the time of original assessment and when that falsity comes to notice, to turn around and say “you accepted my lie, now your hands are tied and you can do nothing”. It would be travesty of justice to allow the assessee that latitude. 22. In our opinion, therefore, in the facts of the present case the Income tax Officer, Azamgarh rightly initiated the reassessment proceedings on the basis of subsequent information, which was specific relevant and reliable, and after recording the reasons for formation of his own belief that in the original assessment proceedings, the assessee had not disclosed the material facts truly and fully and therefore income chargeable to tax had escaped assessment. He, therefore, correctly invoked the provisions of Sections 147(a) and 148 of the Act. The High Court was, thus, perfectly justified in dismissing the writ petition. There is no merit in this appeal which fails and is dismissed but with no order as to costs.” 10. The Ld. A.R. emphasized on the issue that the A.O. should have invoked Section 153A in this case instead of Section 147. He stated that the non obstante provision in Section 153A ousted jurisdiction of Section 147 automatically. We notice that Section 153A is applicable where there is a search u/s 132 or a requisition of documents or assets u/s 132A on the assessee. Nothing has been placed on record to show that there was a search action u/s 132 of the Act conducted in the case of the assessee or any requisition of documents or assets u/s 132A of the Act. The A.O. has received information from the ACB and formed an opinion on the basis thereof that income has escaped assessment. In the absence of any action initiated by the Department u/s 132 or 132A of the Act, there cannot be any question of initiating proceeding u/s 153A of the Act. The issue, that A.O. can in such circumstances initiate reassessment u/s 147 of the Act, is no longer res integra in view of the decision of the Supreme Court in PCIT vs. Abhisar Buildwell (P.) Ltd.: (2023) 454 ITR 212 (SC). The Hon’ble Supreme Court held as under: “14. In view of the above and for the reasons stated above, it is concluded as under: (i) that in case of search under section 132 or requisition under section 132A, the AO assumes the jurisdiction for block assessment under section 153A; (ii) all pending assessments/reassessments shall stand abated; (iii) in case any incriminating material is found/unearthed, even, in case of unabated/completed assessments, the AO would assume the jurisdiction to assess or reassess the ‘total income’ taking into consideration the incriminating material unearthed during the search and the other material available with the AO including the income declared in the returns; and (iv) in case no incriminating material is unearthed during the search, the AO cannot assess or reassess taking into consideration the other material in respect of completed assessments/unabated assessments. Meaning thereby, in respect of completed/unabated assessments, no addition can be made by the AO in absence of any incriminating material found during the course of search under section 132 or requisition under section 132A of the Act, 1961. However, the completed/unabated assessments can be re-opened by the AO in exercise of powers under sections 147/148 of the Act, subject to fulfilment of the conditions as envisaged/mentioned under sections 147/148 of the Act and those powers are saved.” 11. The assessee does not appear to have raised this issue of jurisdiction with reference to Section 153A at the stage of assessment or CIT(A). Grounds of appeal before CIT(A) do not challenge the assessment on this ground. The legality of the search carried out by the ACB has no bearing on the reassessment proceedings in this case. As a result of the search and discovery of cash and assets beyond the assessee’s known sources of income, the ACB had informed the Income Tax Department. Upon receipt of this information the AO has formed an opinion about income escaping assessment and issued notice u/s 148 of the Act after obtaining approval from the Principal CIT. The initiation of reassessment, in view of judicial precedents cited above, suffers from no procedural irregularity. Assessee’s ground of appeal nos. 2 & 3 are dismissed. 12. Therefore, it may be safely inferred that the alleged agreement to sell the agriculture land was a colourable devise and not a genuine transaction in telling transfer of any rights in the land. 5.33 Therefore, considering the facts and circumstance of the case, submission of the assessee, material available on record, findings of the Anticorruption Bureau and findings of the assessing officer, grounds of appeal contested by the assessee at serial No. 1 is dismissed.” 17. Challenging the decision of the Ld. CIT(A), Shri Virat Bhavsar, CA, AR of the assessee has reiterated the submissions made before the authorities below and drew our attention to the evidence filed in two sets of paper book, one dated 16-03-2026 and one dated 21-04-2026. 18. The Ld. A.R. drew our attention to the English translation of the statement of Shri Jitendrabhai Viratbhai Korat. In this statement, Jitendrabhai Korat had stated that Rameshbhai Korat was his father’s younger brother, that they owned land jointly, that while Jitendrabhai Managed farming operations, his uncle Rameshbhai handled all financial transactions. He stated that receipts from cotton sales came into his bank account and all major payments are made out of that account. He also stated that he had visited Umrada, signed a document and a book and stopped at an office where they purchased stamp. While he stated to be unfamiliar with the assessee, he confirmed that the photograph on the document was his. 19. Ld. A.R. also drew our attention to the English translation of the statement of Rameshbhai Korat. He had admitted to the purchase of land and to knowing the assessee’s younger brother even though he did not know the assessee personally. He also admitted to making payment of Rs.28,00,000/- on 10-02-2009 and subsequently another Rs.30,00,000/- for the land transactions. For the second payment of Rs.30,00,000/- Rameshbhai Korat stated to have contributed Rs.15,00,000/- and the balance Rs.15,00,000/- was given by Harishbhai. The total consideration for the land was Rs.78,00,000/-, it was purchased in partnership, with 50% being paid by Harishbhai Dave and the remaining 50% by Rameshbhai and his nephew, Jitubhai (Jitendrabhai). 20. With regard to the A.O’s observation about agricultural income, the Ld. A.R. was drew our attention to two statements showing particulars of income of the assessee and his wife. While the assessee has shown agricultural income in Financial Years 2006-07, 2007-08 and 2008-09, his wife had shown agricultural income from Financial Years 2002-03 to 2008-09. 21. The Ld. A.R. also produced English translation of the two sale deeds executed on 10-11-2009 in favour of Jitendrabhai Korat, Rameshbhai Korat and Harishbhai Dave with the sales amount mentioned as Rs.26,70,000/- and Rs. 41,30,156/- respectively. The description of the land is the same as in the Banakhat and also as admitted by the buyers in their statements given to the ACB in the course of their inquiry. 22. We have given careful consideration to the facts and the conclusions drawn by the CIT(A). The Ld. CIT(A) has laid emphasis on the fact that the agreement to sell, executed by the assessee through his power of attorney holder C J Sarvaiya, did not transfer any rights in the land as the said agreement (Banakhat) was not a registered document. The Ld. CIT(A) also founded his decision on the fact that Jitendrabhai Korat, one of the purchasers, stated not to know anything about the transaction. 23. We note that the unregistered agreement to sell loses significance in view of the subsequent sale deed for the land. The agreement to sell merely noted the land under the transaction and the advance paid in cash for the intended purchase. Non- transfer of title in the land under the agreement is not relevant for the purpose of identifying the source of cash available with the assessee, who was the seller of the land, especially when the land was sold to the same buyers named in the agreement. 24. Further, we note that Jitendrabhai Korat had admitted in his statement that there was some land transaction which was handled by his uncle who managed the finances in their family. He had also admitted to visiting Umrada and signing documents. His statement, read along with the statement of his uncle Rameshbhai Korat, appears to be credible support for the transaction of purchase of land. In our view the Banakhat, supported by the eventual sale deed, and the statements recorded at the time of ACB inquiry, appear to provide adequate explanation for the cash found in the possession of the assessee. the addition made under section 69A cannot be sustained and the same is directed to be deleted. Grounds of Appeal No. 4 to 9 are allowed. 25. Regarding Grounds of appeal No. 10, 11 & 12: Unexplained jewellery 26. At the time of search, the ACB found two gold biscuits valued at Rs.2,80,000/-. In addition, gold ornaments and Jewellery were found. Out of this, jewellery valued at Rs.9,54,269/- found in the locker admittedly pertained to the assessee’s daughter and the same was returned. However, gold/jewellery weighing 478.83 gms. was included by the ACB in the undisclosed assets. The Assessing Officer held that a perusal of details relating to the jewellery reveals that 304.58 gms. of jewellery belonged to the assessee’s wife and 174.25 gms. was related to the assessee. While no addition was made in respect of jewellery belonging to the assessee’s wife, the assessee’s jewellery of 174.25 gms. valued at Rs.2,33,500/- was added as unaccounted investment. In addition, the assessing officer also valued 6 kg of silver utensils at Rs.1,49,500/- and added the same to the assessee’s income. 27. The Ld. CIT(A) upheld the addition with the following observations: “7.5 I have considered facts and circumstance of the case, submission of the assessee, material available on record and findings of the assessing officer it is found that the gold ornaments weighing 174.25 grams valued at Rs 2,33,500/- and silver utensils weighing 6 Kg valued at Rs 1,49,500/- as on 09/04/2009 belonging appellant. It may be appreciated that the Appellant has sought the deduction of gold and jewellery as per the Instruction No. 1916 dated 11th May, 1994 issued by CBDT. However, it may be appreciated that in the matter of seizure of jewellery, the Central Board of Direct Taxes (‘CBDT’) has issued guidelines vide Instruction No. 1916 dated 11th May, 1994. As per the said guidelines, CBDT has laid down instances as to when jewellery found need not be seized. It may be appreciated that gold ornaments weighing 174.25 grams valued at Rs 2,33,500/- and silver utensils weighing 6 Kg valued at Rs 1,49,500/- as on 09/04/2009 were found belonging appellant. It is not disputed that these gold and silver articles were not explained for the purpose of Income Tax Act. Further, it may be appreciated that the provisions of the Instruction No. 1916 dated 11th May, 1994 are applicable in the cases where the search and seizure operations are carried out by the Income Tax Department under section 132 of the IT Act. However, such instructions are not applicable where the search and seizure is carried out By the Anticorruption Bureau.” 28. Aggrieved by the decision of the Ld. CIT(A), the assessee is in appeal before us. We note that the assessee has placed reliance on Instruction No. 1916 dated 11/05/1994 issued by the CBDT. In the said Instruction, issued for the guidance in the cases of Search and Seizure by Income Tax Authorities, the Board had directed that in the case of Wealth Tax assessee, gold, jewellery and ornaments found in excess of the gross weigh declared in the Wealth Tax return only need to be seized. In case of a person not assessed to Wealth Tax gold, jewellery and ornaments to the extent of 500 gms. per married lady, 250 gms. per unmarried lady and 100 gms. per male member of the family need not be seized. The ld. CIT(A) held that since the search in the case of the assessee was carried out by ACB and not by the Income Tax Department, the Instruction issued by CBDT would lose its significance and cannot be applied to provide relief to the assessee. We are unable to agree the decision of the CIT(A). The Instruction is founded on the Indian culture of accumulating gold, jewellery over the life time. Families buy gold jewellery on various occasions right from a child’s birth, at the time of wedding and on other occasions. The use of buying jewellery or gift of jewellery is not limited to women only and men are also beneficiaries of such gifts. They may also buy jewellery occasionally for themselves. Being conscious of these facts, the CBDT had clarified that every family is expected to have jewellery in its possession and therefore laid down reasonable limits within which jewellery found in the possession of an assessee need not be seized. The instruction is also triggered by the fact that considering the manner in which jewellery is purchased, acquired and retained over a long period of time, it may not always be possible for assessees to retain bills or invoices for purchase. Since the instructions were issued in that context, it has to be followed in spirit if not in letter. 29. Considering the fact that the assessee is a married man with a married daughter and he can reasonably be expected to have some jewellery belonging to himself or his wife and the limits set out in CBDT Instruction No. 1916 could be used as a guideline of the reasonable quantity expected to be with the assessee. If the total jewellery is found to be within that limit then no addition as unexplained investment is called for. The addition made on this account is directed to be deleted. Grounds of appeal Nos. 10, 11 and 12 are allowed. 30. Ground of Appeal No. 13 challenges in the decision of the Ld. CIT(A) confirming the addition of Rs.5,00,000/- u/s 69A of the Act. The amount in question was deposited vide two cheques of Rs. 2,50,000/- each on 21-11-2009. The assessee had submitted that this amount was received towards the sale of agreement land from Harishbhai Dave, Ramesh Korat and Jitendra Korat. While the receipt and source of this cheques has not been doubted by the Ld. CIT(A), the addition on that account was confirmed on the ground that the agreement for sale on which the assessee has claimed to receive the amount was decided to be a sham transaction while deciding ground of appeal no. 1 in the same appeal. 31. We have already discussed and dealt with the transaction of sale of land and noted that pursuant to the agreement for sale dated 11-02-2009, there were two sale deeds executed on 10-11- 2009 and on that basis we have already taken a view the land transaction appeared genuine. These two payments of Rs. 2,50,000/- each have come from the buyers of the said land in November 2009 when the sale deed was executed. There is, therefore no basis to treat this sum of Rs.5,00,000/- as unexplained money u/s 69A of the act. This addition is deleted. Ground of appeal No. 13 is allowed. 32. In the result, the appeal of the assessee is partly allowed.

Summary: Rajasthan’s Commercial Taxes Department has prescribed an additional postal-intimation process for specified GST notices and demand orders involving total demands of ₹1 lakh or more. The instruction, issued under Section 168 of the Rajasthan GST Act, covers proceedings under Sections 73, 74, 74A and 122 and requires registered post or speed post with acknowledgement due within 15 days of portal issuance. However, the instruction expressly describes postal communication as being for intimation only and retains the Department’s position that receipt occurs when the document is made available on the common portal. This distinction is important because Section 169 recognises portal availability as a statutory mode of service, while Sections 75, 107 and 78 separately govern adjudication safeguards, appeal limitation and recovery. The article examines the legal effect of portal uploads, the distinction between substantive notices and orders and their DRC-01 and DRC-07 summaries, the requirement of personal hearing and reasoned adjudication, and the consequences of delayed postal communication. It also considers the Madras High Court’s decision in Poomika Infra Developers and the reported Supreme Court stay concerning Bambino Agro Industries. The Rajasthan instruction does not create a nationwide postal-service requirement or automatically invalidate proceedings where additional intimation is omitted. Taxpayers should monitor portal communications, preserve service evidence, obtain complete adjudication records and calculate appeal deadlines conservatively. A notice must reach the taxpayer before the opportunity to defend becomes meaningful A GST dispute does not begin with the adjudication order. It begins when the taxpayer is called upon to explain an alleged default. If that communication is missed, the taxpayer may lose the opportunity to produce reconciliations, contest the calculation, explain a transaction or attend the hearing. If the adjudication order is also missed, the next difficulty may be limitation for appeal or initiation of recovery. Sections 75, 107 and 78 make these stages legally significant. This is why service of notices is not merely a matter of administrative convenience. It connects the Department’s power to determine liability with the taxpayer’s right to defend the case and challenge the resulting order. The essential distinction is between service, actual awareness and a meaningful opportunity to participate. These concepts overlap, but the law does not invariably make service dependent upon the taxpayer personally opening and reading a document. Section 169 expressly recognises several methods of service, including making the document available on the common portal. 2. What exactly has Rajasthan directed? The Rajasthan Commercial Taxes Department has issued instructions under Section 168 of the Rajasthan GST Act bearing: File reference: F.17 (134) ACCT/GST/2017/PART-II; RajKaj reference: 25007477; Date description in the supplied copy: “As per E-sign”; Issuing authority named in the document: Anandhi, Chief Commissioner, State Tax, Rajasthan. The instruction acknowledges representations from trade and industry and judicial pronouncements concerning communication of GST notices and orders by post in addition to the designated portal. It then prescribes an additional postal process for the cases within its scope. Scope and procedure Particular Requirement in the instruction Proceedings covered Specified notices and demand orders under Sections 73, 74, 74A or 122, with references to the applicable GST enactments Monetary threshold Total demand of ₹1 lakh or more Documents described Show-cause notice in FORM GST DRC-01 and demand order in FORM GST DRC-07 Additional mode Registered post/speed post with acknowledgement due Recipient The intended person or authorised representative, at the last known business or residential address Time for dispatch Within 15 days of issuance through the GST portal Records Dispatch register, monitored fortnightly Supervision Monthly supervision and countersignature by the district Additional Commissioner These requirements appear in the supplied two-page instruction. The fifteen-day requirement concerns sending the postal intimation. It is not a fifteen-day extension for filing a reply, nor a requirement that delivery must necessarily be completed within fifteen days. 3. The most important qualification: postal communication is “for the purpose of intimation only” The instruction contains two provisions that must be read together. First, the relevant notices and orders: “Shall, additionally, be sent through registered post/speed post with acknowledgement due…” Second, the postal communication is: “For the purpose of intimation only.” It further states that the taxpayer shall be deemed to have received the notice or order on the date it is made available on the common portal, referring to Section 169(1)(d) and Section 169(2). The instruction therefore does not substitute postal delivery for portal service. It adds another means of bringing the proceeding to the taxpayer’s attention while retaining the Department’s portal-based receipt position. Illustration Suppose an order is made available on the portal on 1 November and the postal copy reaches the taxpayer on 12 November. Under the instruction’s stated position, 1 November remains the relevant receipt date; 12 November does not automatically become a fresh starting point. A taxpayer should not calculate appeal limitation from the later postal date merely because that was when the envelope arrived. The statutory language of Section 107, the evidence of communication and applicable judicial decisions must also be considered. 4. What Section 169 actually provides Section 169(1) states that a decision, order, summons, notice or other communication shall be served by “any one of the following methods”. Its recognised methods include: Direct delivery or tender to the persons identified in clause (a). Registered post, speed post or courier with acknowledgement due. Communication to the email address furnished at registration or subsequently amended. Making the document available on the common portal. Publication in the prescribed local newspaper. Affixture, subject to the conditions in clause (f), where the preceding modes are not practicable. Two consequences follow. Portal service is expressly recognised Section 169(1)(d) provides a statutory basis for making notices and orders available on the GST common portal. A blanket assertion that portal service is inherently invalid is therefore incorrect. All modes need not invariably be used together The expression “any one” is important. It does not impose a general statutory requirement that every notice must simultaneously be delivered by portal, email and post. In Poomika Infra Developers, the Madras High Court treated the modes in clauses (a) to (e) as alternatives and distinguished the conditional resort to affixture under clause (f). Rajasthan’s instruction adds a departmental postal requirement for its specified class of cases. That additional requirement should not be confused with a nationwide amendment to Section 169. 5. Does the taxpayer have to open the document before service becomes effective? Taxpayers should not assume so. In M/s. Poomika Infra Developers v. State Tax Officer, W.P. No. 33562 of 2024 and connected matters, pronounced on 9 April 2025, the Madras High Court upheld service by making notices and orders available on the GST portal. It rejected the proposition that portal service was invalid merely because the taxpayer had not become aware of the uploaded proceedings. The judgment also examined the relationship between Section 169, the common portal and electronic records. It supports the Department’s position that portal availability can constitute service without waiting for the taxpayer’s preferred date of retrieval. Accordingly, “I downloaded the order much later” is not, by itself, a safe legal answer to an objection on limitation. Nevertheless, the precise receipt question has generated litigation Section 169(2) refers to the date on which the document is “tendered or published or a copy thereof is affixed”. It does not separately use the words “uploaded on the portal”. This wording has generated disputes about the interaction between portal service and communication for appeal limitation. Care is necessary when citing Bambino Agro Industries. Available reports record that the Supreme Court, on 29 May 2026, stayed the operation of the Allahabad High Court judgment without deciding the merits. It would therefore be unsafe to present that High Court’s taxpayer-favourable reasoning as an unqualified, finally settled rule. The latest Supreme Court docket should be checked before relying on that litigation in an appeal or publication. 6. DRC-01 and DRC-07 are summaries—not substitutes for the underlying documents The Rajasthan instruction describes the documents as a show-cause notice in DRC-01 and a demand order in DRC-07. For technical legal analysis, the distinction under Rule 142 must be preserved. CBIC Instruction No. 04/2023-GST dated 23 November 2023 explains that: FORM GST DRC-01 is the electronic summary served along with the relevant statutory notice. FORM GST DRC-07 is the electronic summary of the adjudication order, specifying the amounts payable. A taxpayer should therefore ask for and examine both the substantive document and its electronic summary. Document What the taxpayer should examine Detailed show-cause notice Allegations, periods, transactions, legal provisions, calculations and relied-upon material DRC-01 Whether its summary agrees with the detailed notice Detailed adjudication order Findings, treatment of the defence, reasons and determination DRC-07 Whether the demand summary agrees with the adjudication order This approach follows the distinction recognised in CBIC’s instruction and the requirement under Section 75(6) for the order to state relevant facts and the basis of decision. A summary showing only the amount demanded does not, merely by existing, explain how the officer reached the conclusion. If only a summary is accessible, the taxpayer should promptly request the complete notice or reasoned order, preserve evidence of the missing attachment and protect any approaching deadline. Waiting silently for the complete document may create a second dispute over delay. 7. Valid service does not dispense with a proper hearing or a reasoned order Section 169 concerns the method of communication. Section 75 imposes separate safeguards during determination. Hearing: Section 75(4) An opportunity of hearing must be granted where: The person chargeable with tax or penalty makes a written request; or An adverse decision is contemplated against that person. The second limb is important. The statutory protection is not confined to cases where the taxpayer has specifically requested a hearing. Reasons: Section 75(6) The officer must set out the relevant facts and the basis of the decision in the order. Portal upload cannot cure the absence of the reasons required by this provision. Limits of adjudication: Section 75(7) The demand cannot exceed the amount specified in the notice, and confirmation cannot rest on grounds other than those specified in the notice. Thus, proof of service is not proof that the adjudication was otherwise lawful. A taxpayer may have a weak objection to portal service but a strong objection concerning denial of hearing, failure to consider the reply or confirmation on a new ground. 8. Effect on appeal limitation: three months, with a limited further period Section 107(1) allows a taxpayer to appeal within three months from the date on which the decision or order is communicated. Section 107(4) permits a further one month where sufficient cause is established. These are periods expressed in months. They should not casually be described as a universal “90 days plus 30 days” or “120-day” rule. The practical danger is straightforward: the taxpayer may believe the clock started when the postal copy arrived, while the Department relies on the earlier portal date. If the later date is rejected, the appeal may face a limitation objection. A prudent approach Where possible: Work from the earliest reasonably arguable valid communication date. File within that period. Preserve any objection to defective or ineffective communication. If relying on a later date, state the chronology and support it with documents rather than a bare assertion. The object is to avoid making the entire appeal depend on winning a preliminary service dispute. 9. Effect on recovery Section 78 generally allows three months from service of the order for payment before recovery is initiated. The proper officer may specify a shorter period were considered expedient in the interest of revenue, but reasons must be recorded in writing. The date of service therefore affects more than the appeal deadline. It also affects the ordinary payment period preceding recovery. Section 107(7) provides that, where the amount required under Section 107(6) has been paid in the appeal framework, recovery proceedings for the balance are deemed stayed. Merely corresponding with the officer or stating an intention to appeal should not be confused with obtaining that statutory protection. 10. What if the Rajasthan officer does not send the postal intimation? The words “shall, additionally, be sent” impose an express instruction on the officers addressed. Failure to send it is therefore a relevant departure from the prescribed departmental process. However, the supplied instruction does not say that failure to post the intimation automatically nullifies the notice or order. Its express description of post as “intimation only”, together with its portal-receipt clause, makes an automatic-nullity argument particularly uncertain. A more useful representation would establish: That the proceeding falls within the instruction. That no additional postal intimation was sent, or that it was sent late. Whether the detailed notice or order was actually accessible. Whether there was any other communication. What opportunity was lost. How promptly the taxpayer acted after discovering the proceeding. This connects the administrative lapse with actual prejudice rather than treating every postal omission as conclusive. Suggested paragraph for a representation “The present proceeding falls within the monetary and subject-matter scope of the Rajasthan Commercial Taxes Department’s instruction bearing F.17 (134) ACCT/GST/2017/PART-II, RajKaj Reference No. 25007477. The taxpayer requests disclosure of the postal dispatch particulars, acknowledgement and relevant dispatch-register entry. The absence of the prescribed additional intimation is raised together with the enclosed evidence concerning non-accessibility/non-awareness of the proceeding and the resulting loss of an effective opportunity to respond. The taxpayer does not rely on the postal omission alone, but requests consideration of its consequences in the facts of this case.” The factual alternatives in that paragraph should be retained only where supported by the taxpayer’s records. 11. Does the instruction apply to Karnataka taxpayers or Central GST officers? The instruction is addressed to Rajasthan State Tax officers. It should not be represented as a binding nationwide direction to every GST officer. Its circulation to the Chief Commissioner, CGST and Central Excise, Jaipur Zone, for information and necessary action does not by itself establish that CBIC has issued the same postal mandate throughout India. For a Karnataka taxpayer, it can be referred to as an administrative example supporting a request for additional communication. It does not, by itself, prove that a Karnataka notice is invalid because no postal copy was sent. The distinction is useful: an argument for adopting a safeguard is different from an argument that the safeguard already binds the officer concerned. 12. Taxpayer’s practical checklist The following are suggested controls—not additional requirements created by the Rajasthan instruction. Stage Suggested action Routine compliance Allocate responsibility for checking portal communications and the registered email account; do not assume return filing includes notice monitoring On finding a notice Download the detailed notice, summary, annexures and hearing communication Missing documents Seek the complete record immediately and preserve screenshots or grievance acknowledgements Before replying Prepare issue-wise reconciliations and identify the documents needed to answer each allegation Hearing Request a hearing expressly in writing, even though Section 75(4) also protects cases involving a contemplated adverse decision On finding an order Record the order date, portal-availability date, email date, postal date and first-access date separately Before appeal Calculate limitation conservatively and verify the applicable pre-deposit requirements Service dispute Preserve the envelope, tracking record, acknowledgements, portal screenshots and correspondence These controls address the communication risks created by the recognised service methods and the strict appeal framework. 13. Author’s conclusion Rajasthan’s additional postal-intimation requirement is a useful administrative safeguard. Its value lies in giving taxpayers another opportunity to learn of proceedings before their rights are lost through non-participation or delay. But it does not promise that the appeal clock will wait for the postman. The instruction expressly retains the Department’s portal-based receipt position. Taxpayers should therefore neither ignore portal communications nor abandon genuine objections to defective proceedings. The correct approach is to act promptly, preserve the communication record and examine the detailed notice and order—not merely DRC-01 and DRC-07. For the Department, effective tax administration requires more than evidence that a file was uploaded. It also requires compliance with the hearing, reasoning and notice-boundary safeguards in Section 75. For the taxpayer, a communication dispute is strongest when supported by dates, documents and demonstrated prejudice—not simply the statement that the portal was not checked. Publication note: Cite the Rajasthan instruction as “Date: As per E-sign” unless its electronic-signature date is independently verified. Before publication or use in litigation, recheck the status of any pending appeal concerning portal service, particularly the Bambino Agro proceedings.

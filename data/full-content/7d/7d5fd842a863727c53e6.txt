The AMC states that its objective is to provide investment returns which, before expenses, closely correspond to the total returns of securities represented by the Nifty 50 Index. The benchmark is the Nifty 50 Total Returns Index (TRI), which reflects constituent price changes and dividend receipts. Tracking the TRI is not the same as promising that the ETF’s return will exactly match the index after costs. The official scheme page, checked for this 16 October 2026 editorial, shows a NAV of ₹262.3282 dated 15 October 2026. Its monthly performance block separately shows a NAV of ₹258.0021 as at 30 September 2026, with fund manager Himanshu Mange shown as managing the scheme since December 2023. The scheme inception date is 28 December 2001. These figures are clearly time-stamped; they must not be reproduced as a live market quote for 16 October or as a statement about the exchange closing price. A passive index ETF typically seeks to hold constituent stocks in approximately the same weights as the benchmark, subject to cash balances, corporate actions, index rebalancing and permitted portfolio-management practices. Unlike an active equity fund, the manager is not primarily selecting companies on a discretionary view of future winners. However, tracking requires trading, custody, dividends processing, valuation, settlement and compliance, all of which can create small departures from index returns. The benchmark is a total-return index, not a simple price index. Dividends on the underlying companies form part of the TRI calculation, while the ETF’s own cash flows and expenses determine actual NAV performance. If an investor compares the fund with a price-only Nifty 50 chart, the comparison can understate the proper benchmark and exaggerate apparent relative performance. Conversely, a historical near-match does not guarantee future tracking accuracy. Tracking error and tracking difference Tracking error measures variability in the difference between the ETF’s daily returns and those of the benchmark; tracking difference measures the cumulative or annualised performance gap. These metrics answer different questions. A low tracking error does not imply zero annual underperformance, and a one-period tracking difference should not be presented as a forecast. SEBI’s Master Circular for Mutual Funds dated 20 March 2026, effective 1 April 2026, requires passive-fund disclosures and specifies the framework for equity ETF/index-fund tracking and rebalancing. The latest scheme-specific values should be taken from the AMC’s dated disclosure, not inferred from the fund’s historical performance table. For equity ETFs and index funds, the 2026 SEBI Master Circular describes tracking-error disclosure and an ordinary 2% ceiling with exceptional treatment for unavoidable circumstances beyond the AMC’s control. It also requires rebalancing after periodic index constituent changes within seven calendar days, subject to the regulatory text. The fund’s actual current tracking error, tracking difference and expense ratio are not quoted here because a dated, independently verified scheme-specific current disclosure for all three measures was not established in this review. The absence of those numbers is more accurate than inserting a guessed value. NAV, Exchange Price and Bid-Ask Spread The NAV is the value of scheme assets less liabilities divided by units outstanding. The secondary-market price is the price at which a buyer and seller transact on a recognised stock exchange. The two may differ during the trading day because NAV is computed for a valuation date, whereas market quotes respond continuously to orders, liquidity and the estimated value of the basket. A market order can execute above the latest published NAV; a sale can execute below it. These differences are not necessarily an AMC fee and should not be confused with the annual total expense ratio. The AMC page identifies a one-unit exchange minimum. Direct transactions with the mutual fund operate through creation-unit and other regulatory mechanisms, and the AMC page describes creation units of 25,000 units with an additional value condition for large investors. These are institutional/primary-market mechanics, not a statement that every retail investor can directly redeem one ETF unit at NAV with the AMC. For an ordinary secondary-market holder, demat, exchange trading, bid-ask spread, brokerage, taxes and settlement mechanics remain relevant. The scheme must comply with portfolio, valuation, investor disclosure and risk-management requirements. A Nifty 50 ETF is diversified across multiple index constituents, but it remains exposed to equity-market risk, concentration in large benchmark weights, sector movements, corporate actions and tracking risk. Diversification within a 50-stock index is not a guarantee against capital loss. The official AMC page provides links for portfolio disclosures, scheme information documents, tracking error/tracking difference and total expense ratios. Readers should distinguish disclosure dates: a page may show a 15 October NAV, a 30 September performance table and an older portfolio block simultaneously. Only information explicitly tied to its date should be quoted as that period’s fact. This article intentionally does not report AUM, live TER, portfolio weights or riskometer category without a separately verified current dated source. Why a passive scheme still needs governance An index change requires portfolio trades, and a corporate action can temporarily change weights. Cash created by dividends or primary-market flows may cause small differences from full replication. Securities lending, if permitted and undertaken, adds counterparty and operational considerations. The AMC’s trustee oversight, daily valuation and public disclosure processes are important even when investment discretion is narrow. The current SID, KIM, addenda and monthly factsheet should be read together where their dates differ. Capital Gains Tax Under the Income-tax Act, 2025 For a taxpayer who holds units as capital assets and satisfies the equity-oriented-fund and STT conditions, the tax treatment is based on the holding period and the relevant special-rate provisions. Short-term capital gains on qualifying equity-oriented fund units subject to STT are generally taxed at 20% under the operative 2025 Act framework, plus applicable surcharge and cess. For listed equity-oriented ETF units, holdings of more than 12 months generally enter the long-term category. Section 198 of the Income-tax Act, 2025 specifically provides a 12.5% rate on aggregate qualifying long-term capital gains exceeding ₹1,25,000, subject to the section’s STT and other conditions. The ₹1.25 lakh amount is not an exemption available separately for each ETF or each sale. It is an aggregate annual threshold for qualifying long-term gains in the specified category, including eligible listed equity shares, equity-oriented fund units and business-trust units. No indexation is available for this special equity-gains computation. Securities transaction tax paid is not a deductible transfer expense for computing capital gains. Investors should identify purchase dates, sale dates, quantities, acquisition costs and transaction levies for each lot rather than using a single average return number from the factsheet. For a broader comparison of the operative capital-gains structure, TaxGuru’s explanation of capital gains under the Income-tax Act, 2025 addresses the equity ETF holding-period and special-rate rules. The official Income Tax Department text of section 198 remains the controlling source for the long-term computation. Capital Losses, ITR, TDS and Tax Audit Ordinary capital-loss set-off and carry-forward principles are different from the special no-set-off rule applicable to VDA transfers. Short-term capital loss may generally be set off against eligible short- or long-term capital gains, while long-term capital loss is ordinarily restricted to long-term gains; carry-forward requires satisfaction of return-filing conditions and statutory limits. Section 111 of the Income-tax Act, 2025 governs capital-loss carry-forward in the new law. Transaction records must be sufficient to distinguish loss character, sale expenses and STT. A resident individual with capital gains and no business income ordinarily reports them in the applicable capital-gains schedules of the relevant income-tax return, subject to the current form instructions. Where ETF units are genuinely trading stock of a business, turnover, accounting, tax audit and ITR-3 or other business-return considerations can arise. Mere purchase and sale of ETF units does not by itself establish that the person is carrying on a securities-trading business or trigger a tax audit. Tax audit applicability depends on the correct classification and statutory turnover/receipt tests, not the number of demat transactions alone. Resident mutual-fund capital-gain sales on exchange are not generally subject to the 1% VDA TDS rule. The treatment of payments to non-residents, any withholding obligations, and reporting of income distributions depend on the investor’s status, the payer and the statutory provision. A capital-gains computation should not subtract the 1% VDA tax from an equity ETF sale; those are separate legal regimes. NRI, FEMA and Foreign-Asset Reporting An NRI can hold Indian mutual-fund ETF units subject to KYC, demat, bank account, FEMA investment and repatriation conditions. The tax rate, withholding, treaty claim and reporting process may differ from those of a resident. Investors should retain the original source of funds and the NRE/NRO or other permitted banking route, because repatriation rights depend on the investment and funding channel. The fund being Indian does not itself turn an NRI’s holding into a foreign asset for Indian Schedule FA reporting. For a resident taxpayer, an Indian-domiciled ETF holding is not automatically a foreign asset merely because its benchmark contains large companies with overseas businesses. Schedule FA disclosure concerns the person’s actual foreign assets and interests under the return instructions. An Indian ETF can coexist with separately reportable overseas securities, foreign accounts or foreign custodial interests, but the reporting analysis must be asset-specific. Investors should also reconcile AIS, broker capital-gain statements and demat holdings. Accounting and Financial-Statement Treatment An entity applying Ind AS ordinarily assesses ETF units under the financial-instrument framework, including classification, measurement and fair-value changes under Ind AS 109 as applicable. Entities applying non-Ind AS accounting standards must use the relevant AS and statutory reporting framework. An ETF is not cash, a bank deposit or an interest-bearing debt instrument merely because it is exchange-traded. The appropriate accounting depends on the entity, business model and classification. Corporate treasury holdings, trading inventories and long-term investments may require different presentation and disclosure. A practical reconciliation starts with opening units plus purchases minus sales, compared with closing demat units; separately reconcile cash flows, brokerage, STT, other charges, dividend/IDCW receipts if any and the year-end fair-value or cost measurement policy. Tax capital gains can differ from book profit because of measurement, recognition and disallowed charges. Auditors should check valuation date, quoted price source, ownership, related-party transactions and any pledges or restrictions on ETF units. What an editorially complete scheme comparison should not claim The AMC’s 15 October NAV is not a trading signal; historical benchmark-relative returns are not a prediction; an ETF’s nil exit load does not mean cost-free trading; and low historical tracking difference is not a guarantee of future performance. The absence of a verified current TER or AUM in this article should not be filled with a stale number from an unrelated month. A correct dated comparison is more useful than an apparent “live” figure that cannot be independently substantiated.

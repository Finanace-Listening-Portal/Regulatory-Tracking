Summary: The Foreign Assets of Small Taxpayers – Disclosure Scheme, 2026 (FAST-DS) offers eligible resident taxpayers an opportunity to regularise previously undisclosed foreign assets and income while obtaining specified immunity under the Black Money Act, 2015. However, the absence of an express mechanism for claiming foreign tax credit under the Scheme’s first category raises questions where the income has already suffered tax in another country. CA Usha Gayathri K and CA Aishwariya K Rajagopal examine this issue by comparing the Scheme’s 60% tax-and-penalty structure with the ordinary foreign tax credit mechanism under tax treaties, Section 90 and Rule 128. Using an example involving dividends from the United States, the article illustrates how the overall payment can differ between an original return, an updated return and a FAST-DS declaration. It presents arguments favouring foreign tax credit, including treaty relief, consistency and taxpayer fairness, alongside reasons supporting the existing settlement structure, including administrative simplicity and the immunity granted. The authors also discuss uncertainty surrounding the combined use of updated returns and the Scheme’s second category, particularly whether subsequently disclosed income changes the treatment of related foreign assets. The article concludes that clarification from the CBDT would assist taxpayers in evaluating the available routes, their respective costs and the consequences under the Income-tax Act and Black Money Act. However, income declared under the first category of the FAST Disclosure Scheme does not provide any credit for tax already paid in the source country of the respective income. This article examines the issue, sets out the arguments on both sides, and highlights what the Scheme does not offer that remains available through an original or updated return. 1. Introduction Many resident individuals in India hold assets abroad without having reported them correctly or inadvertently omitted reporting them in their income-tax returns. A large number of them are salaried employees of multinational companies who receive shares of the foreign parent company as part of their pay, through Restricted Stock Units or Employee Stock Option Plans. The lapse may lie in the disclosure, the taxation, or both: shares omitted from Schedule FA despite the dividends being taxed, dividends left untaxed despite the shares being disclosed, or neither reported at all. The Foreign Assets of Small Taxpayers – Disclosure Scheme, 2026 (referred to in this article as “the Scheme”, and commonly as FAST-DS) was introduced to give such taxpayers a structured way to set the record straight. As the closing date of 31th December 2026 approaches, one feature of the Scheme has attracted attention among practitioners. Income declared under its first category does not receive any credit for tax that has already been paid on it in another country. This article examines that feature on its merits. It explains how the issue arises, presents the arguments in favour of allowing credit and the reasons the present design may be defended, and discusses the practical choices it leaves for taxpayers and their advisers. It is intended as a general discussion and does not argue for any particular choice. 2. The Scheme in brief The Scheme is contained in Chapter IV of the Finance Act, 2026, comprising sections 130 to 144.1 The Central Board of Direct Taxes (CBDT) notified the Foreign Assets of Small Taxpayers Disclosure Scheme Rules, 2026 on 14th August 2026, with effect from 16 August 2026,2 and has also issued a set of Frequently Asked Questions explaining how the Scheme operates.3 Declarations are filed electronically in Form 1 on the income-tax e-filing portal, which was made available from 1st September 2026. The last date for filing a declaration is 31st December 2026. Where a valid declaration is made and the amount due is paid, the declarant receives immunity under the Black Money (Undisclosed Foreign Income and Assets) and Imposition of Tax Act, 2015 (the “Black Money Act”) in respect of the assets and income declared. This immunity is the principal benefit of the Scheme because non-disclosure or wrong disclosures otherwise provides for substantial penalties and, in certain cases, prosecution for failure to disclose foreign assets. The Scheme operates through two categories, summarised below. Table 1: The two categories under the Scheme Particulars First category Second category What it covers Foreign assets and/or foreign income that were not disclosed and were never offered to tax in India Foreign assets acquired out of income already taxed in India, or acquired while the person was a non-resident, which were not reported in Schedule FA Maximum aggregate value Rs. 1 crore (on an aggregate basis for all the missed years) Rs. 5 crore (on an aggregate basis for all the missed years) Amount payable Tax at 30% of the declared value or income, plus a penalty equal to that tax which is another 30%, i.e. 60% in total A fixed fee of Rs. 1 lakh For a salaried employee holding shares received through an employee stock plan, the shares themselves usually qualify for the second category, because their value was taxed as a perquisite through salary at the time they were allotted. Dividends and gains on sale of those shares are a different matter. If they were not reported in the return, they are foreign income that has not been offered to tax in India, and they fall within the first category. 3. How credit for foreign tax is normally given Dividends paid by a foreign company are usually subjected to tax in the country where the company is resident before they reach the shareholder. For example, under Article 10 of the agreement between India and the United States for the avoidance of double taxation, the United States may deduct tax of up to 25 per cent on dividends paid to an individual resident in India.4 Indian law ordinarily ensures that such income is not taxed twice. Section 90 of the Income-tax Act, 1961 gives effect to India’s tax treaties,5 and Article 25 of the India–United States treaty requires India to allow the tax paid in the United States as a credit against the Indian tax on the same income.4 The procedure for claiming this credit is set out in Rule 128 of the Income-tax Rules, 1962, which requires the taxpayer to furnish a statement in Form 67 along with proof of the foreign tax paid.6 A taxpayer who failed to report such dividends now has two ways of correcting the position, and they treat foreign tax very differently. The first is an updated return under section 139(8A) of the Income-tax Act, 1961 provided the window for filing on an updated return is still available to the taxpayer.7 Here the income is taxed at the normal rates applicable to the taxpayer, together with interest and an additional amount of tax ranging from 25 per cent to 70 per cent, depending on how long after the end of the relevant assessment year the return is filed. Credit for foreign tax remains available on filing Form 67. An updated return does not, however, by itself give any immunity under the Black Money Act. The second is a declaration under the first category of the Scheme. Here a flat 60 per cent of the income is payable, and the declarant receives immunity under the Black Money Act. However, neither the Finance Act, 2026, nor the Rules, nor the CBDT’s Frequently Asked Questions provide for any credit for tax paid abroad on that income under the FAST DS scheme. A simple example illustrates the effect. Consider a resident individual whose income falls in the 30 per cent slab, with health and education cess of 4 per cent and no surcharge. The individual received dividends equivalent to Rs. 1,00,000 from a company in the United States, from which tax of Rs. 25,000 was deducted there. Table 2: Total tax on a foreign dividend of Rs. 1,00,000 under each route Route Tax Paid in India (Rs.) Tax Paid in the United States (Rs.) Effective Total tax (Rs.) Income reported in the original return, with credit (for reference) 6,200 25,000 31,200 Updated return, with credit (approximate with interest and penalty) 10,000 to 15,000 25,000 35,000 to 40,000 First category of the Scheme 1,00,000*60% = 60,000 25000 (for which credit is not available) 85,000 The figures for the updated return are approximate, since the interest and additional tax depend on the year concerned and the date of filing. It is worth looking more closely at why the total under the Scheme is so much higher. The amount of Rs. 60,000 payable under the first category is made up of two equal parts: tax of Rs. 30,000 and a penalty of Rs. 30,000. The penalty is a deliberate feature of the Scheme and this is the price of the immunity the Scheme offers. If credit for the Rs. 25,000 paid in the United States were allowed against the tax portion, the tax payable in India would fall to Rs. 5,000, the total payable in India to Rs. 35,000, and the overall tax on the dividend to Rs. 60,000. In other words, of the total of Rs. 85,000, Rs. 30,000 represents the penalty that the Scheme intends, while Rs. 25,000 represents tax already paid in the United States for which no credit is given. It is this second amount that is the subject of this article. 4. Two forms of double taxation The concern that the same income may be taxed twice arises in two different ways, and it is helpful to keep them separate. The first is international double taxation. The country where the income arouse already taxed the dividend by deduction at source irrespective of whether this money was reinvested or withdrawn, and the scheme then levies 60% on the same dividend under the first category of the Scheme without allowing credit for that tax. The tax treaties in fact are designed to prevent such double taxation. The second is a possible double payment within India. A taxpayer who now reports the income through an updated return, and pays Indian tax on it by claiming the tax credit, may later find that the same income is still regarded as undisclosed for the purposes of the FAST DS Scheme since both are falling under different statutes. In such a case, the income could attract both the tax paid through the updated return and the levy under the first category. Until the CBDT clarifies the position, a taxpayer who chooses the updated return route cannot be certain that doing so will protect them from the Scheme. The first form arises from the design of the Scheme. The second arises from uncertainty, and a clarification from the CBDT could resolve both of them. 5. Arguments in favour of allowing credit What if the scheme allowed credit for foreign tax under the first category. This is persuasive based on the arguments set below. The treaty principle: India’s tax treaties generally require India, as the country of residence, to relieve double taxation by allowing credit for tax paid in the source country where the income arises. It may be argued that the levy under the Scheme on foreign income is, in substance, a tax on that income, and that the relief promised by the treaty should follow the income regardless of the procedure through which it is brought to tax. Consistency between the two routes: The same income receives credit when it is reported through an original or updated return, but not when it is declared under the Scheme. Two routes provided by law for correcting the same omission thus produce materially different results on a relief as basic as the avoidance of double taxation. The burden in absolute terms: The foreign tax may appear modest when expressed as a percentage, but the amount can be significant where dividends have accumulated over several years or where the holding is large. Since the amount under the Scheme must be paid in full within a short period, it may impose a real financial burden on salaried taxpayers who are trying in good faith to regularise past omissions. The effect on participation: Taxpayers who would otherwise settle all their past omissions through a single declaration may instead split the matter between an updated return and the Scheme in order to preserve the credit. This adds to complexity and uncertainty, which a voluntary scheme would ordinarily seeks to avoid. 6. Arguments in support of the present design What if the present design of the scheme is defended? A settlement rather than an ordinary assessment: The Scheme is a voluntary, one-time arrangement. In return for a fixed payment, the declarant receives immunity from penalty and prosecution under the Black Money Act, which ordinary procedures do not offer. Its terms are a package that the taxpayer may accept or decline, and a flat rate without adjustments is part of what keeps that package simple and certain. Simplicity of administration: Verifying the foreign tax paid for each asset, year and country would require documents, currency conversions and examination by the tax authorities. A fixed-rate scheme intended for quick processing of a large number of small cases is designed to avoid this. The levy includes a penalty: Half of the 60 per cent payable is expressly a penalty for non-disclosure. Credit for foreign tax is conventionally allowed against tax, and not against a penalty. Even if credit were allowed, therefore, a substantial part of the difference between the two routes would remain. The scope of the treaties: Whether a levy under a domestic disclosure scheme or such one time window is a tax covered by a particular treaty, and whether the treaty’s relief provisions extend to it, is not self-evident. The answer may depend on the wording of each treaty. A route with credit already exists: A taxpayer for whom the credit is important may use an updated return, within the time permitted, and claim credit under the ordinary rules. Though the Scheme does not recommend this as an option, this choice is open to the taxpayer if they wish to avail the credit under an updated return. Earlier practice: The one-time compliance window provided under Chapter VI of the Black Money Act in 2015 also operated on a flat-rate basis.8 However the option of an updated return was unavailable during that window. 7. Arguments of fairness Beyond the technical points, the issue is sometimes presented as one of fairness, and this framing needs some care. The principles of natural justice, such as the right to be heard and the right to a reasoned decision, relate to the procedure by which a decision is reached. They do not appear to be directly engaged here, since the Scheme does not deny any taxpayer a hearing. The more relevant question is one of equal treatment. On one view, two taxpayers with identical income, who have paid identical foreign tax, may bear very different burdens depending only on the route each has chosen. It may be argued that this distinction is not closely connected to any real difference between them, and that the principle of equality, reflected in Article 14 of the Constitution of India,9 favours a consistent approach to relief from double taxation. On the other view, the Scheme is voluntary. No taxpayer is required to use it, and a taxpayer who does so accepts its terms in exchange for an immunity that the ordinary law does not provide. The legislature is generally allowed wide latitude in framing tax measures and the conditions of one-time schemes, and courts have historically been reluctant to rewrite the terms of such schemes. A difference in outcome between two optional routes may therefore be seen as a consequence of the taxpayer’s own choice rather than as unequal treatment. Both positions have support, and it is not possible to predict with confidence how the question would be decided if it were ever tested. 8. How the Scheme interacts with updated returns In practice, many advisers have suggested a combined approach. The missed income is reported through an updated return, so that credit for foreign tax is preserved, and the related asset is declared under the second category of the Scheme for the fixed fee of Rs. 1 lakh. This approach raises a question that the published material does not clearly answer. The Scheme considers whether an asset or income was disclosed in the return for the relevant year. If income that was left out of the original return is later reported through an updated return, does it remain “undisclosed” for the purposes of the Scheme? If it does, the related asset may be treated as falling within the first category rather than the second, and the taxpayer may end up paying under both routes on the same income. Practitioners have expressed differing views on this point, and at the time of writing this article there does not appear to be a specific clarification from the CBDT. Some taxpayers report having been advised informally to use the Scheme alone, while others prefer the combined approach because of the credit it preserves. Until the position is clarified, each approach carries some degree of uncertainty. 9. Practical considerations For taxpayers deciding how to proceed before the closing date, the following points may be useful. First, it is advisable to begin with a careful review of the returns already filed. For each year, the taxpayer should establish what was actually reported: whether the perquisite was included in salary, whether the asset appeared in Schedule FA, whether dividends and sales were reported, and whether any credit for foreign tax was claimed. The appropriate route depends on what was actually omitted. Second, the cost of each route should be worked out. Where the untaxed income is small, the difference between an updated return and the first category of the Scheme may be modest, and the certainty of a single declaration may outweigh it. Where the income is larger, the value of the credit may justify the additional steps. Third, the time limits for updated returns should be checked. An updated return can be filed only within 48 months from the end of the relevant assessment year. For earlier years, the Scheme may be the only voluntary route available. Fourth, the question of immunity should be considered separately. An updated return settles the position under the Income-tax Act, but it does not by itself provide immunity under the Black Money Act. Fifth, eligibility should be confirmed. Notices or proceedings that are already pending may affect whether either route can be used. Finally, the choice should be documented. Given the uncertainty described above, it is prudent to record the reasoning, the figures and the taxpayer’s informed decision. 10. Conclusion The absence of credit for foreign tax under the first category of the Scheme is not an oversight with an obvious answer. It reflects a balance between the simplicity and finality of a flat-rate settlement on the one hand, and the principle that the same income should not be taxed twice on the other. Reasonable arguments can be made on both sides. What would help taxpayers most is clarity, both on whether credit can be allowed and also on how income regularised through an updated return is treated under the Scheme. Until such clarity is available, the choice rests with each taxpayer, to be made on the basis of their own facts, the amounts involved and a clear understanding of the uncertainty that remains.

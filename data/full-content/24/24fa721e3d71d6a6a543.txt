Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); Evanie Eco Nest Home Buyers Association Vs Evanie Infrastructure Private Limited (NCLT Kolkata) Summary: The NCLT Kolkata admitted the petition filed by Evanie Eco Nest Home Buyers Association under Section 7 of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016 against Evanie Infrastructure Private Limited. The association’s homebuyers had collectively advanced ₹3,43,39,345 towards flats and bungalows in the project. They alleged that construction had not commenced and sought initiation of insolvency proceedings. The principal dispute concerned the numerical threshold for a real estate allottee petition: the petition had originally been filed by 59 allottees on the asserted basis of 540 proposed units, whereas the developer subsequently claimed that there were 966 allottees and that the petition therefore fell short of the statutory requirement. The Tribunal rejected the developer’s reliance on the claimed count of 966. It found serious deficiencies in the supporting records, including preliminary application forms without identifiable flat particulars and entries containing only an applicant’s name and booking date. Applying Manish Kumar v. Union of India, it held that actual bookings or allotments mattered, rather than promises, speculative figures or incomplete applications. It further held that the threshold had to be assessed as of the petition’s filing date and that subsequent additions could not enlarge the denominator to defeat maintainability. On burden of proof, the Tribunal referred to Sections 105 and 109 of the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023 and the Supreme Court’s distinction between the permanent burden of proof and the shifting onus of proof. It concluded that the developer had failed to substantiate its figures or disprove the petitioners’ case and accepted that the threshold was met. The Tribunal dismissed all four intervention applications, the application seeking addition of 49 homebuyers, and the developer’s application seeking dismissal of the main petition. It allowed substitution of the proposed interim resolution professional and appointed Sanjit Kumar Nayak, subject to submission of a valid authorisation for assignment. It initiated CIRP, imposed the Section 14 moratorium, directed public announcement and submission of claims, and required the financial creditors to pay ₹3 lakh as advance IRP fees. The matter was listed for 26 November 2026 for the periodical progress report. Cases Discussed Manish Kumar v. Analysis and Findings 8. We have heard the Learned Counsels for both the parties and have perused the documents and evidence placed on record. 9. After such perusal we have come to the opinion that the arguments and differences boil down to one single question of fact, whether the Applicants meet the threshold to file an application under Section 7 of IBC, 2016.The petitioner claims that he has the requisite number of signatures and crosses the required 10% threshold, while the respondents claim that the threshold is not met. 10. At this juncture we find it pertinent to look into section 7 of the IBC, 2016, as extracted hereunder for clarity: Section 7: Initiation of corporate insolvency resolution process by financial creditor. (1) A financial creditor either by itself or jointly with [other financial creditors, or any other person on behalf of the financial creditor, as may be notified by the Central Government] may file an application for initiating corporate insolvency resolution process against a corporate debtor before the Adjudicating Authority when a default has occurred. [Provided that for the financial creditors, referred to in clauses (a) and (b) of sub-section (6A) of section 21, an application for initiating corporate insolvency resolution process against the corporate debtor shall be filed jointly by not less than one hundred of such creditors in the same class or not less than ten per cent. of the total number of such creditors in the same class, whichever is less: Provided further that for financial creditors who are allottees under a real estate project, an application for initiating corporate insolvency resolution process against the corporate debtor shall be filed jointly by not less than one hundred of such allottees under the same real estate project or not less than ten per cent. of the total number of such allottees under the same real estate project, whichever is less: 11. Generally, when a party comes to court, they rely on a specific law or legal precedent to claim a right or hold the other side liable. However, that legal rule only applies if certain underlying facts exist. The initiating party must therefore assert that these facts are true, while the opposing party denies them to show they are not subject to any duty or liability. In this specific case, the Applicant claims they meet the required threshold under Section 7 of the IBC, 2016. The Respondent, on the other hand, denies this fact, arguing that because the Applicant falls short of the threshold, the application is legally impermissible and lacks merit. 12. Now that we understand the crux of the issue we can move forward to discuss whether the Petitioners meet the 10% threshold as enshrined in Section 7 of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016.The petitioners have asserted that when the Company Petition was filed originally by 59 allottees, they had met the threshold as proposed units/flats to be constructed was 540. The Respondents on the other hand, in their reply filed to the Company petition are conspicuous by their silence on the number of proposed units. 13. We believe it is now pertinent to look into some relevant sections of the Bhartiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023, which are extracted hereunder for clarity: 105. On whom burden of proof lies. —The burden of proof in a suit or proceeding lies on that person who would fail if no evidence at all were given on either side. 109. Burden of proving fact especially within knowledge. —When any fact is especially within the knowledge of any person, the burden of proving that fact is upon him. a. That the applicant has stated an oath that the number of flats proposed to be constructed from the start was 540, the Burden of Proof lies with the applicant but, they have failed to discharge the burden. b. The Respondents could have disproved the number by placing evidence, but they miserably failed, the Burden of Proof lies with the applicant but, they have failed to discharge the burden. c. Under Section 105, if neither side introduced any more evidence from this point on, the respondent’s defence would fail against the claims the applicants have put forward. d. The implication of Section 109 of the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023, is loud and clear that the respondents carry the burden of proving the fact, since the Number of units proposed to be constructed from the start would be specifically within their knowledge. e. Furthermore, Section 109 indicates that because the specific facts supporting the respondent’s defence are within their own unique knowledge, they bear the responsibility to prove them. Therefore, the legal duty to substantiate these facts and disprove the applicant’s case rests entirely on the respondents, which the respondents have miserably failed to discharge. f. Also, Under Section 109 of Bhartiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023, when any fact is especially within the knowledge of a person, the burden of proving that fact rests entirely on them. Because these detailed construction metrics, land records, and financial accounts belong to the respondents’ own operations, they constitute facts uniquely within their own knowledge. Consequently, Section 109 places the legal responsibility to produce this information and substantiate these figures squarely on the respondents. 14. Interestingly the difference between onus of proof and burden of proof was clearly brought out by the Hon’ble Supreme Court R.V.E. Venkatachala Gounder v. Arulmigu Viswesaraswami & V.P. Temple reported in, (2003) 8 SCC 752: 2003 SCC OnLine SC 1117 at page 768 29. In a suit for recovery of possession based on title it is for the plaintiff to prove his title and satisfy the court that he, in law, is entitled to dispossess the defendant from his possession over the suit property and for the possession to be restored to him. However, as held in A. Raghavamma v. A. Chenchamma [AIR 1964 SC 136] there is an essential distinction between burden of proof and onus of proof: burden of proof lies upon a person who has to prove the fact and which never shifts. Onus of proof shifts. Such a shifting of onus is a continuous process in the evaluation of evidence. In our opinion, in a suit for possession based on title once the plaintiff has been able to create a high degree of probability so as to shift the onus on the defendant it is for the defendant to discharge his onus and in the absence thereof the burden of proof lying on the plaintiff shall be held to have been discharged so as to amount to proof of the plaintiff’s title. Based on the principles laid down in R.V.E. Venkatachala Gounder, that there is a clear difference between the permanent burden of proof and the shifting onus of proof, in this matter, we discern that the applicants have successfully established a high degree of probability to support their case, which consequently shifts the onus over to the respondents. Under this legal precedent, the respondents must now actively bring forward evidence to clear themselves of this shifted onus. If they fail to do so, the applicant’s primary burden of proof is legally considered met. Therefore, the responsibility to disprove the claims and present counter evidence rests squarely on the respondents. 15. In Manish Kumar v. Union of India and Anr., (2021) ibclaw.in 16 SC, a case where Hon’ble Supreme Court observed absence of published data and information asymmetry, the Hon’ble Apex Court held as follows: “109. There is an information asymmetry. There is no published data available of status of allotted units. No builder shares the information. It is impossible for the buyers to obtain the information. Ten per cent of allotted units, even it is assumed to be qua letter of allotment, is a dynamic figure and keeps changing. A buyer may calculate ten per cent of the hundred units allotted by morning and it may become 110 by night rendering the filing impossible. 110. Further, it is complained that it is not clear as to whether in determining allottees, in a real estate project, whether it is a tower? the entire colonization? Or a SPV? Ten per cent of a real estate allottees could mean ten per cent of the allotted units or ten per cent of the total legal persons, who have bought into the project, particularly, in cases of multiple ownership of the same property. The provision, in fact, renders group members prone to corruption by cash settlement by the builder. The coram will be disrupted, if one or two members are bought of or even legally settled. This will necessitate fresh filing.” 123. We have noticed Section 11 (1) (b) of RERA. It contemplates details of booking qua apartments and plots. This is sufficient to reject the argument that it could be based on a total number of the units promised. What is required is allotment and not promised flats as per a brochure. It is also not the total constructed units. This is as what is relevant under the impugned provisos read with Section 5(8)(f) explanation and section 2 (d) of RERA read with Section 11(1)(b) and the rules made thereunder is the ‘booking’ of apartments or plots. What is allotted or booked may be more than what is constructed if there is a mismatch at any given point of time. It is the number of units allotted. Now, the allotment and the agreement to sell are not irreconcilable with each other and may signify the same.” (Emphasis Added) 16. The principles laid down by the Hon’ble Supreme Court in Manish Kumar v. Union of India (Supra) make it clear that for the purpose of calculating the threshold, what legally matters is the actual, concrete “booking” or “allotment” of an apartment, rather than mere applications, promises or speculative figures. Applying this standard to the present record, although the Corporate Debtor has placed reliance on a Supplementary Affidavit claiming 966 flat bookings, a closer examination of these documents reveal serious deficiencies. A significant number of these entries are not even valid agreements for sale or definitive allotments but are merely preliminary application forms. In many instances, these forms vaguely note only a tower number and floor, omitting any specific flat number altogether meaning a single unit could easily have been provisionally assigned to multiple applicants simultaneously. Even more glaringly, in several cases, these application forms contain nothing more than the applicant’s name and the date of booking, completely omitting any mention of a tower number, floor, or flat. Such completely unidentifiable records, which remain subject to withdrawal at any given moment, are entirely fluid and tentative. Considering the ruling in Manish Kumar, these incomplete and ambiguous application forms cannot be treated as valid “allotments” or genuine “bookings.” Consequently, the Corporate Debtor cannot rely on them to artificially inflate the number of allotted units to 966, that too long 4 years after filing of the petition. Again, in Manish Kumar v. Union of India and Anr., (Supra) the Honble Supreme Court while emphasising that threshold must be fulfilled as on the date of filing of application has held as follows: “141. The question, then arises, as to the alleged lack of clarity about the point of time, at which the requirements of the impugned provisos, are to be met. Is it sufficient, if the required number of allottees join together and file an application under Section 7 and fulfil the requirements, at the time of presentation? Or, is it necessary that the application must conform the numerical strength, under the new proviso, even after filing of the application, and till the date, the application is admitted under Section 7(5)? There can be no doubt that the requirement of a threshold under the impugned proviso, in Section 7(1), must be fulfilled as on the date of the filing of the application. In this regard, we find support from an early judgment of this Court, which was rendered under Section 153-C of the Companies Act, 1913. Section 153-C is the predecessor to Sections 397 and 398 read with Section 399 of the Companies Act, 1956. Its most recent avatar is contained in Sections 241 and 242 of the Companies Act, 2013 read with Section 244. In fact, Section 399 (3) of the Companies Act, 1956, read as follows: “399 (3) Where any members of a company are entitled to make an application in virtue of sub-section (1), any one or more of them having obtained the consent in writing of the rest, may make the application on behalf and for the benefit of all of them.” 142. In the decision of this Court in Rajahmundry Electric Supply Corporation Ltd. v. A. Nageshwara Rao and others47, the provision in question, viz., Section 153-C of Companies Act, 1913 dealt with the power of the Court to Act, when the Company acts in a prejudicial manner or oppresses any part of its members. It, inter alia, provided that no application could be made by any member, in the case of a company having a share capital unless the member has obtained consent, in writing, of not less than one hundred in number of the members of the company or not less than one-tenth in number of the members, whichever is less. There was also an alternate requirement, to which, resort could be made in regard to company, not having share capital. There was another mode of fulfilling the threshold requirement. In the facts of the said case, the number of the members of the company were 603. Sixty-five members consented to the application. The problem, however, arose as it was contended that 13 of the members who had consented, had, subsequent to the presentation of the application, withdrawn their consent. This Court went on to hold as follows: “5 xxx xxx xxx We have no hesitation in rejecting this contention. The validity of a petition must be judged on the facts as they were at the time of its presentation, and a petition which was valid when presented cannot, in the absence of a provision to that effect in the statute, cease to be maintainable by reason of events subsequent to its presentation. In our opinion, the withdrawal of consent by 13 of the members, even if true, cannot affect either the right of the applicant to proceed with the application or the jurisdiction of the court to dispose of it on its merits.” 143. In the matter of presentation of an application under Section 7, if the threshold requirement, under the impugned provisos, stands fulfilled, the requirement of the law must be treated as fulfilled. The contention, relating to the ambiguity and consequent unworkability and the resultant arbitrariness, is clearly untenable and does not appeal to us. If an allottee is able to, in other words, satisfy the requirements, as on the date of the presentation, the requirement of the impugned law is fulfilled.” (emphasis added) 17. It is thus evident that the Hon’ble Supreme Court in Manish Kumar v. Union of India and Anr., has made the rule on timing straightforward, whether an application meets the statutory threshold must be judged strictly as of the date it was filed. Drawing on its earlier ruling in Rajahmundry Electric Supply Corporation Ltd. v. A. Nageshwara Rao and others, the Hon’ble Court affirmed that a petition validly presented on day one does not lose its maintainability because of later events. Even if individual allottees withdraw their consent after filing, that withdrawal cannot take away the Tribunal’s jurisdiction or stop the case from moving forward. Applying that principle here, we must evaluate the Applicants’ numbers strictly as they stood on the date this petition was presented. Any subsequent cancellations, withdrawals, additions or post-filing changes cannot defeat the Applicants’ right to proceed, nor can they affect this Tribunal’s jurisdiction to decide the matter on its merits. 18. Applying this principle to the present record, it is evident that a number of allottees were included by the Respondent after the filing of this petition. Under the law laid down in Manish Kumar v. Union of India and Anr., , such post-filing additions cannot be permitted or taken into account to alter the baseline. This post-petition expansion of the allottee list is nothing more than a deliberate attempt to artificially inflate the total pool of allottees, designed specifically to ensure the Petitioners fail to hit the mandatory 10% threshold. Since the maintainability of the petition and the total count of allottees must be judged strictly as they stood on the date of filing, these post-filing additions must be completely disregarded. 19. At the same time having noted that the respondents have miserably failed to discharge their burden of disproving the figures indicated by the Petitioner about number of units proposed to be allotted/constructed, the version of Petitioner is found to be acceptable. Hence the Petition cannot be thrown away on the ground of failure to meet the threshold. 20. Inference In the aforesaid backdrop we infer as follows: A. The Status of Petitioners The Respondent Corporate Debtor has failed to challenge the status of the Petitioners as allottees/Homebuyers. Therefore, the status of the Petitioners as Allottees/Homebuyers stand confirmed. B. Meeting of Threshold This Court therefore holds that on the basis of assertion of the Petitioners that the proposed units/flats to be constructed at the initial stage were 540, this Petition filed by Evanie Eco Nest Home Buyers Association who are the Homebuyers/Allottees is squarely maintainable, the threshold in terms of Section 7 of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016 being satisfactorily met.

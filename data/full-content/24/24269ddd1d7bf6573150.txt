Summary: The Liberalised Remittance Scheme (LRS) permits resident individuals, including minors subject to guardian formalities, to remit up to USD 250,000 per financial year for permitted current-account and capital-account transactions or a combination of both. It is a foreign-exchange permission under FEMA, not a general licence for leveraged offshore forex trading. RBI’s LRS Master Direction and FAQs exclude remittances for foreign-exchange trading abroad and margin or margin calls to overseas exchanges or counterparties. Permitted uses can include overseas education, medical treatment, private visits, gifts, maintenance of relatives, qualifying overseas investments and foreign bank accounts, subject to the relevant conditions. From 1 April 2026, section 394(1), Table Sl. No. 7 of the Income-tax Act, 2025 applies TCS at 2% to the portion of eligible education or medical remittances exceeding ₹10 lakh and at 20% to the excess portion for other covered LRS purposes; a qualifying education-loan exception is separately prescribed. Overseas tour programme packages fall under a distinct 2% seller-collection rule. TCS is normally a tax credit, not an additional final tax. Residents must separately examine taxability of overseas income, Schedule FA, foreign tax credit, FEMA reporting and AS 11/Ind AS 21 where applicable. This guide reflects primary RBI and Income Tax Department sources reviewed on 14 October 2026 and distinguishes permission to remit from permission to conduct the underlying activity. Key Takeaways Show all 19 sections Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); Who May Use LRS and What the USD 250,000 Ceiling Means The RBI LRS framework is directed at resident individuals. The annual ceiling is USD 250,000, or equivalent, per individual during the Indian financial year from 1 April to 31 March. It is not a per-bank, per-card or per-transfer limit. Remittances already made under the Scheme reduce the available balance for that financial year, and using a second bank does not create a fresh entitlement. Minors may use LRS subject to the natural guardian’s countersignature and relevant banking requirements. Companies, partnership firms, trusts and HUFs cannot simply rely on an individual LRS entitlement for their own remittances. The ceiling covers both permitted current-account and capital-account uses. Some current-account needs such as qualifying overseas medical treatment or education may be facilitated beyond the standard LRS amount when the prescribed supporting conditions and authorised-dealer assessment are satisfied. Those are specific facilities, not a general power to exceed the cap for any purpose. Exchange rates and bank conversion charges determine the rupee amount debited, but the Scheme ceiling is tracked in the foreign-currency equivalent. Residency is a FEMA concept The status “person resident in India” under FEMA is not identical in every respect to residence under income-tax law. An NRI using a permissible NRE/NRO or other non-resident remittance route should not be assumed to have an ordinary resident-individual LRS allowance. Likewise, an Indian citizen who has moved abroad may have different FEMA and income-tax statuses. The authorised dealer must establish the correct category before applying the Scheme. This list is not an exemption from all other laws. For example, an overseas share acquisition may require examination of the Foreign Exchange Management (Overseas Investment) Rules, Regulations and Directions, 2022 including overseas portfolio investment and overseas direct investment distinctions. A foreign bank account must be used for permitted transactions, and subsequent investment activity must remain lawful. The difference between paying a university fee and buying foreign securities is significant. The first is generally a current-account remittance; the second may create an overseas asset, dividend/interest income, eventual capital gains, and foreign reporting. A payment to an overseas relative can be a gift or maintenance remittance, with separate Indian income-tax and overseas-country implications. Correct purpose-code selection on Form A2 is therefore more than a clerical matter. Form A2, Authorised Dealers and the October 2026 RBI Framework RBI’s Master Direction on LRS prescribes an application-cum-declaration through the authorised dealer, including Form A2, and requires the remitter to confirm ownership of funds and permissible use. The authorised dealer is responsible for customer due diligence, applicable KYC/AML checks, PAN verification and transaction records. The RBI Master Direction on Other Remittance Facilities was updated on 1 October 2026, including directions on Form A2 processing; an actual transaction should be checked against the live circular and the bank’s approved process rather than an older printed form. The RBI framework allows authorised persons to obtain supporting documents as necessary to establish the nature and bona fides of a transaction. For a first-time capital-account remittance, banks may seek additional bank statements or evidence of the source of funds. The customer should preserve the submitted A2, purpose code, remittance advice, foreign-exchange rate, bank debit, TCS certificate and beneficiary confirmation. A cross-border payment intermediary or a familiar website name is not proof that the recipient or platform is authorised for the proposed transaction. Forex Trading Restrictions: LRS Is Not an Offshore Trading Licence RBI’s official FAQ on forex transactions states that residents may transact in foreign exchange only with authorised persons and for permitted purposes. It separately confirms that remittances for margin or margin calls to overseas exchanges or counterparties are not permitted under LRS. The RBI LRS FAQ also lists remittances for trading in foreign exchange abroad among prohibited uses. This applies even where the foreign platform accepts Indian payment cards, provides a demo account or describes the product as education, CFDs or copy trading. The RBI Alert List is not exhaustive: absence of a platform’s name does not establish authorisation. Recognised Indian exchanges and authorised electronic trading platforms operate under different regulatory permissions and product conditions. A trader must check the exact instrument, currency pair, hedging-purpose restriction where applicable, trading venue and intermediary authorisation. A resident should not use LRS to fund an offshore leveraged forex account merely because the platform markets it as globally regulated. Income-tax Act, 2025: TCS on LRS Remittances from 1 April 2026 Section 394(1), Table Sl. No. 7, governs collection by the authorised dealer on LRS remittances above the prescribed ₹10 lakh aggregate threshold. Finance Act, 2026 reduced the rate for education and medical treatment from 5% to 2% with effect from 1 April 2026. The rate for other covered LRS purposes remains 20% on the amount exceeding ₹10 lakh. The threshold is assessed on the aggregate of covered remittances for the relevant tax year; splitting transactions between dates or banks is not a legitimate means of multiplying the threshold. Section 394(4) separately excludes collection by the authorised dealer on an amount remitted out of a loan obtained from a qualifying financial institution for pursuing education, subject to the precise statutory conditions. It also avoids duplicate collection by the authorised dealer on the same amount where the seller has collected TCS under the overseas-tour-package rule. That package rule is distinct: section 394(1), Table Sl. No. 8 generally imposes 2% collection by the seller without the earlier threshold split. A direct hotel booking under LRS and a package bought from a seller should not automatically be treated identically. A remitter may receive TCS credit in the return against income-tax liability, subject to correct PAN mapping and tax-credit reporting; any excess credit follows the ordinary refund mechanism. TCS can affect cash flow materially even where the remitted principal is not income or the remitter has no tax payable on the remittance itself. Foreign Income, Capital Gains and Schedule FA Sending previously taxed savings abroad is not automatically taxable income merely because the funds cross a border. Subsequent overseas interest, dividends, rental income or gains can be taxable in India depending on income-tax residential status and the applicable charging and source provisions. For a resident and ordinarily resident taxpayer, global income is generally relevant; treaty relief and foreign tax credit may be available only on satisfying documentation and statutory conditions. Currency conversion for Indian tax computation must follow the prescribed rules, not simply the rate shown by an investment app. Schedule FA can require disclosure of specified overseas depository/custodial accounts, foreign equity or other financial interests by persons to whom the schedule applies. The reporting period and balance/value fields are not identical to the financial-year period used for computing taxable income. The existence of an overseas account is a reporting fact even if it earns no interest. Non-residents and residents but not ordinarily resident generally have different Schedule FA obligations; the applicable return instructions and individual facts control. For a practical disclosure cross-reference, see TaxGuru’s verified Schedule FA foreign bank-account reporting analysis; it should be read with the return form and instructions for the relevant tax year. Foreign Exchange Differences, Losses and Accounting An LRS remittance is not itself a forex trading profit or loss. The rupee cost of foreign currency, later translation of a monetary asset, and a realised exchange difference can have different tax and accounting consequences. A business maintaining accounts under AS 11 or Ind AS 21 should identify monetary versus non-monetary items, initial recognition at transaction-date rates, closing-rate translation of monetary balances, and recognition of exchange differences under the applicable standard. Ind AS 21 may interact with other standards for financial instruments and overseas operations. For an individual investing overseas, the capital gain or loss on the underlying foreign security is distinct from the bank’s currency conversion spread and from any separately traded derivative. A loss on a foreign security is not automatically an F&O business loss; it requires the appropriate capital-gains analysis. Specified overseas assets, tax treaties and foreign tax credit may affect the computation. A remittance for personal education or medical treatment generally does not create a deductible business expense merely because the payer has business income. NRI Position, Documentation and Compliance Risks LRS is a resident-individual scheme. NRI remittances from NRE/NRO accounts, repatriation of assets, inheritance or property proceeds are governed by their own FEMA and banking provisions and should not be mixed with a resident’s LRS utilisation. Family members may each have separate eligibility, but combining their remittances for an investment requires attention to beneficial ownership and the investment’s own legal limits. A bank may request proof of relationship, tax payment or source of funds. Common compliance failures include selecting an incorrect purpose code, routing prohibited forex-margin payments as an education or travel remittance, omitting overseas assets from the return, failing to account for prior LRS utilisation across banks, claiming the education-loan TCS exception without qualifying evidence, and assuming TCS is a final tax. The correct control sequence is purpose assessment, residency and limit check, underlying FEMA permission, documentary validation, bank processing, TCS reconciliation, and subsequent foreign-income/asset reporting. Frequently Asked Questions 1. What is the LRS annual limit for a resident individual? USD 250,000 or equivalent per Indian financial year for permitted current/capital account uses, subject to specified facilities and exceptions. 2. Can a resident use LRS to fund offshore forex margin trading? No. RBI expressly excludes remittances for overseas forex trading and for margins or margin calls to overseas exchanges or counterparties. 3. Is the ₹10 lakh TCS threshold per remittance? No. The statutory rule considers the applicable aggregate remittances during the tax year; it is not a new allowance for each transaction. 4. What is the TCS rate for overseas education and medical treatment in 2026-27? Section 394(1) generally prescribes 2% on the covered amount exceeding ₹10 lakh, with a separate qualifying education-loan exception. 5. What is the TCS rate for other covered LRS purposes? Generally 20% on the amount exceeding ₹10 lakh, subject to statutory exclusions and the transaction’s correct classification. 6. Is a foreign bank account created through LRS automatically tax-free? The remittance of principal is not automatically income, but later foreign income and Schedule FA disclosure require separate analysis. 7. Does the resident LRS limit apply to NRIs? No. An NRI must use the appropriate non-resident account and repatriation framework, depending on FEMA status and the source of funds. 8. Is TCS a final additional tax on overseas remittances? Ordinarily no. Properly reported TCS is a tax credit against final liability, with refund mechanics for eligible excess credits. Key Takeaways LRS is available to resident individuals and ordinarily has a USD 250,000 aggregate financial-year limit. The underlying transaction must be FEMA-permitted; LRS does not authorise offshore leveraged forex trading. Form A2, authorised-dealer due diligence, purpose coding and source-of-funds evidence are central controls. For tax year 2026-27, covered education/medical LRS remittances above ₹10 lakh generally attract 2% TCS, and other covered LRS remittances 20%. Qualifying education-loan remittances have a separate statutory TCS exclusion. Overseas tour packages use a distinct seller-collection rule of 2%. TCS is ordinarily creditable; foreign income, capital gains and Schedule FA are separate compliance matters. NRI remittance permissions and AS 11/Ind AS 21 accounting must be considered on their own facts.

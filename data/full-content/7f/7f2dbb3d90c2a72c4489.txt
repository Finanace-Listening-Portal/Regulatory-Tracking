(Supreme Court of India) Summary: The Supreme Court, in Ramchand Karunakaran Vs Directorate of Enforcement & Anr., granted bail to the former Managing Director of IL&FS Transportation Networks Limited (ITNL), setting aside the Bombay High Court’s order dated 29 October 2020 rejecting his bail application under the Prevention of Money Laundering Act, 2002 (PMLA). The prosecution arose from alleged financial irregularities involving Infrastructure Leasing and Financial Services Limited (IL&FS), IL&FS Financial Services Limited (IFIN), ITNL and associated infrastructure companies. The appellant was accused of offences punishable under Sections 3 and 4 of the PMLA, arising from scheduled offences investigated by the Economic Offences Wing, Delhi, and the Serious Fraud Investigation Office, Mumbai. The Bombay High Court had refused bail considering the gravity of the alleged economic offences, the applicant’s managerial responsibilities and the investigation material. The Supreme Court subsequently granted conditional bail after considering his prolonged incarceration, senior-citizen status and the fact that charges had not been framed. The proceedings originated from FIR No. 253 of 2018 dated 6 December 2018, registered by the Economic Offences Wing, New Delhi, alleging offences under Sections 120B, 409, 467, 468 and 471 of the Indian Penal Code. The Serious Fraud Investigation Office separately filed Criminal Complaint No. 20 of 2019 dated 30 May 2019 concerning alleged offences under Section 447 of the Companies Act, 2013, read with relevant IPC provisions. These matters constituted the predicate-offence background for the Enforcement Directorate’s investigation. An Enforcement Case Information Report was registered on 19 February 2019, and the appellant was arrested on 19 June 2019. The designated PMLA Court took cognizance of the complaint on 31 August 2019. The prosecution alleged that the applicant participated in financial decisions involving ITNL, IFIN and other companies within the IL&FS group. Before the Bombay High Court, the applicant contended that he had originally joined IL&FS as an employee and was subsequently deputed to ITNL, where he served as Managing Director until October 2018. His counsel argued that he was not a member of the principal Committee of Directors of IFIN responsible for sanctioning loans and that his participation was restricted to infrastructure-project reviews. It was submitted that the deputation costs were paid by ITNL to IL&FS rather than personally to the applicant and that his salary and performance-related perquisites could not automatically be treated as proceeds of crime. The defence disputed the allegation that he had received money from IFIN and maintained that he had cooperated with the investigation. It also relied upon provisional attachment of properties worth approximately ₹21.50 crore and the fact that certain other accused persons had not been arrested by the Enforcement Directorate or had obtained bail. The Enforcement Directorate opposed bail, contending that the applicant exercised substantial managerial authority in infrastructure-project approvals and was a member of the Committee of Directors of IFIN for such projects. It relied upon statements recorded under Section 50 of the PMLA, Credit Approval Memoranda, corporate records and the forensic audit conducted by Grant Thornton. The prosecution alleged that IFIN sanctioned fresh loans to financially distressed borrowing groups, which were then used to repay earlier obligations, thereby delaying recognition of non-performing assets and presenting an artificially favourable financial position. It further alleged that funds were routed through third-party contractors and other entities to ITNL and its special purpose vehicles despite regulatory concerns regarding exposure to group companies. According to the investigation material considered by the High Court, approximately ₹2,270 crore remained outstanding against certain intermediary entities that had received funds from IFIN and subsequently financed ITNL or its special purpose vehicles. The Bombay High Court examined the structure and functioning of IL&FS, IFIN and ITNL, including the Unified Approval Framework and the Committee of Directors responsible for approving financial transactions. It considered the Reserve Bank of India’s inspection observations and the prosecution’s allegation that IFIN continued financing group companies notwithstanding regulatory advice to reduce such exposure. The Court rejected the defence contention that the RBI communication could be disregarded as merely advisory. It also examined the applicant’s association with infrastructure projects, his directorships in related companies and the prosecution’s allegations regarding performance-related remuneration, sitting fees and deputation costs. On the material then available, the Court considered that the applicant had a prima facie connection with the alleged transactions. However, its observations were expressly confined to the bail proceedings and were not intended as final findings establishing the offences. In considering the legal principles governing bail, the Bombay High Court relied upon Y.S. Jagan Mohan Reddy, (2013) 7 SCC 439, concerning the seriousness of economic offences involving substantial public funds and complex conspiracies. It also discussed Union of India Vs Hassan Ali Khan, (2011) 10 SCC 235, in relation to the statutory burden under Section 24 of the PMLA. The Court examined P. Chidambaram Vs Directorate of Enforcement, 2019 SCC OnLine SC 1549, concerning the assessment of gravity, personal circumstances and other bail considerations, and Nikesh Tarachand Shah Vs Union of India, (2018) 11 SCC 1, concerning the earlier twin conditions for bail under Section 45 of the PMLA. Having considered the prosecution allegations, the applicant’s alleged role and the wider economic implications of the IL&FS financial crisis, the High Court concluded that the applicant was not entitled to bail at that stage. On appeal, the Supreme Court considered the subsequent circumstances of the applicant’s continued detention. It recorded that he had been in custody since 19 June 2019 and had completed more than three years of actual incarceration in connection with the PMLA proceedings. It also noted that accused persons numbered 2, 3, 4 and 7 had not yet been arrested and that, although cognizance had been taken, charges remained to be framed. The Court further considered that the appellant was a senior citizen. Taking these circumstances together, the Supreme Court held that he was entitled to bail. The Supreme Court did not finally adjudicate the allegations of money laundering or determine the applicant’s criminal liability. Its order concerned entitlement to conditional liberty in the circumstances prevailing when the appeal was decided. Accordingly, the Supreme Court allowed the appeal, set aside the Bombay High Court’s order and directed that the appellant be produced before the trial Court within three days. The trial Court was directed to release him on bail subject to appropriate conditions. The appellant was required to deposit his passport, report to the Enforcement Directorate on the second and fourth Monday of every month between 11 a.m. and 1 p.m., refrain from influencing witnesses or misusing his liberty, and cooperate with the trial proceedings. He was also required to appear personally unless exempted by the trial Court, and any violation of the conditions could result in cancellation of bail. The combined decisions demonstrate that the seriousness of allegations concerning economic offences must be considered alongside the accused’s personal circumstances, prolonged incarceration and the progress of criminal proceedings. The Supreme Court’s order granted conditional bail without determining guilt or innocence. Cases Discussed 1. Union of India Vs Hassan Ali Khan (Supreme Court); (2011) 10 SCC 235. The Bombay High Court referred to this decision while discussing the burden of proof under Section 24 of the PMLA concerning alleged proceeds of crime and their projection as untainted property. 2. Y.S. Jagan Mohan Reddy Vs Central Bureau of Investigation (Supreme Court); (2013) 7 SCC 439. The High Court relied upon the principles concerning bail in serious economic offences, including the nature and gravity of accusations, the severity of punishment, the possibility of interference with evidence and the larger public interest. 3. Nikesh Tarachand Shah Vs Union of India (Supreme Court); (2018) 11 SCC 1. The High Court discussed this decision concerning the constitutional validity of the earlier twin bail conditions under Section 45 of the PMLA and the consideration of bail applications on their merits. 4. P. Chidambaram Vs Directorate of Enforcement (Supreme Court); 2019 SCC OnLine SC 1549. The High Court examined this authority concerning bail in money-laundering proceedings, including the gravity of accusations, personal circumstances and the assessment of relevant factors in the context of the individual case. This appeal challenges the Judgment and Order dated 29.10.2020 passed by the High Court of Judicature at Bombay in Bail Application No.361 of 2020. 3. The appellant, Original Accused No.vi in P.M.L.A. Special Case No.6 of 2019 on the file of City Civil Court and Additional Sessions Judge, is accused of having committed offences punishable under Sections 3 and 4 of the Prevention of Money Laundering Act, 2002 (for short, the PML Act). The scheduled or predicate offence in the instant case pertain to (a) offences under Section 120-B read with Sections 409, 467, 468 and 471 of the Indian Penal Code in terms of F.I.R. No.253 of 2018 dated 06.12.2018 registered with Economic Offences Wing, New Delhi; and (b) offences under Section 447 of the Companies Act, 2013, read with Section 120B and Section 420 of the Indian Penal Code in relation to Criminal Complaint No.20 of 2019 dated 30.05.2019 filed by the Serious Fraud Investigation office, Mumbai. 4. It appears that on the ground that the sanction issued in the said predicate offences suffered from non-application of mind, the order issuing sanction was quashed by the High Court. The said order is presently under challenge in SLP (Crl.) No.2559 of 2019. 5. Even while quashing the order, the High Court had stayed the operation of its order, which order of interim stay is presently continuing as a result of directions issued by this Court. It was, however, made clear that the order of interim relief so granted by this Court would not in any way affect the orders granting bail to the accused. 6. We are presently concerned with the proceedings arising out of the complaint filed under the provisions of PML Act. In the instant case, the appellant was taken in custody on 19.06.2019 and has remained in custody since then. Thus, the appellant has completed more than three years of actual custody in connection with the offence in respect of PML Act. 7. It has been brought to our notice that Accused Nos. 2, 3, 4 and 7 have not yet been arrested and though the cognizance in the matter has been taken, charges are yet to be framed. 8. Considering the entirety of the matter and the fact that the appellant is a senior citizen, in our view, the appellant is entitled to the relief of bail. We therefore, allow this appeal and while setting aside the order passed by the High Court direct as under: (a) The appellant shall be produced before the concerned trial Court within three days from today. (b) The trial Court shall release the appellant on bail subject to such conditions as the trial Court may deem appropriate to impose. (c) The appellant shall deposit his passport, if not deposited earlier, with the trial Court. (d) The appellant shall mark his presence in the office of the Enforcement Directorate every second and fourth Monday between 11.00 AM and 01.00 PM in a register earmarked for the purposes. (e) The appellant shall not misuse his liberty nor shall he attempt to influence any of the witnesses. (f) The appellant shall cooperate with the proceedings before the trial Court and shall appear in-person unless his presence is exempted by the trial Court. (g) Any infraction of these conditions shall entail in cancellation of the relief for bail 9. With these observations, the appeal is allowed and pending applications stand disposed of. Leave granted. The appeal is allowed and pending applications are disposed of in terms of the signed order.

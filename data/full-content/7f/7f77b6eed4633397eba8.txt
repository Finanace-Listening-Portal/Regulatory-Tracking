Steel Industries Vs Union of India (Allahabad High Court) Credit Blocked, Hearing Denied: Allahabad High Court Directs a Time-Bound Decision on Unblocking ₹3.48 Crore of ITC under Rule 86A 1. Introduction The electronic credit ledger (ECL) is the working capital of a registered person. When the department blocks it under Rule 86A of the CGST Rules, 2017, the taxpayer cannot pay tax from credit. Supplies, dispatches and payments to vendors can slow down within days. In this case, ITC of ₹3,47,96,486 was blocked on 8 October 2026. The petitioner said no notice or prior intimation was given. The Lucknow Bench of the Allahabad High Court did not decide whether the blocking was valid. It disposed of the petition with a direction that the representation be decided after a hearing, preferably within two weeks. The order is short, but it matters in practice. It shows how a writ court may handle a Rule 86A challenge when an efficacious departmental remedy has already been invoked. 2. Facts of the Case The petitioner is M/s R.K.M. Steel Industries, acting through its authorised representative, Anand Savita. Reference No. BL0910260000122 dated 08.10.2026 blocked ITC of ₹3,47,96,486 in the petitioner’s ECL. The petitioner alleged that the blocking was done without any notice or prior intimation. On 12.10.2026, four days after the blocking, it filed a representation with the Additional Commissioner, CGST & Central Excise, Division-I, asking for the ledger to be unblocked. The petitioner then filed the writ petition under Article 226. 3. Reliefs Sought The petitioner asked for two reliefs: Certiorari to quash the blocking reference. It argued that blocking under Rule 86A, with “insertion of negative balance in the ledger”, was wholly without jurisdiction and illegal. Mandamus directing Respondent No. 2 to unblock the ₹3,47,96,486. The phrase “insertion of negative balance” is worth noting. It suggests the blocking may have exceeded the credit actually available in the ledger. This is the “negative blocking” issue discussed below. The Court did not decide it in this order. 4. What the Court Directed The order has five operative parts. The Court noted that a representation for unblocking was already pending. It held that the authority “ought to consider and decide” it within a reasonable, time-bound period. The department’s counsel had no objection to a direction to decide the representation. The authority must give the petitioner an opportunity of hearing. It must then pass a reasoned order, “expeditiously, preferably” within two weeks of receiving a certified copy of the order. The petition was disposed of on those terms. The Court did not quash the blocking, and it did not hold the blocking valid. It did not examine whether “reasons to believe” were recorded, and it did not rule on the negative-balance argument. All contentions are open to the petitioner if the representation is rejected. 5. Legal Background: Rule 86A Rule 86A allows the Commissioner, or an officer not below the rank of Assistant Commissioner, to disallow debit from the ECL. The officer must have reasons to believe that the credit has been fraudulently availed or is ineligible, and those reasons must be recorded in writing. The restriction lapses after one year (Rule 86A (3)). Courts have repeatedly dealt with the safeguards below. Issue Judicial position Written “reasons to believe” The Allahabad High Court in Pilcon Infrastructure v. State of U.P. (29 Oct 2025) held that generic reasons based on an ex parte alert do not meet the requirement. A remark in the ledger is not a substitute for reasons recorded on file. Curing defects later In a 2026 Allahabad ruling, explanations given during litigation could not cure an order that lacked reasons. Fresh action was left open. Negative blocking The Punjab & Haryana High Court in Shyam Sunder Strips (4 Nov 2025) quashed orders blocking more than the available credit. The Bombay High Court has taken a similar view. Prior show cause notice The Punjab & Haryana High Court held that a prior show cause notice is not required, because it would defeat the emergent nature of the power. Interim nature and remedy The Patna High Court treated blocking as an interim measure. It referred the taxpayer to the representation mechanism in Circular No. 20/16/05/2021-GST dated 2 Nov 2021. This is why the Allahabad order is mainly procedural. Since there is no general right to a pre-decisional notice, the real safeguard is a prompt post-decisional hearing and a reasoned decision. Rule 86A also has no formal appeal mechanism. Representation and writ are the only practical remedies. 6. Why the Court Chose This Route A writ court usually exercises restraint when the statute or departmental guidelines already provide a remedy. Here the petitioner had already used the representation route, and the department raised no objection. The Court therefore gave a time-bound direction instead of examining the merits. This approach has practical advantages: Relief arrives faster than a full contested hearing would allow. The department must give a hearing and a reasoned order. A one-line rejection would not comply. The petitioner keeps every ground for a fresh challenge if the order goes against it. The drawback is that the credit stays blocked during the two weeks. The direction also uses the word “preferably”, so it is not a mandatory deadline. 7. Illustration Suppose a steel trader has ₹3.5 crore of ITC in its ledger. On 8 October the department blocks the full amount. The trader receives no notice and discovers the blocking when the ledger shows it. Step 1 (within days): The trader files a written representation with the jurisdictional Additional or Joint Commissioner. It encloses supplier-wise reconciliations, e-way bills, transport records, and proof of payment and receipt of goods. It also asks for the recorded “reasons to believe”. Step 2: If no decision comes, the trader files a writ. It argues that reasons were not recorded, that there was no application of mind, or that the blocking exceeded the available balance. Step 3: The Court may, as here, direct a hearing and a reasoned order within a fixed period. Step 4: If the representation is rejected without reasons, or the reasons are inadequate, the trader returns to the High Court. It relies on Pilcon and the other decisions above. 8. Practical Takeaways for Practitioners File the representation immediately and keep the acknowledgment. In this case, the pending representation of 12.10.2026 is what shaped the Court’s order. Seek the recorded reasons. Ask for the file noting, not just the ledger remark. Compare the blocked amount with the ledger balance on the date of blocking. If the blocking created a negative balance, plead it specifically. Track the one-year limit under Rule 86A (3) from the date of blocking. Ask for a personal hearing in writing. Attend with a document bundle and a short chronology. If the order is not complied with, send a certified copy to the authority. The two-week period runs from receipt of the certified copy. If the decision is adverse, challenge it on grounds of non-recording of reasons, non-speaking order, and failure to consider the documents filed. 9. Limits of the Ruling This order is a disposal on consent without findings. It is not a binding precedent on whether notice is required before blocking, or on negative blocking. It is useful as an example of a time-bound direction for deciding representations, and it shows the Court’s willingness to monitor the process. Do not cite it as authority for the substantive points. Cases Discussed 1. Pilcon Infrastructure v. State of U.P. (Allahabad High Court); 29/10/2025 — Written reasons to believe under Rule 86A. 2. Shyam Sunder Strips v. Union of India (Punjab & Haryana High Court); CWP-23675-2025; 04/11/2025 — Negative blocking beyond available credit. 3. Allahabad High Court ruling on written reasons under Rule 86A (2026) — Subsequent explanations cannot cure the absence of recorded reasons. Heard Sri Vaibhav Krishna, learned counsel for the petitioner and Sri Amit Harsh along with Poonam Maurya, learned counsel for the respondent no.2. 2. This is a writ petition under Article 226 of the Constitution of India, wherein the writ petitioner has sought for the following substantial reliefs:- “A-Issue a writ, order or direction in the nature of Certiorari quashing the Reference No. BL0910260000122 dated 08.10.2026 (Annexure-1) blocking the Input Tax Credit to the tune of Rs.3,47,96,486/-, as the blocking of electronic credit ledger under Rule 86-A of the Rules and insertion of negative balance in the ledger is wholly without jurisdiction and illegal. B-Issue a writ, order or direction in the nature of mandamus directing the Respondent no.2 to unblock the Input Tax Credit (ITC) amounting to Rs. 3,47,96,486/- from the Electronic Credit Ledger (ECL) of the petitioner. “ 3. Learned counsel appearing on behalf of the petitioner submits that the petitioner’s Electronic Credit Ledger was blocked on 8th October, 2026, without issuance of any notice or prior intimation. He further submits that the petitioner submitted a representation dated 12th October, 2026, before the Additional Commissioner, CGST & Central Excise, Division-I, requesting unblocking of the Electronic Credit Ledger. 4. Having regard to the fact that the petitioner has already submitted a representation seeking unblocking of the Input Tax Credit lying in his Electronic Credit Ledger, we are of the considered view that the authority concerned ought to consider and decide the said representation dated 12th October, 2026, within a reasonable and time-bound period. 5. learned counsel appearing on behalf of the State has submitted that he has no objection if this Court directs the authority concerned to take an appropriate decision on the representation submitted by the petitioner on 12th October, 2026. 6. In view of the submissions made by learned counsel for the parties, we direct the authority concerned to afford an opportunity of hearing to the petitioner and thereafter pass an appropriate, reasoned order on the petitioner’s representation dated 12th October, 2026, seeking unblocking of the Input Tax Credit in his Electronic Credit Ledger, expeditiously, preferably within a period of two weeks from the date of receipt of a certified copy of this order. 7. The petition stands disposed of accordingly.

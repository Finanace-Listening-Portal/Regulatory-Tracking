Summary: From 1 October 2026, resident individuals and HUFs purchasing immovable property from non-residents can deduct and deposit TDS through Form 141, Schedule E, without obtaining a TAN. Where a property is jointly owned by a resident and an NRI, the buyer must file separate Form 141 statements under Schedule B for the resident seller and Schedule E for the NRI seller. The applicable TDS rates differ: the resident seller is generally subject to 1% TDS where the ₹50 lakh property threshold is met, while the NRI seller is subject to tax deduction at the applicable rates, including surcharge and cess, without any minimum transaction threshold. The article examines a practical situation where both Form 132 TDS certificates incorrectly reflect the resident co-owner’s name, despite separate deductions for the two sellers. Such errors may arise from incorrect PAN selection in Annexure II, wrong acknowledgement details, filing under the incorrect schedule or technical issues on the portal. The buyer should verify the filed statements, identify the incorrect deductee details and initiate the necessary correction through TRACES before generating fresh certificates. Using an example of a ₹1.20 crore property jointly owned by a resident and an NRI, the article explains the TDS computation, reporting requirements and consequences of incorrect credit allocation in Form 26AS and AIS. It also highlights the importance of timely corrections, accurate PAN selection and verification of Form 132 certificates before the sellers file their income-tax returns. A buyer purchased a residential flat from two co-owners holding it 50:50. One co-owner lives in India; the other is a non-resident. The buyer did what the new rules require. He filed Form 141 under Schedule B for the resident co-owner, deducting 1%, and a separate Form 141 under Schedule E for the NRI’s share. The tax was paid. But when he went to TRACES to download Form 132, both certificates came out in the name of the resident co-owner, and both showed Schedule B. The NRI’s certificate simply did not appear. This is not a one-off. Schedule E has been on the e-filing portal only since 1 October 2026, and for the first time many individual buyers are handling NRI property TDS themselves, without a TAN and without a consultant filing a quarterly return for them. A mixed resident–NRI ownership is exactly where things go wrong, because a single sellers table now carries both kinds of deductee. The discussion below works through the computation, the likely reasons for the error and the correction route. A few points on the portal side are still unsettled, and I have flagged them where they arise. What Has Changed The underlying obligation to deduct tax has not really changed with the Income-tax Act, 2025. The section numbers, forms and certificate have, and for NRI sellers the TAN requirement has gone for most individual buyers. Both sub-rules were amended to take in section 393(2). Schedule E was inserted by the Income-tax (Fifth Amendment) Rules, 2026 (Notification No. 121/2026, G.S.R. 830(E) dated 22 September 2026). Two points about the TAN relief are worth keeping in mind. It is available only where the buyer is a resident individual or resident HUF. Companies, LLPs, firms, trusts and non-resident buyers still have to take a TAN and file quarterly statements. And it applies only to payments made on or after 1 October 2026; the date calendar in Schedule E does not open earlier than that. For our purposes, two features of the portal matter more than anything else. First, a resident seller and a non-resident seller cannot be reported in the same Form 141. The department’s user manual for Schedule E says so in terms. So the buyer in our example had to file two forms, and he did. Second, each form asks for the details of all the sellers, whether resident or non-resident, in the “Details of all deductees (sellers)” table. The seller for whom that particular form is being filed is then picked from a dropdown in Annexure II. That dropdown is where the trouble usually starts, as we shall see. Working Out the TDS for Each Co-Owner The starting point is simple: each co-owner’s share is taxed under its own provision. The two shares should not be averaged or put through at one rate. The Resident Co-Owner TDS is 1% under section 393(1), Table Sl. No. 3(i), on the higher of the agreed consideration and the stamp duty value, applied to the seller’s share. The ₹50 lakh limit is tested on the total consideration for the property, not on each seller’s share. This was clarified for section 194-IA by the Finance (No. 2) Act, 2024, and the restated provision is generally read the same way. So on an ₹80 lakh flat, a resident co-owner holding half still suffers TDS on ₹40 lakh, even though his share is below ₹50 lakh. The NRI Co-Owner Here the deduction is under section 393(2), Table Sl. No. 17, the successor to section 195. There is no threshold at all. The rate depends on the nature of the gain. If the property has been held for more than 24 months, the gain is long-term and the rate is 12.5%, plus surcharge and 4% cess. Surcharge on capital gains is capped at 15%, so the effective rate is 13% up to ₹50 lakh, 14.30% between ₹50 lakh and ₹1 crore, and 14.95% beyond that. For a short-term gain, the NRI’s final tax is at slab rates, but TDS is deducted at the rate in force, which the department puts at 30% for a non-resident individual (35% for a foreign company), plus surcharge and cess. Unless the NRI produces a lower or nil deduction certificate, buyers deduct on the full consideration for his share, since the buyer has no way of knowing the actual gain. Schedule E has fields for a certificate under section 395(1) or 395(2), and the form also recognises a certificate issued by the prescribed income-tax authority under section 395(6). Where the gain is expected to be small, the NRI should apply for one well before the registration date. Two smaller points tend to get missed. If the NRI has no PAN, the higher rate under section 397(2) applies, unless the details prescribed in rule 217 (foreign address, tax residency certificate, tax identification number) are furnished. And the TDS amount entered in Schedule E has to be inclusive of surcharge and cess. Deducting a flat 12.5% is a short deduction. Worked Example A resident buyer purchases a flat for ₹1.20 crore (stamp duty value is the same) from two co-owners in equal shares. The NRI co-owner bought the flat six years ago, so his gain is long-term. He has no lower deduction certificate. The example assumes a single resident individual buyer paying in one lump sum; where there are joint buyers, each buyer files his own Form 141 for his share, and instalments have to be tracked against the earlier acknowledgements. Where the NRI has other substantial income in India, it is safer to apply the higher band and let him claim any excess in his return. Why Both Certificates Show the Resident Seller The name on the certificate is the best clue. Form 132 prints the deductee recorded in the statement. Had the NRI’s PAN been on the second form, his name would have appeared, even if the schedule had been chosen wrongly. Since the resident’s name appears on both, the cause is almost always one of the following, roughly in order of likelihood. The Resident’s PAN Was Selected in the Schedule E Form As noted above, both co-owners are entered in the sellers table of each form. On the screens as they stand at the time of writing, the Annexure II dropdown filters sellers only by type (Corporate or Non-Corporate), not by residential status. Two individual co-owners will both appear in the list, and the portal does not stop you choosing the resident. The user manual only cautions that the user must ensure the PAN belongs to a non-resident deductee. In a hurried filing, this is an easy slip. The TRACES Request Was Made With the Wrong Details Form 132 is requested with the tax year, the Form 141 acknowledgement number and the seller’s PAN. If the Schedule B acknowledgement number, or the resident’s PAN, was used both times, the same certificate will be generated twice. This is the easiest cause to rule out. The NRI’s Share Went Through Schedule B If the property tile under section 393(1) was used for the second form, the schedule will be wrong. On its own, though, this would still show the NRI’s name, so it usually goes with the first cause. A Glitch in the New Schedule E Schedule E and the related changes to Form 132 are new. I have not come across an official advisory on this issue, so I would treat it as a possibility only after the first three have been eliminated. Fixing It The sequence I would follow is below. It starts with the check that costs nothing. 1. Download both filed statements from the e-filing portal (e-File > e-Pay Tax > Payment History). In each, look at the schedule, the residential status selected, the PAN in Annexure II, the share, and the rate and amount of TDS. 2. Log in to TRACES as a taxpayer with the buyer’s PAN. Under Downloads > Form 132 (for buyer), request the certificate again, this time with the Schedule E acknowledgement number and the NRI’s PAN. If the correct certificate is generated, the problem was only in the request. 3. If the statement itself carries the resident’s PAN, it has to be corrected. The Form 141 FAQs on the e-filing portal state that corrections are made only on TDS-TRACES, not on the e-filing portal, and that the user must register on TRACES as a “Taxpayer”. The buyer, being the deductor, has to file it. Change the Annexure II PAN to the NRI’s PAN and check the residential status and share while you are at it. 4. Once the correction is processed, generate a fresh Form 132 for each seller. A certificate cannot be edited directly; it can only be regenerated from corrected data. 5. If the NRI’s share was filed under Schedule B, check with the TRACES helpdesk before paying again. It is not yet clear whether a correction can change the schedule itself. Under the old regime, a 26QB filed for an NRI could not be converted into a section 195 deposit; the practical answer was a fresh deposit under the right provision and a refund claim for the wrong one. 6. Only if the filed Schedule E correctly shows the NRI’s PAN, and the certificate still comes out wrong, raise a grievance on TRACES with copies of both challan-cum-statements and the certificates generated. A word of caution on timing. Section 397(3)(f) allows a correction statement only within two years from the end of the tax year in which the original statement was due, against six years under the old law. Whether TRACES will allow every kind of correction to a Form 141 within that window, a change of schedule for instance, remains to be seen, so it is best to correct the statement straight away. Interest for late deduction or payment and the fee for late filing continue broadly as under the old sections 201(1A) and 234E; their new section references should be checked before quoting them to a client. Form 132 itself is to be issued within 15 days from the due date for furnishing Form 141. What This Means for the Sellers If the wrong PAN has gone into the Schedule E statement, the problem is not limited to the certificate. The credit itself has been given to the wrong person. The resident co-owner will see two credits in his AIS and Form 26AS: his own 1% and the NRI’s 14.30%. He should claim only his own. The extra credit will be reversed once the statement is corrected, and if he claims it, a demand will follow. The NRI is worse off. He will see no credit at all, and CPC generally allows TDS credit only to the extent it appears in Form 26AS. In our example, that is ₹8.58 lakh. If he files before the correction goes through, the credit is likely to be denied or reduced, and he may end up with a demand instead of a refund. So the practical rule is to complete the correction before either seller files his return. If the NRI’s due date comes first, he should still file on time, claim the TDS actually deducted, keep the challan-cum-statement on record, and seek rectification once Form 26AS is updated. A Short Checklist Before Filing For any property with co-owners of different residential status: 1. Confirm each seller’s residential status for the year of transfer before working out the TDS. 2. File one Form 141 for residents (Schedule B) and a separate one for non-residents (Schedule E). 3. In each form, check that the PAN chosen in Annexure II is that of the seller the form is meant for. 4. Test the ₹50 lakh limit on the total consideration; for the NRI, there is no threshold. 5. Enter the NRI’s TDS inclusive of surcharge and cess, at the rate for his holding period. 6. Collect the NRI’s TRC, TIN, foreign address and any lower deduction certificate before making the payment. 7. For payments made before 1 October 2026, follow the TAN route, not Schedule E. 8. When downloading Form 132, use the correct acknowledgement number and seller PAN, and check the name before handing it over. Conclusion Schedule E is a welcome change. An individual buying a flat from an NRI no longer has to apply for a TAN, file a quarterly statement or engage someone to do it for one transaction. The PAN-based route that buyers already knew from 26QB now covers non-resident sellers too. The flip side is that the same Form 141 now carries residents and non-residents in a single sellers table, and the portal leaves it to the user to pick the right one. In a property held jointly by a resident and an NRI, the single most important check is the PAN selected in Annexure II of each form. A wrong selection does not just produce a wrong certificate; it moves several lakh rupees of credit to the wrong person’s Form 26AS. The remedy is available, but the window is shorter than it used to be. With corrections now limited to two years, buyers and their advisers should check both certificates as soon as they are generated, and correct any error before the sellers file their returns. Get that right, and the certificates, the credits and the sellers’ returns fall into place. ***** References Income-tax Act, 2025: sections 393(1) [Table Sl. No. 3(i)], 393(2) [Table Sl.

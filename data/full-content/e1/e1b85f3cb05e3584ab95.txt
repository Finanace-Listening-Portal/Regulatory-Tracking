Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); Ashish Enterprises Vs Principal Commissioner CGST & Central Excise (CESTAT New Delhi) Summary: The Customs, Excise and Service Tax Appellate Tribunal (CESTAT), New Delhi, allowed the appeal filed by M/s. Ashish Enterprises, Gwalior, against the order dated 28 June 2024 of the Principal Commissioner, CGST and Central Excise, Bhopal, confirming central excise duty with interest and penalty. The appellant manufactured and sold Karamchand-brand Pan Masala and maintained that its clearances were covered by tax-paid invoices and disclosed in returns. It stated that its proposed manufacture of KC-brand Zafrani Zarda did not progress beyond unsuccessful trial production. The dispute arose from allegations of clandestine manufacture and clearance of these products without payment of applicable duty or tax. The Directorate General of Goods and Services Tax Intelligence searched the appellant’s premises and the premises of transporters, distributors and sub-distributors. The Department relied on notebooks, registers and diaries recovered from Sarco Roadlines, Data Goods Carrier and Harikesh Agencies, together with statements of the proprietor, his brother, drivers and other persons. According to the adjudicating authority, these materials showed unaccounted movements of Pan Masala and Zafrani Zarda, including consignments allegedly described through the code words ‘Chhuara/Chhuari’. The appellant disputed the attribution of the third-party records, denied clandestine clearances and relied on retractions and evidence elicited in cross-examination. The Tribunal examined the evidentiary requirements for proving clandestine removal, referring to Arya Fibers Pvt. Ltd. and Kuber Tobacco Products Ltd. It identified the need for proof of raw-material procurement, manufacture of finished goods, their clandestine removal and receipt of consideration. It found no reliable evidence linking the appellant to unaccounted procurement, production or sale proceeds. No parallel invoices, incriminating accounts, transport documents identifying the appellant as consignor, or unexplained cash were recovered from its premises. The Tribunal also noted that the Department had not correlated the alleged scale of production with the machinery’s capacity or electricity consumption. On witness statements, the Tribunal considered the retractions and the testimony recorded in cross-examination. Several witnesses denied their earlier allegations or stated that their statements had been obtained under coercion. The transporters’ diaries did not sufficiently identify the appellant, the alleged buyers or the relevant goods; the authors of important records were not examined, and vehicle movements, consignees and payment trails were not independently verified. The Tribunal held that entries in third-party records could not be treated as proof of clandestine removal merely because a name resembling ‘Ashish’ appeared in a register. It also found that the records seized from Harikesh Agencies and the sub-distributors did not establish a reliable connection to the appellant. The Tribunal concluded that the Department had not substantiated the charge of clandestine manufacture and removal through tangible and corroborative evidence. It accepted the appellant’s objection that the alleged volumes could not be sustained without establishing actual production capacity, relevant power consumption and the associated movement of raw materials and finished goods. Consequently, it set aside the Principal Commissioner’s order dated 28 June 2024 insofar as it concerned Ashish Enterprises and allowed the appeal. The decision underscores that third-party diaries and disputed statements, without a demonstrated evidentiary link to the manufacturer, are insufficient to support an excise demand for clandestine clearance. Before appreciating the contentions that have been advanced, it will be useful to examine the essential ingredients that are required to be established to prove clandestine removal of goods. 30. This issue was examined by the Tribunal in Arya Fibers Pvt. Ltd. vs. Commissioner of C. Ex., Ahemdabad-II11 and it was observed: “40. After having very carefully considered the law laid down by this Tribunal in the matter of clandestine manufacture and clearance, and the submissions made before us, it is clear that the law is well-settled that, in cases of clandestine manufacture and clearanc-es, certain fundamental criteria have to be established by Revenue which mainly are the following : (i) There should be tangible evidence of clandestine manufacture and clearance and not merely inferences or unwarranted assumptions; (ii) Evidence in support thereof should be of : (a) raw materials, in excess of that contained as per the statutory records; (b) instances of actual removal of unaccounted finished goods (not inferential or assumed) from the factory without payment of duty; (c) discovery of such finished goods outside the factory; (d) instances of sale of such goods to identified parties; (e) receipt of sale proceeds, whether by cheque or by cash, of such goods by the manufacturers or persons authorized by him; (f) use of electricity far in excess of what is necessary for manufacture of goods otherwise manufactured and validly cleared on payment of duty; (g) statements of buyers with some details of illicit manufacture and clear-ance; (h) proof of actual transportation of goods, cleared without payment of duty; (i) links between the documents recovered during the search and activities being carried on in the factory of production; etc. Needless to say, a precise enumeration of all situations in which one could hold with activi-ty that there have been clandestine manufacture and clearances, would not be possible. As held by this Tribunal and Superior Courts, it would depend on the facts of each case. What one could, however, say with some certainty is that inferences cannot be drawn about such clearances merely on the basis of note books or diaries privately main-tained or on mere statements of some persons, may even be responsible officials of the manufacturer or even of its Directors/partners who are not even permitted to be cross-examined, as in the present case, without one or more of the evidences referred to above being present. In fact, this Bench has considered some of the case-law on the subject in Centurian Laboratories v. CCE, Vadodara [2013 (293) E.L.T. 689]. It would ap-pear that the decision, though rendered on 3-5-2013, was reported in the issue of the E.L.T., dated 29-7-2013, when the present case was being argued before us, perhaps, not availa-ble to the parties. However, we have, in that decision, applied the law, as laid down in the earlier cases, some of which now have been placed before us. The crux of the de-cision is that reliance on private/internal records maintained for internal control cannot be the sole basis for demand. There should be corroborative evidence by way of statements of purchasers, distributors or dealers, record of unaccounted raw material pur-chased or consumed and not merely the recording of confessional statements. A co-ordinate Bench of this Tribunal has, in another decision, reported in the E.L.T. issue of 5-8-2013 (after hearings in the present appeals were concluded), once again reiterated the same principles, after considering the entire case-law on the subject [Hindustan Machines v. CCE [2013 (294) E.L.T. 43]. Members of Bench having hearing initially differed, the matter was referred to a third Member, who held that clandestine manufacture and clearances were not established by the Revenue. We are not going into it in detail, since the learned Counsels on either side may not have had the opportunity of examining the decision in the light of the facts of the present case. Suffice it to say that the said decision has also tabu-lated the entire case-law, including most of the decisions cited before us now, considered them, and come to the above conclusion. In yet another decision of a co-ordinate Bench of the Tribunal [Pan Parag India v. CCE, 2013 (291) E.L.T. 81], it has been held that the theory of preponderance of probability would be applicable only when there are strong evidences heading only to one and only one conclusion of clandestine activities. The said theory, cannot be adopted in cases of weak evidences of a doubtful nature. Where to manufacture huge quantities of final products the assessee require all the raw materials, there should be some evidence of huge quantities of raw materials being purchased. The demand was set aside in that case by this Tribunal.” (emphasis supplied) 31. It is seen that the four essential ingredients that required to be estab-lished for establishing clandestine removal of goods are: (a) Procurement of raw materials; (b) Manufacture of final products; (c) Clandestine removal of goods; and (d) Receipt of consideration against such removal. 32. In the present case, findings have been recorded against the appellant on the basis of Diaries recovered from the premises of the third parties and the oral statements of the witnesses. The demand cannot be sustained merely on the basis of third-party docu-ments and statements, in the absence of any tangible or corroborative evidence. 33. There is nothing on the record which may establish procurement of raw materi-als for the manufacture of goods in the factory of the appellant. There is also no proof of receipt of consideration against the alleged illicit clearance by the appellant. Even in re-spect of transportation, no incriminating evidence was found in the premises of the appel-lant. The documents recovered from transporters do not establish any case of clandestine removal of goods by the appellant. Thus, neither of the four conditions enumerated above are satisfied. 34. In Kuber Tobacco Products Ltd. Commissioner of C. Ex., Delhi12, the Tribunal by majority held that charge of clandes-tine removal cannot be sustained unless it is first established that there was clandestine manufacture of the goods and for this purpose it is incumbent upon the department to es-tablish procurement of requisite quantity of raw materials and the actual manufacturing capacity of the assessee to produce such quantities of finished goods. The relevant para-graph of the decision is reproduced below: “49. As regards the contention regarding the raw material, it can hardly be disput-ed that there cannot be any clandestine removal of the goods unless the assessee manu-factures the same clandestinely and for that purpose procures raw materials clandestinely or utilizes the raw materials without disclosing its utilization. Learned Commis-sioner has clearly observed in the impugned order that there is no evidence of purchase of major raw materials like supari, to-bacco etc. even in the loose sheets. In other words, the Department could not bring any evidence about procurement of raw materials sufficient to manufacture the final product of the quantity which is alleged to have been clandestinely removed.” (emphasis supplied) 35.

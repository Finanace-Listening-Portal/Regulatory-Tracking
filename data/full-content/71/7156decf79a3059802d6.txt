Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); Evie Real Estate Private Limited Vs ACIT (ITAT Mumbai) Detailed Summary: The Income Tax Appellate Tribunal, Mumbai Bench “E”, comprising Shri Amit Shukla, Judicial Member, and Shri Girish Agrawal, Accountant Member, decided seven connected appeals involving Evie Real Estate Private Limited and the Income Tax Department by a consolidated order dated 14 July 2026. The appeals related to Assessment Years 2019-20, 2020-21, 2021-22, 2023-24 and 2024-25 and arose from reassessment proceedings under Section 147 and scrutiny assessments under Section 143(3) of the Income-tax Act, 1961. The principal controversies concerned alleged unaccounted cash consideration received from property purchasers, commonly described as on-money, unexplained cash expenditure under Section 69C, suspected non-genuine subcontracting expenditure, payments for professional and financial advisory services, alleged cash receipts from scrap sales and the applicability of Section 43CA to under-construction property transactions. The Tribunal examined whether the entire amount of alleged unrecorded business receipts could be taxed as income or whether only the profit component embedded in those receipts was assessable. It also considered whether expenditure allegedly incurred from the same unaccounted receipts could be subjected to a separate addition, whether adverse material discovered during a third-party search justified disallowance in the assessee’s hands, and whether stamp duty valuation adjustments could be made before the relevant project revenue was recognised. The assessee was engaged in the development and construction of residential and commercial real estate projects in Mumbai and formed part of the Runwal Group. A search and seizure operation under Section 132 was conducted in the group on 6 October 2023. During the search, the Department obtained statements from employees and connected persons, seized certain loose papers and diaries, and retrieved digital communications, including WhatsApp and Signal messages. The Assessing Officer interpreted this material as evidence of a systematic practice of collecting part of the consideration for flats and commercial units in cash over and above the amounts recorded in sale agreements. For Assessment Year 2023-24, the assessee had filed its return on 30 October 2023 declaring a loss of Rs. 4,03,44,086. Following the search, its return was selected for scrutiny and notices were issued under Sections 143(2) and 142(1). The Assessing Officer examined the seized material and statements and concluded that unaccounted receipts and cash expenditure had arisen in connection with the assessee’s real estate business. These findings formed the basis of additions under the business income provisions and Sections 69A and 69C, together with disallowances and adjustments relating to construction costs and property transactions. For Assessment Year 2023-24, the Assessing Officer treated Rs. 13,88,98,000 as alleged on-money received from purchasers of flats and sought to tax the entire amount as undisclosed business income under Section 28. The assessee challenged the addition, contending that no purchaser had confirmed making any unaccounted payment and that the Department had not produced a direct cash trail, bank withdrawal records, seized receipts or contemporaneous acknowledgements establishing actual receipt of cash by the assessee. It argued that the statements relied upon by the Department had subsequently been retracted through affidavits and that statements of employees or connected persons could not, without independent corroboration, establish receipt of undisclosed consideration. The assessee also emphasised that no unaccounted cash had been recovered from it during the search. According to the assessee, entries in a blue-coloured diary recovered from the cabin of Smt. Lucy Roychoudhary and other materials relied upon by the Assessing Officer did not conclusively establish that the amounts recorded therein represented actual cash receipts from identified purchasers. The Revenue, however, maintained that the statements recorded under Section 132(4), diary entries and electronic communications collectively established the existence of unrecorded transactions. The Commissioner (Appeals), after examining the material and rival submissions, did not accept the assessee’s request for complete deletion of the on-money addition. The appellate authority considered that the seized records and statements indicated a pattern of unaccounted cash collections in certain property transactions. Nevertheless, the Commissioner (Appeals) held that the entire gross receipt could not automatically be treated as taxable income because the receipts represented suppressed business turnover rather than pure profit. Real estate development ordinarily involves project expenditure, approval-related costs, construction outgoings and other expenses, and unaccounted receipts may also be utilised for meeting unrecorded expenditure. Applying this distinction between gross receipts and income, the Commissioner (Appeals) restricted the addition to 15% of Rs. 13,88,98,000, resulting in an addition of Rs. 2,08,34,700, and granted relief in respect of the balance. Both sides challenged this determination. The assessee contended that even 15% was excessive and that the appropriate rate, if any estimation was justified, should be closer to 5% or 8%. The Revenue maintained that the entire amount should be assessed because the assessee had not demonstrated actual expenditure corresponding to 85% of the receipts. Before the Tribunal, the assessee stressed that the project involved mid-segment residential housing at Kanjurmarg, catering substantially to middle-class purchasers, and was not a premium development commanding exceptionally high profit margins. It submitted that the rate of 15% adopted by the Commissioner (Appeals) lacked an empirical or comparative foundation. The Departmental Representative argued that once the receipt of unaccounted consideration was established, the entire amount was liable to taxation unless the assessee independently proved the expenditure incurred against it. The Revenue also submitted that expenditure incurred in cash could attract Section 40A(3), and that the assessee could not claim an assumed deduction of 85% without furnishing supporting evidence. The Tribunal considered these competing submissions and framed the central issue as whether unrecorded sale consideration represented income in its entirety or whether only a reasonable profit element could be assessed. It observed that the distinction between a business receipt and the profit embedded in that receipt was fundamental to the computation of taxable income. The Tribunal referred to JCIT Vs Narayan Land Estate, where the Ahmedabad Bench had recognised that documents showing unaccounted collections must be examined holistically, particularly where the material also indicates project-related cash expenditure. It also considered CIT Vs President Industries, reported in 258 ITR 654 (Gujarat High Court), for the principle that undisclosed sale proceeds cannot automatically be equated with undisclosed income. Further reliance was placed on decisions involving real estate transactions, including Guruprerna Enterprises, Abhishek Corporation Vs DCIT, ITO Vs Anand Builders, Hema Haresh Mehta Vs DCIT and Kishor Mohanlal Telwala. The Tribunal observed that these decisions recognised the need to estimate the income component of unrecorded receipts on a rational basis, taking into account the nature of the business and the circumstances of the particular case. It noted that in certain comparable cases an 8% rate had been considered reasonable. Although Section 44AD was not directly applicable to the assessee because of its turnover and other statutory conditions, the Tribunal considered that the statutory presumptive rate could provide broad guidance for a reasonable estimate, without being applied as a governing provision. After evaluating the nature of the assessee’s projects, the evidence on record, the rival submissions and the judicial authorities, the Tribunal rejected both extreme positions. It declined to delete the addition altogether because the seized documents and statements, as considered by the lower authorities, indicated unaccounted receipts in some transactions. At the same time, it rejected the Revenue’s contention that the entire gross amount represented taxable income. The Tribunal held that the 15% rate adopted by the Commissioner (Appeals) was excessive and was not supported by any specific comparative or empirical analysis. It considered an estimate of 8% to be fair and reasonable in the circumstances. The Tribunal also referred to coordinate-bench decisions involving the assessee’s sister concerns, Horizon Projects Private Limited, in ITA Nos. 7786 to 7788/Mum/2025, and Runwal Enterprises Limited, in ITA Nos. 8931 to 8935/Mum/2025, where similar issues had been considered. Applying judicial consistency, it directed that the additions on alleged on-money receipts should be restricted to 8% of the amounts adopted by the Assessing Officer. For the disputed Rs. 13,88,98,000 relating to Assessment Year 2023-24, this rate produces an estimated profit of Rs. 1,11,11,840, subject to the Tribunal’s directions and consequential computation. The second major controversy concerned alleged unexplained cash expenditure under Section 69C. The Assessing Officer had identified entries and statements suggesting that cash had been spent on liaisoning activities, brokerage and other project-related matters outside the regular books. For Assessment Year 2023-24, the Revenue challenged deletion of an addition of Rs. 1,25,02,040 representing alleged unaccounted expenditure. The assessee disputed the evidentiary basis of the addition, contending that the Department had not produced reliable vouchers, acknowledgements, confirmations from recipients or other evidence demonstrating that the amounts had actually been paid by it. Without prejudice, it argued that even if some expenditure had been incurred, the Department’s own case was that the assessee was generating unaccounted cash through on-money receipts and utilising the same funds for project-related expenditure. Consequently, taxing the receipts and again making an independent addition for expenditure incurred from those receipts would amount to taxing the same underlying funds twice. The assessee relied upon the principles discussed in CIT Vs Jawanmal Gemaji Gandhi and CIT Vs Golani Brothers in support of telescoping. The Commissioner (Appeals) had accepted the principle of telescoping, holding that expenditure arising from the same unaccounted business stream could be adjusted against the corresponding receipts. The Revenue disputed this approach and argued that there was no established one-to-one linkage between particular receipts and particular payments. It also contended that the allowance of an estimated expenditure component of 85% was unjustified where the assessee had not produced evidence of equivalent cash outgoings. The Tribunal examined the commercial and factual relationship between the alleged receipts and expenditure and concluded that the Department had not demonstrated that the cash expenditure arose from an independent source. On the contrary, the Revenue’s own case was that cash collected outside the books had been deployed for project-related purposes. The Tribunal held that once the income embedded in the gross receipts was estimated at 8%, the corresponding expenditure was effectively accounted for in arriving at the estimated profit. A further independent addition for the same expenditure would ordinarily produce duplication. Accordingly, the Tribunal upheld the principle of telescoping and directed that no separate addition for corresponding unexplained cash expenditure should survive beyond the estimated profit, in the circumstances considered in the order. A separate and substantial dispute concerned construction expenditure booked through Senghani Creators Private Limited. During search proceedings involving that concern, the Department obtained statements indicating that invoices were prepared using rough workings received from subcontractors and that some preliminary worksheets were subsequently destroyed. Spot verification also suggested that certain downstream subcontractors were not operating at their recorded addresses. The Assessing Officer treated these circumstances as grounds for questioning the genuineness of expenditure claimed by Evie Real Estate through Senghani Creators. The assessee maintained that the findings concerned a separate entity and its subcontracting chain and did not establish that the work executed for Evie Real Estate was fictitious. It produced work orders, invoices, measurement sheets, quantity summaries, material reconciliation statements, challans, quality certificates, labour compliance records, payment certificates, ledger accounts and bank statements to demonstrate that construction work had actually been carried out and that payments had been made through banking channels. The Commissioner (Appeals) accepted that the Assessing Officer had not conclusively demonstrated that the entire expenditure was non-genuine, but nevertheless sustained an estimated disallowance of 5%. For Assessment Year 2023-24, the assessee challenged the sustained disallowance of Rs. 2,57,371, being 5% of Rs. 51,47,419, and the corresponding reduction of Rs. 40,32,224 from Work-in-Progress, representing 5% of Rs. 8,06,44,470. The assessee argued that the expenditure had been capitalised as project cost and had not necessarily been claimed as a revenue deduction in the profit and loss account for that year. Therefore, an arbitrary reduction in Work-in-Progress would distort the project’s cost base and could affect the computation of taxable income in subsequent years when the project revenue was recognised. The assessee further contended that irregularities in a contractor’s dealings with its own vendors could not justify disallowance in the hands of a separate customer that had maintained extensive documentation of actual work performed and payments made. The Tribunal examined the documentary record and held that the assessee had discharged its initial evidentiary burden. The Assessing Officer had not identified specific false invoices, inflated quantities, fabricated work orders, discrepancies in measurement records, non-execution of construction work or evidence that payments made through banking channels had returned to the assessee in cash. The Tribunal observed that a suspicion arising from a search conducted in the case of a contractor could justify further investigation, but could not automatically be transferred to the assessee without establishing a direct nexus between the adverse material and the particular expenditure claimed. It also considered the scope of the presumption under Section 132(4A), observing that material found in the possession or control of one person could not, without additional evidence, be treated as conclusive proof against a separate assessee. The Tribunal found that the Commissioner (Appeals) had adopted the 5% rate without demonstrating a rational factual basis or meaningful comparability with the decision in Middle Earth Enterprises relied upon by that authority. Following the reasoning in the related Horizon Projects Private Limited decision, the Tribunal directed deletion of the entire disallowance relating to Senghani Creators and the corresponding adverse adjustment to Work-in-Progress. The appeals also involved alleged unaccounted cash receipts from the sale of scrap. For Assessment Year 2023-24, the Assessing Officer had treated Rs. 29,41,143 as unaccounted cash received on scrap sales and sought to assess the amount under Section 69A. The assessee disputed the allegation and argued that the addition had been made substantially on the basis of a statement attributed to Shri Vijay Bhaskar Madne, without adequate independent corroboration. The Commissioner (Appeals) restricted the addition to 15% of the alleged receipts, amounting to Rs. 4,41,171. The assessee sought deletion or a lower estimate, whereas the Revenue argued that the entire amount should be treated as unexplained money. The Tribunal considered that receipts from the sale of scrap arising in the course of business could not automatically be treated as unexplained money under Section 69A merely because the Department alleged that they were received in cash and not recorded in the books. Applying the same principle used for the on-money receipts, it held that only the income component embedded in the scrap receipts was liable to tax and restricted the addition to 8% of Rs. 29,41,143. This represented an estimated profit of approximately Rs. 2,35,291, subject to consequential computation. Another important issue concerned the addition under Section 43CA arising from the difference between the agreement value of a residential flat and the stamp duty value adopted by the Assessing Officer. For Assessment Year 2023-24, the Assessing Officer made an addition of Rs. 1,60,68,801 on the premise that the stamp duty valuation exceeded the consideration disclosed in the relevant property agreement. The assessee submitted that the project was still under construction, that no completed transfer of the property had taken place during the relevant year and that it was following the Project Completion Method of accounting. Accordingly, the revenue from the transaction had not been recognised in the accounts for that year. The Commissioner (Appeals) accepted the assessee’s submissions, referring to the absence of a completed transfer and to an apparent error in the stamp duty valuation adopted by the Assessing Officer. The appellate authority also relied upon Shree Laxmi Estate Pvt. Ltd. Vs ITO, reported in 108 taxmann.com 195, and Goodwill Constructions Vs DCIT, ITA No. 3233/Mum/2022, concerning the treatment of under-construction property transactions. The Tribunal, however, did not simply affirm the Commissioner (Appeals)’s reasoning on Section 43CA. It recognised that the statutory provision operates as a deeming mechanism in respect of consideration for transfer of land or building or both held as stock-in-trade. At the same time, it considered the timing of income recognition relevant to the manner in which the provision should be given effect. The Tribunal set aside the Commissioner (Appeals)’s findings and directed the Assessing Officer to apply Section 43CA in the year in which the revenue from sale of the relevant flats was recognised and offered to tax. The ruling therefore did not amount to a permanent exemption from the operation of Section 43CA merely because the project was under construction during the assessment year under appeal. Rather, the Tribunal linked the consequential adjustment to the year in which the corresponding sale revenue entered the computation of taxable business income. This distinction is important because the Revenue’s appeal was partly allowed on this issue, notwithstanding the assessee’s success on several other grounds. The Revenue also challenged the deletion of disallowances concerning payments made to Last Mile Enterprises Limited for professional and financial advisory services. The Assessing Officer had disallowed Rs. 1,83,00,000 under Section 37(1) and made a consequential reduction of Rs. 28,67,00,000 from Work-in-Progress, relying substantially on a statement recorded from Shri Sachin Battalwar, an employee associated with the group. The assessee contended that the payments represented genuine fees for assistance in raising finance, including borrowings from HDFC Limited. It produced the service agreement, tax invoices, ledger accounts, bank statements evidencing payments through banking channels, details of tax deducted at source and audited financial statements showing the borrowings raised. The assessee further submitted that Last Mile Enterprises had offered the relevant receipts to tax and that the Assessing Officer had not undertaken independent verification from that company. The Commissioner (Appeals) deleted the disallowances, finding that the allegation was not supported by reliable evidence of sham transactions or non-rendering of services. The Tribunal upheld the relief granted by the Commissioner (Appeals) in relation to Last Mile Enterprises. It noted that the contractual documents and banking records established the commercial basis of the payments and that the Assessing Officer had not demonstrated that the documents were fabricated, that the services were not rendered or that any payment had been returned to the assessee in cash. No incriminating document showing an accommodation arrangement involving Last Mile Enterprises had been found during the search, and no direct enquiry had been conducted with the service provider to test the allegation. The Tribunal considered that an employee’s statement, without independent corroboration linking the assessee to a fictitious arrangement, could not justify the disallowance. The order also referred to authorities including Kailashben Manharlal Chokshi Vs CIT, Skylark Build Vs ACIT, CIT Vs Harjeev Aggarwal and CIT Vs S. Jayalakshmi Ammal, which addressed the evidentiary treatment of statements and the need for supporting material. The Tribunal concluded that the Revenue had not displaced the documentary evidence supporting the services and payments and therefore sustained deletion of the disallowance and related Work-in-Progress adjustment. The Tribunal considered the connected appeals for the remaining assessment years in the light of its findings on the principal issues. It observed that the controversies concerning alleged on-money receipts, related cash expenditure, contractor payments and scrap sales arose from substantially similar search material and assessment approaches. The Tribunal therefore sought consistency in the treatment of the same business transactions across assessment years, while recognising that the quantum of receipts, expenditure and additions differed from year to year. It reiterated that estimation of income from alleged unaccounted business receipts must be based on a rational profit rate rather than the automatic taxation of gross turnover. It also maintained that where the same cash stream was alleged to have financed project expenditure, the computation should avoid duplication through separate additions of receipts and expenditure. In relation to subcontracting costs, it applied the principle that documentary evidence of actual work and banking payments cannot be disregarded solely because of adverse findings against another entity. The Tribunal accordingly extended its conclusions to the connected years where the facts and issues were comparable. In its concluding analysis, the Tribunal summarised the principles governing the consolidated appeals. First, alleged on-money collections in real estate transactions were treated as suppressed business receipts, with only the profit component liable to assessment on the facts considered. Secondly, the Tribunal considered an 8% profit estimate reasonable in place of the 15% rate adopted by the Commissioner (Appeals). Thirdly, expenditure intrinsically connected with the same unaccounted receipts could not ordinarily be assessed again under Section 69C once the income component had been estimated, unless the Department established an independent source or other distinguishing circumstances. Fourthly, the entire disallowance relating to Senghani Creators was deleted because the assessee’s documentary evidence had not been rebutted by specific adverse findings. Fifthly, the scrap sale receipts were treated as business receipts and the taxable component was restricted to 8%. Finally, the Tribunal directed the Assessing Officer to give effect to Section 43CA in the year in which the relevant property sale revenue was recognised and offered to tax, rather than treating the issue as conclusively disposed of merely because the project was under construction in the earlier year. In the final result, the assessee’s appeals in ITA Nos. 346, 347, 349 and 350/Mum/2026 were partly allowed. The Revenue’s appeals in ITA Nos. 706 and 996/Mum/2026 were dismissed, while its appeal in ITA No. 995/Mum/2026 was partly allowed. The decision is particularly relevant to real estate developers facing search-related assessments involving alleged cash consideration, parallel records, employee statements, third-party contractor investigations and unrecorded project expenditure. It demonstrates that the tax treatment of alleged undisclosed business receipts must distinguish turnover from income, that estimation requires a reasonable factual foundation, and that separate additions should not duplicate amounts already accounted for through a coherent profit computation. The decision also underscores the importance of maintaining contemporaneous work orders, invoices, measurement records, payment evidence and other supporting documentation, while clarifying that the timing of Section 43CA adjustments must be examined with reference to the recognition of the corresponding property sale revenue.

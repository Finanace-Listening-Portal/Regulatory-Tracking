Karia and Justice Nirzar S. Desai, dismissed the Revenue’s appeal challenging relief granted to M/s Zinzuwadia and Sons, a partnership firm engaged in trading gold and silver bullion and jewellery. The dispute arose from alleged bogus cash sales during demonetisation in November 2016. The Assessing Officer had made an addition of Rs. 7,88,85,082 under Section 68 of the Income-tax Act, 1961, alleging that the firm generated backdated sales invoices to introduce unaccounted cash into its regular books. The Commissioner (Appeals) and Tribunal rejected the allegation of fictitious sales on the evidence available, although a separate gross-profit addition of Rs. 96 lakh was sustained. The High Court held that the concurrent factual findings disclosed no perversity or substantial question of law warranting interference. Following the announcement of demonetisation on 8 November 2016, the Income Tax Department received information suggesting that certain businesses had deposited unaccounted cash by recording fictitious sales against old high-denomination currency. A search was conducted at the residence of a partner of the assessee firm on 24 January 2017, accompanied by a survey at its business premises. During the survey, a digital device was impounded and subjected to forensic examination. The Department concluded that entries totalling Rs. 7.88 crore had been backdated to present transactions recorded after demonetisation as sales made on earlier dates. Based principally on the digital forensic report and unusual sales patterns, the Assessing Officer treated the recorded sales as unexplained cash credits. One significant objection concerned sales of approximately Rs. 3.5 crore to 223 customers between 8 p.m. and midnight on 8 November 2016. According to the Assessing Officer, serving so many customers within 240 minutes was commercially improbable because ordinary jewellery sales required selection, weighing, negotiation, billing and payment. The officer also relied on the absence of CCTV footage and a substantial increase in cash sales compared with earlier periods. The records showed increases of approximately 1673.10% in October 2016 and 1128.66% in November 2016 compared with corresponding months of the preceding year. The Revenue argued that these circumstances, together with the forensic findings, demonstrated that the recorded transactions were not genuine sales but an attempt to accommodate demonetised currency. The assessee disputed this inference and explained that the unprecedented rush following demonetisation had fundamentally altered its normal sales process. Instead of permitting customers to select individual jewellery pieces, the firm supplied ready-prepared packets of gold ornaments. Administrative staff and family members assisted in preparing and distributing the packets, while designated employees handled computerised billing. The ornaments already carried barcode labels containing the details required for invoice preparation. Scanning those barcodes substantially reduced billing time. The Commissioner (Appeals) accepted that this arrangement could explain the unusually large number of customers served within four hours. The Tribunal agreed, observing that the explanation was commercially plausible in the exceptional circumstances prevailing immediately after demonetisation. The High Court also judicially noted the heavy public rush to purchase jewellery following the announcement. The appellate authorities additionally examined the sharp increase in recorded cash sales. An important factual consideration was that the assessee had commenced its full-fledged retail jewellery operations only in July 2016. Consequently, comparisons with corresponding months of the preceding financial year did not provide a reliable measure of ordinary business activity. The Commissioner (Appeals) also observed that the Assessing Officer had selectively questioned increases in October and November while not applying the same scrutiny to substantial increases occurring in other months, including March 2017. The Tribunal accepted that demonetisation had created exceptional demand and that a sales increase, without supporting evidence of fictitious transactions, could not independently establish the Revenue’s allegation. A decisive factor was the availability and reconciliation of stock. The assessee maintained purchase registers, sales registers, daily stock statements, stock ledgers, cash books and quantitative records. The Tribunal observed that the Assessing Officer had neither disputed the recorded purchases nor established that corresponding purchases were fictitious. There was also no demonstrated negative stock arising from the disputed sales. If sales of approximately Rs. 7.88 crore had been entirely fictitious, the appellate authorities considered that corresponding anomalies would ordinarily be expected in the quantitative stock records or physical stock position. However, the survey conducted shortly after demonetisation did not reveal excess stock commensurate with the alleged fictitious sales. The separate stock discrepancy identified during the survey was significantly smaller. These findings substantially weakened the Revenue’s theory that the cash sales existed only as accounting entries. The digital forensic evidence was also examined. The Department relied on transaction numbers, timestamps and the sequence of entries in the assessee’s ORNET accounting software, which used a FoxPro database. According to the forensic report, certain invoices carried earlier dates but appeared at later positions in the electronic records, suggesting that they had been entered subsequently. The assessee explained that reopening an existing voucher and selecting the save or print function could generate a new transaction number and alter its position within the database without changing the original transaction date. This explanation was supported by a letter dated 14 December 2018 from D’soft Infotech Pvt. Ltd., the software developer. The Commissioner (Appeals) and Tribunal considered the software explanation together with the purchase, sales and stock records. The High Court found no justification for disturbing their conclusion that the alleged sequence anomalies did not establish fictitious sales on the facts. The Tribunal further observed that the disputed sales receipts had already been recorded in the books and included in the assessee’s declared business receipts. Treating the same receipts as unexplained cash credits, without establishing that the sales were fictitious, would disregard their existing accounting treatment. The Revenue relied on the principles of human probability laid down by the Supreme Court in Sumati Dayal Vs CIT and CIT Vs Durga Prasad More, but the appellate authorities found that the surrounding circumstances had to be assessed alongside the actual documentary evidence. The High Court agreed that the recorded sales, undisputed purchases, sufficient stock and supporting reconciliations could not be displaced merely by suspicion arising from unusually high sales or alleged digital irregularities. However, the relief granted to the assessee was not unconditional or complete. The Commissioner (Appeals) found that the assessee’s gross-profit position was unsatisfactory and rejected the books under Section 145. Instead of sustaining the Rs. 7.88 crore addition as unexplained cash credits, the appellate authority estimated an additional Rs. 96 lakh as normal business income, thereby granting relief of Rs. 6,92,85,082. The Tribunal upheld this treatment, and the assessee did not pursue any further appeal against the sustained gross-profit addition. The High Court expressly recognised that rejection of the books and estimation of gross profit did not automatically render the separately maintained stock registers irrelevant. The gross-profit issue and the allegation of fictitious sales therefore required distinct consideration. Another dispute concerned an addition of Rs. 1,14,24,765 under Section 69B relating to unexplained investment in stock. During the survey, discrepancies were identified between recorded stock and physical stock. The assessee contended that 1,510.210 grams of 18-carat gold jewellery kept on the first floor had been omitted from the physical-stock calculation. The Commissioner (Appeals) considered the registered valuer’s findings and recalculated the difference after including this quantity. The revised computation resulted in excess stock valued at Rs. 95,41,057. Separate unaccounted sales of Rs. 5,31,201 were also considered in the reconciliation, and the appellate authority accepted that overlapping amounts should not be added twice. The Tribunal upheld the revised computation because the Department had not effectively rebutted the underlying quantitative findings. The High Court found no demonstrated perversity in this approach. The Department also challenged deletion of an addition of Rs. 13,33,961 concerning the difference between physical cash and the cash-book balance. The cash book reflected Rs. 15,96,561, whereas physical cash found during the survey amounted to Rs. 2,62,600. Thus, the difference represented a shortage of physical cash compared with the books, rather than physical cash exceeding the recorded balance. The assessee explained that Rs. 5,93,082 related to cash purchases subsequently recorded in the accounts and disallowed in its return under Section 40A(3). The remaining Rs. 7,40,879 was attributed to withdrawals made by the partners and subsequently reflected in their capital accounts and the firm’s cash records. The Commissioner (Appeals) accepted the reconciliation and deleted the addition, and the Tribunal upheld that finding. The High Court declined to interfere in the absence of any established factual perversity. Before the High Court, the Revenue maintained that the appellate authorities had incorrectly disregarded the forensic report, the alleged impossibility of serving 223 customers within four hours, the abnormal sales increases and admissions concerning stock differences. It argued that the assessee had failed to discharge the burden of establishing genuine transactions and that the material justified the additions. The assessee, however, relied upon the quantitative reconciliation, the software developer’s explanation, the accepted purchase records, the actual stock position and the fact that the sales receipts were already included in taxable business income. The assessee also relied on the Gujarat High Court’s decision in PCIT Vs Jewel World, decided on 23 June 2026, in support of its position. After considering the rival submissions, the Gujarat High Court held that sufficient stock was available, both physically and in the records, to support the disputed sales. The Court accepted that the Commissioner (Appeals) and Tribunal had properly separated the sales issue from the gross-profit and stock-discrepancy issues. It further held that the Revenue could not shift the burden to the assessee merely because substantial cash sales were recorded during demonetisation, when purchases were not disturbed and the sales had already been offered as business receipts. The Court specifically rejected the contention that such circumstances justified treating the receipts as unexplained cash credits under Section 68, attracting the higher tax treatment under Section 115BBE, on the facts established in this case. Accordingly, by its judgment dated 22 October 2026, the High Court held that the concurrent findings of the Commissioner (Appeals) and Tribunal suffered from no legal infirmity and did not give rise to any substantial question of law under Section 260A. The Revenue’s appeal was dismissed. The decision is significant because it requires allegations of bogus demonetisation cash sales to be tested against the complete evidentiary record, particularly accepted purchases, quantitative stock movement, transaction records and credible explanations for software anomalies. At the same time, the judgment preserves the distinction between rejection of a fictitious-sales allegation and the separate taxation of established gross-profit deficiencies or stock discrepancies. The Rs. 96 lakh gross-profit addition remained undisturbed, the stock discrepancy was restricted to Rs. 95,41,057, and the cash-difference addition was deleted. Conclusion: The judgment demonstrates that an extraordinary increase in cash sales, even when accompanied by forensic irregularities, does not automatically establish unexplained income under Section 68. Where the appellate authorities have evaluated the evidence and recorded sustainable factual findings, the High Court will ordinarily not reappreciate that evidence in an appeal under Section 260A merely because the Revenue advances an alternative interpretation. The ruling is fact-specific and does not provide automatic immunity for demonetisation-period cash Introduction The Gujarat High Court has dismissed the Revenue’s appeal challenging relief granted to a jewellery firm concerning alleged bogus cash sales during demonetisation. The dispute involved an addition of ₹7,88,85,082 under Section 68, based on an unusual sales increase and forensic findings suggesting backdated billing. The Court upheld the concurrent factual findings favouring the assessee, noting that purchases were not disputed, sufficient stock was available and the sales had been included in the accounts. However, the decision did not eliminate all additions. A ₹96 lakh gross profit addition remained undisturbed, along with the appellate findings concerning stock discrepancies. Background of the Case The assessee was a partnership firm dealing in gold and silver bullion and jewellery. Following demonetisation on 8 November 2016, the Department investigated suspected introduction of unaccounted cash into bank accounts. A search was conducted at a partner’s residence on 24 January 2017, alongside a survey at the firm’s business premises. A digital device found during the survey was impounded and subjected to forensic examination. The Department alleged that sales entries had been backdated to justify cash deposited during the demonetisation period. The Assessing Officer treated ₹7.88 crore of sales as bogus. Separate additions were made for stock discrepancies of ₹1,14,24,765 and a cash difference of ₹13,33,961. Why the Revenue Doubted the Sales The Assessing Officer questioned sales of approximately ₹3.5 crore to 223 customers within four hours on the evening of 8 November 2016. According to the officer, completing so many jewellery transactions within 240 minutes was practically impossible. Non-production of CCTV footage and sharp increases in cash sales reinforced the suspicion. The Revenue also relied on digital record sequences and timestamps, contending that invoices bearing earlier dates had actually been entered later. It argued that the appellate authorities had failed to give adequate weight to the forensic evidence and surrounding circumstances. Assessee’s Explanation and Supporting Records The assessee explained that the exceptional rush following the demonetisation announcement required a different sales process. Instead of allowing customers to select individual ornaments, it supplied ready-prepared jewellery packets. Staff and family members assisted, while barcode-based computerised billing accelerated invoice generation. The appellate authorities accepted this explanation as commercially plausible in the unusual circumstances. The assessee also produced purchase registers, sales records, stock registers and quantitative reconciliations. Its full-fledged retail business had commenced only in July 2016, making straightforward comparisons with earlier periods unreliable. Stock Availability Supported the Sales The High Court judicially noted the heavy rush to purchase jewellery after the demonetisation announcement. It found that sufficient physical and recorded stock was available to support the disputed sales. The purchases had not been disturbed, and the records did not disclose negative stock supporting the allegation of fictitious sales. Although stock discrepancies were separately identified during the survey, they were not commensurate with the alleged ₹7.88 crore bogus sales. The Court therefore upheld the rejection of the Revenue’s backdating theory on the facts established by the appellate authorities. Forensic Findings Had to Be Considered with Other Evidence The assessee explained that opening and saving or printing an existing voucher could generate a fresh transaction sequence in its accounting software. The appellate authorities considered this explanation alongside the stock and transaction records. The High Court found no basis to overturn their concurrent conclusions. The ruling thus turned on the combined evidentiary record. It does not establish that forensic findings are irrelevant; rather, the alleged anomalies did not justify the addition in the circumstances proved here. Gross Profit Addition Remained The CIT(A) rejected the books under Section 145 and estimated an additional ₹96 lakh as normal business income, addressing the unsatisfactory gross profit position. The Tribunal upheld that addition, and the assessee did not pursue a further appeal against it. The High Court observed that rejection of books did not automatically invalidate the stock register. The stock reconciliations remained relevant when examining whether the sales were fictitious. Stock and Cash Differences The appellate authorities recomputed the stock discrepancy at ₹95,41,057, after considering 1,510.210 grams of 18-carat jewellery omitted from the physical-stock calculation. The ₹13,33,961 cash difference was also explained. Physical cash was lower than the book balance, and the reconciliation concerned cash purchases and partners’ withdrawals subsequently recorded. The purchases had also been disallowed by the assessee under Section 40A(3). The Court found no demonstrated perversity in these factual conclusions. Conclusion The High Court held that no substantial question of law arose and dismissed the Revenue’s appeal. On these facts, demonetisation cash sales alone could not justify shifting the burden to the assessee to support a Section 68 addition attracting Section 115BBE, where purchases, stock availability and recorded sales supported its explanation. The judgment highlights the importance of quantitative stock reconciliation and transaction evidence, while leaving the sustained gross profit addition undisturbed.

Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); Lingeswaran Vs Thirunagalingam (Supreme Court of India) Summary: The Supreme Court, in Lingeswaran and Others Vs Thirunagalingam, dismissed special leave petitions challenging the judgment of the Madras High Court which had set aside the trial court’s orders condoning substantial delays in applications to set aside an ex parte decree. The litigation arose from a suit for specific performance concerning an agreement for sale dated 17 August 2015. The agreed consideration for the property was ₹3,00,000, of which the plaintiff stated that ₹2,60,000 had been paid in advance, with the remaining ₹40,000 payable within one month. According to the plaintiff, he was ready to pay the balance on 10 September 2015 but the first defendant did not execute the sale deed. The plaintiff subsequently discovered that the first defendant had transferred the property to the second defendant and instituted O.S. No. 110 of 2015 before the Sub Court, Paramakudi. The first defendant disputed the nature of the agreement, maintaining that it was merely security for a loan rather than an enforceable contract for sale. Both defendants entered appearance and filed written statements but did not participate further in the trial. The suit was consequently decreed ex parte on 7 February 2017. The plaintiff stated that possession had been given and that the sale deed had thereafter been executed through the court. The resulting applications to reopen the proceedings raised the central issue whether the defendants’ unexplained inaction could be excused in the interest of providing them an opportunity to contest the suit on merits. The defendants sought condonation of delays of 467 days and 712 days in presenting applications to set aside the ex parte decree. Their affidavits attributed the non-appearance principally to illness, including a plea that the second defendant had suffered from jaundice, and referred to their claimed subsequent knowledge of the decree. The plaintiff opposed the applications, emphasising that the defendants had been fully aware of the suit, had already filed written statements and had nevertheless remained absent during trial. The plaintiff also pointed out that the sale deed had already been executed pursuant to the decree, so that reopening the dispute after prolonged inaction would cause substantial hardship. On 19 August 2019, however, the trial court condoned the delays upon payment of costs. The plaintiff approached the Madras High Court in C.R.P. (MD) Nos. 1688 and 1689 of 2019, contending that the defendants had not shown sufficient cause under Section 5 of the Limitation Act, 1963. The dispute therefore concerned not merely the length of the delay but the adequacy and bona fides of the explanation for failing to use a legal remedy within time. A court’s discretionary power to condone delay operates within the statutory requirement of sufficient cause; the desirability of a merits hearing does not dispense with that requirement. The Madras High Court examined the defendants’ conduct in the context of the earlier proceedings and found their explanations insufficient. It noted that both defendants knew of the litigation because they had participated initially by filing written statements, but later failed to pursue their defence. The assertions regarding illness were vague and unsupported by an adequate explanation for the prolonged period of inactivity. The Court also considered it significant that the first defendant had lodged a caveat after the plaintiff demanded execution of the sale deed, indicating prior awareness of the prospect of litigation. The defence that the agreement was actually a loan security and the challenge to the plaintiff’s readiness and willingness went to the merits of the underlying suit; these contentions did not, by themselves, explain why the defendants had failed to apply within time to set aside the decree. The Court found that the trial court had wrongly treated compensation by costs as an adequate reason for overlooking the failure to establish sufficient cause. In view of the absence of satisfactory reasons and the fact that the sale deed had already been executed in favour of the plaintiff, the High Court set aside the orders dated 19 August 2019 and allowed both civil revision petitions. Its ruling is consistent with the broader judicial distinction between a genuinely explained procedural lapse and unexplained or negligent delay. When the defendants challenged the High Court’s decision, the Supreme Court reviewed the orders of both courts and specifically examined the trial court’s own findings. It observed that the trial court had expressly found that, in the absence of material evidence, the delay had not been satisfactorily explained and that the applications lacked merit. Despite reaching those conclusions, the trial court had proceeded to condone the delay in the belief that both sides should receive a fair hearing on the merits and that the plaintiff could be compensated through heavy costs. The Supreme Court rejected that reasoning. Once the trial court concluded that the delay was not satisfactorily explained, it was required to reject the applications rather than reward the defendants’ delay and laches. Referring to Popat Bahiru Goverdhane Vs Land Acquisition Officer, (2013) 10 SCC 765, the Court reiterated that the law of limitation must be applied according to the statutory mandate even where its application causes hardship; equitable considerations cannot independently override the legislative scheme. It also relied on Maniben Devraj Shah Vs Municipal Corporation of Brihan Mumbai, (2012) 5 SCC 157, recognising that limitation serves public policy by requiring litigants to pursue remedies without unreasonable delay, while still permitting condonation where sufficient cause is genuinely established. The Court’s emphasis was on proper exercise of discretion, not on a blanket prohibition against condonation of substantial delays. In the result, the Supreme Court expressed complete agreement with the Madras High Court and dismissed the special leave petitions, leaving the High Court’s reversal of the trial court’s condonation orders undisturbed. The connected pending applications were also disposed of. The combined decisions establish that the opportunity to contest a civil dispute on merits is an important consideration but cannot replace the threshold requirement of a credible, sufficiently supported explanation for delay. The number of days involved is relevant, but a court must principally assess the applicant’s conduct, awareness of the proceedings, diligence and the reasons offered for the entire period of default. Payment of costs is not a substitute for establishing sufficient cause; nor can a broad appeal to substantial justice justify condonation after the court has expressly found the explanation wanting. The decisions also underscore that the successful litigant’s position and the procedural finality of an executed decree deserve consideration. For practical purposes, a party seeking to set aside an ex parte decree should act promptly, support allegations such as illness or inability to attend with credible material, and address why its earlier participation in the suit did not lead to timely steps. A similar judicial emphasis on examining the entire period of delay is reflected in TaxGuru’s discussion of partial or selective explanations for condonation of delay. The present Supreme Court order decided the condonation issue and did not adjudicate whether the underlying agreement for sale was enforceable. FULL TEXT OF THE SUPREME COURT JUDGMENT/ORDER 1. Feeling aggrieved and dissatisfied with the impugned judgment and order dated 09.11.2021 passed by the High Court of Judicature at Madras, Bench at Madurai in C.R.P. (MD) Nos. 1688 & 1689 of 2019, by which the High Court has set aside the order passed by the learned trial Court condoning the huge delay of 467 days in preferring the application for setting aside the ex-parte decree, the original defendants have preferred the present special leave petitions. 2. We have heard Mr. K.V. Vijayakumar, learned counsel appearing on behalf of the petitioners at length. 3. We have gone through the impugned judgment and order passed by the High Court as well as the order passed by the learned trial Court by which the learned trial Court condoned the huge delay of 467 days. 4. Having gone through the order passed by the learned trial Court, even the learned trial Court also specifically observed that, in the absence of material evidence, it cannot be said that the delay has been explained. The trial Court has also observed that the Court feels that there are no merits in the application. Still the trial Court condoned the delay by observing that an opportunity of fair trial should be given to both the parties to put-forth their case on merits. The trial Court also observed that, on allowing the application for condonation of delay, no prejudice will be caused to the plaintiff and, therefore, the delay can be condoned by compensating the plaintiff by way of heavy costs. The said order has been set aside by the High Court by the impugned judgment and order. 5. We are in complete agreement with the view taken by the High Court. Once it was found even by the learned trial Court that delay has not been properly explained and even there are no merits in the application for condonation of delay, thereafter, the matter should rest there and the condonation of delay application was required to be dismissed. The approach adopted by the learned trial Court that, even after finding that, in absence of any material evidence it cannot be said that the delay has been explained and that there are no merits in the application, still to condone the delay would be giving a premium to a person who fails to explain the delay and who is guilty of delay and laches. At this stage, the decision of this Court in the case of Popat Bahiru Goverdhane v. Land Acquisition Officer, reported in (2013) 10 SCC 765 is required to be referred to. In the said decision, it is observed and held that the law of limitation may harshly affect a particular party but it has to be applied with all its rigour when the statute so prescribes. The Court has no power to extend the period of limitation on equitable grounds. The statutory provision may cause hardship or inconvenience to a particular party but the Court has no choice but to enforce it giving full effect to the same. 5.1 In the case of Maniben Devraj Shah v. Municipal Corporation of Brihan Mumbai, (2012) 5 SCC 157, in paragraph 14, it is observed and held as under: “The law of limitation is founded on public policy. The limitation Act, 1963 has not been enacted with the object of destroying the rights of the parties but to ensure that they approach the court for vindication of their rights without unreasonable delay. The idea underlying the concept of limitation is that every remedy should remain alive only till the expiry of the period fixed by the legislature. At the same time, the courts are empowered to condone the delay provided that sufficient cause is shown by the applicant for not availing the remedy within the prescribed period of limitation.” 6. In view of the above and for the reasons stated above, we are in complete agreement with the view taken by the High Court. The Special Leave Petitions stand dismissed. Pending application, if any, also stands disposed of. The Special Leave Petitions stand dismissed in terms of the signed order.

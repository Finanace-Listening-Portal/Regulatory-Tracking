Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); Siemens Financial Services Private Limited Vs Rasik Khandelwal (NCLT Allahabad) Summary : The National Company Law Tribunal, Allahabad Bench, admitted Siemens Financial Services Private Limited’s application under Section 95 of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016 and initiated the Personal Insolvency Resolution Process against Rasik Khandelwal, personal guarantor to Rasik Products Private Limited. The application, filed on 19 April 2024, concerned unpaid dues of ₹3,81,04,993 arising from four secured loan facilities sanctioned during 2022. The finance agreements contained unconditional and irrevocable guarantees making the guarantor jointly and severally liable with the borrower. Following repayment defaults, the accounts were classified as non-performing assets on 14 December 2023. The creditor invoked the guarantee through a notice dated 16 February 2024 under Section 13(2) of the SARFAESI Act, 2002, followed by a statutory Form B demand notice dated 12 March 2024. The guarantor did not discharge the liability within fourteen days of service of the statutory notice. The Resolution Professional, appointed on 18 October 2024, submitted a report dated 1 November 2024 recommending admission. He recorded that the application satisfied the statutory requirements, the finance agreements established the personal guarantee, and the guarantor had not produced proof of repayment despite requests for information. The guarantor subsequently challenged maintainability, alleging that repayment dates and the date of default were insufficiently specified, the creditor’s status and computation of approximately ₹3.81 crore were unjustified, additional interest had been improperly charged, and the creditor had proceeded against him without first making substantial recovery efforts against the borrower. He also questioned the timing and contents of the NeSL record and alleged that the Resolution Professional had mechanically recommended admission. In disputing the debt computation, he cited Central Bank of India v. Ravindra & Ors., (2002) 1 SCC 367. The creditor answered that signed repayment schedules and statements of account established the obligations and default. It stated that the instalment default occurred on 14 September 2023, followed by NPA classification on 14 December 2023, and relied on the contractual acceleration and cross-default clauses. Citing Laxmi Pat Surana v. Union Bank of India & Anr., (2021) 8 SCC 481, it submitted that the borrower and guarantor could be proceeded against simultaneously. It also relied on the NeSL Form D recording default on 14 September 2023 and on communications concerning payment assurances and settlement. The Tribunal held that the application was within the three-year limitation period under Article 137, considering the repayment default, guarantee invocation and filing date. Rejecting the requirement of prior recovery against the borrower, the Tribunal noted that CIRP against Rasik Products Private Limited had already commenced by an order dated 8 September 2025. It further relied on Mahendra Kumar Jajodia v. State Bank of India, (2022) ibclaw.in 32 SC for the position that commencement of CIRP against the corporate debtor was not a condition precedent to personal guarantor insolvency. It held that detailed computation of the default amount would be undertaken by the Resolution Professional during collation of claims and that, at admission, the default need only prima facie exceed the applicable threshold. The order recorded that the claimed default exceeded ₹1 crore. It also rejected the procedural and default-date objections, finding compliance with Section 95 and the applicable rules. NeSL records were held not to be a prerequisite because debt and default could be established through finance agreements, repayment schedules, statements of account and other documents. The Tribunal found the guarantee absolute, unconditional and irrevocable and applied the co-extensive liability principle under Section 128 of the Indian Contract Act, 1872. It accepted that the guarantor had failed to pay despite invocation and statutory demand, admitted the petition and continued Amit Ojha as Resolution Professional. A moratorium was declared for 180 days from admission or until an order on the repayment plan under Section 114, whichever occurred earlier. The Tribunal directed publication of notice, invitation and collation of creditor claims, preparation of a repayment plan and compliance with the subsequent statutory process. The Resolution Professional was directed to submit progress reports every thirty days, and the matter was listed for further proceedings on 16 November 2026. Cases Discussed Mahendra Kumar Jajodia v. State Bank of India, (2022) ibclaw.in 32 SC (Supreme Court) — Relied upon by the Tribunal for the position that initiation of CIRP against the principal borrower or corporate debtor is not a condition precedent to insolvency proceedings against its personal guarantor. Laxmi Pat Surana v. Union Bank of India & Anr., (2021) 8 SCC 481 (Supreme Court) — Cited by the financial creditor to support simultaneous proceedings against the borrower and guarantor and the co-extensive nature of the guarantor’s liability. Central Bank of India v. Ravindra & Ors., (2002) 1 SCC 367 (Supreme Court) — Cited by the personal guarantor while disputing the computation of the outstanding debt and interest; the Tribunal rejected his objection to admission based on the disputed computation. Siemens Financial Services Private Limited v. Rasik Products Private Limited, CP (IB) No. 38 of 2023, order dated 08.09.2025 (NCLT Allahabad) — The connected corporate debtor proceeding was referred to while rejecting the objection that remedies against the principal borrower had not been pursued; the Tribunal recorded that CIRP had already commenced. FINDINGS OF THE TRIBUNAL 20. Heard the submissions made by the Learned Counsel for the Applicant, Resolution Professional, Respondent/ Personal Guarantor and perused the report submitted by the Resolution Professional. 21. As regards the issue of limitation, the date of default, as mentioned in Part III, pertains to September 2023, and the Principal Borrower/Corporate Debtor was classified as NPA on 14.12.2023. Further, the Personal Guarantee was invoked vide notice dated 16.02.2024 issued under Section 13(2) of the SARFAESI Act, 2002, in terms of Clause 11 of the finance agreements. Since no payment was made thereafter, the Applicant, owing to the continued default in repayment of the entire outstanding debt, filed the present Application on 19.04.2024, which is well within the period of three years prescribed under Article 137 of the Limitation Act, 1963, applicable to proceedings under the IBC, 2016. 22. As regards the objection of the Respondent/ Personal Guarantor, with respect to exhaustion of remedies against the Corporate Debtor, we find that this objection is not factually correct as in the present case, CIRP against the Corporate Debtor has already been initiated in CP(IB) No.38 of 2023 vide order dated 08.09.2025. However, it has also been settled law that for initiation of insolvency resolution process against the personal guarantor, initiation of CIRP against the Principal Borrower/ Corporate Debtor is not a condition precedent, as laid down by the Hon’ble Supreme Court in the case of Mahendra Kumar Jajodia v. State Bank of India, (2022) ibclaw.in 32 SC. 23. With respect to the second leg of the contention of the Respondent/ Personal Guarantor on the transparency in computation of default amount, we cannot accept the argument of the Respondent, as the computation of default amount is to be made by the RP itself at the stage of collation of claims of creditors after initiation of PIRP. At the stage of considering the application for admission it has to be only seen whether default amount is prima-facie more than the threshold limit. In the present case, we find that the default amount is over and above the threshold limit as prescribed under the Code, 2016, being more than Rs. 1 Crore. 24. With respect to the third leg of the contention of the Respondent/ Personal Guarantor on procedural irregularities in filing Form-C and subsequently not specifying date of default in the present Petition, we find that this argument of the Respondent/ Corporate Debtor is vague and cannot be accepted, as upon perusal of the present petition, it has been found, that it has fulfilled all the procedural requirements as specified under Section 95 of the Code, 2016 and corresponding Rules and Regulations. 25. Regarding the Contention of Respondent with respect to the specific date of default in the petition, we observe that the manner in which the date of default is stipulated in Form C does not constitute a valid ground for the rejection of a petition filed under Section 95 of the IBC, 2016. Furthermore, it is evident that the Applicant has duly satisfied the mandatory procedural prerequisites by invoking the guarantee against the Personal Guarantor vide Notice dated 16.02.2024 prior to the issuance of the statutory demand notice in Form B dated 12.03.2024 for the initiation of the PIRP. 26. With respect to the contention of the Respondent/ Personal Guarantor on delay and ambiguity in filing NeSL Records, this contention cannot be accepted as the NeSL records is not a condition precedent for filing a petition under Section 95 of IBC, 2016. It has already been settled that debt and default can be established through other documents in a Petition under Section 95 of IBC, 2016. Furthermore, in the present petition, the Financial Creditor has relied on establishing debt and default through Finance Agreements dated 31.01.2022, 31.01.2022, 22.09.2022 and 14.11.2022, Repayment Schedules annexed as Exhibits ‘H’, ‘K’, ‘N’ and ‘Q’, respectively, Statement of Accounts annexed hereto as Exhibits ‘R’, ‘S’, ‘T’ and ‘U’, respectively, and by further classifying the account of the Corporate Debtor as NPA on occurrence of default. 27. Having regard to the above, we are satisfied that the Application is filed well within limitation and maintainable in law. 28. As regards the debt and default, the report filed by the RP under Section 99 and material submitted by the Financial Creditor under section 95 have been considered. 29. As per the submissions and records placed before us through the present Application filed under Section 95, it is evident that the Personal Guarantor, viz., Mr. Rasik Khandelwal, executed personal guarantee in favour of the Financial Creditor through four Finance Agreements dated 31.01.2022, 31.01.2022, 22.09.2022 and 14.11.2022, thereby securing the loan facilities aggregating to Rs. 6.50 crores availed by the Corporate Debtor/Principal Borrower. The relevant Clause 11 of the Finance Agreements has already been reproduced in para 5 above, which stipulates the joint and several liability of the Respondent/Personal Guarantor in respect of the obligations secured thereunder. 30. The record further reflects that the Corporate Debtor failed to maintain financial discipline and failed to clear the remaining outstanding debt due to the Financial Creditor. Hence, the Financial Creditor issued a Section 13(2) Notice dated 16.02.2024 calling upon the Corporate Debtor and the Guarantor to jointly and severally repay outstanding dues of Rs. 3,81,04,993/- (including interest), thereby invoking the guarantee. However, neither was any payment made towards the outstanding dues nor was any reply received to the said notice; thereby, the outstanding debt remained due and payable. 31. Consequently, the Financial Creditor thereafter issued a statutory demand notice in Form B dated 12.03.2024 under Section 95(4)(b) of the Code to the Respondent /Personal Guarantor, demanding payment of Rs. 3,81,04,993/- due as on 14.02.2024. The Guarantor again failed to discharge the liability within 14 days of service of notice as mandated under the Code. 32. The RP has categorically recorded in its report that the defaulted amount, as prayed by the Applicant, remains due and payable, and that the Guarantor has committed default in making the payment despite service of notices. Considering these documents, and making further enquiries, as provided under section 99 of the Code, the RP u/s 99 has submitted the report vide I.A. No. 646/2024 recommending initiation of insolvency against the Respondent/ Personal Guarantor, which is considered by us while passing this order. 33. The provisions of Section 128 of the Indian Contract Act, 1872 render the liability of the guarantor co-extensive with that of the principal debtor, unless otherwise provided. The personal guarantee executed by the Respondent herein is absolute, unconditional, and irrevocable. Further, we find that the Respondent had undertaken to pay forthwith, upon demand, all sums payable by the Corporate Debtor/Principal Borrower under the facility documents. In the present case, despite the demand notice issued under section 95(4)(b) of the IBC, the Guarantor has failed to discharge his liability. 34. We have gone through the report dated 01.11.2024, filed by the RP, and we find that: i. The Insolvency Petition satisfies the requirement of Section 95 of IBC, 2016 and has been filed in the requisite form, in terms of Section 95(1) read with Rule 7(1) and Rule 7(2) of the PIRP Rules, 2019, supported by requisite fee and documents. ii. The Respondent Guarantor after being duly served with a demand notice dated 12.03.2024, has committed default in repayment of his debts; therefore, the requirement as set out under Section 95(4)(b) is satisfied. iii. That the debts mentioned in the application are not excluded debts. iv. That the Personal Guarantor is not eligible for a fresh start process provided under Chapter II of IBC, 2016. 35. Considering the above facts and circumstances and upon perusal of the documents on record, the CP (IB) No. 79/ALD/2024 filed under Section 95 of the IBC, 2016 is hereby admitted, and the Personal Insolvency Resolution Process (PIRP) stands initiated against Mr. Rasik Khandelwal viz. the Respondent herein.

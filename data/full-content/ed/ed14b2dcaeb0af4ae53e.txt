K R Balasubramanyam & Manu P Toms ET Bureau Published On Nov 24, 2026 at 03:43 PM IST var news_msid = 136126642; console.log(news_msid); Set ETBFSI as your preferred source on Google Copy Link Share on WhatsApp Share on Linkedin Share on X Share on Telegram Share on Facebook ET Bengaluru: Narayana Health founder Dr Devi Shetty has called for scrapping the 18% GST on group health insurance, saying the levy makes it harder to extend affordable cover to working-class Indians.Dr Shetty said group insurance was particularly important for workers in the informal and gig economies, who may find it difficult to buy individual health cover. "They have to remove the 18% GST on group insurance," Dr Shetty told ET. The tax becomes significant when insurers try to offer policies with relatively low premiums of ₹5,000, ₹10,000 or ₹20,000, he said.NH is also exploring ways to extend insurance to groups such as autorickshaw drivers and is working with Namma Yatri on a pilot involving about 5,000 drivers. "Working-class people can only be sold group insurance," Dr Shetty said, arguing that the tax ultimately gets added to the premiums paid by subscribers. Advt The Centre last year exempted individual health policies from GST while retaining the levy on group and employer-sponsored health policies.'Healthcare has outpriced itself'The NH founder said India could become the first country to demonstrate that the quality of healthcare a citizen receives need not depend on the wealth of the individual or family."I believe India will become the first country in the world to dissociate healthcare from wealth," Dr Shetty said, adding that this could happen within the next five to seven years.India has substantially expanded medical education, while private investment has added hospital beds, especially in large cities, he said. The bigger problem now is patients' ability to pay for treatment."The average occupancy of a bed in an Indian corporate hospital is 50-60%. We have beds, doctors, nurses and technicians. Then why is half the bed capacity empty when so many people require treatment? Because we have outpriced ourselves," he said.Hospitals are largely fixed-cost businesses, and higher utilisation can spread costs across many more patients, helping bring down treatment prices, Dr Shetty said. "My bet is that the cost of healthcare will go down in India. It will not go up," he said.The same economics could work in diagnostics, where greater utilisation of expensive equipment such as CT scanners would reduce the cost per test and make preventive screening affordable for more people, he said. Dr Shetty said wider preventive screening could help detect cardiovascular disease and cancer earlier, significantly reducing both treatment costs and mortality. Advt NH is looking to demonstrate its affordability model through smaller hospitals. "What is desperately needed in India is a hospital for the middle-class person earning ₹30,000 to ₹60,000 a month, where they can go with dignity and get treatment," he said. NH wants to expand the model through hospitals with about 100-150 beds, using its larger infrastructure and digital capabilities to centralise support functions.

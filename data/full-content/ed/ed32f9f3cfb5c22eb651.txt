(Supreme Court India) Non-Signatories May Be Referred to Arbitration as Real Parties to the Dispute; Final Jurisdiction Remains with the Tribunal Background: A Partnership Asset and a Disputed Retirement The dispute concerned a commercial property measuring 3,500 square feet at Manish Commercial Centre, Worli, Mumbai. The property had been acquired in 1977 by the partnership firm, Prospective Traders, originally constituted by three family trusts. Under a reconstitution deed dated 9 December 1985, the appellants and the first two respondents were inducted as partners. This deed contained the arbitration clause relevant to the proceedings. The original partners subsequently retired. A further retirement deed dated 3 July 1995 purportedly recorded the retirement of the appellants. However, the appellants alleged that this retirement deed was forged and maintained that they had never ceased to be partners. The subsequent changes in the business proceeded on the footing that the appellants had retired. In 2005, the retirement of the second respondent resulted in the business continuing as the first respondent’s proprietorship. In 2009, the third and fourth respondents were inducted into a new partnership, and the first respondent thereafter retired. The central controversy was therefore whether the property continued to belong to the partnership governed by the 1985 deed or whether rights over it validly passed under the subsequent arrangements. High Court Excluded the Later Partners An earlier arbitral award obtained at the appellants’ instance had been set aside under section 34 of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996. The appellants subsequently approached the High Court under section 11. While deciding the application, the High Court answered the limitation issue in the appellants’ favour concerning the first two respondents. However, it declined to include the third and fourth respondents in the arbitration. The High Court reasoned that these respondents were not signatories to the 1985 arbitration agreement and did not fall within the recognised exceptions concerning persons claiming through or under a signatory or the group of companies doctrine. The Supreme Court examined whether they could nevertheless be treated, at the referral stage, as “veritable parties”—in substance, real parties—to the arbitration. Absence of Signature Is Not Conclusive The Supreme Court referred to the Constitution Bench decision in Cox & Kings Limited v. SAP India Private Limited, which recognised that the requirement of a written arbitration agreement under section 7 does not exclude the possibility of binding non-signatories. The Court explained that a rigid approach excluding every person who has not signed the agreement would overlook the significance of conduct, relationships and involvement in obligations connected with the dispute. However, it expressly observed that the doctrines of claiming through or under and group of companies did not apply to the present proceedings. The broader principles explained in Cox & Kings nevertheless assisted in deciding the factual question before it. That judgment also preserved the application of other doctrines and principles concerning non-signatories. Referral Court May Examine the Question Prima Facie The Court also considered Hindustan Petroleum Corporation Limited v. BCL Secure Premises Private Limited. That decision recognised the referral court’s authority under section 11 to examine, on a prima facie basis, whether an arbitration agreement exists and whether a non-signatory is a real party to it. The answer depends on the particular facts and circumstances. Such preliminary examination does not displace the arbitral tribunal’s authority to decide its jurisdiction after considering the evidence and applicable legal principles. The Supreme Court thus distinguished between permitting a non-signatory to enter the arbitration process and finally determining that the arbitration agreement binds that person. Later Partners’ Rights Depended on the Challenged Retirement The Court found that the third and fourth respondents’ rights over the property were directly connected with the validity of the appellants’ alleged retirement. If the retirement deed dated 3 July 1995 were held invalid, the parties would necessarily revert to the partnership arrangement under the 1985 deed. That outcome would affect the rights asserted by the later partners under the subsequent partnership arrangements. The third and fourth respondents were consequently veritable parties to the dispute, since the arbitration could substantially affect their claimed rights over the very asset in controversy. Decision and Questions Left Open The Supreme Court modified the High Court’s order to include the third and fourth respondents in the arbitration despite their not having signed the 1985 partnership deed. However, the finding was expressly prima facie. The arbitral tribunal remained free to determine whether they were bound by the arbitration agreement. Their objections concerning limitation and estoppel also remained open. In particular, they could contend that the appellants had omitted them from the earlier arbitration despite knowing of the retirement deed. The appeal was disposed of with this limited modification. Author’s Comments The judgment is particularly relevant where successive partnership arrangements create competing claims over the same asset. A later entrant’s absence from the original arbitration agreement may not conclude the referral enquiry when that entrant’s rights depend upon the validity of the very transaction under challenge. Nevertheless, the decision does not establish that everyone affected by an arbitration automatically becomes bound by it. The Court relied on the specific chain of partnership transactions and preserved the tribunal’s final jurisdictional determination. Equally, inclusion in the arbitration did not validate the appellants’ allegations of forgery or defeat the respondents’ limitation and estoppel objections. The judgment opened the door to their participation while leaving those substantive and jurisdictional questions for adjudication. Cases Discussed Hindustan Petroleum Corporation Limited v. SAP India Private Limited and Another, (2024) 4 SCC 1; 2023 INSC 1051 (Supreme Court, Constitution Bench) FULL TEXT OF THE SUPREME COURT JUDGMENT Leave granted. 2. ‘Veritable Party’ in a constituted arbitration is a vexing question that props up oftener than ever, which question has to be determined more on the facts; the multiple aspects arising therefrom, which cannot be put down in a strait jacket formula. 3. We have heard Sri Ashim Sood, learned Counsel for the appellants, Sri Sanjay Sarin, learned Counsel for the 1st and 2nd respondents and Sri Ritin Rai, learned Senior Counsel for the 3rd and 4th respondents. 4. The issue is with respect to whether the 2nd and 3rd respondents must be included in the arbitration; whom, if we find to be veritable parties on a prima facie consideration of the facts and circumstances, still the question would be open before the Arbitrator appointed. 5. The High Court, considering the application under Section 11 of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 19961, first considered the aspect of limitation, especially since there was an earlier Arbitral Award at the instance of the appellants themselves, which was set aside under Section 34 of the said Act. The question of limitation was found in favour of the appellants, which we need not engage with, especially since the issue of limitation is deemed to have been answered only as against 1st and 2nd respondents; and with respect to 3rd and 4th respondents, prayer to join them in the arbitration was declined. The rejection of such prayer was also on account of the finding that they are not signatories to the Arbitration Agreement and do not fall within the recognized exceptions as person/persons claiming through or under a signatory or covered under the Group of Companies doctrine. 6. We would first look at the facts which led to the application before the High Court. The dispute specifically arises with respect to a property numbered as Unit No.2 admeasuring 3,500 sq. ft. in the ground floor of Manish Commercial Centre, 216-A, Dr. Annie Besant Road, Worli, Mumbai – 400 030, which is hereinafter termed as the ‘subject asset’. This originally belonged to a partnership firm called M/s Prospective Traders. The original deed of partnership was executed between three family trusts, respectively of V. U. Shah, N.U. Shah and C.U. Shah; who are referred to as the original partners, by a deed of partnership executed on 02.04.1977. The asset was acquired by the partnership on 03.10.1977. On 09.12.1985, the partnership was reconstituted by a deed, wherein the appellants; Hemant D. Shah HUF and the wife of Hemant D. Shah, the 1st and 2nd appellants as also Chittaranjan D. Shah HUF and the daughter in law of C.D. Shah, the 1st and 2nd respondents were inducted as partners. 7. Subsequently on 30.12.1987, by another deed of partnership; which is also disputed by the appellants, the partnership firm was reconstituted and the original partners retired. On 03.07.1995, by a deed of retirement, the appellants are asserted to have retired from the partnership firm, which document is alleged to be a forged one by the appellants. Later, on 01.07.2005, the 2nd respondent retired from the partnership firm, resulting in dissolution of the said partnership firm and it continued as a proprietorship with its assets vested with the 1st respondent. Again, by a deed of 30.09.2009, 3rd and 4th respondents were brought as partners into the proprietorship, constituting a new partnership. On retirement of the 1st respondent, by a deed of 04.11.2009, 3rd and 4th respondents continued as partners of the firm. 8. The 3rd and 4th respondents distanced themselves from the partnership agreement of 09.12.1985; which contains an arbitration clause and the 1st and 2nd respondents assert the dissolution of the firm on the retirement of 2nd respondent, subsequent to the retirement of the appellants. The appellants, on the other hand, assert that they never retired from the firm and the partnership continues even now, as also the assets belong to the original partnership. 9. Learned Single Judge has relied on Cox & Kings Limited v. SAP India Private Limited and Another2 and Hindustan Petroleum Corporation Limited v. BCL Secure Premises Private Limited3. 10. Cox & Kings Limited2 elaborately considered the principle of ‘claiming through or under’ and the ‘doctrine of group companies’, which we are of the opinion, does not apply to the present proceedings. However, the principle stated therein would assist us in resolving the question raised herein, which has to be resolved on the facts arising in the present case. Holding arbitration law to be an autonomous legal field, there was a distinction drawn from the corporate law and contract law, insofar as the main purpose of the latter is imputation of substantive legal liability while the law of arbitration determines whether an Arbitration Tribunal has jurisdiction over the disputes arising between the parties to an arbitration agreement. A non-signatory, who has not put pen to paper, indicates an intention, not to assume rights or responsibilities under the agreement. But, it was held that the Courts and Tribunals cannot adopt a rigid approach to exclude all persons or entities, who through conduct and relationship with the signatories intended to be bound by the underlying contract containing the arbitration clause (para 100). 11. It was held in paragraph 132 of Cox & Kings Limited 2 that ‘Looking at the factors holistically, it could be inferred that the non-signatories, by virtue of their relationship with the signatory parties and active involvement in the performance of commercial obligations which are intricately linked to the subject-matter, are not actually strangers to the dispute between the signatory parties.’(sic) 12. From the conclusion of Cox & Kings Ltd.2, we extract paragraphs 170.3, 170.12 and 170.13 as under: ‘170.3. The requirement of a written arbitration agreement under Section 7 does not exclude the possibility of binding non-signatory parties; xxx xxx xxx 170.12. At the referral stage, the referral court should leave it for the Arbitral Tribunal to decide whether the non-signatory is bound by the arbitration agreement; and 170.13. In the course of this judgment, any authoritative determination given by this Court pertaining to the Group of Companies doctrine should not be interpreted to exclude the application of other doctrines and principles for binding non-signatories to the arbitration agreement.’ 13. Hindustan Petroleum Corporation Limited3 referred to Cox & Kings Limited2 and culled out the principle of a prima facie satisfaction being entered by the Referral Court, that there exists an Arbitration Agreement and the non-signatory being a veritable party. It was found that despite such prima facie satisfaction entered, the jurisdiction of the Arbitral Tribunal to decide on it, on the basis of factual evidence and the application of legal doctrine, is preserved. However, it was held that the Referral Court under Section 11 of the Act is not deprived of its jurisdiction from examining whether the non-signatory, in the real sense, is a party to the agreement, the answer to which will depend on the facts and circumstances of each case. Therein the appellant Corporation floated a tender, the successful tenderer in which gave a sub-contract to the respondent. There was a civil suit and later an arbitration attempted by the sub-contractor to settle the disputes with the original contractor. That having failed, the sub-contractor filed an application under Section 11 of the Act against the appellant Corporation, which stood allowed. In the factual scenario, it was found that there was not even a semblance of an intent to affect a legal relationship between the sub-contractor and the awarder or that such a third party was a veritable party. 14. Be that as it may, in the present case, the dispute revolves around the ‘subject asset’ which is an immovable property. Admittedly, the asset was acquired by the partnership firm, in which, later the appellants and the 1st and 2nd respondents were partners, along with the original partners. The original partners retired and though the said document is disputed by the appellants, the original partners do not raise any dispute on the said document. The retirement of the original partners also does not affect the rights of the appellants, if the deed of 09.12.1985 subsists. The bone of contention is the deed of retirement dated 03.07.1995, by which the appellants are said to have retired; which is alleged to be a forged document. 15. In fact, it is this retirement which led to the eventual constitution of another partnership by the 1st and 2nd respondents; subsequently with the retirement of 2nd respondent, the firm continued as a proprietorship with the 1st respondent, who inducted 3rd and 4th respondents and then retired from the firm. If the deed of retirement of 03.07.1995 is found to be invalid on the allegations raised, then necessarily the parties revert to the partnership of 09.12.1985. In which event, it would affect the rights of the 3rd and 4th respondents, who were inducted by the 1st respondent, as partners who obtained rights over the ‘subject asset’ by that partnership deed, in which neither the appellants nor the 1st and 2nd respondents are partners. The dispute raised for arbitration revolves around the ownership of the ‘subject asset’; whether it be by virtue of the deed of 09.12.1985 or by the deed of 04.11.2009. 16. On the facts and circumstances of this case, we are of the opinion that 3rd and 4th respondents are veritable parties to the dispute, since it would substantially affect their rights also as per the partnership deed of 30.11.2009 which can be valid only if the deed of retirement of the appellants is valid. However, this is not to say that the questions of limitation raised by 3rd and 4th respondents and whether the appellants are estopped from including 3rd and 4th respondents, as they were not included in the earlier arbitration proceedings, despite knowledge of the retirement deed at that point of time, would not survive. These aspects are definitely left to be agitated before the Arbitral Tribunal. 17. The impugned order is modified only to the extent of finding the 3rd and 4th respondents to be veritable parties to the arbitration, despite the said respondents being not signatories to the partnership deed of 09.12.1985. 18. We hasten to observe that our findings on 3rd and 4th respondents being veritable parties, is a prima facie finding, which would not govern the adjudication of the said issue or the issue of limitation or the ground raised of an estoppel against the appellants by 3rd and 4th respondents, which would be alive; left to be considered by the Arbitral Tribunal. 19. With the above observations and modification to the impugned order, the appeal stands disposed of. 20. Pending application(s), if any, shall stand rejected.

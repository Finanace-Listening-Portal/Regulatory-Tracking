The proposal comes after the RBI rejected Tata Sons’ application to surrender its registration as a core investment company and directed the holding company to comply with the regulatory framework applicable to an upper-layer non-banking financial company (NBFC-UL). Sections 230-232 of the Companies Act, 2013, provide a statutory mechanism for corporate restructuring, including demergers and transfer of undertakings. However, whether such restructuring can take Tata Sons outside the listing requirement would depend on how the RBI treats the resulting entities. The Tata Sons board has decided to move towards public listing, while Noel Tata has opposed it. The board’s September 17 decision came shortly after the RBI rejected the deregistration application. “Legally, a company may undertake corporate restructuring or demerger, subject to the approval of the NCLT and the shareholders. However, mere restructuring or splitting may not, by itself, avoid the listing requirement applicable to an NBFC in the upper layer,” said Diviay Chadha, partner, Singhania & Co. He said RBI norms concerning regulatory classification and asset-size thresholds might require the assets of multiple NBFCs under common promoter management to be considered together. “Accordingly, unless the RBI or other competent regulatory authority formally permits surrendering the registration or grants a specific exemption, restructuring the entity may not automatically remove the applicable listing obligation,” Chadha said. The issue has become significant after the RBI rejected Tata Sons’ deregistration request and directed it to take steps to ensure compliance with the applicable regulations. Tata Sons had sought deregistration in March 2024 after repaying its outstanding debt. NCLT approval may not settle RBI issue The key hurdle is that an NCLT-sanctioned scheme will not bind the RBI on matters within its regulatory jurisdiction, said Utsav Trivedi, managing partner, TAS Law. “The RBI could say restructuring does not change the underlying regulatory position sufficiently, and therefore the listing obligation continues. That would be the fundamental regulatory fight,” he said. This could be particularly relevant if restructuring merely divides Tata Sons’ existing investment among entities without fundamentally changing the group’s economic and governance structure. Even if assets are divided among several entities and none crosses the threshold of ₹1 trillion applicable to the upper layer, the resulting entities could still fall within other NBFC categories, depending on their activities, Trivedi said. The legal question is whether NCLT-approved reconstruction creates entities whose regulatory classification is assessed afresh or whether the RBI could treat restructuring as a continuation of the existing upper-layer obligation, he said. “That is not answered merely by the Companies Act. The RBI will have to accept the regulatory consequences of the restructuring,” Trivedi said. Hurdles: Shareholders and taxes A Tata Sons demerger will also involve shareholder and creditor approvals, valuation, share-exchange ratios, accounting treatment, transfer of assets and liabilities, employee and contractual arrangements and regulatory clearances. Chadha said a statutory demerger would require prescribed meetings and approvals, including approval by 75 per cent in the value of creditors and shareholders voting on the scheme, along with regulatory approvals from authorities such as the RBI, Securities and Exchange Board of India, and the Competition Commission of India, wherever applicable. The scheme will have to consider affirmative voting rights under Tata Sons’ Articles of Association and the possibility of minority shareholders raising claims under Section 241 of the Companies Act, he said. Stamp duty and the time required for NCLT approval could add to the implementation timeline. Tata Trusts holds about 66 per cent in Tata Sons, while the Shapoorji Pallonji group has 18.37 per cent. The latter has supported public listing, according to recent reports. Supriya Majumdar, partner, Elarra Law Offices, said restructuring was “a legally feasible option to resolve the conundrum of listing obligations” through a demerger or by reducing assets below the applicable regulatory threshold. However, she said implementation would be challenging and time-consuming, particularly after the RBI’s rejection of Tata Sons’ application. She also highlighted the implications of moving high-value investment held by Tata Sons. “Given that the company holds high-value equity stakes in giants like Tata Consultancy Services, dividing these assets would likely have adverse tax and regulatory triggers,” she said. Restructuring could also alter the way dividend income from cash-generating businesses is channelled towards capital-intensive businesses and other group objectives, she said.

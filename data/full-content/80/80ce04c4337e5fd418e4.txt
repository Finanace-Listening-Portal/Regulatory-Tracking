Chunnilal Choudhary (ICAI Disciplinary Committee) Summary: The ICAI Disciplinary Committee, following findings dated 31 August 2026 and a punishment order dated 15 September 2026, held CA. Chunnilal Choudhary guilty of professional misconduct under Clauses (6), (7), (8) and (9) of Part I of the Second Schedule to the Chartered Accountants Act, 1949 in relation to his statutory audit of Varun Industries Limited for financial year 2011–12. The matter was reheard pursuant to an Appellate Authority order dated 26 May 2026. The Committee scrutinised material discrepancies concerning inventories and overseas receivables, including an approximately ₹922 crore difference identified between stock-audit figures and audited debtors. It found that reliance on management explanations, debtor settlement deeds, corporate debt restructuring and financial-statement disclosures did not substitute for sufficient appropriate audit evidence. Requests for confirmations had been sent before the audit report, but responses arrived afterwards. The Committee also held that management’s going-concern assumption required critical evaluation against overdue receivables, weak liquidity, banking difficulties and reliance on restructuring; accounting profits, positive net worth and continued operations alone were insufficient. After considering the respondent’s written and oral representations, the Committee ordered removal of his name from the Register of Members for one year and imposed a fine of ₹1,00,000 payable within sixty days from receipt of the order. Background of the Proceedings The disciplinary proceedings concerned the statutory audit of Varun Industries Limited for FY 2011-12, conducted by CA. Chunnilal Choudhary. The allegations related to material irregularities in stock and book debts, inadequate audit evidence and failure to properly evaluate the company’s ability to continue as a going concern. The matter came before the Committee afresh following an Appellate Authority order dated 26 May 2026. After rehearing, the Committee held the auditor guilty of professional misconduct under Clauses (6), (7), (8) and (9) of Part I of the Second Schedule to the Chartered Accountants Act, 1949. The central question was whether management explanations, disclosures in the accounts and a proposed corporate debt restructuring could adequately support the auditor’s conclusions when substantial contrary financial indicators existed. Stock and Debtors Required Independent Examination The complaint alleged irregularities involving stock and book debts of approximately ₹1,150 crore. The auditor disputed the allegation of siphoning, arguing that no identifiable bank transfer or other documentary evidence established diversion of that amount. He also contended that the complainant’s comparison omitted debtors belonging to several business divisions. The Committee focused on the auditor’s obligation to examine the underlying balances. It observed that the absence of an identifiable bank transfer did not answer whether stock and receivables were correctly stated. According to the Committee’s findings, the stock-audit figures, after considering subsequent sales, indicated debtors of approximately ₹562 crore, whereas the audited accounts reflected ₹1,484 crore. The Committee treated the resulting ₹922 crore difference as an apparent overstatement for which reasonable justification had not been provided. The decision therefore rests on inadequate examination and reporting of material balances, rather than merely on the allegation that funds had been siphoned off. Restructuring and Disclosure Did Not Replace Audit Evidence The auditor relied on the company’s Corporate Debt Restructuring proposal, explaining that its difficulties arose from delayed overseas collections, reduced drawing power and other commercial circumstances. He submitted that the bankers knew of the financial difficulties and were addressing them through restructuring. The Committee rejected the proposition that bankers’ consideration of restructuring established the reliability of the financial statements. A restructuring exercise and a statutory audit serve different purposes. Similarly, disclosure of approximately ₹160 crore of discounts granted to overseas debtors, coupled with deferment of recovery for five years, did not establish the existence, valuation or recoverability of those receivables. The Committee emphasised that the auditor had to obtain sufficient appropriate evidence supporting the balances and their accounting treatment. Disclosure of a doubtful transaction does not, by itself, discharge the duty to verify it. Sending Confirmation Requests Was Insufficient The auditor submitted that direct confirmations had been sought from overseas debtors and that alternative procedures, including examination of settlement deeds, had been undertaken. The Committee noted that confirmation requests were sent before the audit report was signed, but confirmations were received afterwards. Merely sending requests could not establish that sufficient appropriate audit evidence existed when the audit opinion was issued. It also found deficiencies in evidence concerning inventory verification and valuation. An incomplete stock-audit report could not substitute for adequate evidence supporting the statutory auditor’s own conclusions. Further, information contained in the restructuring proposal was available before the report dated 9 August 2012. The Committee held that its implications for asset carrying values required examination, including consideration of adjustments under AS 4 for conditions existing at the balance-sheet date. Profits and Positive Net Worth Did Not Resolve Going-Concern Doubts The auditor relied on positive net worth, accounting profits, continued operations, the absence of NPA classification at the balance-sheet date and proposed banking support. The Committee held that these factors were insufficient without critically evaluating liquidity and the ability to generate cash flows. Substantial overdue receivables, prolonged recovery periods, working-capital irregularities, banking difficulties and dependence on restructuring had to be assessed collectively under SA 570. The Committee acknowledged that an auditor does not guarantee an enterprise’s survival. Nevertheless, continued physical operations and accounting profits cannot conclusively establish the ability to meet financial obligations. The auditor was required to evaluate management’s assessment and determine whether a material uncertainty concerning going concern existed. Punishment Imposed The Committee ordered removal of the auditor’s name from the Register of Members for one year and imposed a fine of ₹1 lakh, payable within sixty days of receipt of the order. Author’s Comments The practical message is that audit procedures must support the conclusion actually reached. Sending confirmations, collecting management representations and referring to disclosures are steps in the process; their adequacy depends on whether they resolve the material risks identified. At the same time, the decision should not be presented as making auditors guarantors of recoverability or future survival. The Committee expressly recognised the limits of an audit. Its criticism concerned the failure to critically evaluate substantial warning signs using evidence available before signing. A particularly useful distinction is between profitability and liquidity. A company may report profits and continue operating while facing serious uncertainty about meeting its obligations. Going-concern documentation must address that uncertainty. For practitioners, the lesson is clear: management’s explanation must be tested, restructuring support must be evaluated, and material discrepancies must be resolved or appropriately reflected in the audit report.

(Bombay High Court) PMLA Arrest on Existing Material Without Fresh Circumstances Held Illegal: Bombay High Court Summary: The Bombay High Court, in Jitendra Pandey v. Directorate of Enforcement & Anr., held that the Enforcement Directorate could not, in the circumstances of the case, arrest a person on the same material on which it had sought judicial permission for further questioning, without demonstrating fresh incriminating material or supervening circumstances. The Court declared the arrest illegal, quashed the remand orders and directed the petitioner’s immediate release. The judgment was delivered by Justice Milind N. Jadhav on 27 October 2026 in Writ Petition No. 3476 of 2026, as confirmed by the Bombay High Court’s official judgment listing. Background of the case The proceedings originated from an FIR registered at Naupada Police Station, Thane, concerning alleged cheating, forgery, criminal conspiracy and related offences. The ED subsequently registered an ECIR under the Prevention of Money Laundering Act, 2002. The prosecution alleged that the petitioner, acting with others, established numerous firms and companies using documents obtained from unsuspecting persons and operated 269 mule bank accounts. These accounts were allegedly used to remit money abroad through fabricated invoices, bills of lading and banking documents, including Forms 15CA, 15CB and A2. According to the allegations, the remittances facilitated differential payments for imported goods whose values had been understated to evade customs duty. The ED also alleged that the petitioner earned commissions and acquired properties through the proceeds of his activities. Following his arrest in the predicate case in March 2024, the petitioner obtained regular bail in June 2024. The ED conducted searches and recorded his statements repeatedly before arresting him under the PMLA on 23 June 2026. Arguments advanced by the petitioner The petitioner challenged the arrest and consequential remand orders on the ground that there was no demonstrated necessity for arrest and no fresh material warranting that action. He submitted that he had participated extensively in the investigation. His statements were recorded on 38 occasions, including three occasions after the ED arrest, over approximately eighteen months. Consequently, the allegation of non-cooperation required scrutiny against the actual record. It was further argued that Section 19(1) permits arrest only upon satisfaction of its statutory conditions. Custody could not be sought merely to continue questioning or facilitate an investigation that was already proceeding through Section 50. ED’s justification for arrest The ED maintained that the petitioner controlled shell entities used for substantial fraudulent foreign remittances. It relied on statements concerning dummy directors, forged documents, commissions and the acquisition or disposal of properties. The agency alleged that the petitioner had withheld details of the ultimate owners of funds, foreign beneficiaries and intermediaries. Custodial questioning, according to the ED, was necessary to uncover the complete network and trace the funds. The Court nevertheless found, upon examining the statements and disclosures, that the petitioner had cooperated with the investigation. Court’s analysis of Sections 19 and 50 The Court emphasised that Section 19 confers a drastic and discretionary power. The authorised officer must possess material supporting a reason to believe that the person is guilty of an offence under the PMLA, and must record those reasons in writing. The expression “may arrest” was significant. The availability of the power did not make its exercise automatic. In this case, the ED had invoked Section 50(2) and Section 50(3) and obtained judicial permission to confront the petitioner with material already in its possession and record further statements. The Court held that the agency could not abandon that course and invoke Section 19 on the very same material, unless fresh incriminating material or a supervening exigency justified the change. The judgment records the ED’s admission that no new incriminating material or development had emerged during the relevant interval preceding arrest. The Court also noted that no attempt had been made to question the petitioner for more than a month after his last pre-arrest statement. In those circumstances, reliance on alleged evasiveness or non-cooperation did not establish the necessity for arrest. Judicial scrutiny and statutory safeguards Referring to decisions including Arvind Kejriwal, Radhika Agarwal and Deepak Singh, the Court reiterated the importance of safeguards governing arrest. The seriousness of allegations could not displace examination of compliance with Section 19. The Court also discussed access to the recorded “reasons to believe”, subject to justified redactions requiring judicial consideration. On the facts before it, the Court characterised the impugned use of the arrest power as a colourable exercise of power. Decision and significance The High Court declared the arrest violative of Section 19(1) and Articles 14 and 21, quashed the remand orders and directed immediate release. It also declined the ED’s request for a two-week stay. The ruling underscores that arrest requires a legally sustainable justification grounded in the particular record. Its finding concerning fresh material must be understood in the context of the petitioner’s cooperation and the ED’s existing court-approved questioning process. The present issue can be safely decided on the basis of interpretation of the statutory provisions of Section 19 read with Section 50 of the PMLA. Both the learned Advocates at the bar have laid substantial stress on interpretation of the provisions of Section 19, since the Petitioner has been arrested. For ease of reference, Section 19 of the PMLA is reproduced hereunder and it reads thus:- “19. Power to arrest.— (1) If the Director, Deputy Director, Assistant Director or any other officer authorised in this behalf by the Central Government by general or special order, has on the basis of material in his possession, reason to believe (that reason for such belief to be recorded in writing) that any person has been guilty of an offence punishable under this Act, he may arrest such person and shall, as soon as may be, inform him of the grounds for such arrest. (2) The Director, Deputy Director, Assistant Director or any other officer shall, immediately after arrest of such person under sub-section (1), forward a copy of the order along with the material in his possession, referred to in that sub-section, to the Adjudicating Authority, in a sealed envelope, in the manner, as may be prescribed and such Adjudicating Authority shall keep such order and material for such period, as may be prescribed. (3) Every person arrested under sub-section (1) shall, within twenty-four hours, be taken to a 2[Special Court or] Judicial Magistrate or a Metropolitan Magistrate, as the case may be, having jurisdiction: Provided that the period of twenty-four hours shall exclude the time necessary for the journey from the place of arrest to the 1[Special Court or] Magistrate’s Court.” 10. Sub-section (1) of Section 19 states that “the concerned officer has on the basis of material in his possession, reason to believe that any person has been guilty of an offence punishable under PMLA may arrest such person and shall inform him of the grounds for such arrest.” The crucial words occurring in this Sub-section are ” has on the basis of material in his possession “. Thus, it is the satisfaction of the Authorized Officer to arrive at the reasons to believe that a person is guilty on the basis of material in his possession (emphasis supplied). 11. In the present case, on the basis of the material in possession with the Authorized Officer, he has infact under the provisions of Section 50 filed an Application for the third time before the Special Court on 04.07.2026 to record the Petitioner’s statements as contemplated by PMLA. It is seen that by the remand order dated 04.07.2026 by which Petitioner’s judicial custody was extended till 18.07.2026, it is recorded that learned Advocate for the accused has not objected for judicial custody. Remand order dated 04.07.2026 is appended at Exhibit – ‘P’ at page No.844 of the Petition. 12. At this juncture, it will be worthwhile to note and reproduce the provisions of Section 50 of the PMLA which concern powers of authorities regarding summons, production of documents and to give evidence, etc.. Section 50 of PMLA reads thus:- “50. Powers of authorities regarding summons, production of documents and to give evidence, etc. ” (1) The Director shall, for the purposes of section 12, have the same powers as are vested in a civil Court under the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 (5 of 1908) while trying a suit in respect of the following matters, namely:- (a) discovery and inspection; (b) enforcing the attendance of any person, including any officer of a [reporting entity] and examining him on oath; (c) compelling the production of records; (d) receiving evidence on affidavits; (e) issuing commissions for examination of witnesses and documents; and (f) any other matter which may be prescribed. (2) The Director, Additional Director, Joint Director, Deputy Director or Assistant Director shall have power to summon any person whose attendance he considers necessary whether to give evidence or to produce any records during the course of any investigation or proceeding under this Act. (3) All the persons so summoned shall be bound to attend in person or through authorised agents, as such officer may direct, and shall be bound to state the truth upon any subject respecting which they are examined or make statements, and produce such documents as may be required. (4) Every proceeding under sub-sections (2) and (3) shall be deemed to be a judicial proceeding within the meaning of section 193 and section 228 of the Indian Penal Code (45 of 1860). (5) Subject to any rules made in this behalf by the Central Government, any officer referred to in sub-section (2) may impound and retain in his custody for such period, as he thinks fit, any records produced before him in any proceedings under this Act: Provided that an Assistant Director or a Deputy Director shall not- (a) impound any records without recording his reasons for so doing; or (b) retain in his custody any such records for a period exceeding three months, without obtaining the previous approval of the 2[Joint Director].” 13. Sub-section (2) of Section 50 states that the Authorized Officer shall have power to summon any person whose attendance he considers necessary whether to give evidence or to produce any record during the course of any investigation or proceeding under PMLA. Sub-section (3) states that all persons so summoned shall be bound to attend in person or through authorized agent as such officer may direct and shall be bound to be examined or make statements and produce such documents as may be required. 14. In view of the above provisions, when the Application dated 04.07.2026 appended at page No.833 is seen, it specifically reads that it is made under Section 50(2) and (3) of the PMLA qua the Petitioner. In paragraph No.18 of the said Application, it is stated that in statement dated 09.03.2026, Petitioner admitted that over the past few years he was using bank accounts in his name and in the names of his family members for depositing cash and funds generated through his hawala operations, and that these accounts were also used for routing cash through a cheque-discounting mechanism wherein, in exchange for cash, he procured Pay Orders issued from bank accounts maintained in the names of shell/ dummy entities with the said Bank, which were further utilized for acquisition of properties beneficially owned by him. 15. Kedar Dighe, Ashish Desai and others, and he has purposefully disposed of / sold many immovable properties with the sole intent of layering and laundering the proceeds of crime and hiding the same from legal proceedings such as attachment. 16. As a sequitur of these reasons, in paragraph No.29, Respondent No.1’s case is that the Respondent No.1 – ED requires to question the Petitioner and therefore prays for permission to record the statement of the Petitioner. This Application is allowed by the Special Court on the same day vide order dated 04.07.2026. 18. Thus, it is seen that the Application made by Respondent No.1 under the provisions of Section 50 and more specifically for compliance with Sections 50(2) and (3) having been allowed itself shows that the Respondent No.1 has invoked the said statutory provision to confront and question the Petitioner with the material which it has in its possession.It is an admitted fact that from the date of Petitioner’s arrest i.e. 31.03.2024 and the ECIR having been filed, Respondent No.1 collected material and recorded various statements which according to them, were required to be confronted with the Petitioner. It is also an admitted position which has been duly and candidly admitted by the learned Advocate for ED during his submissions that between 22.05.2026(Petitioner’s 5th statement recorded) and 23.06.2026 (when Petitioner was arrested), there was no new, incriminating, or extraneous material or development which was unearthed by Respondent No.1 on the basis of which the Petitioner was arrested. He would however justify this issue by arguing that power under Section 19 is such that once there is satisfaction of the Authorized Officer at any stage and he has reasons to believe that any person is guilty of an offence, he can arrest such person by following the procedure prescribed therein, notwithstanding the fact that, at the time of arrest any other statutory procedural requirement invoked by the Authorized Officer / Agency may have remained pending. I respectfully disagree with this submission made by the learned Advocate for ED for the simple reason that power to arrest is a very drastic and extreme action. In fact, sub-section (1) of Section 19 specifically uses the word ‘may arrest’ which is again discretionary. Further power to investigate, summon and confront exercised under Section 50(2) and (3) is a statutory power which if invoked cannot be simply wished away, since invoking this power, the Authorised Officer has already made up his mind to investigate and question the accused person on the basis of the very same material in his possession and custody. 19. However, in the present case, once a very valuable statutory power having been invoked by Respondent No.1 under Section 50 and more specifically under Section 50(2) and (3), then it cannot be open to Respondent No.1 to forego the same and notwithstanding completion of that action invoke Section 19 to arrest the Petitioner, unless a very strong and fresh circumstance and material incriminating the Petitioner is placed on the record before the Court and the Court feels that arrest is absolutely imminent on the basis of such material and records its reasons to believe on the basis of such new found material. This is primarily because sub-section (4) of Section 50 categorically states that every proceeding under sub-sections (2) and (3) shall be deemed to be a judicial proceeding within the meaning of Section 193 and Section 228 of the Indian Penal Code, 1860 (for short ‘IPC’). Section 193 of the IPC reads thus:- “193. Punishment for false evidence.— Whoever intentionally gives false evidence in any stage of a judicial proceeding, or fabricates false evidence for the purpose of being used in any stage of a judicial proceeding, shall be punished with imprisonment of either description for a term which may extend to seven years, and shall also be liable to fine; and whoever intentionally gives or fabricates false evidence in any other case, shall be punished with imprisonment of either description for a term which may extend to three years, and shall also be liable to fine. Explanation 1.— A trial before a Court-martial is a judicial proceeding. Explanation 2.— An investigation directed by law preliminary to a proceeding before a Court of Justice, is a stage of a judicial proceeding, though that investigation may not take place before a Court of Justice.” Illustration A, in an enquiry before a Magistrate for the purpose of ascertaining whether Z ought to be committed for trial, makes on oath a statement which he knows to be false. As this enquiry is a stage of a judicial proceeding, A has given false evidence. Explanation 3.— An investigation directed by a Court of Justice according to law, and conducted under the authority of a Court of Justice, is a stage of a judicial proceeding, though that investigation may not take place before a Court of Justice. Illustration A, in any enquiry before an officer deputed by a Court of Justice to ascertain on the spot the boundaries of land, makes on oath a statement which he knows to be false. As this enquiry is a stage of a judicial proceeding. A has given false evidence. 20. Explanation 2 and Explanation 3 to Section 193 prima facie apply to the present case. This is so because Respondent No.1 has chosen to invoke the provisions of Section 50(2) and (3) readwith (3) on the basis of the very same material in its possession to interrogate and record the statement of Petitioner which is the same material for effecting his arrest. Therefore, once this satisfaction stands recorded by the first Application filed by Respondent No.1 on 23.06.2026 and the said Application having been allowed by a judicial order of the Special Court, unless and until there is prima facie fresh incriminating material or such supervening circumstances which were not there before the Authorised Officer earlier and which came to the knowledge of Respondent No.1 later, Respondent No.1 cannot abandon the exercise which has been permitted by Court by order dated 23.06.2026 and simultaneously invoke the provisions of Section 19 to arrest the Petitioner by furnishing him the ground of arrest on the basis of the very same material which was already in possession of Respondent No.1. It is seen that said remand period was further extended by fresh remand orders dated 29.06.2026 and 04.07.2026 on the basis of very same material. 21. Though Mr. Pendse has persuaded me and taken me through the grounds of arrest to argue the gravity of the offence concerned, that will not deter me from linking the said gravity of the offence to the issue which I am required to decide in this Petition, once it is brought to my notice that Respondent No.1 had made up its mind and obtained a judicial order from the Court to investigate, confront and record further statement of the Petitioner which was allowed by the Court. It cannot be lost sight of that prior to this Petitioner has recorded his statements on 38 different occasions for over 18 months and therefore based on the material disclosed by his further statements were to be recorded for which the Application dated 04.07.2026 who made and allowed by the Court. It is also prima facie evident from the material placed on record that no new material whatsoever has surfaced between 22.05.2026 and 23.06.2026 so as to take the drastic step of arresting the Petitioner without recording his statement. This has been accepted by Respondent No.1 also during the course of arguments, but it is its contention that the gravity of the offence can authorise such arrest. 22. In the aforesaid background, this Court is called upon to judicially review the legality of the arrest of the Petitioner. It is seen that power to arrest under Section 19(1) is not for the purpose of investigation as held in paragraph No.34 of the decision in the case of Radhika Agarwal (supra). Power to arrest can and should wait and the power in terms of Section 19(1) of the PMLA can be exercised only when the material with the designated officer enables him to form an opinion by recording reasons in writing that the arrestee is guilty. In the present case, when the designated officer has himself filed the first Application dated 23.06.2026 wherein he himself, in paragraph No.31(i) to 31(v), calls upon the Court to allow him to examine, confront the Petitioner with the material in his possession and record his statement on the basis of the submissions made in paragraph Nos.22 to 26 of the said Application appended at Exhibit – ‘J’ at page No.489 of Petition, then in such a case, by foregoing the exercise of recording the statements, Respondent No.1 cannot arrest the Petitioner. Such exercise of power of arrest shall amount to a colourable exercise of power on the part of Respondent No.1 – ED. 23. It is seen that Petitioner’s 5thstatement was recorded on 15.05.2026. It is seen that despite having period of more than a month, no attempt was made to question the Petitioner before he was arrested on 23.06.2026. In that view of the matter, Petitioner’s non-cooperation or evasiveness would never arise, rather there would be a question mark on the conduct of the Respondent No.1. The Application dated 23.06.2026, does not refer to any supervening circumstances and hence, there was no need and necessity to arrest the Petitioner at all. 24. It is pertinent to note that the special Acts are enacted to achieve specific purposes and objectives. The power of judicial review in cases of arrest under such special Acts should be exercised very cautiously and in rare circumstances to balance individual liberty with the interest of justice and of the society at large. Any liberal approach in construing the stringent provisions of the special Acts may frustrate the very purpose and objective of the Acts. It hardly needs to be stated that the offences under the PMLA or the Customs Act or FERA are the offences of very serious nature affecting the financial systems and in turn the sovereignty and integrity of the nation. The provisions contained in the said Acts therefore must be construed in the manner which would enhance the objectives of the Acts, and not frustrate the same. Frequent or casual interference of the courts in the functioning of the authorised officers who have been specially conferred with the powers to combat the serious crimes, may embolden the unscrupulous elements to commit such crimes and may not do justice to the victims, who in such cases would be the society at large and the nation itself. With the advancement in technology, the very nature of crimes has become more and more intricate and complicated. Hence, minor procedural lapse on the part of authorised officers may not be seen with magnifying glass by the courts in exercise of the powers of judicial review, which may ultimately end up granting undue advantage or benefit to the person accused of very serious offences under the special Acts.

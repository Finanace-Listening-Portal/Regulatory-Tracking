Summary: GST on education in India depends on the nature of the service, the status of the supplier and recipient, the course or qualification offered, and the conditions prescribed under the applicable exemption notifications. Entry 66 of Notification No. 12/2017–Central Tax (Rate) exempts specified educational services, but the relief does not automatically extend to commercial coaching, every outsourced service or all digital learning programmes. Affiliation fees, examination and admission services, vocational training, hostel and mess facilities, electronic books, charitable activities and educational donations require separate examination under the relevant provisions. Recent clarifications concerning affiliation services and NSDC-linked vocational training have added important compliance considerations. Educational institutions must also evaluate input tax credit restrictions and apportionment requirements under Sections 17(2) and 17(5) of the CGST Act and Rules 42 and 43. Cross-border tutoring, online learning and student-placement arrangements raise additional questions concerning export eligibility, place of supply and intermediary status under the IGST Act, 2017. A transaction-specific assessment of the applicable exemption entry, contractual arrangement and relevant legal developments is therefore essential to determine GST liability and minimise compliance disputes. While recognised educational institutions may qualify for exemption under Entry 66 of Notification No. 12/2017–Central Tax (Rate), the applicability of GST depends on the nature of the service, the status of the supplier, the course offered and the specific conditions prescribed under the exemption framework. The ICAI’s GST Sectoral Guide on Education and Training Services, published in September 2026, examines the statutory provisions, exemption entries, judicial pronouncements, advance rulings and practical compliance challenges affecting this sector. It also addresses contentious areas such as affiliation fees, outsourced services, vocational training, online education, export of services and input tax credit restrictions. 1. Recognised Educational Institutions Under GST: Understanding the Scope of Entry 66 Entry 66 of Notification No. 12/2017–Central Tax (Rate) provides exemption for specified services relating to education. However, the exemption is not available to every institution that provides educational or training services. The institution must satisfy the prescribed definition, which covers pre-school education and education up to higher secondary level, education forming part of a curriculum leading to a qualification recognised by law, and approved vocational education courses. The distinction between recognised education and commercial coaching is therefore crucial in determining GST liability. Institutions must examine the nature of the course, the qualification offered and the applicable exemption conditions before treating their services as exempt. A claim based merely on the fact that the activity involves teaching or training may not be sufficient. 2. Outsourced Services to Schools, Colleges and Universities: Where Does the Exemption End? Entry 66(b) of Notification No. 12/2017–Central Tax (Rate) provides exemption for specified services supplied to educational institutions, including transportation, catering, security, cleaning and housekeeping. However, this exemption is subject to important restrictions. The exemption for transportation, catering and security or housekeeping services is limited to institutions providing pre-school education and education up to higher secondary level. Consequently, similar services supplied to colleges and universities may not qualify for the same relief. The distinction becomes particularly important where educational institutions outsource their operations to third-party service providers. For instance, catering services supplied to a school may qualify for exemption, whereas similar services provided to a university may attract GST, subject to the applicable provisions. Therefore, service providers must examine the category of the recipient institution and the specific exemption entry rather than assuming that every service connected with education is exempt. 3. Affiliation Fees Under GST: Government Schools, Private Schools and Universities The GST treatment of affiliation fees depends on the nature of the educational body providing the service and the category of institution receiving it. Entry 66A of Notification No. 12/2017–Central Tax (Rate), inserted with effect from 10 October 2024, provides an exemption for specified affiliation services supplied by Central or State educational boards and similar bodies to Government schools. This relief is not extended in the same manner to private schools, making the status of the recipient institution a critical factor. University affiliation fees have also generated interpretational disputes. CBIC Circular No. 234/28/2024-GST dated 11 October 2024 clarifies the tax treatment of affiliation services provided by universities, generally treating them as taxable at 18%. However, differing High Court views have added complexity to the issue. Educational bodies and institutions must therefore examine the applicable exemption entry, the nature of the affiliation service and relevant judicial developments before determining GST liability. 4. Examination and Admission Services: Identifying the Boundaries of GST Exemption Entry 66 of Notification No. 12/2017–Central Tax (Rate) provides exemption for specified examination and admission-related services. Services supplied by eligible educational institutions for conducting entrance examinations, including the collection of entrance fees, may qualify for exemption under Entry 66(aa). Further, services received by educational institutions from third-party providers in relation to admission or the conduct of examinations may be exempt under Entry 66(b)(iv), subject to the prescribed conditions. However, the exemption is not available to every examination-related activity. The status of the organisation conducting the examination, the nature of the service and its connection with admission or examination activities must be examined. Accreditation, registration and professional certification services may receive different GST treatment. Therefore, institutions and service providers must distinguish between exempt examination services and other taxable activities before determining their GST obligations. 5. Coaching Centres and EdTech Platforms: Why Education Does Not Always Mean GST Exemption Coaching and test-preparation services, including preparation for competitive examinations such as UPSC, NEET, JEE and CA, generally do not qualify for the exemption available to recognised educational institutions. Since these services ordinarily do not form part of a curriculum leading to a qualification recognised by law, they are generally taxable at 18% GST. Similarly, online learning subscriptions and executive certificate programmes require separate examination of their nature and applicable exemption conditions. The key issue is distinguishing recognised education from commercial coaching and digital learning services. Merely providing instruction, training or educational content does not automatically qualify a supplier for exemption under Entry 66 of Notification No. 12/2017–Central Tax (Rate). Educational businesses must assess the course structure, recognition of the qualification and nature of the supply before determining the applicable GST treatment. 6. Vocational Training and NSDC-Linked Services: Understanding Entries 69, 70 and 71 GST law provides exemptions for specified vocational training, skill-development and assessment services under Entries 69, 70 and 71 of Notification No. 12/2017–Central Tax (Rate), subject to the applicable conditions. Eligibility depends on factors such as the nature of the training, the recognition or approval of the course, and the status of the training provider. Therefore, not every skill-development programme or vocational course automatically qualifies for exemption. Changes relating to the National Council for Vocational Education and Training (NCVET) and NSDC-linked training partners have created important compliance considerations. The ICAI’s September 2026 Guide discusses the restoration of relief for eligible NSDC partners from 16 January 2025 and CBIC Circular No. 245/02/2025-GST, which addresses the intervening period through an “as is where is” approach. Training providers must carefully examine the relevant dates and eligibility conditions before determining their GST liability. 7. GST on Hostel, Mess and Bundled Educational Services: Identifying the Correct Tax Treatment Hostel accommodation, mess facilities and other services provided by educational institutions require careful examination under GST. Under Entry 66(a) of Notification No. 12/2017–Central Tax (Rate), services supplied by an eligible educational institution to its students, faculty and staff may qualify for exemption. However, where catering or mess services are outsourced to an independent contractor, the tax treatment may differ depending on the nature of the supply and the applicable exemption provisions. Another important consideration is whether multiple services supplied together constitute a composite supply or a mixed supply. The contractual arrangement, the principal supply and the manner in which consideration is charged can influence the GST treatment. Educational institutions should therefore assess each arrangement separately rather than assuming that hostel, food and allied services automatically share the exemption applicable to core educational services. 8. E-Books, Charitable Trusts and Educational Donations: Exemption or Taxable Supply? The GST treatment of books and educational donations depends on the nature of the supply and the conditions prescribed under the applicable provisions. Printed books are generally exempt from GST, whereas qualifying electronic books may attract GST at 5%. Educational institutions and publishers must therefore distinguish between printed and electronic publications while determining the applicable tax treatment. Similarly, charitable trusts and non-profit organisations cannot claim a blanket GST exemption merely because they hold registration under Sections 12AA or 12AB of the Income-tax Act. The actual nature of their activities and the existence of a supply for consideration must be examined. Genuine donations without a corresponding benefit may be treated differently from commercial sponsorships involving promotional benefits, making the underlying arrangement crucial for determining GST liability. 9. Input Tax Credit in the Education Sector: Applying Sections 17(2) and 17(5), and Rules 42–43 Educational institutions providing both exempt and taxable services must carefully examine their eligibility to claim Input Tax Credit (ITC). Under Section 17(2) of the CGST Act, where goods or services are used partly for taxable supplies and partly for exempt supplies, ITC is restricted to the portion attributable to taxable supplies. Rules 42 and 43 prescribe the mechanism for apportionment and reversal of credit relating to inputs, input services and capital goods. Institutions must also consider the restrictions under Section 17(5), which blocks credit on specified goods and services, subject to applicable exceptions. The coexistence of exempt educational services and taxable activities, such as certain coaching programmes or commercial facilities, can make ITC computation particularly complex. Proper classification of expenses, maintenance of supporting records and periodic reconciliation are therefore essential to minimise compliance risks and disputes with the GST authorities. 10. Export of Educational Services and Cross-Border Online Learning: Taxability, Intermediary Status and Compliance Risks Educational institutions and training providers supplying services to overseas clients must carefully evaluate whether their transactions qualify as exports of services under the IGST Act, 2017. The applicable treatment depends on conditions such as the location of the supplier and recipient, place of supply, receipt of consideration and whether the supplier and recipient are merely establishments of the same person. Services involving overseas university recruitment or student placement also require scrutiny to determine whether the provider acts as a principal service provider or an intermediary. Cross-border tutoring, digital subscriptions and online educational platforms present additional classification and place-of-supply challenges. The contractual arrangement, actual nature of the service and role performed by each party are important in determining GST liability. A transaction involving a foreign recipient does not automatically qualify as an export, making a case-specific assessment essential for claiming zero-rated treatment and avoiding future disputes.

Summary: Three significant Supreme Court decisions delivered in 2026 clarify important aspects of the Goods and Services Tax framework concerning input tax credit, extended limitation and export refunds. In Bhandari Scrap Traders v. Union of India, decided on 24 July 2026, the Court upheld the constitutional validity of Section 16(2)(c) of the CGST Act and declined to read down the requirement that tax charged by the supplier must actually be paid to the Government for the recipient to claim input tax credit. In Tata Steel Limited v. Union of India, decided on 25 August 2026, the Court held that an allegation of fraud, wilful misstatement or suppression cannot be mechanically inserted into a show cause notice merely to invoke the extended limitation period under Section 74. The foundational facts supporting such allegations must be evident from the notice itself, and statutory time limits governing both notices and adjudication orders must be observed. In Goodluck India Limited v. Union of India, decided on 6 August 2026, the Court held that the omission of Rule 96(10) of the CGST Rules without a saving clause applies to proceedings pending when the rule was omitted, thereby providing relief in disputes concerning IGST refunds on exports. These decisions demonstrate that statutory conditions governing ITC cannot be disregarded on general equitable grounds, while the Department must establish the legal and factual prerequisites for invoking extended limitation and cannot continue pending proceedings solely under an omitted restriction. Their practical significance lies in identifying the precise statutory issue, factual foundation, procedural status and scope of relief in each case rather than treating the rulings as universally favourable to either taxpayers or the Revenue. The decisions in Bhandari Scrap Traders, Tata Steel and Goodluck India deal with different provisions, but each turns on the same discipline—the statutory conditions must be applied as enacted, not enlarged or diluted to suit the circumstances of a particular dispute. These rulings cannot accurately be classified as a general victory either for taxpayers or for the Revenue. The Court has supported the statutory condition linking credit to payment of tax by the supplier, while rejecting an unsupported invocation of extended limitation and the continuation of pending proceedings under an omitted refund restriction. Their significance lies in the precise propositions decided and, equally, in the questions left open. 1. Bhandari Scrap Traders: Supplier’s Tax Payment Remains a Condition for ITC Bhandari Scrap Traders v. Union of India — order dated 24 July 2026. The issue Section 16(2)(c) of the Central Goods and Services Tax Act, 2017 links the recipient’s entitlement to input tax credit with actual payment to the Government of the tax charged on the supply. The difficulty is familiar: a purchaser may receive the goods, hold the invoice and pay the full consideration, including GST, but still face reversal of credit because the supplier has not discharged the corresponding tax liability. The challenge before the Court sought to question the constitutional validity of this condition or have it read down to protect a bona fide purchaser. The Gujarat High Court had declined both forms of relief. What the Supreme Court decided In its order dismissing the special leave petitions, the Supreme Court expressly agreed with the Gujarat High Court’s conclusion that no grounds had been established either to declare Section 16(2)(c) unconstitutional or to read down its provisions. The Court also noticed the statutory mechanism under which credit reversed on account of the supplier’s default may be re-availed after the supplier discharges the tax liability. The Court considered the distinction between the GST framework and the provisions of the earlier Delhi VAT law on which purchasers had relied. It did not accept that the protection developed under that VAT framework could simply be carried into Section 16(2)(c). The proper scope of the ruling The immediate consequence is that a purchaser cannot proceed on the assumption that payment of GST to the supplier necessarily satisfies every condition for credit. The statutory requirement concerning payment to the Government remains operative. Commercial bona fides, by themselves, do not justify rewriting that condition. The ruling must nevertheless be kept within its proper limits. A decision supporting the validity of Section 16(2)(c) does not, without more, establish that every departmental allegation of supplier default is factually correct. Nor does the constitutional conclusion itself determine the evidence, quantification, interest or penalty in each individual proceeding. The distinction is between the validity of the condition and its application to the transactions under examination. For businesses, the exposure is therefore not confined to invoice verification. Supplier-compliance monitoring and contractual protection against tax default assume greater importance, although neither can replace fulfilment of the statutory conditions. EY’s analysis of the decision likewise identifies vendor-level checks and contractual safeguards as relevant responses to this risk. 2. Tata Steel: Section 74 Cannot Be Invoked by Repeating the Language of Fraud M/s Tata Steel Limited v. Union of India through the Secretary, Ministry of Finance and others — 2026 INSC 920, decided on 25 August 2026. The dispute The proceedings concerned financial years 2018–19, 2019–20 and 2020–21. They arose from audit objections relating to input tax credit mismatches and short payment of tax. The show cause notice was issued on 13 June 2025 under Section 74. Tata Steel challenged the invocation of the extended period on the ground that the notice did not disclose the facts necessary to establish fraud, wilful misstatement or suppression. There was a further difficulty in the Department’s position. The record showed that the audit objection had itself been contested before the Public Accounts Committee. The notice was placed in the “call book”, or kept in abeyance, and subsequently revived with reference to a protective demand. An audit objection is not the statutory satisfaction The Supreme Court held that proceedings under Sections 73 and 74 require the satisfaction of the assessing officer. An objection raised in audit does not dispense with that requirement. For Section 74, the officer must be satisfied not merely that a mismatch or short payment exists, but that fraud, wilful misrepresentation or suppression led to it. That distinction is the substance of the decision. The existence of a tax difference and the existence of deliberate conduct attracting the extended period are not interchangeable propositions. In paragraph 14, the Court stated: “The foundational facts which led to the inference arrived at of fraud/willful misrepresentation/suppression should be evident from the notice itself.” The Court added that the relevant words could not be mechanically recited to enable recovery beyond the normal limitation period. In the case before it, the general statement that credit had been availed without documentary evidence and that facts had been suppressed did not supply the missing factual foundation. Limitation governs the order, not merely the commencement of correspondence The Department argued that proceedings had commenced before the Section 73 limitation expired. The Court rejected that answer. Section 73(10) prescribes the time for issuing the adjudication order, while Section 73(2) requires the notice to precede that deadline by the stipulated interval. Earlier correspondence could not, by itself, preserve proceedings beyond the statutory period. This aspect deserves attention in pending cases. A chronology containing audit letters, requests for records and replies does not answer the separate question whether the statutory notice and final order were issued within the applicable time limits. Relief and reservation The Supreme Court set aside both the show cause notice and the consequential order dated 26 December 2025. It nevertheless reserved liberty to the Department to initiate appropriate proceedings under Section 74, if considered warranted, provided the notice itself disclosed the foundational facts and the order was passed before 28 February 2027. That deadline formed part of the relief granted on the facts and limitation computation in this case; it should not be treated as a universal deadline for other taxpayers. The judgment did not pronounce that Tata Steel’s disputed credit was allowable on merits. It held that the proceedings, as framed, could not be sustained. For practitioners, the relevant inquiry is therefore not whether the notice contains the word “suppression”, but whether it identifies what was suppressed, how that conduct is attributed to the taxpayer and why it supports the extended period. The Court’s insistence is on particulars, not statutory vocabulary. 3. Goodluck India: Omission of Rule 96(10) Benefits Pending Proceedings M/s Goodluck India Limited and another v. Union of India and others and connected matters — 2026 INSC 821, decided on 6 August 2026. The controversy Rule 96(10) restricted the availability of the integrated-tax refund route where specified benefits had been availed in relation to supplies or imports. The rule was omitted by Notification No. 20/2024 with effect from 8 October 2024. The question before the Supreme Court was whether that omission benefited proceedings pending on the date of omission, or whether the restriction could continue to be applied in those proceedings. The Revenue relied on the GST Council’s recommendation that the omission should operate prospectively. The taxpayers relied on the absence of a saving clause preserving proceedings under the omitted rule. The notification, not the recommendation, controlled the result The Supreme Court held that the recommendation was advisory and did not bind the rule-making authority. What mattered was the legal instrument by which Rule 96(10) had been omitted. That instrument contained no saving clause or sunset provision preserving the pending proceedings. Applying the Constitution Bench decision in Kolhapur Canesugar Works Ltd. v. Union of India, the Court upheld the benefit of omission for proceedings pending when the rule ceased to operate. It also noted the stated reason for the change: Rule 96(10) had caused unnecessary complications without serving the intended benefit. The Revenue’s appeals were dismissed. The Court directed circulation of its order to the High Courts so that pending matters concerning this issue could be placed before the appropriate Benches for expeditious disposal. What the decision does—and does not—settle For a pending dispute resting on the restriction in Rule 96(10), the judgment provides a direct answer to the contention that the omitted rule must nevertheless continue to govern the proceeding. The absence of a saving provision was central to that answer. The decision should not, however, be described as an unconditional refund sanction for every exporter. It removes the particular restriction in the pending proceedings covered by the ruling; it does not establish every other factual and statutory requirement of a refund claim. Nor should the decision be presented as automatically reopening every concluded matter. The question decided concerned pending proceedings. Whether a particular case remained pending, and what relief follows in that procedural position, must be examined against its own record. A further point is often overlooked: the Court dismissed the assessees’ separate challenges to the vires of Rule 96(10) as infructuous in view of the relief flowing from its omission. The operative decision was therefore not a declaration that the rule had always been unconstitutional. 4. The Decisions Must Be Read Together, Not Reduced to Headlines Bhandari Scrap Traders and Tata Steel address different stages of a credit dispute. The first supports the statutory condition governing entitlement where the supplier has not paid tax. The second requires a factual basis before the Department can invoke the more serious allegations and extended limitation associated with Section 74. A failure to satisfy a credit condition does not, merely by that description, supply every fact necessary for an allegation of deliberate evasion. Goodluck India addresses a different question again: whether the legal restriction supporting a pending proceeding survived its omission. The Court’s answer depended on the absence of a saving clause, not on a general preference for retrospective taxpayer relief. These distinctions matter when preparing replies and appeals. A constitutional challenge to a credit condition, a challenge to the factual allegation of default, an objection to extended limitation and a plea based on omission of a rule are separate grounds. Combining them without identifying their respective legal foundations can obscure an otherwise sound case. 5. Consequences for Pending GST Matters The decisions suggest three immediate areas for review. First, supplier-default credit cases require a transaction-specific record. The constitutional argument against Section 16(2)(c) must be distinguished from the factual question whether the alleged default exists and the statutory mechanism for reversal and subsequent re-availment applies. Second, notices invoking Section 74 should be examined at the notice stage for their factual foundation. Tata Steel requires more than an allegation of a mismatch followed by a recital of fraud or suppression. The notice must disclose the facts supporting that inference, and the limitation analysis must address the deadline for the adjudication order as well as the notice. Third, pending export-refund matters founded on Rule 96(10) should be examined in light of Goodluck India. The relevant inquiry is whether the proceeding was pending when the omission took effect and whether the disputed restriction is the basis on which relief has been denied or recovery pursued. Conclusion The Court’s recent GST decisions demand precision from both sides. A taxpayer cannot replace an express credit condition with a general plea of fairness. Equally, the Department cannot replace the factual foundation for extended limitation with a formulaic allegation, or preserve an omitted restriction merely by referring to the policy recommendation that preceded the amendment. For professional practice, the task is to identify exactly what each decision governs: entitlement to credit, the lawful invocation of Section 74, or the survival of pending proceedings after omission of a rule. The strength of a reply or appeal will depend less on the number of judgments cited than on whether the proposition relied upon answers the defect in the particular proceeding.

Summary: Foreign collaboration helps Indian businesses obtain foreign investment, advanced technology, technical knowledge and access to international markets. However, such arrangements require careful examination of income-tax withholding on royalty and technical fees, GST on imported services under the reverse charge mechanism, and compliance with FEMA provisions. Businesses should review the collaboration agreement, applicable tax laws, treaty benefits and foreign exchange requirements before making payments. Proper documentation, timely reporting and professional advice can help avoid unnecessary tax disputes and regulatory difficulties. Keep the Agreement and Records Clear Conclusion Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); Introduction Foreign collaboration is an important way for Indian businesses to obtain new technology, technical knowledge, foreign investment and access to international markets. An Indian company may enter into an agreement with a foreign company for the use of a patent, trademark, software, manufacturing process or technical services. Sometimes the foreign company also invests money in the Indian business. The agreement may appear to be a commercial matter between two companies, but it also creates tax and regulatory responsibilities in India. In practice, the payment terms, nature of services and documents available with the Indian company become very important. A mistake at the time of signing the agreement or making payment may create problems later. In my view, foreign collaboration agreements should be examined from three important angles: income-tax withholding, GST on imported services and compliance under the Foreign Exchange Management Act (FEMA). 1. TDS on Royalty and Technical Fees One of the first questions is whether tax must be deducted when an Indian company pays royalty or technical fees to a foreign company. The answer depends on the nature of the payment, the applicable income-tax law, the provisions of the relevant Double Taxation Avoidance Agreement (DTAA), and the facts of the transaction. The name given to a payment in the agreement is not always conclusive. The actual rights granted and services provided must also be examined. For example, an Indian manufacturer may pay a foreign company for permission to use its trademark, patent or manufacturing technology. Another company may pay for technical consultancy, installation assistance or specialised services. These payments should not automatically be treated as identical. The agreement and the applicable tax provisions must be checked separately. The Indian company should examine the taxability of the payment in India before remitting the amount. Where withholding is applicable, the correct tax treatment should be determined before payment. The relevant DTAA may provide a different rate or conditions, subject to fulfilment of the applicable requirements. Documents such as the tax residency certificate, Form 10F where applicable, and evidence relating to the recipient’s eligibility for treaty benefits may be required. The company should also examine the applicable withholding provisions under the income-tax law in force for the relevant period. Another important issue is the remittance documentation. The requirements relating to Form 15CA and Form 15CB should be checked according to the nature and amount of the payment and the applicable rules. These forms should not be treated as a substitute for deciding whether the payment is taxable. A common practical mistake is to release the payment first and examine the tax consequences afterwards. The better approach is to review the agreement, determine the tax treatment and complete the required documentation before remittance. 2. GST on Services Received from a Foreign Company The second important issue is GST. An Indian company may receive technical support, consultancy, software access, engineering services or management assistance from a foreign collaborator. Under the Integrated Goods and Services Tax Act, 2017, the definition of import of services requires examination of the supplier’s location, the recipient’s location and the place of supply. Where the transaction qualifies as an import of services and is taxable, IGST may be payable under the reverse charge mechanism, subject to the applicable provisions and notifications. For example, if an Indian company receives technical consultancy from a foreign company for its manufacturing business, the Indian company should examine whether the service is an import of services and whether GST is payable under reverse charge. The company should not assume that GST is not applicable merely because the foreign supplier has not issued an Indian GST invoice. The Indian recipient may have its own compliance responsibility. The next question is whether input tax credit can be claimed for the IGST paid. This depends on the eligibility conditions under the GST law, the use of the service, the availability of proper records and any applicable restrictions. Payment of GST under reverse charge does not automatically mean that the entire amount is available as input tax credit. The accounting team should therefore maintain the foreign agreement, invoice or debit note, payment details, calculation of taxable value, evidence of tax payment and records supporting the input tax credit claim. The applicable reporting in the GST returns should also be checked. It is equally important to distinguish between different types of payments. Royalty for intellectual property, technical consultancy and reimbursement of expenses may require different factual and legal analysis. Their treatment should not be decided merely by looking at the description appearing in the foreign invoice. 3. FEMA and Foreign Investment Compliance The third area is FEMA compliance. Foreign collaboration may involve only a technical agreement, or it may also involve foreign investment in the Indian company. These are not the same arrangements and should be examined separately. Where a non-resident invests in an Indian company, the business must examine the applicable foreign investment policy, sectoral conditions, pricing requirements and reporting obligations. The permitted route may depend on the sector, investor and nature of the transaction. Where the agreement involves payment of royalty, technical fees or other amounts to a foreign collaborator, the company should also check the applicable foreign exchange rules and the requirements of its authorised dealer bank. The agreement, invoice and purpose of remittance should be consistent with the actual transaction. If shares or other eligible securities are issued to a foreign investor, the applicable reporting requirements must be examined. The company should not assume that signing the agreement completes all regulatory formalities. Reporting may be required after the relevant transaction takes place. The payment schedule should also be reviewed. The agreement may provide for an advance, a lump-sum technical fee, recurring royalty or payment linked to sales. Each payment should be supported by the relevant contractual terms and records. Failure to follow the applicable FEMA requirements may result in regulatory difficulties and possible penalties. Businesses should therefore consult their authorised dealer bank and obtain professional advice where the transaction requires it. 4. Keep the Agreement and Records Clear In my opinion, the most useful step is to examine the agreement before signing it. The agreement should clearly explain what technology or rights are being provided, what services will be performed, how the payment will be calculated and who will bear applicable taxes. The business should also consider whether the payment is linked to production, sales, use of intellectual property or a fixed technical service fee. These details may affect the tax analysis. The accounts team should keep one complete file containing the signed agreement, amendments, invoices, correspondence, tax working papers, remittance documents and proof of applicable compliance. The records should be reconciled with the books of account and bank payments. Where the foreign collaborator and Indian company are related parties, transfer-pricing requirements may also need examination. The company should not overlook this issue simply because the payment is made under a technical collaboration agreement. Conclusion Foreign collaboration can help Indian businesses grow, but the agreement should not be treated as only a commercial document. TDS, GST and FEMA requirements must be examined according to the actual transaction and the law applicable at that time. Businesses should take tax and regulatory advice before making foreign payments, maintain proper records and complete the required reporting within the prescribed time. A little care at the beginning can prevent avoidable disputes and compliance problems later. This article is for general information. The treatment of an individual transaction depends on the agreement, facts and applicable law.

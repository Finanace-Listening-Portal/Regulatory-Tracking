Summary: Export of services from India to Dubai and other parts of the UAE may qualify as zero-rated supplies under the Integrated Goods and Services Tax (IGST) Act, 2017, provided all statutory conditions are satisfied. The supplier must be located in India, the recipient must be located outside India, the place of supply must be outside India, payment must be received in convertible foreign exchange or Indian rupees wherever permitted by the Reserve Bank of India, and the supplier and recipient must not merely be establishments of the same legal person. A foreign customer’s address and receipt of foreign currency alone do not establish export eligibility. Particular attention must be given to the place-of-supply provisions, intermediary classification, services connected with immovable property, events, recruitment, marketing and other cross-border arrangements. The applicable legal position must also be examined with reference to the relevant supply period and statutory amendments. Eligible registered exporters can generally supply services without payment of IGST under a valid Letter of Undertaking (LUT) and claim refunds of eligible unutilised input tax credit, subject to prescribed conditions. Proper export invoices, foreign inward remittance evidence, timely realisation of export proceeds, accurate GST return reporting and reconciliation of refund claims are essential. Indian businesses providing consulting, software development, digital marketing, accounting, recruitment and other services to UAE customers should review their contracts and actual operating arrangements to establish genuine export eligibility, minimise GST disputes and protect refund claims. The customer is outside India, so the transaction must be a GST-free export—correct? Not necessarily. Under GST, the customer’s UAE address and receipt of foreign currency are important, but they do not independently make a service an export. The transaction must satisfy every condition prescribed for an “export of services.” If even one condition fails, the supply may become taxable in India despite being described commercially as an overseas project. This distinction is particularly relevant to Indian businesses serving Dubai and the wider UAE. Technology support, consulting, digital marketing, recruitment, design, accounting, market research and commission-based services can look similar on an invoice while receiving very different GST treatment. A defensible zero-rating position begins with the contract and operating model—not with the words “export invoice” added after the service has been completed. Zero-rated does not mean exempt Exports of services are treated as zero-rated supplies under the Integrated Goods and Services Tax framework. This treatment allows an eligible exporter to make the supply without charging output GST under a Letter of Undertaking and, subject to the prescribed conditions, claim a refund of unutilised input tax credit. An exempt supply works differently. Input tax credit connected with exempt supplies is generally restricted or may require reversal. In contrast, input tax credit can ordinarily be retained for genuine zero-rated supplies, subject to the general credit conditions. Zero-rating is therefore more favourable than an ordinary exemption. However, it is available only when the transaction legally qualifies as an export. Five conditions that must be satisfied 1. A service supplied from India to a UAE customer qualifies as an export only when all the following conditions are met: 2. The supplier of the service is located in India. 3. The recipient of the service is located outside India. 4. The place of supply is outside India. 5. Payment is received in convertible foreign exchange or in Indian rupees wherever permitted by the Reserve Bank of India. 6. The supplier and recipient are not merely establishments of the same legal person treated as distinct establishments. These are cumulative conditions. Satisfying four out of five is not sufficient. A service provider should therefore test every material UAE contract against all five requirements before issuing a zero-rated invoice. Location of the Indian supplier The first condition is usually straightforward. It asks whether the supplier of the service is located in India for GST purposes. For an Indian company, partnership, LLP, proprietorship or professional establishment performing and invoicing the service from India, the supplier will ordinarily be located in India. The analysis can become more complicated where: The supplier has establishments in both India and the UAE; A Dubai branch negotiates the contract while an Indian office performs the work; Employees work from multiple locations; An Indian company operates through a fixed establishment abroad; or The invoice is raised by an entity different from the establishment actually supplying the service. The establishment most directly concerned with supplying the service should be identified. The place where the invoice is generated is not always decisive. The recipient must be located outside India A UAE address on the contract is useful evidence, but the commercial reality must support it. The recipient is generally the person contractually entitled to receive the service and responsible for paying the consideration. This may be established through: Service agreement; Purchase order; Statement of work; UAE trade license; Recipient’s tax registration details; Commercial correspondence; Invoice; Bank remittance information; and Evidence showing who consumed and controlled the service. Difficulties arise where a UAE parent company signs the contract but the service is actually ordered, controlled and used by its Indian subsidiary. Similarly, an Indian group company should not be recharacterised as a UAE recipient merely because payment is routed through a Dubai entity. The agreement, work instructions, deliverables and payment arrangement should all identify the same commercial recipient. Place of supply is often the decisive condition The third condition causes the largest number of export disputes. Where either the supplier or recipient is outside India, the general rule ordinarily places the supply at the recipient’s location. If an Indian consultant supplies an independent advisory service to a Dubai company, the place of supply may therefore be outside India. However, several categories are governed by special place-of-supply rules. Depending on the service, the place of supply may be determined by: Location where goods are physically made available; Location where an individual is physically present; Location of immovable property; Place where an event is held; Location of the supplier in specified cases; Place where transportation terminates; Location connected with banking or account-holder services; or Other specific statutory criteria. The general “foreign customer means foreign place of supply” assumption should not be applied before checking whether a special rule covers the transaction. Services connected with UAE immovable property Services directly connected with immovable property generally follow the location of that property. For example, an Indian architect designing a building in Dubai, or an Indian consultant advising directly on a particular UAE property, may have a place of supply outside India because the property is situated in the UAE. However, the connection must be sufficiently direct. General business consulting for a UAE real-estate company is not automatically a property-related service merely because the customer owns buildings. The nature of the contractual obligation should be examined rather than relying only on the customer’s industry. Events, exhibitions and conferences in Dubai Indian businesses frequently provide services relating to exhibitions, conferences and corporate events held in Dubai. The GST result depends on the precise service. Services involving admission to, or organisation of, an event may be linked to the place where the event is actually held. If the qualifying event is held in Dubai, the place of supply may be outside India. On the other hand, a remote marketing, design or technology service supplied to the event organiser may require analysis under the general rule. Calling every assignment “event support” is not enough. The agreement should clearly identify whether the supplier is organising the event, arranging admission, providing an ancillary service or supplying an independent service on its own account. The intermediary risk Intermediary classification is one of the most important GST risks for Indian businesses working with UAE customers. An intermediary broadly arranges or facilitates a supply between two or more persons without supplying the main goods, services or securities on its own account. Typical risk situations include an Indian person who: Finds Indian customers for a Dubai company; Introduces buyers and sellers; Coordinates negotiations between two parties; Receives commission when a third-party transaction is completed; Facilitates orders for a UAE principal; or Acts as a broker or agent without assuming responsibility for the main supply. Where the Indian supplier is treated as an intermediary under the applicable provisions, the place of supply may be in India. The transaction can then fail the export condition even though the customer is located in Dubai and the fee is received in foreign currency. The commercial substance of the arrangement is more important than labels such as “consultant,” “marketing partner” or “business development adviser.” Independent service or intermediary service? Consider two Indian businesses working with the same Dubai client. The first business is engaged to conduct market research and deliver a UAE expansion report. It decides its methodology, performs the research and supplies the completed report directly to the client for an agreed fee. It does not arrange any third-party transaction. The second business identifies Indian purchasers for the Dubai client’s products, introduces the parties and receives a percentage of every successful order. The first business is more likely to be supplying a service on its own account. The second presents a stronger intermediary risk because its role is connected with facilitating supplies between the Dubai company and Indian customers. The following questions can help distinguish the two: Is the Indian supplier responsible for delivering an independent service? Are there three identifiable parties in the commercial arrangement? Is another supply being arranged or facilitated? Is payment linked to a third-party transaction? Can the service provider determine how the work is performed? Does it assume responsibility for the principal deliverable? Is the supplier merely introducing, coordinating or negotiating for another party? A carefully written agreement is helpful, but it must reflect the actual conduct of the parties. Support services supplied on the provider’s own account Not every outsourced activity is an intermediary service. An Indian business may provide back-office support, software development, customer assistance, data processing, accounting, design or research to a UAE customer on a principal-to-principal basis. If the Indian supplier performs the agreed work on its own account and does not arrange a separate supply between the UAE customer and a third party, it may not be an intermediary merely because its work supports the customer’s overseas business. The scope of work should clearly describe: Deliverables; Responsibility of the Indian supplier; Service standards; Pricing method; Ownership of work product; Relationship between the parties; and Absence of authority to bind the UAE customer, where factually correct. Generic phrases such as “facilitation,” “liaison” or “coordination” should not be used carelessly where they do not accurately describe the service. The distinct-establishment restriction A supply between establishments of the same legal person may fail to qualify as an export of services. For example, an Indian head office providing services to its own Dubai branch is not in the same position as an Indian company supplying services to a separately incorporated UAE subsidiary. A branch ordinarily forms part of the same legal entity. A subsidiary is a separate legal person, even if it is wholly owned by the Indian company. This distinction requires careful analysis because a transaction between separate companies may qualify as an export, while an internal allocation between an Indian office and its own foreign branch may fail the statutory export definition. Related-party valuation and transfer-pricing considerations may also arise when an Indian company supplies services to a separately incorporated UAE group company. Receipt of export proceeds Payment for exported services must be received in convertible foreign exchange or in Indian rupees where receipt in rupees is permitted by the Reserve Bank of India. Indian exporters commonly receive payment from UAE customers in: US dollars; UAE dirhams; Euros; Pounds sterling; or Another freely convertible currency. The payment should be received through a permitted banking channel and should be traceable to the relevant invoice. Receipt in Indian rupees does not automatically disqualify the transaction, but the exporter must establish that the receipt mechanism is permitted under the applicable RBI framework. A customer simply transferring rupees from an unrelated Indian account may not satisfy the condition. Payment by a third party A payment may sometimes be received from a group company, payment platform or another person on behalf of the UAE customer. Such arrangements deserve additional documentation. The exporter should be able to connect: Contractual recipient; Invoice; Person making payment; Bank advice; Purpose of remittance; and Relationship between the payer and the recipient. Where the payment is received from an Indian entity while the invoice is addressed to a Dubai company, the transaction can attract questions about the real recipient and the permitted mode of receipt. The contract should address third-party payment where it is commercially expected. Supporting confirmation from the UAE customer may also be necessary. Bank evidence and remittance records Indian service exporters should preserve reliable banking evidence. Depending on the banking channel and transaction, this may include: Foreign Inward Remittance Certificate; Bank realisation advice; Electronic bank realisation certificate, where applicable; SWIFT or remittance message; Bank statement; Purpose code; Invoice-level remittance reconciliation; and Correspondence explaining deductions or payment differences. A credit entry in the bank statement is useful but may not prove every element of the export. The documentation should identify the sender, currency, invoice and purpose of payment. Time limit for realising payment Where services are exported without payment of integrated tax under a Letter of Undertaking, the exporter is expected to realise payment within the period prescribed under Rule 96A, subject to any extension permitted by the competent authority. Broadly, payment should be received within one year from the date of the export invoice unless a longer period is permitted. If the export proceeds are not realised within the applicable period, the exporter may become liable to pay the tax due with interest in accordance with Rule 96A. The zero-rating position should therefore be monitored after filing the return; it is not completed merely because the original invoice was reported as an export. Businesses with long collection cycles should maintain an ageing report specifically for export invoices. Letter of Undertaking A registered person intending to export services without payment of integrated tax generally furnishes a Letter of Undertaking in Form GST RFD-11 on the GST portal. The LUT is normally furnished for a financial year and should be in place before making zero-rated supplies without payment of tax. The facility is broadly available to registered exporters, except for persons falling within the prescribed prosecution-related restriction. An exporter should not assume that an LUT filed in an earlier year remains valid indefinitely. A fresh LUT should be furnished for each financial year in which exports will be made. Invoice requirements An export invoice should contain the usual prescribed tax-invoice particulars together with the appropriate export endorsement. For an export made under LUT without payment of integrated tax, the invoice should state: “SUPPLY MEANT FOR EXPORT UNDER BOND OR LETTER OF UNDERTAKING WITHOUT PAYMENT OF IGST” The invoice should also correctly state: Supplier’s name, address and GSTIN; Consecutive invoice number and date; UAE customer’s legal name and address; Country of destination; Description of the service; SAC, where required; Value and currency; LUT reference, where operationally appropriate; Place of supply; Payment terms; and Signature or permitted authentication. An incorrect or missing endorsement may create a procedural defect, even where the underlying service otherwise qualifies as an export. Reporting in GST returns Export invoices should be reported correctly in the applicable GST returns. The details furnished in the statement of outward supplies should be consistent with: Invoice register; Financial accounts; Foreign-exchange records; LUT; Bank realisation evidence; Refund application; and GST payment return. Incorrect classification as domestic, exempt or non-GST turnover can affect both compliance and refund eligibility. Businesses should reconcile export turnover periodically rather than waiting until the annual close or refund filing. Can the exporter pay IGST and claim a refund? Older summaries frequently describe two unrestricted options: Export under LUT without paying IGST and claim refund of accumulated input tax credit; or Pay IGST on the export and claim refund of the tax paid. That description should not be applied mechanically under the current version of Section 16 of the IGST Act. The standard statutory route is export under bond or LUT without payment of integrated tax, followed by a claim for refund of eligible unutilised input tax credit. Payment of IGST followed by refund is available only to classes of persons or supplies notified by the Government, subject to the prescribed conditions. An exporter should therefore verify eligibility before adopting the tax-payment route. For most Indian service exporters dealing with UAE clients, the LUT route is the practical starting point. Refund of accumulated input tax credit A registered exporter supplying services under LUT may claim a refund of eligible unutilised input tax credit in Form GST RFD-01. The refund is computed under the prescribed formula and is influenced by matters such as: Export-service payments received during the refund period; Services completed against advances received earlier; Advances for services not yet completed; Eligible net input tax credit; Adjusted total turnover; and Restrictions applicable to particular credits. Export turnover for the refund formula is closely connected with realisation. Consequently, invoice value and refund turnover may not always fall in the same tax period. A business issuing a large invoice to a Dubai customer in March but receiving payment in May should account for this timing difference when preparing the refund claim. Documents for a service-export refund The precise documents depend on the facts and refund value, but a well-prepared claim may require: LUT acknowledgement; Export invoices; Statement of export invoices; Service agreements and purchase orders; Foreign-remittance evidence; Bank realisation documents; GST return reconciliation; Input tax credit ledger; Statement of eligible and ineligible credits; Declaration regarding prosecution, where applicable; Undertakings prescribed under the refund rules; and Computation of the refund amount. The officer may also examine whether the services genuinely qualify as exports. An LUT and foreign remittance do not prevent scrutiny of the recipient, place of supply or intermediary classification. Refund claims must be filed within time GST refund applications are subject to the statutory limitation period, generally calculated from the relevant date applicable to the category of refund. For exported services, the relevant date depends on the prescribed rule and may be linked to receipt of payment where the service was completed before payment, or to invoice issuance where advance payment was received before the invoice. The calculation should be made invoice by invoice where necessary. Exporters should not wait until the end of several financial years to organise refund documentation. Old claims can become time-barred, and older banking records may be difficult to reconcile. GST registration for service exporters A business supplying services from India to overseas customers should separately examine whether GST registration is required. Exports are inter-State supplies. However, specific registration exemptions and threshold-related provisions may affect smaller service providers. Registration should not be decided solely from the statement that “all exports require GST registration.” The person’s aggregate turnover, nature of supply, compulsory-registration provisions and current notifications must be considered together. Where a person voluntarily obtains GST registration, the person must comply with return filing, invoicing and other GST obligations even if the turnover remains below the general threshold. A registered exporter who wishes to use the LUT and input-tax-credit refund mechanism must maintain complete GST records. Example: Indian software developer serving a Dubai company An Indian software company enters into a contract with a separately incorporated Dubai business to develop a customer-management platform. The Indian company: Develops the software on its own account; Delivers it electronically to the Dubai customer; Has no role in arranging supplies between the customer and third parties; Receives payment in UAE dirhams through an authorised bank; Holds a valid LUT; and Issues a properly endorsed export invoice. Subject to the complete facts, the transaction may qualify as an export of services and a zero-rated supply. If the Indian company instead merely introduces the Dubai business to software vendors and earns commission when the Dubai company purchases a system, intermediary classification may need to be examined. Example: Indian marketing agency serving a UAE brand An Indian agency is engaged by a Dubai company to create social-media content, prepare advertising designs and deliver monthly campaign reports. If the agency supplies these deliverables on its own account to the UAE recipient, the service may qualify under the general place-of-supply rule, subject to all other export conditions. The outcome may change if the agency’s real role is to locate Indian distributors, negotiate transactions and earn commission on sales completed between the UAE brand and Indian buyers. The expression “marketing service” does not determine the GST result. The actual contractual and commercial role does. Example: recruitment service for a Dubai employer An Indian recruitment company identifies candidates for a Dubai employer and receives a placement fee. The GST analysis can be sensitive. The company must determine whether it supplies an independent recruitment service on its own account or merely arranges another supply in an intermediary capacity. Relevant facts include: Who is the contractual recipient; Whether the recruiter is responsible for a defined recruitment deliverable; Whether candidates pay any fee; How consideration is calculated; Whether the recruiter can bind either party; Whether three-party facilitation is the substance of the arrangement; and What the agreement and actual conduct demonstrate. A foreign client and foreign-currency payment do not remove the need for this analysis. Frequent mistakes in UAE service exports Indian exporters commonly weaken their zero-rating position by: Treating every invoice to Dubai as an export; Failing to examine the place-of-supply rules; Ignoring intermediary exposure; Filing an LUT after exports have already begun; Using an expired LUT from an earlier financial year; Omitting the required export endorsement; Receiving payment from an unexplained Indian account; Failing to link remittances with invoices; Treating an Indian branch and UAE head office as separate persons; Using contracts that do not identify the actual recipient; Reporting export invoices incorrectly in GST returns; Claiming refunds before reconciling turnover and remittances; Including blocked or ineligible input tax credit; Missing the refund limitation period; and Failing to monitor overdue export receivables. Practical compliance checklist Before treating a service to a UAE customer as zero-rated, the supplier should confirm: 1. The Indian establishment is the actual supplier. 2. The contractual recipient is genuinely located in the UAE. 3. The place of supply is outside India under the applicable rule. 4. The service is supplied on the exporter’s own account and is not an intermediary activity. 5. The supplier and recipient are not merely establishments of the same person. 6. Payment will be received through a method permitted by the RBI. 7. The current financial year’s LUT has been filed before export. 8. The invoice contains the prescribed export endorsement. 9. The contract, invoice and remittance name the same recipient or properly explain any difference. 10. Export proceeds are monitored within the permitted realisation period. 11. GST returns reflect the invoices accurately. 12. Input tax credit is supported and eligible. 13. Refund calculations are reconciled with payments received. 14. The refund application is filed within the statutory period. Conclusion Exporting services to Dubai or another part of the UAE can provide valuable GST zero-rating benefits, but the treatment is not automatic. The supplier, recipient, place of supply, payment method and legal relationship between the parties must all satisfy the export definition. Among these requirements, place of supply and intermediary classification usually create the greatest practical risk. Indian service providers should review their UAE contracts before work begins, furnish the LUT on time, issue correctly endorsed invoices and maintain a clear connection between each contract, invoice, remittance and GST return. The most defensible zero-rating position is one in which the legal documents and actual business conduct tell the same story: an Indian supplier independently provided a service to a genuine UAE recipient, the place of supply was outside India and the export proceeds were received through a permitted channel.

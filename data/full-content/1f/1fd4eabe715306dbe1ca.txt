Summary: The India-Mauritius Double Taxation Avoidance Agreement (DTAA) has undergone significant changes as India seeks to balance cross-border investment opportunities with measures preventing treaty abuse and tax avoidance. The article traces the historical development of the treaty, beginning with its original residence-based capital gains taxation framework, which made Mauritius a favourable route for foreign direct investment and portfolio investment into India. It examines concerns surrounding treaty shopping, round-tripping and commercial substance, followed by the amendments introduced through the 2016 Protocol. These amendments provided for source-based taxation of specified capital gains, grandfathering of earlier investments, transitional concessional tax rates and the Limitation of Benefits clause. The article also discusses the Supreme Court’s decision in the Tiger Global International case dated 15 January 2026, arising from the transfer of shares in Flipkart’s Singapore parent company during Walmart’s acquisition of Flipkart. It examines the rejection of advance ruling applications involving alleged tax avoidance arrangements and the evidentiary significance of Tax Residency Certificates in determining treaty entitlement. Further, the article discusses the 2024 Protocol, notified on 13 October 2026, which introduces Article 27B and the Principal Purpose Test to address inappropriate treaty benefits. The discussion highlights the importance of examining relevant facts and circumstances, commercial substance and the object and purpose of treaty provisions. It concludes by considering the challenges of implementing anti-abuse measures while maintaining tax certainty and facilitating legitimate cross-border transactions and investments. It is intended to prevent double taxation on a single transaction of a taxpayer. The India-Mauritius DTAA has stood as a practical example since 1983, with prominent developments since the year 2016. The treaty was primarily aimed at provisions in respect of capital gains for shares sold in the Union of India. Mauritius acts as a favorable destination for the capital gain transactions as they are not taxable as per the country’s taxation rules. Historical Context Phase 1: The formal recognition and execution of the treaty India and Mauritius first signed and enforced the treaty on 1 April, 1983. It’s provisions for capital gains provided taxing rights on such transactions to the country of residence of the seller instead of the source country (majorly India). Since Mauritius did not tax capital gains, the sellers did not have to pay any tax in either country. This benefit along with other favorable conditions made Mauritius a leading route for Foreign Direct Investments (FDI) and portfolio investments into India. The Pre-2016 Treaty Framework: Before the 2016 amendments, there was a challenge ahead for the governments to preserve the substance of the treaty by identifying legitimate business transactions from the ones done purely to avoid tax without any other primary reason. The framework also drew criticism over whether some Mauritius based shell companies used the relaxation as a treaty shopping benefit without any commercial substance of such companies the country of residence. A recurring concern cited was “round-tripping”: Indian capital leaving and returning as an FDI from Mauritius. Phase 2: the 2016 protocol (effective 1 April, 2017) The Third protocol was signed on 10 May, 2016. It largely focused on capital account transactions, enhancing the taxation rights of the source country, and formally introducing the Limitation of Benefits (LOB) clause. It also facilitated exchange of information and collection provision between the two nations and it is regarded as one of the major amendments in the agreement since the year 1983. Its main features include: Article 13(3A): gains from the transfer of shares of an Indian company that were acquired on or after 1 April, 2017 were now taxable in the source country (India). Grandfathering: shares that were acquired before 1 April, 2017 stayed under the old rule with taxability in the country of residence. Article 13(3B): this is a transition provision for shares that were acquired on or after 1 April, 2017 and sold on or before 31 March, 2019, invited tax rate only up to 50% of the Indian domestic tax rates, subject to the LOB. From 1 April, 2019 onwards the full Indian domestic tax rate would be applicable. Article 27A (LOB): The Limitation of Benefits (LOB) provision was meant to benefit only legitimate transactions. Therefore, a resident would be denied the treaty benefits if it is established that the transactions and the affairs were organized with the sole purpose of taking advantage of the benefits under the treaty. The Tiger Global ruling: the case at the center of the story The Authority for Advance Rulings (Income Tax) v. Tiger Global International in the court of India became the center of the story and a major point of discussion of the India Mauritius DTAA. Background of the dispute: The dispute arose from the 2018 sale of shares held by Mauritius entities of Tiger Global group in Flipkart’s Singapore parent company, to Walmart in its acquisition of Flipkart. Where Tiger Global claimed exemption from the Indian capital marker under article 13(4) of the treaty on the basis that since the shares were bought before 1 April, 2017, they were grandfathered and they held a valued Mauritius Tax Residency Certificate (TRC). However, the authorities questioned whether the Mauritius entities engaged in a genuine business transaction including whether the exemprion was a prima facie tax avoidance arrangement which lacked commercial substance and decision making in Mauritius. The Authority for Advance Rulings (AAR) rejected their applications at the threshold, finding that the transaction was prima facie designed for tax avoidance. The Delhi High Court subsequently set aside that decision, but the Supreme Court restored the AAR’s order. Supreme Court of India’s decision: On 15 January, 2026 the Supreme Court held that the Authority for Advance Rulings (AAR) could reject an application for exemption where it was prima facie satisified hat such transaction was carried out for tax avoidance. The court also held that a Tax Residency Certificate (TRC) cannot be treated as a conclusive proof of entitlement to the benefits of the treaty. The tax authorities may examine the facts of the arrangement. Therefore, a TRC may be relevant evidence of residence but it does not act as a safeguard against scrutiny of a potential abuse of agreement. The 2024 Protocol India and Mauritius signed the 2024 protocol on 7 March, 2024 which came into force on 10 August, 2026 and was notified by the Indian government on 13 October, 2026. This has been one of the major amendments in the treaty which inserts Article 27B, titles “Entitlement to Benefits”. Its main purpose is to curb the use of treaty arrangement for tax avoidance, treaty shopping arrangements and the abuse of treaty benefits. Article 27B provides for a Principal Purpose Test (PPT) where a treaty benefit can be denied if it is established that, having regard to all the facts and circumstances of the case, one of the principals/primary purposes of the transaction was to obtain, directly or indirectly, benefit of the treaty. Exception: The provision also states that the benefit under the treaty may be granted if it is established that doing so is withing the objective or purpose of the treaty. It must be noted that, PPT is not simply a rule that denies the benefits of a treaty but a provision to protect the object and purpose of the treaty under which relief is being provided. It requires the consideration of all relevant facts and circumstances of the case before a decision is made. Conclusion and Critical Analysis: Treaty Objectives Verses Anti-Abuse Measures The India Mauritius DTAA highlights a critical issue in internal taxation: treaties are intended to facilitate, simplify cross border transactions and reduce double taxation, but they must also safeguard the objects of the treaty against arrangements and transactions that misuse the benefits of the treaty. The challenge ahead is to apply the PPT in a manner such that it protects the treaty from abuse while ensure that the intended benefits are provided to legitimate taxpayers and transactions without creating unnecessary uncertainty. It will be imperative to enforce substance over form to protect the interests of the both the nations. While a structured process and systemized approach to taxation and cross border transaction is required in the contemporary times.

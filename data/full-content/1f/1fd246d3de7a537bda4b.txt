(Supreme Court of India) The Supreme Court, in Gulf Goans Hotels Co. Ltd. & Anr. Vs Union of India & Ors., considered whether hotels, beach resorts and beach bungalows constructed in Goa before the Coastal Regulation Zone (CRZ) Notification of 19 February 1991 could be demolished for allegedly violating earlier environmental guidelines. The appellants were owners of coastal properties that had been constructed after obtaining permissions and sanctions from the competent authorities. However, the State authorities initiated demolition proceedings on the ground that the buildings were located approximately 90 to 200 metres from the High Tide Line (HTL), contrary to environmental restrictions said to prohibit construction within 500 metres of the coastline. The Bombay High Court, by orders dated 13 July 2000, upheld the demolition directions. The property owners consequently approached the Supreme Court, contending that the constructions were lawful when undertaken and that administrative guidelines lacking statutory force could not retrospectively render them illegal. The principal dispute concerned the legal status of environmental instructions issued before the introduction of the statutory CRZ framework. The authorities relied on the Prime Minister’s communication dated 27 November 1981, the Environmental Guidelines for Development of Beaches issued in July 1983, and guidelines issued through an inter-ministerial arrangement in June 1986. According to the Government, these measures reflected India’s commitments under the Stockholm Declaration of 1972 and were intended to protect coastal ecosystems from unregulated development. The Government argued that the executive was competent under to issue such directions and that the enactment of the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986 subsequently supplied the statutory authority necessary to enforce them. It maintained that the guidelines governed coastal construction until the CRZ Notification came into force on 19 February 1991. The appellants disputed the proposition that the 1981, 1983 and 1986 guidelines constituted enforceable law. They submitted that the guidelines were advisory in nature and lacked the definite commands, legal form and statutory authority required to impose restrictions upon citizens. The property owners emphasised that their buildings had been constructed with official permission and were not situated within the 90-metre coastal restriction which, according to them, applied when the permissions were granted. They also relied on Goan Real Estate and Construction Ltd. v. Union of India, (2010) 5 SCC 388, to argue that the CRZ Notification was prospective and protected transactions and constructions undertaken before its commencement. Further reliance was placed on Vellore Citizens’ Welfare Forum v. Union of India, (1996) 5 SCC 647, concerning sustainable development and the need to balance environmental protection with legitimate development. The appellants contended that international environmental commitments could not independently create enforceable restrictions without an appropriate legal framework. The Union of India and Goa Foundation defended the demolition orders by emphasising the constitutional importance of environmental protection. They argued that India’s participation in the Stockholm Conference had created obligations which could be implemented through executive measures even before comprehensive environmental legislation was enacted. Reliance was placed on Gramophone Company of India Ltd. v. Birendra Bahadur Pandey, (1984) 2 SCC 534, concerning the incorporation of international law into domestic law, and Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan, (1997) 6 SCC 241, concerning the use of international conventions to frame guidelines in the absence of legislation. Goa Foundation further referred to Vineet Narain v. Union of India, (1998) 1 SCC 226, and Rai Sahib Ram Jawaya Kapur v. State of Punjab, AIR 1955 SC 549, in support of the executive’s authority to act where statutory provisions were absent. It also argued that the passage of time and appreciation in property values could not legitimise constructions that were environmentally impermissible. The Supreme Court examined the distinction between governmental policy and enforceable law. Referring to Bennett Coleman & Co. v. Union of India, (1972) 2 SCC 788, the Court observed that a policy may acquire the force of law only when it satisfies the necessary legal requirements. Although form is relevant, the existence of a clear mandate and an identifiable purpose is essential. Applying this principle, the Court found that the environmental guidelines relied upon by the Government did not contain sufficiently clear, unambiguous and enforceable commands regulating the conduct of citizens. The guidelines appeared to represent evolving recommendations intended to identify appropriate standards for coastal protection rather than binding legal restrictions. The Stockholm Declaration itself expressed broad principles of environmental protection and sustainable development but did not prescribe a universally applicable prohibition on construction within a particular distance from the High Tide Line. Consequently, the Court rejected the contention that the 500-metre restriction could be treated as an automatically incorporated rule of international law. The Court further examined the constitutional requirements governing the issuance and authentication of executive orders. Under Article 77 of the Constitution, executive action of the Union Government must be expressed and authenticated in the prescribed manner. The Court noted that the disputed guidelines had not been issued under any existing statutory provision and had not complied with the applicable authentication requirements. Relying on State of Uttaranchal v. S.K. Vaish, (2011) 8 SCC 670, it explained that administrative opinions and file notings cannot automatically be treated as binding governmental decisions. The Court also referred to Harla v. State of Rajasthan, AIR 1951 SC 467, and B.K. Srinivasan v. State of Karnataka, (1987) 1 SCC 658, for the principle that laws affecting citizens must be duly promulgated or published through a legally recognised procedure. Since the environmental guidelines had not been properly authenticated or gazetted, they could not be enforced to the prejudice of the property owners. The Supreme Court accordingly held that the pre-1991 environmental guidelines did not possess the necessary characteristics of enforceable law and could not provide a valid basis for ordering demolition of the appellants’ constructions. The Court rejected the argument that alleged environmental harm under Article 21 could independently justify demolition without legally prescribed standards defining permissible and impermissible conduct. It observed that such standards were subsequently established through the statutory CRZ Notification issued under the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986, with effect from 19 February 1991. Having found that the earlier guidelines were unenforceable, the Court considered it unnecessary to determine whether the enactment of the 1986 Act could retrospectively supply enforcement powers in respect of those guidelines. The Supreme Court allowed the appeals, set aside the demolition orders and quashed the Bombay High Court’s orders dated 13 July 2000. The judgment establishes that environmental objectives, however important, must be pursued through legally enforceable measures and that unpublished or unauthenticated executive guidelines cannot, by themselves, impose adverse legal consequences on citizens.

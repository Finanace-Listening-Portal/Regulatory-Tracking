Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); Nita Puri Vs Union of India (Delhi High Court) Summary: The Delhi High Court, in Nita Puri Vs Union of India, examined the legality of an order dated 5 September 2024 issued by the Ministry of Corporate Affairs under Section 212(1)(c) of the Companies Act, 2013, directing the Serious Fraud Investigation Office (SFIO) to investigate the affairs of Moser Baer India Limited (MBIL), including its subsidiaries, joint ventures and associate companies. The petitioner, Nita Puri, a former/suspended director of MBIL, challenged the investigation order on the ground that the Central Government had exercised its statutory powers without satisfying the necessary legal prerequisites. The principal question before the Court was whether the Government could direct an SFIO investigation based on allegations purportedly arising from forensic audit reports when those reports did not establish the irregularities attributed to them. The Court considered the scope of governmental discretion under Sections 210 and 212, the requirement of demonstrable circumstances justifying an investigation in public interest, the necessity of independent application of mind, and the effect of earlier judicial decisions concerning the same company. Justice Sachin Datta ultimately held that an SFIO investigation is a serious statutory measure carrying substantial consequences for companies and individuals and cannot be initiated through routine administrative action, unsupported assumptions or incorrect interpretations of investigative material. The dispute originated from the financial difficulties encountered by MBIL, a company engaged in manufacturing CDs, DVDs and other optical media products. In 2012, the company sought financial restructuring under the Reserve Bank of India’s Corporate Debt Restructuring (CDR) framework. Following a stock audit conducted by RRCA & Associates and a techno-economic viability study prepared by Ernst & Young, the lending consortium classified MBIL as a Class B borrower and admitted it into the restructuring mechanism. The petitioner emphasised that the company had not been classified under categories ordinarily associated with suspected fraud or serious financial misconduct. Subsequently, MBIL entered corporate insolvency resolution proceedings before the National Company Law Tribunal in November 2017. During those proceedings, the Interim Resolution Professional, acting at the instance of the Committee of Creditors, commissioned a special-purpose forensic audit by Kashyap Sikdar & Co. covering financial years 2015-16, 2016-17 and part of 2017-18. The audit examined transactions potentially falling within the preferential, undervalued, fraudulent and extortionate categories under the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016. The report did not identify transactions falling within those categories, and its findings were accepted by the Committee of Creditors. A separate forensic audit by GSA & Associates covering an earlier period was subsequently submitted to the liquidator in June 2019. These reports later became central to the controversy because the Government’s investigation order attributed findings of serious financial irregularities to them. The petitioner further relied upon earlier proceedings initiated by Bank of Baroda and the State Bank of India concerning allegations of wilful default by former directors of MBIL. Bank of Baroda had issued a show-cause notice in March 2020 and subsequently declared Nita Puri and Ratul Puri wilful defaulters. However, in Ratul Puri Vs Bank of Baroda, decided on 29 February 2024, the Delhi High Court set aside the relevant decisions of the bank’s Identification Committee and Review Committee. The appeal against that judgment was dismissed by a Division Bench on 8 August 2024. Separately, proceedings concerning a wilful-defaulter declaration by the State Bank of India were addressed in Ratul Puri Vs State Bank of India & Anr., decided on 20 March 2023, in which the bank undertook not to act upon the Review Committee’s findings and the proceedings stood dropped. According to the petitioner, these judicial developments were directly relevant because the subsequent SFIO investigation order substantially relied upon the same forensic audit material and allegations that had already been examined in the banking proceedings. The petitioner argued that the Ministry of Corporate Affairs had failed to consider these binding and relevant judicial developments before exercising its powers under Section 212(1)(c). The Government disputed this contention, maintaining that proceedings relating to wilful default were legally distinct from an investigation into corporate affairs and that the earlier judgments did not invalidate the underlying forensic audit reports or prohibit investigation into other possible irregularities. The petitioner challenged the SFIO order on several independent legal grounds. Relying principally upon Parmeshwar Das Agarwal & Ors. Vs Additional Director (Investigation), Serious Fraud Investigation Office & Ors., the petitioner submitted that Section 212(1)(c) requires the Central Government to identify relevant circumstances demonstrating not merely the necessity of investigating a company’s affairs but also why such investigation must specifically be entrusted to the SFIO. The petitioner further relied upon the Supreme Court decisions in Barium Chemicals Ltd. Vs Company Law Board, Rohtas Industries Vs S.D. Aggarwal, and Rampur Distillery Vs Company Law Board to contend that although formation of governmental opinion may involve subjective satisfaction, the existence of relevant circumstances supporting that opinion remains subject to judicial scrutiny. It was argued that the impugned order incorrectly stated that the forensic audit reports had identified preferential, undervalued, fraudulent and extortionate transactions, whereas the reports themselves did not support that conclusion. The petitioner also contended that the Government had disregarded the findings in the earlier Bank of Baroda and State Bank of India proceedings. In response, the Union of India submitted that the scope of an SFIO investigation was wider than the issues considered in wilful-defaulter proceedings, that the audit reports covered different periods, and that an investigation would not prejudice the petitioner. The Government maintained that the findings recorded in the investigation order were based upon the Sikdar and GSA reports and attempted to justify the order through additional explanations placed before the Court. The Delhi High Court examined the statutory framework governing inspection, inquiry and investigation under Chapter XIV of the Companies Act, 2013, particularly Sections 206, 208, 210, 211 and 212. Referring extensively to the Bombay High Court’s decision in Parmeshwar Das Agarwal, the Court observed that the Central Government must demonstrate the existence of relevant circumstances supporting its decision to order an investigation under Section 212(1)(c). It is insufficient merely to state that an investigation is necessary in public interest or to reproduce general allegations without identifying the material justifying the exercise of such power. The Court emphasised that the statutory scheme distinguishes an ordinary investigation under Section 210 from an investigation entrusted to the SFIO under Section 212. Consequently, the Government must consider not only whether an investigation is warranted but also whether the circumstances justify the involvement of the specialised agency. The Court noted that the Supreme Court had dismissed the Special Leave Petition against the Bombay High Court’s decision on grounds of delay as well as merits and treated that development as reinforcing the legal position adopted in the earlier judgment. Applying these principles, the Delhi High Court found that the impugned order failed to explain the necessity of an SFIO investigation. The Court also examined the earlier inquiry initiated under Section 206(4) following a complaint received in July 2018. Although that inquiry had resulted in a recommendation for inspection of the company’s books and records under Section 206(5), the Government had not adequately explained whether the recommended inspection was undertaken or why the statutory process had not been pursued before directing an SFIO investigation. A decisive consideration was the Court’s examination of the forensic audit reports relied upon by the Ministry of Corporate Affairs. The impugned order asserted that the Sikdar and GSA reports had identified transactions falling within the preferential, undervalued, fraudulent and extortionate categories. However, the Court found that this assertion was inconsistent with the contents of the reports. The Sikdar Report had expressly examined transactions under Sections 43, 45, 49, 50 and 66 of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code and had not identified transactions attracting the relevant provisions during the period examined. Similarly, the GSA Report did not establish the conclusions attributed to it in the Government’s order. Although the GSA Report referred to certain financial circumstances, including historical investments and advances involving associate companies, it did not conclude that the alleged PUFE transactions had occurred as asserted by the Government. The Court held that an opinion formed under Section 212(1)(c) cannot be sustained when its factual foundation consists of nonexistent circumstances or findings incorrectly attributed to source documents. The Court further examined the Government’s attempt to supplement the investigation order through explanations contained in its counter-affidavit. Applying the principles laid down in Mohinder Singh Gill Vs Chief Election Commissioner, Opto Circuit India Ltd. Vs Axis Bank, and Ritesh Tiwari Vs State of Uttar Pradesh, it reiterated that the legality of an administrative or statutory order must be assessed on the grounds recorded when the order was made. Subsequent affidavits cannot cure an order that was legally defective at its inception. The Court also observed that the relevance and implications of the earlier Bank of Baroda judgment ought to have been considered before the investigation order was issued, although it did not find it necessary to conclusively decide every contention concerning the effect of that judgment. The Delhi High Court consequently allowed the writ petition and quashed the Ministry of Corporate Affairs’ order dated 5 September 2024 directing an SFIO investigation into MBIL, together with all consequential proceedings arising from that order. The Court held that the Government had failed to demonstrate the relevant circumstances necessary for forming a legally sustainable opinion under Section 212(1)(c), had incorrectly attributed findings of fraudulent and other questionable transactions to the forensic audit reports, and had failed to adequately justify the necessity of entrusting the investigation to the SFIO. The judgment emphasised that an SFIO investigation is not a routine administrative measure but an exceptional statutory action with potentially grave consequences for the entities and individuals concerned. Therefore, the Central Government must exercise its powers with due deliberation, proper examination of relevant records and genuine application of mind. The decision establishes that the existence of circumstances supporting an SFIO investigation is open to judicial scrutiny and that boilerplate reasoning, inaccurate references to audit findings or subsequent attempts to improve an investigation order cannot substitute for compliance with statutory safeguards. Importantly, the ruling concerns the legality of the particular investigation order and does not amount to a general prohibition against future investigation where independently established circumstances and applicable statutory requirements justify such action.

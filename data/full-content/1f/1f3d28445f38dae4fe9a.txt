Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); Ashok Leyland Ltd Vs DCIT (ITAT Chennai) Summary : The Chennai ‘D’ Bench of the Income Tax Appellate Tribunal partly allowed Ashok Leyland Ltd.’s appeal and dismissed the Revenue’s cross-appeal for AY 2019–20 against the CIT(A)/NFAC order dated 16 March 2024. Judicial Member Aby T. Varkey and Accountant Member Amitabh Shukla delivered the order on 7 July 2025 after hearing the parties on 4 June 2025. The company manufactured and sold commercial vehicles. The disputes concerned transfer pricing of corporate guarantees, disallowance relating to exempt income, scientific-research expenditure, deduction for its Pantnagar manufacturing unit and deduction for CSR-related donations. The Tribunal condoned the Revenue’s 16-day filing delay, noting the explanation and the absence of serious objection from the assessee. On corporate guarantees, the company had guaranteed loans obtained by its foreign subsidiaries, Optare Plc and Albonair GmbH, without charging commission. The TPO applied a 1% arm’s-length guarantee fee, resulting in the disputed adjustment of ₹1,22,93,500. The assessee argued that the guarantees were shareholder or stewardship activities without any bearing on its profits, income, losses or assets. The Tribunal rejected that contention in light of the retrospective Explanation to Section 92B and the decisions in CIT v. Everest Kanto Cylinders Ltd. and PCIT v. Redington (India) Ltd. It held that issuing a corporate guarantee was an international transaction subject to transfer-pricing provisions, even where no fee had actually been charged.The Tribunal nevertheless reduced the guarantee adjustment from 1% to 0.5% of the guarantee value. The assessee’s alternative request for 0.325%, supported by a bank quotation, was rejected because bank guarantees and corporate guarantees could not be equated. Applying the authorities discussed in the order, the Bench directed the Assessing Officer to restrict the adjustment to 0.5%. The corporate-guarantee ground was therefore partly allowed; the Tribunal did not accept the broader contention that the transaction fell entirely outside transfer pricing.The Tribunal deleted the Section 14A disallowance of ₹18,82,33,000, computed under Rule 8D as 1% of average investments, because the assessee had earned no exempt income during the year. The CIT(A) had sustained the addition by relying on CBDT Circular No. 5/2014. The Bench followed the jurisdictional decisions in Chettinad Logistics, Redington India, Celebrity Fashion and Marg Ltd., which explained the relationship between actual exempt income, the statutory provision and the computation rule. It also considered PCIT v. Era Infrastructure (India) Ltd. and PCIT v. Avantha Realty Ltd. on the prospective operation of the Finance Act, 2022 amendment. That amendment did not justify a disallowance for AY 2019–20. The scientific-research dispute concerned revenue expenditure of ₹538,76,62,852 at the approved in-house R&D facility and capital expenditure reported at ₹127,72,94,730. The assessee had claimed 150% weighted deduction on the revenue expenditure and initially restricted its capital-expenditure claim to ₹87,88,04,510. The Assessing Officer denied the weighted component in the absence of Form 3CL. During the first appeal, the assessee produced DSIR’s certificate approving revenue expenditure of ₹529,78,40,000 and capital expenditure of ₹114,69,23,000. The CIT(A) nevertheless restricted capital relief to the original claim and treated part of the revenue claim as excessive by comparing the amount certified with the expenditure debited to the profit and loss account. The Tribunal held, following Tejas Networks Ltd. v. DCIT, that the lower tax authorities could not sit in judgment over the expenditure quantified and approved by DSIR under Section 35(2AB) read with Rule 6(7A). The facility had been approved in Form 3CM, and the relevant expenditure had been certified in Form 3CL. It therefore held the assessee entitled to weighted deduction at 150% on approved revenue expenditure of ₹529,78,40,000 and approved capital expenditure of ₹114,69,23,000. The finding concerned the statutory R&D deduction; its availability was not determined merely by how the expenditure appeared in the financial statements. Addressing the alleged excess revenue claim, the Bench accepted that ₹304,48,15,892 had been charged to the profit and loss account, while ₹234,28,46,960 relating to salaries, raw materials, components and ongoing R&D projects had been capitalised in the books under the applicable Ind AS requirements. Since the latter amount had not been debited to the profit and loss account, its separate consideration in the tax computation did not amount to the duplicate claim assumed by the CIT(A). The Tribunal directed deletion of that alleged excess claim. It further directed allowance of ₹794,67,60,000 as weighted deduction on the DSIR-approved revenue expenditure and normal deduction under Section 35(1) for the remaining revenue expenditure of ₹8,98,22,852. The Tribunal also entertained the additional capital-expenditure claim despite its omission from the original return. Relying on NTPC v. CIT and CIT v. Perlo Telecommunication and Electronic Components India Pvt. Ltd., it distinguished the restriction applicable to the Assessing Officer from the appellate authorities’ powers to consider legally available claims. It directed weighted deduction of ₹40,21,77,735 on the additional DSIR-approved capital expenditure of ₹26,81,18,490, being the difference between ₹114,69,23,000 and ₹87,88,04,510. The Assessing Officer was directed to recompute the deductions under Sections 35(2AB), 35(1)(i) and 35(1)(iv) in accordance with the findings. These grounds were formally allowed for statistical purposes. For Section 80-IC, the assessee claimed ₹371,73,37,546 for its Pantnagar commercial-vehicle manufacturing unit, supported by audited stand-alone accounts and Form 10CCB. The order described AY 2019–20 as the unit’s tenth eligible year. The Assessing Officer substituted a 3% margin for the reported 11.51%, disallowing ₹303,22,16,976; the CIT(A) adopted 6.09% and restricted the disallowance to ₹168,14,24,514. The Tribunal followed its decision in the assessee’s own case for AY 2018–19, since the facts and the parties’ submissions were similar and the Revenue identified no distinguishing feature. It held that rejecting the audited unit accounts and estimating lower profits on the same reasoning was unjustified. The earlier-year reasoning reproduced and followed in the order addressed the identification of Pantnagar products through chassis numbers, recognition of sales at prices charged to independent customers, and allocation of corporate, R&D, finance, marketing and other common costs on appropriate parameters. It rejected further hypothetical segregation of manufacturing profits from marketing and R&D efforts where the related costs had already been allocated. Higher profitability alone did not establish an arrangement under Section 80-IA(10), and defects in the accounts or the statutory basis for substituting profits had not been demonstrated. The earlier decision also distinguished GST discharge on stock movements from recognition of customer sales. Applying that reasoning to the year under appeal, the Tribunal directed allowance of the Section 80-IC deduction as claimed, allowed the assessee’s ground and dismissed the Revenue’s corresponding ground. Finally, the Tribunal upheld deletion of the ₹4,32,97,964 disallowance of Section 80G deduction relating to CSR donations. The assessee had incurred CSR expenditure of ₹34.06 crore and had already disallowed the relevant expenditure under Explanation 2 to Section 37(1). The Bench held that the restriction in computing business income could not automatically be imported into the separate Chapter VI-A deduction. It relied on JMS Mining and the reasoning reproduced from Sikka Ports & Terminals, which addressed the Revenue’s voluntariness objection and distinguished payments involving material consideration. Specific CSR exclusions concerning the Swachh Bharat Kosh and Clean Ganga Fund did not create a general prohibition against other qualifying donations. The CIT(A)’s direction to allow relief after verifying Section 80G conditions remained intact. The Revenue’s appeal was dismissed, while the assessee’s appeal was partly allowed.

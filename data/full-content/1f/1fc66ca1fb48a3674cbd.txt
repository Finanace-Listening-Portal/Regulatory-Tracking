Acting on intelligence alleging that gold smuggled from Bangladesh was being transported to Delhi, officers of the Directorate of Revenue Intelligence intercepted the appellants at Howrah Railway Station on 16 March 2022. The officers recovered 15 gold bars, biscuits and cut pieces from their possession. The adjudicating authority ordered absolute confiscation and imposed penalties of ₹10 lakh each on Tirlok Singh and Geetanshu Aggarwal and ₹5 lakh on Dharmender Kumar Jha. The Commissioner (Appeals) upheld the order on 27 December 2024, leading to the appeals before the Tribunal. The appellants challenged the confiscation on the ground that the seized gold bore no foreign markings and that the Department had failed to establish its alleged foreign origin. They contended that their initial statements, recorded on 16 and 17 March 2022, had been obtained under threat and coercion and were subsequently retracted through a prison petition dated 23 March 2022 and communications dated 20 April 2022. The appellants also submitted documentary evidence supporting domestic ownership. Tirlok Singh relied on an invoice dated 15 March 2004 showing purchase of gold by his mother, Krishna Devi. Geetanshu Aggarwal relied on his mother’s declaration concerning ancestral jewellery, documents relating to the Voluntary Disclosure of Income Scheme and a declaration from his father. Dharmender Kumar Jha explained that the gold had passed through his family and produced an affidavit supporting its ownership. The Tribunal noted that the adjudicating and appellate authorities had neither adequately considered nor controverted these materials. Examining the seizure, the Tribunal observed that the gold consisted of pieces of non-standard weights, with purity below 99.9%, and did not bear foreign markings. The goods had not been recovered from a customs bonded area or a specified area under Section 11H of the Customs Act. Referring to Notification No. 31/2008-Customs (N.T.), the Tribunal also noted that Howrah Railway Station was situated more than 150 kilometres from the Bangladesh international border. No independent evidence established that the seized gold had been brought from Bangladesh or any other foreign country. The Tribunal held that foreign origin could not be established through assumptions or presumptions and that the domestic procurement evidence produced by the appellants had not been disproved. On the applicability of Section 123, the Tribunal explained that the burden of proving that gold is not smuggled shifts to its possessor or owner only when the goods are seized on a reasonable belief that they are smuggled. The mere absence of purchase documents at the moment of interception did not automatically establish smuggling, particularly when supporting evidence was produced subsequently. Since the Revenue had not established the required reasonable belief and the appellants’ evidence remained unrebutted, the Tribunal held that Section 123 was inapplicable. It further concluded that confiscation under Sections 111(b) and 111(d) could not be sustained without proof that the gold was imported or smuggled. The Tribunal referred to judicial decisions concerning the evidentiary requirements for confiscation and the burden of proof in gold-seizure cases. The Tribunal also examined the penalties under Section 112(a)/(b) and found that the essential ingredients for imposing them had not been established. There was no specific finding demonstrating the appellants’ knowledge, conscious involvement or conduct rendering the goods liable to confiscation. Relying on the principles governing penalty under Section 112, the Tribunal held that the penalties were unsustainable. It separately considered the confiscation of ₹97,000 under Section 121 as alleged sale proceeds of smuggled gold and found no supporting evidence. Consequently, the Tribunal set aside the confiscation of all 3,152.900 grams of gold, the penalties imposed on the three appellants and the confiscation of ₹97,000. All three appeals were allowed with consequential relief in accordance with law. Cases Discussed Kranti Associates (P) Ltd. Vs Masood Ahmed Khan, 2011 (273) E.L.T. 345 (S.C.) Century Textile & Ind. Gold is an item notified under Section 123 of the Customs Act,1962. Section 123 of Customs Act, 1962 stipulates that the onus to prove that the gold is not of smuggled in nature is on the person who claims the ownership of the gold. For ready reference the said section 123 is reproduced below: SECTION 123. Burden of proof in certain cases. (1) Where any goods to which this section applies are seized under this Act in the reasonable belief that they are smuggled goods, the burden of proving that they are not smuggled goods shall be – (a) in a case where such seizure is made from the possession of any person, – (i) on the person from whose possession the goods were seized; and (ii) if any person, other than the person from whose possession the goods were seized, claims to be the owner thereof, also on such other person; (b) in any other case, on the person, if any, who claims to be the owner of the goods so seized. (2) This section shall apply to gold, and manufactures thereof, watches, and any other class of goods which the Central Government may by notification in the Official Gazette specify. 7.1. In this case, we observe that all the three appellants claimed the ownership of the gold which were in their possession. However, the gold was seized as they could not produce any valid documents for the legal purchase of the said gold at the time of their interception. Not having any document with them at the time of interception would not ipso facto make the gold is of smuggled in nature. If the appellants could produce valid documents for the licit purchase of the gold subsequently, it cannot be ignored. 7.1. In the present case, we find that the appellant Shri. Tirlok Singh has produced copy of invoice No. 52 dt. 15.03.2004 of M/s Manik Jewellers, Mall Road, Ludhiana substantiating purchase of impugned gold by his mother Smt. Krishna Devi along with Affidavit dt. 23.03.2023, which was submitted to the ld. Adjudicating Authority and Appellate Authority. However, both the authorities neither considered nor controverted the said submissions. 7.2. We find that the appellant Shri. Geetanshu Aggarwal has submitted that the seized gold was derived from family’s old ancestral jewellery which was under the possession of his mother Smt. Mamta Aggarwal and the said fact was declared by her vide affidavit dt. 03.12.1997 wherein she had admitted that she was in possession of 1004.20 gram of gold jewellery and had paid income tax of Rs. 65,848/- and accordingly she had received a certificate in terms of section 68 (2) of Voluntary Disclosure of Income Tax Scheme. Further, the appellant submitted that the said gold was melted and refined to 919.84 gram of pure gold in the form of strips from which a couple of cut-pieces were obtained for its easy conveyance to Kolkata so that same be crafted into gold jewellery of new design. Out of 1112.75 gram of seized gold, procurement of 919.84 gram of gold was from old jewellery and 193 gram of gold approx.. was given to appellant by his father Sh. Vijender Kumar Aggarwal – declaration to this effect by appellant’ s father submitted to ld. Adjudicating Authority. Thus, we find that the appellant Geetanshu Aggarwal has produced enough evidence for the ownership of the gold which was found in his pocession. However, the Ld. authorities have not considered his submission and not given any findings on the submissions made by him. 7.3. The appellant Shri. Dharminder Kumar Jha The gold weighing 872.910 gram valued at Rs. 46,04,600/- was seized on 16.03.2022. The appellant vide mail dt. 20.04.2022 informed DRI that the seized gold was their ancestral property and he carried the impugned gold to Kolkata for making ornaments. The impugned gold weighing 872.910 gram, 24 carat was given by his grand father Sh. Nikunta Jha to his father Sh. Makeshar Jha. The appellant’s father gave the impugned gold to appellant for making ornaments for distribution among his children. As he could not get proper jewellery designs for ornaments, the gold as such was carried back and same was seized on 16.03.2022 by DRI officials. To this effect the appellant has filed an affidavit dt. 10.07.2023 before the Ld. Adjudicating Authority. However, no finding has been given in the impugned order against the claim made by him in the impugned order. 7.4. We observe that the section 123 stipulates its applicability where goods are seized on the reasonable belief that the said goods were smuggled in nature, then the burden of proving the same shall shift to the person who claims ownership of the goods. In the present case, we observe that the impugned gold were neither bearing foreign marking nor seized from customs area. Further, we observe that the appellants have submitted enough evidence for domestic procurement of the gold, which has not been negated by the investigation. Accordingly, we hold that the provisions of Section 123 of Customs Act, 1962 is not applicable in this case and the burden of proving that the gold seized is not of smuggled in nature is not on the appellants. 7.5. Regarding the confiscation of the gold seized, we observe that the ld. Appellate Authority has upheld the absolute confiscation of the impugned gold under section 111 (b) & 111 (d) of Customs Act, 1962. For ready reference, the provisions of the said sections 111(b) and 111 (d) of Customs Act, 1962 are reproduced below: SECTION 111 of Customs Act, 1962- Confiscation of improperly imported goods, etc. The following goods brought from a place outside India shall be liable to confiscation: — (a) …. (b) any goods imported by land or inland water through any route other than a route specified in a notification issued under clause (c) of section 7 for the import of such goods; (c) … (d) any goods which are imported or attempted to be imported or are brought within the Indian customs waters for the purpose of being imported, contrary to any prohibition imposed by or under this Act or any other law for the time being in force; 7.6. We observe that the sections 111(b) and 111(d) are applicable only when it is established that the goods are of foreign origin and smuggled into the country without payment of applicable customs duties. In this case, we find that there is nothing records to substantiate the allegation that the impugned goods seized from the appellants were smuggled from Bangladesh or any other foreign country 7.7. Our view is supported by the decision of the CESTAT in the cases of:- i) Principal Commissioner of Customs (Prev), Delhi Vs. Ahamed Mujjaba Khaleefa 2019 (366) ELT 337 (T) dismissed the appeal of revenue holding that jewellary not bearing any foreign marking – other than statement of passenger no other proof produced by revenue to substantiate claim that jewellary smuggled into India. ii) Commissioner of Customs (Prev), Shillong Vs. Manisha Devi Jain 2019 (370) ELT 401 (T) held that indian origin remolten gold/gold ornaments could not be legally confiscated as its possession not prohibited under any provisions of law. 7.8. Thus, by relying on the decisions cited above, we observe that the provisions of section 111(b) and 111(d) of Customs Act, 1962 are confined to the imported goods. As there is no evidence brought on record to substantiate the allegation that the impugned gold was imported, we hold that the provisions of sections 111(b) and 111 (d) of Customs Act, 1962 are not applicable to the seized gold in this case. 7.9. We also hold that the evidences submitted by the appellants establish that the gold weighing 3152.900 were procured from domestic sources and hence not liable for confiscation. In view of the above, the answer to question nos. (ii) and (iii) in para 6.1 above, are in the negative. (iv) Whether penalties imposed on the Appellants under section 112(a)/(b) of the Customs Act, 1962 are sustainable in this case? 8. We observe that penalties have been imposed on the appellants under sections 112(a)/(b) of the Customs Act, 1962. For the sake of ready reference, the provisions of the said section is reproduced below: Section 112 Penalty for improper importation of goods, etc. Any person, – (a) who, in relation to any goods, does or omits to do any act which act or omission would render such goods liable to confiscation under section 111, or abets the doing or omission of such an act, or (b) who acquires possession of or is in any way concerned in carrying, removing, depositing, harbouring, keeping, concealing, selling or purchasing, or in any other manner dealing with any goods which he knows or has reason to believe are liable to confiscation under section 111, shall be liable, – (i) in the case of goods in respect of which any prohibition is in force under this Act or any other law for the time being in force, to a penalty not exceeding the value of the goods or five thousand rupees, whichever is the greater; (ii) in the case of dutiable goods, other than prohibited goods, subject to the provisions of section 114A, to a penalty not exceeding ten per cent. of the duty sought to be evaded or five thousand rupees, whichever is higher: Provided that where such duty as determined under sub- section (8) of section 28 and the interest payable thereon under section 28AA is paid within thirty days from the date of communication of the order of the proper officer determining such duty, the amount of penalty liable to be paid by such person under this section shall be twenty-five per cent. of the penalty so determined; (iii) in the case of goods in respect of which the value stated in the entry made under this Act or in the case of baggage, in the declaration made under section 77 (in either case hereafter in this section referred to as the declared value) is higher than the value thereof, to a penalty not exceeding the difference between the declared value and the value thereof or five thousand rupees, whichever is the greater; (iv) in the case of goods falling both under clauses (i) and (iii), to a penalty [not exceeding the value of the goods or the difference between the declared value and the value thereof or five thousand rupees, whichever is the highest; (v) in the case of goods falling both under clauses (ii) and (iii), to a penalty not exceeding the duty sought to be evaded on such goods or the difference between the declared value and the value thereof or five thousand rupees, whichever is the highest. 8.1. The essential ingredients for invoking the provisions of Section 112 are as under: a) act or omission on part of person which rendered the goods liable to confiscation under section 111 ibid. b) knowledge or c) reason to belief on the part of the person who is in any way concerned in any goods which he knows or has reason to believe are liable to confiscation under section 111 ibid. 8.2. We find that the ingredients for invocation of section 112 are completely absent in the instant case. We also find that there are no specific allegation/findings regarding contravention of section 112 (a) and/or section 112 (b) of Customs Act, 1962, in the impugned order. 8.3. The Hon’ble Delhi High Court in the case Commissioner of Customs Vs. Trinetra Impex Pvt. Ltd – 2020(372) ELT 332 (Del) on imposition of penalty under section 112 (b) held that the said provisions clearly reveals that the penalty therein can be imposed wherever there is an element of mens rea or conscious knowledge, which is a sine qua non for imposition of the penalty. This is evident from a plain reading of Sections 112 of the Act, which uses the expressions “does or omits to do”, “or abets the doing or omission of such act”, “which he knows or has reason to believe are liable to confiscation under Section 111″. From the facts of the case, we find that there is no such element of mens rea or conscious knowledge established on the part of the appellants. 8.4. We also find that the ld. Adjudicating Authority has imposed penalty under section 112(a)/112(b) of Customs Act without disclosing specific contravention of section 112(a) and 112 (b) of Customs Act. The specific section under which the penalty has been imposed also not clear from the impugned order. Accordingly, we hold that the penalties imposed on the appellants under sections 112(a)/(b) of the Customs Act, 1962 is not sustainable and hence, we set aside the same. Accordingly, we answer to question no. (iv) in para 6.1 above is in the negative. 9. In view of the above discussions, we summarize the answers to the questions raised in para 6.1 supra as follows: (i) We hold that the evidence on record does not indicate that the gold seized is of foreign origin and smuggled from Bangladesh. (ii) We hold that the provisions of Section 123 of Customs Act, 1962 is not applicable in this case and the burden of proving that the gold seized is not of smuggled in nature is not on the appellants. (iii) We hold that the evidences submitted by the appellants establish that the gold weighing 3152.900 were procured from domestic sources and hence not liable for confiscation. Accordingly, the absolute confiscation of the gold is set aside. (iv) We hold that the penalties imposed on the appellants under sections 112(a)/(b) of the Customs Act, 1962 is not sustainable and hence, we set aside the same. 10. Regarding the Indian Currency of Rs. 97,000/- confiscated from the appellants, we observe that the said currency was confiscated in terms of section 121 of the Customs Ac, 1962, on the ground that it was sale proceeds of smuggled gold. However, we find that the investigation has not brought in any evidence to substantiate this allegation. Accordingly, we hold that the Indian Currency is not liable for confiscation and hence we set aside the same. 11. In view of the above discussions, (i) We set aside the confiscation of the gold weighing 3152.900 grams seized from all the three appellants together. (ii) We set aside the penalties imposed on all the three appellants. (iii) We set aside the confiscation of Indian Currency of Rs. 97,000/-. (iv)Thus, the appeals filed by the appellants are allowed with consequential relief, if any, as per law.

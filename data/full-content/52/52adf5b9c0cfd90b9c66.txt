Summary: The India–UAE Double Taxation Avoidance Agreement (DTAA) provides tie-breaker rules under Article 4 to resolve situations where an individual qualifies as a resident of both countries. The article explains the distinction between domestic tax residence and treaty residence, emphasising that a UAE residence visa, Emirates ID, company licence or Tax Residency Certificate does not automatically establish eligibility for treaty benefits. Individuals claiming UAE residence under the treaty must examine the specific requirement of physical presence for at least 183 days during the relevant calendar year. Where dual residence exists, the treaty applies a sequential examination of permanent home, centre of vital interests, habitual abode, nationality and mutual agreement between competent authorities. Practical examples illustrate how family connections, residential accommodation, employment, business operations, banking arrangements and actual management responsibilities influence the residence determination. The article also discusses the separate place of effective management test for companies and the importance of genuine commercial substance in UAE business arrangements. Treaty residence does not eliminate India’s right to tax income under applicable source-based treaty provisions. Additional guidance covers Tax Residency Certificates, Form 41 under the Income-tax Act, 2025, earlier Form 10F requirements, documentary evidence and differences between Indian tax-year and UAE calendar-year calculations. A practical checklist helps entrepreneurs assess their treaty position, maintain consistent records and identify common mistakes before claiming relief under the India–UAE DTAA. When both countries have a valid residence claim, counting days alone may not settle the final treaty position. Article 4 of the India–UAE Double Taxation Avoidance Agreement contains tie-breaker rules for such cases. These rules determine the country in which a dual-resident individual will be treated as resident for applying the treaty. The sequence generally examines: Permanent home; Centre of vital interests; Habitual abode; Nationality; and Mutual agreement between the competent authorities. The order matters. A taxpayer should not begin with nationality or simply select the country in which more days were spent. Each test is considered only when the preceding test does not resolve the residence conflict. For Dubai-based Indians, the most important practical point is that a UAE visa, Emirates ID or company license does not establish treaty residence. The individual must first qualify as a resident under Article 4 before the tie-breaker rules can be used. Why dual residence can arise India and the UAE have their own domestic residence frameworks. India generally determines individual tax residence using physical-presence tests and additional rules applicable to Indian citizens and persons of Indian origin. An individual may become resident because of: Presence in India for 182 days or more during the relevant tax year; Presence in India for at least 60 days during that year and 365 days during the preceding four years; The modified 120-day rule applicable to certain visiting Indian citizens or persons of Indian origin; Special rules concerning an Indian citizen leaving for employment abroad; or The deemed-residence provision applicable in specified circumstances. The UAE domestic framework considers factors such as: Physical presence for at least 183 days in a relevant 12-month period; Physical presence for at least 90 days together with specified UAE connections; The individual’s usual or primary residence; and The location of the individual’s financial and personal interests. The two countries do not use identical tests or identical measuring periods. India normally applies a tax year running from 1 April to 31 March, while relevant UAE tests may use a consecutive 12-month period or calendar year, depending on the purpose. Consequently, the same person may satisfy both domestic frameworks. Domestic residence must be tested first The treaty tie-breaker is not the first step in a residence analysis. The correct sequence is: 1. Determine whether the individual is resident in India under Indian domestic law. 2. Determine whether the individual qualifies as a UAE resident under the treaty definition. 3. Apply the tie-breaker only if both residence claims exist for the relevant period. If an individual is non-resident under Indian domestic law, there may be no dual-residence conflict to resolve. Similarly, if the individual does not qualify as a UAE resident for purposes of the India–UAE treaty, the tie-breaker cannot be used to create UAE treaty residence. This distinction is frequently overlooked. UAE domestic residence is not automatically UAE treaty residence The UAE domestic rules permit residence to be established through more than one route. For example, an individual may satisfy a 90-day test where the prescribed UAE residence, employment, business or permanent-home conditions are also met. However, the India–UAE DTAA contains its own specific definition for an individual claiming UAE residence. Article 4 generally requires the individual to be present in the UAE for an aggregate period of at least 183 days in the calendar year concerned. Therefore, an Indian entrepreneur who spends 130 days in Dubai may potentially satisfy a UAE domestic residence condition but may not satisfy the India–UAE treaty’s specific 183-day definition. In that situation, the individual should not proceed directly to the permanent-home or centre-of-vital-interests tests. The threshold question—whether the person is a UAE resident under Article 4—has not yet been resolved in the person’s favour. A treaty tie-breaker does not alter domestic law Suppose an individual is resident in India under Indian law but is treated as resident of the UAE after applying Article 4. The treaty result does not rewrite the person’s historical travel record or erase the domestic residence condition. Instead, it determines how the person is treated for the purposes of applying the India–UAE DTAA. India may continue to tax income that the treaty permits it to tax. The person may also retain Indian return-filing, reporting or documentation obligations. The practical result must be examined article by article for: Employment income; Business profits; Dividends; Interest; Royalties; Capital gains; Directors’ fees; Professional income; Immovable-property income; and Other income. Treaty residence is important, but it is not a blanket exemption from Indian taxation. The tie-breaker sequence Where an individual qualifies as resident of both India and the UAE under Article 4, the treaty applies a sequence of connecting factors. The analysis proceeds through the following stages: 1. Permanent home; 2. Centre of vital interests; 3. Habitual abode; 4. Nationality; and 5. Mutual agreement. A later test should not replace a conclusion already reached under an earlier test. For example, if an individual has a permanent home available only in Dubai, it may be unnecessary to examine nationality. If permanent homes are available in both countries, the analysis moves to the centre of vital interests. First test: permanent home The first question is whether the individual has a permanent home available in India, the UAE or both. A permanent home does not necessarily mean a property owned by the taxpayer. It may include: Owned apartment or villa; Long-term rented accommodation; Family home continuously available to the individual; Employer-provided residence; or Another dwelling maintained for continuing personal use. The important issue is whether the home is genuinely and continuously available, rather than being temporary accommodation used for a short visit. A hotel room booked for a business trip would ordinarily carry less weight than a Dubai apartment maintained throughout the year. A property rented to an unrelated tenant and unavailable to the owner may not have the same significance as a vacant residence retained for personal use. When a family home remains available in India An Indian moving to Dubai may retain a house in India where the spouse, children or parents continue to live. If the individual can use that property whenever visiting India, the home may remain available for treaty purposes. The fact that the property is occupied by family members does not necessarily remove it from consideration. Relevant evidence may include: Ownership documents; Lease agreements; Utility bills; Access and use of the property; Location of personal possessions; Family residence; Frequency of stays; and Whether the property has been leased to a third party. Selling an Indian home is not a mandatory condition for becoming a UAE treaty resident. However, continuing availability of the home may cause the analysis to move to the next test. Example: home available only in Dubai Assume Arjun relocates to Dubai with his spouse and children. He rents an apartment under an annual tenancy contract and uses it as the family’s regular home. His former Indian property has been leased to an unrelated tenant under an agreement that does not permit Arjun to use it during the tenancy. If Arjun otherwise qualifies as resident in both countries, the permanent-home test may favour the UAE because a home is continuously available there while the Indian property is not available for his personal use. The complete facts and period of availability must still be verified. Example: homes available in both countries Assume Kavita maintains a Dubai apartment and also owns a furnished house in India that remains available throughout the year. Because permanent homes are available in both countries, the first test does not settle the conflict. The analysis must move to Kavita’s centre of vital interests. It would be incorrect to stop the review merely because she has an Ejari tenancy contract in Dubai. Second test: centre of vital interests Where permanent homes are available in both countries, the treaty asks where the individual’s personal and economic relations are closer. This is known as the centre of vital interests. It is generally the most fact-sensitive part of the tie-breaker analysis. No single document or factor automatically determines the outcome. The complete pattern of the individual’s life and business must be considered. Personal relations Personal connections may include: Location of spouse and dependent children; Children’s school; Residence of close family; Social relationships; Membership of clubs and professional bodies; Community involvement; Medical and personal arrangements; Location of personal possessions; and Place where the individual normally returns after travelling. Family location is often influential, but it is not necessarily decisive in every case. A spouse may remain in India temporarily because of education, medical care or another transitional reason while the individual’s long-term life has genuinely moved to Dubai. The reason, duration and surrounding evidence should be considered. Economic relations Economic connections may include: Place of employment; Location of active business operations; Country from which companies are managed; Principal source of income; Location of investments; Banking and financial relationships; Place where commercial risks are controlled; Location of business assets; Directorships and management responsibilities; and Place where major financial decisions are made. The value of assets is not the only consideration. A person may own substantial passive investments in India while carrying on the principal business and employment activities in Dubai. Conversely, holding a UAE company license will carry limited weight where the company has no genuine operations and every commercial decision is made from India. Why business management matters Indian entrepreneurs sometimes obtain a Dubai investor visa, lease a flexi-desk and open a UAE bank account while continuing to manage the business from India. Such facts may weaken the claim that the centre of economic relations has moved to the UAE. Authorities may examine: Where customer contracts are approved; Where pricing decisions are made; Who controls internet banking; Where senior employees report; Where board and management meetings occur; Where the owner performs daily work; Where business records are maintained; and Whether the UAE company has employees and operating expenditure. The treaty analysis follows substance rather than the address printed on the trade license. Personal and economic factors may point in different directions A common difficulty arises where personal relations remain in India but economic relations move to Dubai. For example, an entrepreneur may operate a genuine UAE company and work full-time in Dubai while the spouse and children temporarily remain in India. The treaty does not provide a mathematical score. The weight of each connection depends on its quality, permanence and significance. The following questions may help: Is the separation temporary or open-ended? Where is the principal family home? Where does the person spend non-working time? Which country is the base for day-to-day life? Where are the individual’s principal responsibilities? Is the UAE business genuine and operational? Does the person continue to manage Indian businesses actively? Which connections are historical, and which reflect current life? A written factual analysis is advisable where the connections are divided. Example: centre of vital interests in Dubai Assume Sameer has permanent homes available in both India and Dubai. During the relevant period: His spouse and children live with him in Dubai; His children attend school in Dubai; He works from the UAE; His operating company has employees and an office in Dubai; Management decisions are made in the UAE; His Indian property is retained mainly as an investment; and Indian visits are limited. These facts may support a UAE centre of vital interests, provided Sameer first satisfies the treaty’s UAE residence definition. Example: centre of vital interests in India Assume Neeraj spends sufficient time in the UAE to satisfy the treaty’s physical-presence condition and rents an apartment in Dubai. However: His spouse and children live permanently in India; He maintains the principal family home in India; He manages an Indian company; Major customer and investment decisions are made in India; The Dubai company has no operating employees; and He returns to India whenever not travelling for business. Although Neeraj has a physical presence and home in Dubai, the overall personal and economic connections may point more strongly to India. Third test: habitual abode If the centre of vital interests cannot be determined, or the individual has no permanent home available in either country, the treaty considers habitual abode. Habitual abode is broader than a mechanical day-count comparison. It examines where the person normally or customarily lives. Relevant matters may include: Frequency of stays in each country; Duration of visits; Pattern of travel over a meaningful period; Regularity of presence; Nature of accommodation; Daily routine; and Whether presence is part of settled life or occasional travel. The analysis should consider the individual’s complete pattern of living rather than only an isolated trip. Habitual abode is not always the country with more days Suppose an individual spends 190 days in the UAE, 150 days in India and the remaining days travelling elsewhere. The UAE has the higher day count, but that fact alone may not complete the habitual-abode analysis. It remains necessary to examine whether the stays in the UAE form part of the person’s established life and whether Indian stays show a separate settled pattern. Day counts are important evidence, but habitual abode concerns the frequency, duration and regularity of living arrangements. Fourth test: nationality If habitual abode does not resolve the residence conflict, the treaty moves to nationality. An individual who is an Indian citizen and not a UAE national would ordinarily be treated as resident of India at this stage. A UAE residence visa, Emirates ID or Golden Visa does not amount to UAE nationality. Immigration residence and nationality are legally distinct. For many Indian expatriates, the nationality test will therefore favour India if the earlier tests remain inconclusive. This is why a UAE-residence position should not be built on ambiguous facts with the expectation that nationality will help. In most cases involving an Indian citizen living in Dubai, it will not. Fifth test: mutual agreement If the individual is a national of both countries or neither country and the earlier tests do not resolve the matter, the competent authorities must settle the question by mutual agreement. The mutual agreement procedure is a government-to-government process under the treaty. It is not an informal agreement between the taxpayer and the tax officer. The taxpayer presents the case to the competent authority within the applicable treaty time limit, supported by a detailed explanation of: Domestic residence claims; Treaty provisions; Tax assessments or withholding positions; Travel history; Homes; Family and economic connections; Income involved; and Double taxation or other treaty-inconsistent treatment. Following the Multilateral Instrument modification, a case concerning taxation not in accordance with the treaty generally must be presented within three years from the first notification of the action giving rise to the issue. The process should not be treated as a substitute for maintaining evidence from the beginning. Tie-breaker rules for companies The permanent-home, centre-of-vital-interests, habitual-abode and nationality sequence applies to individuals. A different rule applies to a person other than an individual. Under Article 4 of the India–UAE DTAA, where a non-individual person is resident of both countries, it is deemed resident of the country in which its place of effective management is situated. For a UAE company, treaty residence already requires careful consideration because Article 4 refers to a company incorporated in the UAE and managed and controlled wholly in the UAE. A Dubai incorporation certificate is therefore not the complete test. Place of effective management of a UAE company The place of effective management generally concerns where the key management and commercial decisions necessary for conducting the business as a whole are made in substance. Relevant factors can include: Location of board and senior-management meetings; Place where strategic decisions are made; Authority over customer contracts; Control of bank accounts; Location of senior executives; Place where budgets and business plans are approved; Location of accounting and corporate records; and Whether UAE directors exercise genuine authority. Consider an Indian-owned Dubai company whose trade license, bank account and registered office are in the UAE. If all decisions are made by the shareholder from India and the UAE director merely signs documents, the company may face both Indian domestic residence and treaty-residence concerns. Board minutes prepared in Dubai will not necessarily outweigh evidence showing that the actual decision-making occurred in India. Treaty residence should not be confused with permanent establishment A conclusion that an individual or company is resident of the UAE for treaty purposes does not mean India has no taxing rights. India may still tax income where the treaty permits taxation because of: Indian immovable property; Indian permanent establishment; Employment exercised in India; Directors’ fees from an Indian company; Indian-source dividends, interest or royalties; Taxable capital gains; or Other source-based provisions. Residence determines which treaty articles and relief mechanisms apply. It does not eliminate source-country taxation permitted by those articles. Tax Residency Certificate and supporting documentation A person seeking India–UAE treaty benefits should ordinarily obtain a Tax Residency Certificate from the relevant tax authority. For a UAE individual, an application for an India–UAE treaty-purpose TRC should be supported by evidence including: Passport; Emirates ID; Official entry-and-exit report; UAE salary or income evidence; Residence documents; and Any additional evidence required under the treaty. The certificate should cover the relevant period. A TRC is fundamental evidence, but it does not conclusively settle the tie-breaker. Indian authorities may still examine whether: The individual satisfies the treaty’s UAE definition; A permanent home remains available in India; Personal and economic relations are closer to India; The certificate covers the correct period; The treaty claim is consistent with actual conduct; and Applicable anti-abuse provisions are satisfied. Current Indian documentation A UAE resident claiming treaty relief in India must also comply with the prescribed Indian documentation requirements. For tax years governed by the Income-tax Act, 2025, the relevant additional information is furnished through Form 41 under the applicable rules. Earlier periods governed by the Income-tax Act, 1961 may involve Form 10F. Depending on the transaction, the taxpayer may also need: Permanent Account Number or permitted alternative details; Beneficial-ownership declaration; No-permanent-establishment declaration; Income classification note; Withholding-tax documentation; Contract and invoice; Travel records; and Tax-return disclosures. Possession of a UAE TRC should not lead to incomplete Indian compliance. Evidence for each tie-breaker stage A strong residence file should be organised according to the treaty sequence. Permanent home evidence Useful records may include: UAE tenancy contract or title deed; Indian property documents; Lease granted to a third party; Utility bills; Evidence of continuous availability; Family occupation; and Records of actual use. Centre-of-vital-interests evidence Useful records may include: Spouse and children’s residence; School enrolment; Employment contract; UAE company documents; Board and management records; Bank mandates; Details of active businesses; Investment statements; Insurance and medical records; and Evidence of social and community connections. Habitual-abode evidence Useful records may include: Official immigration report; Passport and travel history; Airline tickets; Accommodation records; Calendar of physical presence; and Explanation of the purpose and pattern of stays. Nationality evidence Relevant evidence generally includes the individual’s passport and citizenship documentation. The documents should be consistent. A UAE-residence claim can be weakened where the TRC application, Indian return, banking declarations and immigration records present different factual positions. Timing mismatch between India and the UAE The India–UAE residence analysis can be complicated by different measurement periods. Indian residence is generally examined for an April-to-March tax year. The treaty definition of a UAE-resident individual refers to physical presence in the calendar year concerned. An individual may therefore need two separate travel computations: Indian presence during the relevant Indian tax year; and UAE presence during the relevant calendar year. For example, a person moving to Dubai in July may become non-resident in India for a particular Indian tax year depending on the applicable Indian test, yet fail to reach 183 UAE days in the first calendar year. The individual should not assume that one spreadsheet covering April to March is sufficient for both analyses. Practical tie-breaker review Before claiming UAE treaty residence, an individual should work through the following questions in order: 1. Is the individual resident in India under the domestic law applicable to the relevant tax year? 2. Has the individual spent at least 183 days in the UAE during the calendar year required by Article 4? 3. Is an appropriate UAE treaty-purpose TRC available? 4. Is a permanent home available in India, the UAE or both? 5. Where are the individual’s spouse and dependent children living? 6. Where is the principal employment or active business? 7. Where are important commercial and investment decisions made? 8. Where does the individual ordinarily return after travel? 9. What does the longer-term pattern of physical presence show? 10. Does nationality resolve the issue if the earlier tests remain inconclusive? 11. Are Indian forms, returns and withholding documents consistent with the treaty position? 12. Does India retain source-based taxing rights over the relevant income? A written residence memorandum is advisable where the tax exposure is material or the individual has substantial connections with both countries. Common mistakes Common errors in India–UAE tie-breaker cases include: Starting with nationality instead of permanent home; Treating an Emirates ID as proof of treaty residence; Relying on the UAE domestic 90-day condition for the India–UAE treaty; Ignoring the treaty’s 183-day calendar-year condition; Counting days only under the Indian April-to-March year; Assuming property ownership automatically determines permanent home; Ignoring a family home that remains available in India; Treating the centre of vital interests as a pure income comparison; Relying only on the country with the larger day count; Equating a UAE Golden Visa with UAE nationality; Believing a TRC conclusively settles dual residence; Ignoring Indian-source income after claiming UAE residence; Managing a UAE company entirely from India; Using board minutes that do not reflect actual decisions; and Maintaining inconsistent positions across tax returns, banks and government applications. Conclusion The India–UAE DTAA tie-breaker rules provide an orderly method for resolving genuine dual residence, but they do not allow a taxpayer to choose a preferred country. The analysis begins only after the individual qualifies as resident of both countries under Article 4. It then examines permanent home, centre of vital interests, habitual abode, nationality and, where necessary, mutual agreement between the competent authorities. For most Indian entrepreneurs living in Dubai, the decisive questions are not limited to travel days. Family location, availability of homes, place of employment, business management, banking control and the individual’s established pattern of life can all influence the outcome. A credible UAE treaty-residence position should be supported by real relocation, consistent conduct and contemporaneous records. When the personal and economic facts point in different directions, the residence analysis should be completed before filing the return or claiming treaty relief—not after the position is challenged.

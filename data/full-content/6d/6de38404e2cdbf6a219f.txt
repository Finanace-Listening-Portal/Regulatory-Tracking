We didn't anticipate, at least at that time. So this surprise is what is getting built into the forecast. It's not that fundamentally things have changed. I think we underestimated the activity," he said. Advt Q2 momentum, exports provide supportJoshi said high-frequency indicators and the second-quarter performance have remained strong, with exports also providing support to the growth outlook. While some moderation could emerge in the second half, he indicated that the full-year growth outcome could remain close to the revised 7% projection.The composition of exports, however, remains an area to watch. India has historically seen services gain share in its export basket, while the recent acceleration in goods exports marks a shift that needs to be tested for sustainability. This also places greater importance on the country's expanding network of free trade agreements, particularly in labour-intensive manufacturing.“India had a key disadvantage in labour-intensive products, textiles, etc. because the other countries used to get preferential treatment on tariffs, lower tariffs. So now I think that is getting equalized. So that improves your chances of competing. So that is only the first part. The first benefit will go to the labour-intensive segment," Joshi said.FTAs could broaden growth beyond exportsJoshi said the impact of FTAs on sectors such as hard-core manufacturing and defence is likely to take longer to materialise, making domestic competitiveness critical to converting market access into sustained export growth. Advt “After that, I think the other sectors, which are hard-core manufacturing-related, defence, etc., they are medium-term play. I think they won't show up immediately. They'll take four to five years to show up, depending on what we do domestically as well.”He also pointed to investment commitments embedded in some of India's newer trade agreements, which could bring capital and technology alongside market access.“There is some foreign-directed investment also, which is tied to the FTAs. Not all FTAs, but at least the New Zealand and the EFTA, I think they had commitment of foreign-directed investment as well. So that also brings in investment technology. So all these things together will allow you to move faster on the export front," he added. Advt For Joshi, however, the trade agreements alone will not guarantee a durable advantage.“But I think the precondition there is, for a sustained advantage, you need to improve your domestic competitiveness.”FTA impact to be measured through FDI, jobs and remittancesResponding further to ETBFSI's question, Deepa Kumar, Head of Asia Pacific Country Risk, S&P Global Market Intelligence said the success of newer FTAs should increasingly be assessed beyond their impact on merchandise exports, particularly as investment provisions and services become a larger part of India's trade strategy.She highlighted the difference between headline FDI numbers and actual net flows, arguing that the investment impact of FTAs needs closer scrutiny. Advt “Gross number is great. When you look at net number, not so great. Is FTA, can they start unlocking that? That's one outcome. The second is, and we talk about this a lot, is a lot of FTAs are traditionally focused on goods," she said.Kumar also flagged services as a critical area given India's growing role in global services trade.“One of India's biggest trends, and again, like one of the panelists was saying it today, is how we have grown in services and how we continue growing in services. So what are we looking at in these new FTAs that are about services?”She said overseas employment and the resulting remittance flows could emerge as another important measure of whether India's trade agreements are delivering broader economic gains.“So the fact that we're trying to facilitate more employment happening in different countries and the knock-on effect of that is remittances, which is already at 3.5% of GDP. Does that grow further, especially so? I feel like there are two other outcomes of FTAs that now we are very keen to look at. One is the level of investment, and second is the level of employment generated for Indians in different parts of the globe and what that does for remittances," Kumar added.The broader S&P Global assessment sees private investment, FDI, trade agreements, deeper capital markets and stronger financial intermediation as key components of India's next growth phase.

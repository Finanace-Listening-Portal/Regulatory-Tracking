Vs ACIT (ITAT Delhi) Standard Airline and Hotel Booking Platform Fees Are Outside Article 12 of the India–US DTAA Where Customers Receive No Rights in the Technology and No Technical Know-How Is Made Available The Dispute Before the Tribunal The Delhi Income Tax Appellate Tribunal allowed three appeals by Sabre GLBL Inc., a US tax resident providing technology solutions to the travel and tourism industry. The dispute concerned the taxability of fees for airline and hotel solution products delivered through Sabre’s proprietary software. In the lead appeal for AY 2020–21, the Assessing Officer brought ₹59,25,92,157 to tax as fees for technical services (FTS) under section 9(1)(vii) of the Income-tax Act, 1961, and fees for included services (FIS) under Article 12(4) of the India–US DTAA, following the directions of the Dispute Resolution Panel. How the Booking Services Were Provided Sabre facilitated airline reservations and bookings under agreements with Indian and international airlines. It also had agreements with hotels in India. Sabre submitted that it had no permanent establishment in India and that the systems used to deliver the services were located outside India. Its case was that customers received a standard technology-enabled service through offshore systems and servers, with the service being provisioned without human intervention. Sabre relied on Bharti Cellular Ltd. and Kotak Securities Ltd. in support of its arguments on automation and standardised facilities, respectively. Sabre also submitted that customers received training to operate the products. According to the company, this training enabled customers to use the service while they continued to depend on Sabre for the underlying technology. Customers could use the booking functions without acquiring the technical knowledge, skill or know-how needed to apply that technology independently. That distinction formed the basis of Sabre’s argument under the treaty’s “make available” test. Why the Group Company’s Decision Mattered Sabre relied on Sabre Marketing Nederland v. ACIT, ITA Nos. 410, 7534 and 381/Del/2019, decided on 7 January 2026. It submitted that the Delhi ITAT had already examined materially identical facts in the case of its group company. The Revenue relied on the Assessing Officer’s findings. The Tribunal followed the coordinate bench and reproduced the passages dealing with royalty under the India–Netherlands DTAA. The earlier bench had examined what customers actually acquired under the arrangements. It found that the customers were given no right to use the provider’s intellectual property and received no secret process or formula. The precedent drew on CIT v. Telstra Singapore Pte. Ltd., 467 ITR 302 (Delhi), and DIT v. New Skies Satellite BV, 382 ITR 114 (Delhi). The latter also supported the proposition that a domestic amendment expanding the scope of royalty does not automatically enlarge the treaty definition. The coordinate bench also considered Salesforce.com Singapore Pte. v. DDIT (IT), 137 taxmann.com 3 (Delhi ITAT), for the specific limb dealing with information concerning industrial, commercial or scientific experience. In Salesforce, the provider processed customers’ data and supplied the resulting reports through a hosted platform. Customers obtained the output without acquiring the provider’s methodology or experience. The Sabre group precedent applied that reasoning to the royalty issue before it. What the Delhi ITAT Decided The Tribunal applied the reasoning in Sabre Marketing Nederland and decided grounds 1 to 7 in Sabre GLBL’s lead appeal in the assessee’s favour. Those grounds challenged the classification of the receipts as FTS under section 9(1)(vii) and FIS under Article 12(4). They also covered offshore automated delivery, standardised services, the scope of customer training and the Assessing Officer’s reliance on the proposed UN Model provision for automated digital services. The Tribunal applied its finding to the other two assessment years and allowed all three appeals. Author’s Comments The practical question under the “make available” test is what the customer can do with the technical knowledge after receiving the service. An airline employee may learn to make or amend a reservation using Sabre’s platform. That training gives the employee a skill in operating the product. The relevant treaty enquiry is whether the airline thereby acquires the underlying technical capability to perform the service independently. The agreement and training materials can help establish what knowledge, if any, passed to the customer. The operative result in Sabre GLBL concerns the FTS and FIS additions, while the reasoning reproduced from the group company’s order concerns royalty under a different treaty. The distinction matters when citing the decision. The Tribunal disposed of the FTS and FIS grounds by following the coordinate bench, with no separate, detailed analysis of each limb of Article 12(4) of the India–US DTAA. The ruling is therefore best cited for its treatment of these standard booking platform receipts on the facts before the Tribunal, with the customer’s contractual rights and the scope of training central to any comparison. These are a batch of three appeals pertaining to the same assessee. Since the issues are common across these appeals, all these three appeals are being disposed of through a single order. For the purposes of this adjudication, ITA 2211/Del/2023 pertaining to AY 2020- 21, is being taken as the lead case. 2. ITA 2211/Del/2023 arises from order of Ld. Assessing Officer, order dated 15.06.2023, passed u/s 143(3) r.w.s. 144C(13) of the Income Tax Act, 1961 (hereafter as “the Act”). 2.1 In this case, the assessee is a company incorporated in USA. During the year under consideration, there are seen to be earnings as fees from various entities amounting to Rs.59,25,92,157/-. The assessee is a tax resident of USA and provides technology solutions to the Global Travel & Tourism Industry. The primary activity of the assessee in India is to facilitate airline reservations and bookings and for this purpose, the assessee is seen to have entered into agreements with various Indian and International Airlines. We find that there are agreements with various hotels in India also. The assessee claimed the entire receipts as exempt from taxation in India on the ground that there is no office or any physical business in India. Moreover, there is a claim that the receipts are not taxable as Royalty or Fees for Technical Services (FTS)/FIS under the India US – DTAA. 2.2 The Ld. Assessing Officer has given a detailed reasoning, following the DRP’s directions dated 10.05.2023, that the entire set of receipts were taxable as FIS/FTS. 2.3 The aggrieved assessee has approached the ITAT with the following effective grounds of appeal: 1. “That on facts and circumstances of the case and in law, the Ld. Assessing Officer (hereinabove referred to as the ‘ld. AO’)/Dispute Resolution Panel (hereinabove referred to as the ‘DRP’) have erred inholding that receipts amounting to Rs.59,25,92,157/- from customers in India qualify as Fee for Technical Services (‘FTS’) under the provisions of Section 9(1)(vii) of the Income Tax Act, 1961 (‘the Act’); 2. That on the facts and circumstances of the case and in law, the Ld. AO/DRP have erred in holding that receipts amounting to Rs.59,25,92,157/- from customers in India qualify as Fee for Included Services (‘FIS’) under the provisions of Article 12(4) of the Double Taxation Avoidance Agreement between India and USA (‘the Treaty’); 3. That on facts and circumstances of the case and in law, the Ld. AO/DRP have erred in not appreciating that services provided by Appellant to its Indian customers are provisioned through system/servers located outside India, without human intervention, and thus, the services do not qualify as FTS/FIS under the provisions of the Act or the treaty, in accordance with the judgment of Hon’ble Supreme Court in the case of Bharti Cellular Ltd. vs. CIT (2010) 193 Taxman 97 (SC); 4. That on facts and circumstances of the case and in law, the Ld. AO/DRP has erred in not appreciating that the Appellant provides similar standard services and solutions to all its customers in India, and therefore the consideration received by the Appellant does not qualify as FTS/FIS under the provisions of the Act or the Treaty, in accordance with the judgement of Hon’ble Supreme Court in the case of Kotak Securities Ltd. [(2016) 67 taxmann.com 356]; 5. That on facts and circumstances of the case and in law, the Ld. AO/DRP have erred in not appreciating that services rendered by the Appellant do not “make available” any technical knowledge, skill, know-how etc. to its customers in India such that the customers can apply the same on its own without recourse to the Appellant; 6. That on facts and circumstances of the case and in law, the Ld. AO/DRP have erred in not appreciating that trainings provided by the Appellant to its customer are only for use of services and not for transfer of knowledge or skills to enable customers to apply such services independently in future. 7. That on facts and circumstances of the case and in law, the Ld. AO has erred in holding that the scope of income taxable as FIS/FTS is at par with the proposed taxation of automated digital services under the proposed Article 12B of the United Nations (‘UN’) Model Convention and therefore receipts from customers in India qualify as FTS/FIS.” …………. 3. Before us the Ld. AR argued with the help of detailed paper books and submitted that in all the three appeals the central issue was whether the fees received by the assessee for provision of Airline/Hotel Solution Products, which are implemented on its proprietary software, are chargeable to tax in India as FTS/FIS or not. It was the submission that the assessee does not have a PE in India and the systems used to provide the impugned services are located outside India. The Ld. AR, thereafter placed for our perusal a coordinate bench decision in the case of Sabre Marketing Nederland vs. ACIT, ITA Nos. 410, 7534 & 381/Del/2019, order dated 07.01.2026, admittedly a group company of the assessee. It was pointed out by the Ld. AR that an identical issue has been decided in this case in favour of the assessee. The Ld. AR stated that the facts in the present set of three appeals were identical to the facts prevailing in the Sabre Marketing Nederland (supra) case. Accordingly, the Ld. AR pointed out that in pages 7 to 16 of this order the issue has been discussed and decided in favour of the assessee. The Ld. AR took us through the order of the coordinate bench of ITAT in this regard. 3.1 The Ld. DR, on the other hand, relied on the detailed findings in the Ld. AO’s order. 4. We have considered the submissions of Ld. AR/DR. We find that the impugned issue has been decided in favour of the assessee in the case of Sabre Marketing Netherland (supra). At this stage we can do no better than to extract the relevant portions from this order and apply the same to the facts of the present three appeals. The extracts are as under: “7. In the case before us, the Assessee had not given any use or right to use any Intellectual Property Rights to the Customers. In fact this has been addressed by the Hon’ble Delhi High Court in the case of CIT vs. Telstra Singapore PTE Ltd. reported in 467 ITR 302 (Del.). The relevant operative portion of the said judgment is reproduced below: ……. 8. As per the definition of Royalty given in the Treaty, the Assessee should have given the Secret Process or Formula to the Customers in India, which is factually not done in the instant case before us. Reliance in this regard has been rightly placed by the Learned AR on the decision of the Hon’ble Delhi High court in the case of DIT vs. New Skies Satellite BV reported in 382 ITR 114 (Del.) wherein the relevant operative portion is reproduced hereunder: ——– 8.1 Though this decision was rendered in the context of functions performed by the Transponder, the analogy could be drawn to the facts of the instant case before us. One of the essences of this judgment is that any amendment made in the domestic law cannot be read automatically into the Treaty for the purpose of taxability of Royalty or otherwise. 9. The third limb of the definition of Royalty under the treaty speaks about information concerning industrial, commercial or scientific experience. To explain the same, the Learned AR rightly placed reliance on the decision of this Delhi Tribunal in the case of Salesforce.com Singapore Pte vs. DDIT (IT) reported in 137 taxmann.com 3 (Del Trib.) dated 25.03.2022 wherein the relevant operative portion is reproduced hereunder: “13. We have given thoughtful consideration to the contentions of the Ld. DR and have duly considered the written submissions. In our understanding of the facts, the assessee provides web- based online access to its customer’s data hosted on servers located in data centers maintained by the assessee outside India. The assessee does not have any data centers in India and hence it cannot be considered to have a fixed place of business in India. The assessee neither has a place of management in India nor has any equipment or personnel in India. This fact has also been accepted by the Ld. CIT(A) in his order. Therefore, in the absence of granting any control over the equipment belonging to the assessee to its customers, the allegation of the AO that the amount so received will constitute ‘Royalty’ is not acceptable. 14. Further, the assessee does not provide any information concerning industrial, commercial, scientific experience. The assessee only processes the proprietary data of the customers and provides the result in form of desired reports etc. On this count also, it cannot be said that consideration for CRM services are in the nature of royalty. 15. In our considered opinion, if the services have been rendered de hors imparting of knowledge or transfer of any knowledge, experience or skill, then such services will not fall within the ambit of article 12 of the treaty. 16. Further, by granting access to the information forming part of the database, the assessee neither shares its own experience, technique or methodology employed in evolving databases with the users, nor imparts any information relating to them. 17. In our considered view, the income earned by the assessee form the Indian customers with respect to the subscription fees for CRM cannot be taxed as royalty as per Section 9(1)(vi) of the Act as well as article 12(3) of the treaty.” 9.1 It is pertinent to note that this decision has been affirmed by the Hon’ble Delhi High Court reported in 165 taxmann.com 580 (Del HC). This decision is relied upon only for the limited purpose of non-taxability of Royalty under the Treaty using the third limb of the definition given in the Treaty. 10. In view of the aforesaid observations and respectfully following the various judicial precedents relied upon hereinabove, we hold that the amounts received by the Assessee from its Indian customers would not constitute Royalty as per Article 12(4) of India- Netherlands Treaty and accordingly the Assessee was duly justified in not offering the same to tax in India.” Respectfully following the findings given in the case of Sabre Marketing Netherland (supra), the issue pertaining to ground nos. 1 to 7 in ITA 2211/Del/203 are decided in favour of the assessee. 4.1 Considering the decision rendered in ITA 2211/Del/2023, the finding therein will apply, mutatis mutandis, to the remaining two appeals for AYs 2021-22 & 2022-23 also. 5. In the result, the three appeals of the assessee are allowed.

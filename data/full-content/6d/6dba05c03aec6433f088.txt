Vs ACIT (ITAT Rajkot) Debtors’ Silence Cannot Turn Explained Cash Deposits into Unexplained Income Under Section 68 Summary: The Rajkot ITAT deleted a ₹96.03 lakh addition under Section 68 in a demonetisation cash deposit case where the assessee traced the deposits to collections from trade debtors. The Tribunal held that the debtors’ failure to respond to notices under Section 133(6), coupled with doubts about the timing and volume of the collections, was insufficient to sustain the addition in the face of contemporaneous business records. Cash Deposits During Demonetisation Vento Foam Pvt. Ltd., a manufacturer of LDPE granules and packing materials, filed its return for AY 2017–18 declaring income of ₹10,52,230. Its books were audited under Section 44AB. During scrutiny, the Assessing Officer examined deposits of specified bank notes aggregating to ₹1,10,64,620 made during the demonetisation period. The company explained that the cash represented collections from sundry debtors against sales already recorded in its books. It furnished debtor-wise details, including names, addresses, PANs, opening balances, amounts received and closing balances. The Assessing Officer issued notices under Section 133(6) to the concerned parties. Only one party, M/s Bagtag, responded. The Assessing Officer questioned whether debtors, particularly those outside the State, would have made substantial cash payments shortly before demonetisation. He also compared the cash balance in the books with a computed average cash balance. He eventually added ₹96,03,034 under Section 68, and the CIT(A) sustained the addition. How Much Weight Can a Debtor’s Silence Carry? The Tribunal held that a third party’s failure to respond must be assessed alongside the other evidence on record. The assessee had supplied the debtors’ identity particulars and transaction details, and the Department had issued notices at the addresses furnished. In these circumstances, the absence of replies did not establish that the recorded transactions were fictitious. The Tribunal drew support from Pr. CIT v. Wel Intertrade (P.) Ltd., ITA No. 135/2023, decided by the Delhi High Court on 7 March 2023. That case concerned unsecured loans. The Rajkot Bench applied its evidentiary principle to trade collections, holding that non-response by an independent party cannot by itself justify an addition where the assessee has furnished relevant documentary evidence explaining the credit. Recovery of a Trade Receivable and the Risk of Double Taxation A credit sale creates a trade receivable. When the customer pays, the receipt increases the cash balance and reduces the amount outstanding from that customer. Here, the underlying sales had already been recorded as turnover, and the debtor accounts reflected the corresponding receivables. To sustain the addition on these facts, the Revenue needed material showing that the sales were bogus, the receivables were fictitious, or the cash came from a different source. The Tribunal found no specific defect in the sales bills, ledgers, stock records or VAT records. The Assessing Officer had also left the books undisturbed under Section 145(3). The Bench referred to Tack Exim (P.) Ltd. v. ACIT, ITA No. 324/LKW/2024, where the Lucknow ITAT held that taxing the subsequent collection of accepted sale proceeds would result in double taxation. It also discussed ACIT v. HN Agri Serve (P.) Ltd., DCIT v. Bawa Jewellers (P.) Ltd. and Bhaumik Jewellers (P.) Ltd. v. ITO in the context of demonetisation deposits and the supporting business records. The Gujarat High Court’s decision in CIT v. President Industries, 258 ITR 654 (Guj.), was cited for the distinction between gross business receipts and the income embedded in them. Can an Average Cash Balance Displace the Cash Book? The Tribunal accepted that human probability is relevant when weighing evidence and that unusual cash deposits during demonetisation warranted scrutiny. It held, however, that an estimated average could not displace the cash balance recorded in the books without identifying why the actual entries were unreliable. The timing of the receipts and the debtors’ silence, even when considered together, did not establish that the deposits represented unexplained income. The Tribunal accordingly deleted the entire addition of ₹96,03,034 and allowed the appeal. Author’s Comments The practical value of this ruling lies in tracing the money from the original sale to the bank deposit. A sales invoice explains how the receivable arose. The debtor ledger shows the amount outstanding and its subsequent reduction. The cash book and bank statement then show how the collection was recorded and deposited. Read together, these records answer the question Section 68 poses about the nature and source of the amount. The ruling is particularly useful where a customer ignores a departmental notice despite the assessee producing a coherent record of the transaction. Its application will turn on the quality of that record. A debtor-wise reconciliation of invoices, outstanding balances, receipts and bank deposits gives the appellate authority a concrete basis to assess whether an unusually large cash deposit is explained by ordinary business transactions. FULL TEXT OF THE ORDER OF ITAT RAJKOT Captioned appeal filed by the assessee, pertaining to Assessment Year (AY) 2017-18, is directed against the order under section 250 of the Income-tax Act, 1961 [hereinafter referred to as ‘the Act’] passed by the National Faceless Appeal Centre [hereinafter referred to as ‘NFAC’], dated 22.07.2025, which in turn arises out of an order passed by assessing officer u/s. 143(3) of the Act, dated 29.12.2019. 02. Brief facts of the case are that the assessee, is a company engaged in the business of manufacturing LDPE granules and plastic and rubber packing materials in EPE foam. For the assessment year 2017-18, the assessee filed its return of income on 09.09.2017, declaring total income of Rs.10,52,230/-. The books of account were duly audited under section 44AB of the Income-tax Act, 1961.The case was selected for scrutiny under CASS and notice under section 143(2) of the Act was issued. During the course of assessment proceedings, the Assessing Officer called for various details and information, which were furnished by the assessee through the e-proceeding portal. The Assessing Officer specifically sought an explanation regarding the cash/SBN deposits made into the assessee’s bank accounts during the demonetisation period. The assessee had deposited specified bank notes (SBNs) aggregating to Rs.1,10,64,620/- during the relevant period. In support of the deposits, the assessee furnished details and documentary evidence explaining the availability and source of the cash/SBNs, including the names and addresses of persons from whom cash was stated to have been received against sales of goods. However, the Assessing Officer was not satisfied with the explanation furnished by the assessee and treated an amount of Rs.96,03,034/- as unexplained cash credit under section 68 of the Act, alleging the same to be unexplained cash deposited in the bank accounts during the demonetisation period. Consequently, the assessment was completed under section 143(3) of the Act vide order dated 29.12.2019, making the aforesaid addition of Rs.96,03,034/-. The Assessing Officer also charged interest under sections 234A, 234B, 234C and 234D of the Act and initiated penalty proceedings under section 271AAC of the Act. 03. Aggrieved by the assessment order, the assessee preferred an appeal before the Ld. CIT(A) where the appeal of the assessee was dismissed by sustaining the order of the AO. 04. Still aggrieved by the order of the Ld. CIT(A) assessee is in appeal before this tribunal. 05. At the time of hearing the Ld. AR submitted that the during the course of assessment proceedings, the assessee furnished the details and explanations called for by the Assessing Officer through the e-proceeding portal. The Assessing Officer specifically examined the cash deposited in the bank accounts during the demonetisation period. The Ld. AR submitted that the assessee had deposited specified bank notes aggregating to Rs.1,10,64,620/- during the demonetisation period. The assessee explained that the cash represented realisation from sundry debtors against sales made in the ordinary course of business. In support thereof, the assessee furnished the relevant documentary evidence, including debtor-wise details, names and addresses of the debtors, PAN, opening balances, receipts and closing balances and other books of account. It was submitted that the assessee had sufficient outstanding trade receivables and the cash receipts were duly recorded in the regular books. The corresponding sales had already been recorded as business turnover and offered to tax. Thus, the subsequent realisation of the outstanding trade receivables could not be treated as a fresh unexplained credit. The Ld. AR further submitted that the Assessing Officer issued notices under section 133(6) of the Act to the concerned debtors. Out of the parties to whom notices were issued, only one party, namely M/s Bagtag, responded. The Ld. CIT(A) relied substantially upon the non-response of the remaining debtors and held that the assessee had failed to establish their identity, creditworthiness and genuineness. It was submitted that the Ld. CIT(A) observation that the assessee failed to establish the identity of the debtors was factually incorrect. The assessee had furnished the names, addresses and PAN of the debtors and the notices under section 133(6) of the Act were issued by the Assessing Officer at the addresses furnished by the assessee. Therefore, the identity of the debtors was not in dispute merely because the respective parties did not respond to the departmental notices. He further submitted that the non-response of an independent third party to a notice issued by the Department could not be attributed to the assessee. Reliance was placed upon the judgment of the Hon’ble Delhi High Court in Pr. CIT v. Wel Intertrade (P.) Ltd., ITA No.135/2023, order dated 07.03.2023, wherein the Hon’ble High Court upheld the tribunal’s finding that merely because a creditor had not responded to a notice under section 133(6) of the Act, an addition under section 68 of the Act could not be sustained when the assessee had otherwise discharged the primary onus by furnishing relevant documentary material. The Ld. AR next submitted that the very nature of the impugned receipt takes it outside the scope of section 68 of the Act. According to him, section 68 of the Act applies where a sum is found credited in the books and the assessee fails to satisfactorily explain its nature and source. In the present case, the impugned amount represented collection of existing trade receivables. The debtors were already reflected as assets in the books pursuant to sales recorded in the regular course of business. The realisation of such debtors merely reduced the outstanding receivable and increased cash/bank balance and did not constitute a fresh unexplained credit of the nature contemplated under section 68 of the Act. In support of the above proposition, reliance was placed upon the decision of the Amritsar Bench of the tribunal in ACIT v. HN Agri Serve (P.) Ltd., ITA Nos.150 & 94/Asr/2021, order dated 31.01.2023, wherein, in the context of demonetisation-period cash deposits and sundry debtors/advances to growers, the tribunal held that section 68 of the Act could not be invoked merely in respect of sundry debtors/advance to growers where the relevant balances were reflected in the books. Reliance was also placed upon DCIT v. Bawa Jewellers (P.) Ltd., ITA No.352/Del/2021, order dated 09.06.2023, wherein the Delhi Bench of the tribunal considered an addition under section 68 relating to cash deposits during the demonetisation period and, after considering the regular business records and the absence of material establishing unaccounted income, upheld deletion of the addition. The Ld. AR further relied upon Tack Exim (P.) Ltd. v. ACIT, ITA No.324/LKW/2024, order dated 29.11.2024, wherein the Lucknow Bench considered cash deposits during demonetisation, including realisation from debtors, and held that where sales were accepted, the cash deposited in the bank representing the balance of sale proceeds could not again be brought to tax, as it would amount to double taxation. The Tribunal accordingly directed deletion of the addition. The Ld. AR submitted that the principle against taxing the same business receipt twice was also supported by the judgment of the Hon’ble Gujarat High Court in CIT v. President Industries, 258 ITR 654 (Guj.), wherein it was held, in the context of undisclosed sales, that the entire sales consideration could not represent the income of the assessee and that, in the absence of material regarding suppression of the investment/cost relating to the goods sold, the entire sales could not be treated as income. The Ld. AR also placed reliance upon the recent decision of the Ahmedabad Bench in Bhaumik Jewellers (P.) Ltd. v. ITO, ITA No.1642/Ahd/2024, order dated 03.02.2026, concerning an addition under section 68 of the Act on account of demonetisation-period cash deposits. It was submitted that the tribunal considered the assessee’s explanation regarding business receipts and the evidentiary material on record and held that an addition under section 68 of the Act could not be sustained merely on suspicion where the Revenue failed to establish that the cash deposits represented unaccounted income. The Ld. AR further submitted that the Assessing Officer had neither rejected the books of account under section 145(3) of the Act nor pointed out any specific defect in the sales bills, ledger accounts, stock records or VAT returns. There was no finding that the sales were bogus, that the stock was deficient, or that the corresponding trade receivables were fictitious. It was submitted that the Assessing Officer had instead proceeded on the basis of a comparison of the cash balance with a mechanically computed average cash balance and had drawn an inference that it was improbable for debtors, particularly debtors situated outside the State, to make cash payments shortly before demonetisation. According to the Ld. AR, such an inference based on human probability could not displace the actual cash balance recorded in the regularly maintained and audited books in the absence of rejection of the books or any specific defect therein. The Ld. AR therefore submitted that the assessee had discharged the initial burden by furnishing complete particulars of the trade debtors and the corresponding transactions. Once the primary documentary evidence was furnished, the mere failure of the third parties to respond to notices under section 133(6) of the Act could not be used as the sole basis for making an addition. It was accordingly prayed that the addition of Rs.96,03,034/- made under section 68 of the Act and sustained by the Ld. CIT(A) be deleted and consequential relief may be granted to the Assessee. 06. On the other hand the Ld. DR supported the orders of the Assessing Officer and the Ld. CIT(A). The Ld. DR submitted that the assessee had deposited substantial cash/SBNs during the demonetisation period and claimed that the same represented recovery from sundry debtors. However, when the Assessing Officer independently verified the alleged debtors by issuing notices under section 133(6) of the Act, the overwhelming majority of the parties failed to respond. According to the Ld. DR, the assessee was required to establish the identity of the persons, their capacity to make the payments and the genuineness of the transactions. According to the Ld. DR, the timing and magnitude of the cash receipts raised serious doubts regarding the explanation furnished by the assessee. The Assessing Officer had therefore rightly applied the test of human probability and concluded that the explanation regarding the source of the cash deposits was not satisfactory. 07. We have carefully considered the rival submissions and perused the material available on record. We have also gone through the assessment order passed under section 143(3) of the Act and the order of the Ld. CIT(A) dated 22.07.2025. The controversy before us relates to the addition of Rs.96,03,034/- made under section 68 of the Act out of the cash/SBN deposits made by the assessee during the demonetisation period. At the outset, it is relevant to note the undisputed factual position. The assessee is engaged in the regular business of manufacturing LDPE granules and plastic and rubber packing material in EPE foam. The assessee maintains regular books of account which were duly audited under section 44AB. The assessee had disclosed its business turnover and filed its return declaring total income of Rs.10,52,230/-.During assessment, the assessee explained that the cash deposits represented realisation of outstanding trade receivables arising from sales made in the ordinary course of business. The assessee furnished debtor-wise particulars, including names, addresses, PAN, opening balance, receipts and closing balance. We find that the principal basis on which the explanation was rejected was the non-response of the concerned debtors to notices issued under section 133(6) of the Act. In our considered view, such non-response, by itself, cannot be treated as conclusive against the assessee. The assessee had furnished the particulars of the debtors and the Department issued notices at the addresses so furnished. Therefore, the fact that an independent third party did not respond to a departmental notice cannot automatically establish that the underlying transaction recorded in the assessee’s books was fictitious. The Hon’ble Delhi High Court in Pr. CIT v. Wel Intertrade (P.) Ltd (supra). specifically considered a situation where the Assessing Officer had drawn an adverse inference because the creditor did not respond to a notice under section 133(6) of the Act. The Hon’ble High Court held that such non-response, in the circumstances of that case, could not by itself justify the addition, particularly where the assessee had placed relevant documentary evidence on record. The principle is equally relevant here, subject, of course, to the factual distinction that the present case concerns trade debtors and cash realisations rather than unsecured loans. The important point is that non-response to a departmental enquiry must be considered along with the entire evidentiary material and cannot automatically replace the Revenue’s obligation to establish that the assessee’s explanation is false or that the receipt represents income from an undisclosed source. We further find considerable force in the assessee’s contention regarding the nature of the impugned amount. The corresponding sales were recorded in the regular books of account. The resulting trade receivables were also recorded in the books of account. The impugned cash receipts have been explained as subsequent realisation of those outstanding receivables. A trade debtor is an asset appearing in the books of the assessee. When the debtor pays the outstanding amount, the receivable is realised and the cash balance correspondingly increases. Such realisation, in its ordinary character, is not a fresh loan, deposit or unexplained liability. Therefore, before treating the subsequent cash realisation as unexplained under section 68 of the Act, it was necessary for the Revenue to bring material demonstrating either that the underlying sales were not genuine, that the trade receivables were fictitious, or that the cash did not in fact represent realisation of such receivables. No such specific finding has been brought to our notice. Moreover, the Assessing Officer has not rejected the books of account under section 145(3) of the Act. More importantly, no specific defect has been pointed out in the sales bills, ledger accounts, stock records or VAT records so as to demonstrate that the sales giving rise to the impugned debtors were fictitious. This factual aspect assumes significance because the entire explanation of the assessee is founded upon the existence of the trade receivables in the regular books. The decision of the Amritsar Bench in HN Agri Serve (P.) Ltd. (supra) is also relevant in this context. In that case, the tribunal considered substantial cash deposits during the demonetisation period against the backdrop of sundry debtors/advances to growers and held that section 68 of the Act could not be invoked merely in respect of such sundry debtors/advances when the relevant balances were reflected in the books. Similarly, in Tack Exim (P.) Ltd. (supra), the Lucknow Bench considered cash deposits during demonetisation, including realisation from debtors, and held that where the sales were accepted, the cash deposited representing sale proceeds could not again be brought to tax, since that would result in double taxation. The decision in Bawa Jewellers (P.) Ltd. (supra) also dealt with cash deposits during the demonetisation period and an addition under section 68 of the Act. The tribunal considered the regular business records and absence of material establishing unaccounted income and upheld deletion of the addition. The principle laid down by the Hon’ble Gujarat High Court in CIT v. President Industries, 258 ITR 654 (Guj.), though arising in the context of undisclosed sales, also illustrates the fundamental distinction between a business receipt and the income embedded therein. The Hon’ble jurisdictional High Court observed that the entire sales consideration cannot automatically be treated as income, particularly in the absence of a finding regarding suppression of the corresponding investment/cost. We also take note of the recent decision of the Ahmedabad Bench in Bhaumik Jewellers (P.) Ltd. (supra), wherein the tribunal considered an addition under section 68 of the Act relating to demonetisation-period cash deposits and examined whether the Revenue had brought sufficient material to establish that the cash represented unaccounted income. Coming to the particular reasoning adopted by the Assessing Officer, we find that considerable emphasis has been placed upon the alleged improbability of the debtors making cash payments shortly before demonetisation and upon comparison of the actual cash balance with a computed average. In our view, the test of human probability can certainly be applied while appreciating evidence, however, it cannot be used to substitute evidence where the assessee has produced contemporaneous books and primary records and the Revenue has not demonstrated any specific defect therein. The Hon’ble Supreme Court has repeatedly cautioned against sustaining additions merely on suspicion, conjectures or surmises where the factual foundation for the addition is not established by evidence. In the present case, the actual cash balance reflected in the books cannot simply be substituted by an estimated or average cash balance without first demonstrating that the books do not correctly reflect the financial affairs of the assessee. We also find that the Assessing Officer has not established that the corresponding sales were bogus or that the stock necessary for making such sales was unavailable. There is no finding of suppression of purchases, inflation of sales, discrepancy in stock, or fabrication of the corresponding debtor balances. Thus, the Revenue’s case essentially rests upon the mode and timing of realisation and the subsequent deposit of SBNs. The demonetisation period undoubtedly warranted detailed scrutiny of unusual cash deposits. However, the fact that a deposit was made during the demonetisation period does not, by itself, convert an otherwise explained business receipt into unexplained income. The ultimate question remains whether the assessee’s explanation regarding the source of the cash is supported by the books and other material and whether the Revenue has brought sufficient contrary evidence on record. In the present case, we find that the assessee had furnished the debtor-wise particulars and the corresponding balances. The identity particulars were available with the Department. The sales and receivables were recorded in the books. The books were not rejected. No specific defect in the sales, stock or accounting records has been established. The only significant adverse circumstance relied upon is the non-response of the debtors to notices under section 133(6) of the Act, coupled with an inference regarding the probability of cash realisation. In our considered view, these circumstances, taken alone or collectively in the facts of the present case, are insufficient to establish that the impugned cash represented unexplained income of the assessee. We therefore hold that the assessee has furnished a plausible and documentary-supported explanation of the source of the cash deposits and that the Revenue has failed to bring sufficient contrary material to establish that the cash represented an unexplained credit within the meaning of section 68 of the Act. The addition of Rs.96,03,034/- made under section 68 of the Act and sustained by the Ld. CIT(A) is accordingly not sustainable. Consequently, the addition is directed to be deleted. Accordingly, the grounds raised by the assessee are allowed. 08. In the result, the appeal of the assessee is allowed. Order pronounced in the open court on this 17th day of November, 2026.

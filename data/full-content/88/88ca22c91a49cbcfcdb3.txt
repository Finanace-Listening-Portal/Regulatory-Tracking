(Supreme Court of India) Summary: The Supreme Court of India, in X Vs Principal Secretary, Health and Family Welfare Department, Govt. of NCT of Delhi & Anr., Civil Appeal No. 5802 of 2022, arising out of SLP (C) No. 12612 of 2022, delivered a significant judgment on 29 September 2022 concerning the reproductive rights of unmarried women under the Medical Termination of Pregnancy Act, 1971 (MTP Act), and the Medical Termination of Pregnancy Rules, 2003. The principal question was whether an unmarried woman whose pregnancy resulted from a consensual relationship could seek termination of pregnancy between twenty and twenty-four weeks under Section 3(2)(b) of the MTP Act read with Rule 3B of the MTP Rules. The appellant, an unmarried woman aged approximately twenty-five years, was about twenty-two weeks pregnant when she approached the Delhi High Court. She stated that her partner had refused to marry her, that she lacked the financial resources necessary to raise a child independently, and that the prospect of continuing the pregnancy would cause grave injury to her mental health. She also expressed apprehension regarding the social stigma and harassment faced by unmarried mothers. By its order dated 15 July 2022, the Delhi High Court declined interim permission for termination, holding that an unmarried woman whose pregnancy arose from a consensual relationship was not expressly covered by the categories prescribed under Rule 3B. The High Court observed that the constitutional validity of the rule could be examined in the pending proceedings but that interim relief could not be granted contrary to its existing wording. The appellant consequently approached the Supreme Court, raising questions involving statutory interpretation, equality, bodily autonomy, reproductive choice and the constitutional protection of personal dignity. The Supreme Court initially considered the urgency arising from the advanced stage of pregnancy. By an interim order dated 21 July 2022, it directed the Director of the All India Institute of Medical Sciences, New Delhi, to constitute a medical board to assess whether termination could safely be undertaken. The Court directed that, if the medical board found that the procedure could be performed without danger to the appellant’s life, a team of doctors at AIIMS should carry out the termination after confirming her wishes and obtaining her written consent. The medical board subsequently examined the appellant, recorded her consent and concluded that the procedure could be undertaken safely. The termination was thereafter performed. Although the immediate medical relief had been granted, the Supreme Court proceeded to determine the substantial legal issue because the interpretation of Rule 3B affected the reproductive rights of unmarried and single women generally. The appellant argued that excluding unmarried women from access to abortion between twenty and twenty-four weeks was arbitrary and discriminatory, particularly when the consequences of an unwanted pregnancy could be equally serious regardless of marital status. The Additional Solicitor General supported an interpretation extending the protection of Rule 3B(c) to unmarried women experiencing a change in their relationship circumstances. The Court accordingly examined the statutory scheme, the amendments introduced in 2021, the legislative purpose underlying the MTP Act, and the constitutional values informing its interpretation. The central issue was not whether every pregnancy could be terminated without restriction, but whether an otherwise eligible unmarried woman could be denied access to the statutory framework solely because she had not entered into a legally recognised marriage. Examining the statutory provisions, the Supreme Court explained that Section 3 of the MTP Act establishes the conditions under which registered medical practitioners may lawfully terminate pregnancies. Under Section 3(2)(a), termination of a pregnancy not exceeding twenty weeks is permitted subject to the statutory medical opinion and other applicable requirements. Section 3(2)(b) permits termination between twenty and twenty-four weeks for prescribed categories of women where at least two registered medical practitioners form the requisite good-faith opinion concerning risk to life, grave injury to physical or mental health, or substantial risk of serious foetal abnormality. Section 3(3) expressly permits consideration of the pregnant woman’s actual or reasonably foreseeable environment when assessing the risk of injury to her health. Section 3(4) makes the pregnant woman’s consent essential, subject to the special requirements applicable to minors and women covered by the statutory guardian-consent provision. The Court also examined the presumptions concerning mental-health injury arising from contraceptive failure and pregnancies alleged to have resulted from rape. Rule 3B identifies categories eligible for termination between twenty and twenty-four weeks, including survivors of sexual assault, rape or incest; minors; women experiencing a change in marital status during pregnancy; women with specified disabilities or mental illness; cases involving substantial foetal abnormalities; and pregnancies arising in prescribed humanitarian, disaster or emergency situations. The Court observed that the 2021 amendments had broadened access to reproductive healthcare and that the statutory language reflected a movement away from restricting reproductive protection to married women. In particular, the use of the expression “any woman or her partner” in the amended contraceptive-failure provision demonstrated legislative recognition that reproductive rights are not confined to marital relationships. A narrow interpretation of Rule 3B that excluded unmarried women merely because their relationships lacked formal marital recognition would therefore undermine the purpose of the amended legislation. The Supreme Court adopted a purposive interpretation of Rule 3B(c), which refers to a change of marital status during an ongoing pregnancy, illustrated by widowhood and divorce. The Court held that the provision must be understood in the context of the broader statutory objective of enabling women facing materially changed circumstances to access safe and lawful abortion. A married woman whose relationship ends through divorce or the death of her husband may experience financial, emotional and social consequences affecting her ability or willingness to continue a pregnancy. Comparable consequences may arise where an unmarried woman’s partner abandons her, refuses to continue the relationship or otherwise withdraws support during pregnancy. The Court found no reasonable basis for treating the latter situation as categorically outside the statutory protection merely because the relationship had not been formalised through marriage. It emphasised that modern Indian society recognises diverse forms of intimate and familial relationships, including live-in partnerships, unmarried relationships, single-parent households and other non-traditional family arrangements. Referring to the principles recognised in S. Khushboo v. Kanniammal, (2010) 5 SCC 600, Badshah v. Urmila Badshah Godse, (2014) 1 SCC 188, and Deepika Singh v. Central Administrative Tribunal, 2022 SCC OnLine SC 1088, the Court observed that legal protection and social welfare benefits cannot be denied merely because individuals or families do not conform to conventional marital structures. The Court also referred to legislation governing maternity benefits, succession, adoption and guardianship, noting the progressive recognition of equal legal rights for married and unmarried women. It concluded that Rule 3B must be interpreted consistently with this development and with the MTP Act’s protective purpose. Accordingly, unmarried and single women whose circumstances fall within the properly interpreted statutory framework cannot be excluded from access to termination between twenty and twenty-four weeks merely because of their marital status. The judgment extensively examined the relationship between reproductive autonomy and the constitutional guarantees of equality, privacy, dignity and personal liberty. The Supreme Court recognised that a woman’s decision whether to continue a pregnancy involves intimate questions concerning her body, health, education, employment, family circumstances and future life. Compelling a woman to continue an unwanted pregnancy can have profound physical, psychological, social and economic consequences. Drawing upon the constitutional principles articulated in Justice K.S. Puttaswamy (Retd.) v. Union of India, (2017) 10 SCC 1, and Navtej Singh Johar v. Union of India, (2018) 10 SCC 1, the Court affirmed that bodily integrity, decisional autonomy and reproductive choice form integral components of the right to privacy and personal liberty under Article 21. It emphasised that reproductive autonomy includes the ability to make meaningful decisions concerning whether and when to become a parent, subject to the applicable statutory framework. The Court further held that distinguishing between married and unmarried women for the purpose of access to abortion, without a constitutionally sustainable justification, would offend the guarantee of equality under Article 14. The physical and mental consequences of an unwanted pregnancy do not depend upon the existence of a marriage certificate. Nor can constitutional protection be conditioned upon conformity with prevailing social expectations concerning sexuality, marriage or motherhood. The Court rejected approaches that subordinate individual reproductive choices to paternalistic assumptions about the role of women in society. It observed that a woman must be treated as an autonomous individual capable of making decisions concerning her reproductive life, rather than as a person whose choices require approval from her partner, family or community. These constitutional values reinforced the conclusion that the MTP Act and Rules must be interpreted to provide equal statutory protection to eligible married and unmarried women. The Supreme Court also considered the barriers that prevent women from obtaining safe and lawful abortion services. It observed that inadequate healthcare infrastructure, limited public awareness, social stigma, fear of prosecution and unnecessary procedural requirements frequently obstruct access to reproductive healthcare. Registered medical practitioners may be reluctant to perform otherwise lawful abortions because of the interaction between the MTP Act and the criminal law provisions governing miscarriage and termination of pregnancy. This apprehension may lead healthcare providers to insist upon additional conditions, such as consent from family members, documentary proof beyond statutory requirements or prior judicial authorisation. The Court made clear that such extra-legal requirements have no foundation in the MTP Act and should not be imposed upon women who satisfy its conditions. The Court referred to Z v. State of Bihar, (2018) 11 SCC 572, in which delays and negligence by public authorities had prevented timely access to lawful abortion and resulted in an award of compensation. It also discussed Surendra Chauhan v. State of Madhya Pradesh, (2000) 4 SCC 110, illustrating the serious dangers associated with unsafe abortions performed by unqualified persons in unapproved facilities. The judgment recognised that unmarried women may face particularly severe obstacles because of stigma surrounding premarital relationships and unmarried motherhood. The Court further examined the meaning of rape for the limited purposes of the MTP Act and recognised that sexual violence, including non-consensual sexual intercourse within marriage, may be relevant to the statutory framework governing pregnancies resulting from rape. This interpretation was directed to the operation of the MTP Act and did not determine the broader criminal-law controversy concerning the marital rape exception. The Court emphasised that the pregnant woman’s mental health must be assessed with reference to her actual circumstances and reasonably foreseeable environment, rather than through rigid assumptions concerning marital status or social acceptability. In its concluding analysis, the Supreme Court held that a purposive interpretation of Section 3(2)(b) of the MTP Act and Rule 3B of the MTP Rules was necessary to give effect to the legislative objective of protecting reproductive health and ensuring non-discriminatory access to safe abortion. The expression concerning change of marital status could not be applied so narrowly as to exclude unmarried women experiencing comparable changes in their relationships and personal circumstances. The Court affirmed that unmarried women are entitled to the same statutory protection as married women where the applicable requirements for termination between twenty and twenty-four weeks are satisfied. It also emphasised that the statutory framework must be administered consistently with constitutional commitments to equality, dignity, privacy and reproductive autonomy. In discussing the State’s positive obligations, the Court highlighted the need to remove institutional and social barriers to reproductive healthcare, promote awareness of lawful abortion services, protect confidentiality and ensure that medical practitioners do not impose conditions beyond those prescribed by law. It also clarified that its interpretation of the MTP Act did not dilute the prohibition on sex-selective practices under the Pre-Conception and Pre-Natal Diagnostic Techniques (Prohibition of Sex Selection) Act, 1994. Since the appellant had already undergone termination pursuant to the Supreme Court’s interim directions, the remaining proceedings concerned the authoritative interpretation of the statutory provisions and the rights of similarly situated women. The appeal and the transferred writ petition were disposed of accordingly. The judgment establishes that marital status alone cannot determine access to reproductive healthcare under the MTP Act and that statutory provisions governing abortion must be interpreted in a manner consistent with women’s autonomy, dignity and equal protection of the law.

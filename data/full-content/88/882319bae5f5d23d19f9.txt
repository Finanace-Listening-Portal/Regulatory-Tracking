Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); Central Organisation for Railway Electrification Vs ECI SPIC SMO MCML (JV) A Joint Venture Company (Supreme Court of India) Summary: The Supreme Court’s five-Judge Constitution Bench answered a reference concerning unilateral appointment of arbitrators and compulsory selection from panels curated by one disputing party. The majority opinion of Chief Justice D.Y. Chandrachud, joined by Justices J.B. Pardiwala and Manoj Misra, held that equality applies from the appointment stage, that a party cannot unilaterally appoint a sole arbitrator, and that a public sector undertaking cannot compel its counterparty to choose its nominee from a panel exclusively curated by that undertaking. Justices Hrishikesh Roy and P.S. Narasimha delivered separate opinions differing on material aspects of the reasoning and permissible appointment arrangements.The reference arose from conflicting decisions on the extent of party autonomy under the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996. In Voestalpine Schienen GmbH v. Delhi Metro Rail Corporation Ltd., the Court had addressed appointment from a government entity’s panel and required a broad-based panel. In TRF Ltd. v. Energo Engineering Projects Ltd., it held that a managing director who was statutorily ineligible to act as arbitrator could not nominate another arbitrator. Perkins Eastman Architects DPC v. HSCC (India) Ltd. extended the protection against an interested party’s control over appointment of a sole arbitrator.The earlier CORE decision concerned Clause 64(3)(b) of the Railways’ General Conditions of Contract. Railways would forward at least four retired railway officers’ names; the contractor would suggest at least two; and the General Manager would choose at least one as the contractor’s nominee, appoint the remaining arbitrators and identify the presiding arbitrator. That decision treated the contractor’s limited choice as a counterbalance to the General Manager’s powers. In Union of India v. Tantia Constructions Limited, a three-Judge Bench expressed prima facie disagreement and sought consideration by a larger Bench. After allowing time for government consideration of an expert committee’s recommendations, the Constitution Bench proceeded with the reference. Counsel challenging these procedures submitted that party autonomy remained subject to mandatory equality, independence and impartiality requirements. A party controlling the panel could restrict its opponent’s choice and influence the constitution of the tribunal despite the nominees’ individual eligibility. They relied on Sections 11(8), 12 and 18, the principles in TRF and Perkins, and the constitutional and public-policy reasoning considered in Lombardi Engineering Limited v. Uttarakhand Jal Vidyut Nigam Limited. Their objection was to structural imbalance in the appointment mechanism, rather than merely to the qualifications of a particular nominee. Counsel defending the arrangements stressed Section 11(2), which allows parties to agree on an appointment procedure. They distinguished a person’s ineligibility to act as arbitrator from that person’s contractual power to appoint an eligible arbitrator. They argued that Section 18 governed treatment by a constituted tribunal, while disclosures, the Fifth and Seventh Schedules, challenges under Sections 13 and 14, and award review under Section 34 supplied sufficient safeguards. They also referred to practical appointment arrangements used by government bodies and non-banking financial companies. The majority traced the Act’s foundations to party autonomy, minimum judicial intervention and the UNCITRAL Model Law. It recognised the parties’ extensive freedom over the tribunal, procedure and conduct of arbitration, but distinguished derogable provisions from mandatory protections. Agreeing to private adjudication does not permit parties to discard the essential safeguards that make adjudication fair. Equality and an independent decision-maker are integral to the legitimacy of arbitration, rather than optional contractual conveniences displaced simply by acceptance of a standard-form clause. On Section 18, the majority rejected an interpretation confining equality to hearings after the tribunal had been constituted. Examining the Model Law’s development and the statutory scheme, it held that equal treatment extends to every stage, including appointment. Otherwise, one party could obtain an advantage in constituting the forum before the provision’s safeguards began operating. Government bodies enjoy no special exemption from these standards when they arbitrate with private parties; the Act does not create a privileged appointment regime for the State or its instrumentalities. The majority examined arbitrators’ adjudicatory role and the rule against bias, including the distinction between actual bias and an objectively justified apprehension of bias. Drawing on Indian and comparative authorities, it considered how a fair-minded and informed observer would assess independence and impartiality. The absence of proven misconduct by an individual arbitrator does not by itself answer an objection to an appointment process controlled by an interested disputant. The statutory disclosure and challenge framework reinforces the requirement that adjudication inspire confidence in its neutrality. For sole arbitrators, the majority upheld the approach in TRF and Perkins: exclusive appointment power with one interested party gives rise to justifiable doubts and excludes the other party’s equal participation. In an ordinary three-member tribunal, each side’s genuine freedom to nominate its own arbitrator may provide a counterbalance, with the presiding arbitrator appointed through the agreed balanced process. That reasoning does not justify forcing one side to choose only from names selected by its opponent, especially where the opponent retains further control over the final nominee and presiding arbitrator. Applying these principles to the Railways’ mechanism, the majority found two layers of restriction on the contractor. Its suggestions had to come from the Railways’ four-name panel, and even its choice between those names remained subject to the General Manager’s selection. Meanwhile, the General Manager could appoint the remaining members from within or outside that panel and identify the presiding arbitrator. This produced unequal participation and a process prejudiced in favour of Railways. Maintaining a pool of potential arbitrators remained permissible; making the counterparty’s choice compulsorily dependent on that pool did not. The majority also examined public-private contracts through the Contract Act and Article 14. Government contracting must satisfy fairness and non-arbitrariness, and contractual acceptance does not place an unreasonable appointment mechanism beyond scrutiny. Referring to public-policy and unconscionability jurisprudence, it held that unilateral appointment clauses in public-private contracts violate Article 14. Its discussion of Section 34 connected the fairness of the arbitral process with the fundamental policy of Indian law and basic notions of justice, while recognising the limited statutory grounds for judicial interference with awards. Waiver remained subject to an express and informed post-dispute choice. The majority applied the principle in the proviso to Section 12(5) to waiver of an objection founded on unilateral appointment and the nemo judex rule. After disputes arise, parties can decide whether to waive the relevant protection through the required express agreement; an earlier contractual clause is insufficient by itself. Separately, referring to In Re: Interplay Between Arbitration Agreements and the Indian Stamp Act, the majority discussed the limited Section 11 inquiry into the existence of an arbitration agreement and the tribunal’s competence to examine jurisdiction, including a detailed challenge to the appointment clause’s validity. The majority expressly limited the temporal effect of its new ruling concerning three-member tribunals. TRF and Perkins already governed sole-arbitrator appointments, whereas departure from Voestalpine and the earlier CORE reasoning could unsettle numerous completed and ongoing arbitrations involving three-member tribunals. To avoid disturbing established commercial arrangements, it directed that the law declared in the reference would apply prospectively to arbitrator appointments made after the judgment, specifically stating that this direction applies to three-member tribunals. The reference was answered and pending applications were disposed of; the judgment did not determine the underlying contractual claims on their merits. Justice Hrishikesh Roy agreed that Section 18 operates at the appointment stage and that government parties receive no special treatment. However, he opposed importing constitutional and administrative-law principles into arbitration and disagreed with treating unilateral appointment as inherently impermissible. In his view, the statutory scheme distinguishes unilateral appointment from the appointee’s ineligibility; an eligible arbitrator could be appointed under the agreed procedure, subject to statutory safeguards. He favoured narrow judicial intervention, with Section 11 assistance where consensus failed, and examination of independence and impartiality within the Arbitration Act. Justice P.S. Narasimha emphasised the balance between party autonomy and the parties’ duty to establish an independent and impartial tribunal. He located that duty in the Arbitration Act and Sections 23 and 28 of the Contract Act, rather than constitutional or administrative law, and disagreed with extending Section 18 to obligations of contracting parties at appointment. He rejected an advance declaration invalidating every unilateral appointment clause and preferred scrutiny of each agreement’s text and context when proceedings under Sections 11, 14 or 34 arose. His conclusions required courts to invalidate arrangements that failed the independence and impartiality requirement while preserving contractual freedom where that requirement was satisfied.

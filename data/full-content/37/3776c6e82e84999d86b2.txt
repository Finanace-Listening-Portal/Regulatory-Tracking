Summary: Raising investment capital is not proof that a startup has achieved commercial success, sustainable profitability or product–market fit. Using WeWork’s rise, failed IPO and subsequent bankruptcy as an example, Aashok Kumar Sharma explains why founders should evaluate funding decisions beyond headline valuations and celebratory announcements. The article examines how liquidation preferences, anti-dilution protections, investor veto rights, ownership dilution and fund exit timelines can materially affect founders’ financial returns and control over their businesses. It introduces the Waterfall Test, under which founders calculate shareholder distributions at different exit valuations before signing investment agreements. A hypothetical D2C business example illustrates how a 2x liquidation preference can significantly reduce the founder’s share of sale proceeds, even where the headline ownership percentage appears attractive. The article also discusses the dangers of excessive valuations, undisciplined spending, inadequate due diligence and dependence on repeated fundraising. It highlights the importance of maintaining accurate capitalisation tables, monitoring cash runway, establishing measurable operating milestones and ensuring that investor expectations align with the company’s long-term objectives. Alternative financing options, including bootstrapping, customer advances, revenue-based financing, strategic partnerships and debt, are considered alongside venture capital. The Indian regulatory context includes Companies Act requirements, FEMA compliance for foreign investment and the abolition of angel tax from assessment year 2025–26. The central message is that founders should assess the actual economic and governance consequences of investment terms rather than treating the amount raised as evidence of business success. A big funding round validates an investor’s belief. Only customers and cash flow validate the business. In January 2019, WeWork’s largest investor valued the company at about US$47 billion. Its founder was a magazine-cover fixture, and money appeared limitless. Before the year ended, its planned public listing had fallen apart. In November 2023, it filed for bankruptcy protection in the United States. The billions proved that investors believed. They never proved that the business worked. Friends call. The team celebrates. And in all the excitement, one fact slips by: On the day the money landed, nothing about the business changed. The product is the same. So are the buyers and the margins. All that has changed is that the company now owes someone a return — and has agreed terms that will shape every decision it takes from here on. Funding can be powerful. It can pay for products, new markets, talent and scale faster than internal cash ever could. But it doesn’t prove that customers want what you sell, nor that the economics work. It proves only that an investor judged the possible outcome worth the risk. That judgement may be right or wrong. WeWork’s long-term lease commitments ran headlong into flexible, short-term revenue — a mismatch no amount of capital could fix. Money can buy time. It can’t buy product–market fit. What history teaches: the merchant venture Outside investment in risky ventures goes back centuries. In medieval Italian ports, the commenda contract allowed an investor to finance a merchant’s voyage in return for a share of the profit. Later, joint-stock firms funded long trading expeditions. Those early contracts already grasped what today’s founders frequently overlook: the terms decided who was paid first, who absorbed the losses and who steered the voyage. The size of the ship was never the full story. The Waterfall Test This is the most useful exercise a founder can carry out before signing a term sheet. I call it the Waterfall Test. A “waterfall” reveals how the proceeds of a future sale flow, in order, to each shareholder. Before you sign, model three outcomes: 1. The company sells for half its post-money valuation. 2. It sells for exactly its post-money valuation. 3. It sells for twice its post-money valuation. In each case, calculate what every investor and every founder actually receives. Many founders are stunned at how little reaches them in the first two scenarios. The reason lies in the terms. The bitter truths hidden in terms 1. Valuation is the headline. Terms are the reality. Liquidation preference determines who is paid first when the company is sold or wound up. Under a standard 1x non-participating preference, investors typically recover their money first — or convert to ordinary shares if that pays more. With higher multiples or “participating” preferences, investors can take their money back and share in what is left, leaving founders with far less. Anti-dilution protection can move ownership towards investors if a later round is priced lower. “Full ratchet” versions are particularly harsh on founders; “broad-based weighted average” versions are more common and gentler. Protective provisions and vetoes can give investors a say over senior hires, new debt, share issues, selling the company or changing its direction. Fix: Run the Waterfall Test. Have your lawyer explain every clause in plain language. Negotiate the terms, not just the valuation. 2. A lofty valuation can turn into a trap Raise at a price the business can’t grow into, and the next round gets harder. A “down round” can trigger anti-dilution clauses, hurt morale and weaken your bargaining position. Fix: Raise at a valuation you can convincingly beat by the next round — not the highest figure anyone will offer. 3. Dilution builds up silently Every round, option-pool top-up and convertible note shrinks the founders’ stake. After a few rounds, founders may find they no longer control their own company. Fix: Keep a clean, up-to-date cap table, and model ownership through at a minimum the next two rounds before agreeing to any new instrument. 4. Investors reset the clock Most venture funds have a fixed life — frequently about ten years — and need exits within it. Their timetable becomes yours. A company growing a profitable 20% a year can be an excellent business and still disappoint a fund that needs a far larger result. Fix: Find out your investor’s fund age, exit horizon and return targets, and ensure they fit what you want to build. 5. Money changes behaviour Easy capital frequently brings plush offices, aggressive hiring, deep discounts and marketing that buys growth rather than earning it. When the money runs low, those commitments stay. Fix: Spend raised capital as though it were the last you will ever get. Link spending to milestones that prove the model — retention, margin, repeat purchase — not vanity metrics. Quick recap The short version: a term sheet sets the rules on who gets paid, who decides and who bears the risk — read it as carefully as a loan secured on your home. Compliance is part of the deal Raising money brings legal and regulatory obligations that founders frequently underestimate. Investors will carry out due diligence. Missing board minutes, unfiled returns, unclear IP ownership, informal staff arrangements and untidy accounts can delay a round, cut the valuation or kill the deal. Fix: Do your own due diligence first. Clean books, current filings, signed employment and IP assignment agreements and an organised data room are not red tape. They are bargaining power. Alternatives worth considering Venture capital is one route, not the only one. Bootstrapping preserves control and enforces discipline, though growth may be slower. Customer prepayments and annual contracts fund growth with buyers’ money. Revenue-based financing ties repayments to revenue instead of equity. Strategic partners may bring both capital and distribution. Government schemes and credit guarantees support MSMEs without giving up equity. Debt, where cash flows are predictable, can work out cheaper than equity over time. The right choice follows from the business model — not from fashion. Signs of progress Good funding decisions prove themselves over years, not at signing. Track: Founder ownership after each round, against plan. Use of funds — money spent on what the round was raised for, not on unplanned costs. Milestones achieved before the next raise. Runway — months of cash remaining — never dropping low enough to force a rushed raise. Investor relations — regular, candid updates, bad news included. The decade ahead Funding markets run in cycles. The easy money of 2020–2021 gave way to a sharp correction, and AI startups have since drawn sizeable rounds again. Revenue-based finance, venture debt and crowdfunding offer alternatives to equity. The founders who fare best will choose capital that suits their business — not the capital that makes the best announcement. Clean books, strong governance and steady unit economics will remain the strongest bargaining chips a founder can hold. Next steps for each role Founder: run the Waterfall Test on your current or proposed term sheet at three exit values. CFO: work out runway assuming the next round slips by 12 months. Board: ask whether the company could reach profitability without raising again. Advisers: review liquidation preferences and anti-dilution clauses before the founder signs. A smaller-scale example Take a hypothetical founder of a fast-growing D2C snacks brand who is offered ₹5 crore for 25% of the company, with a 2x liquidation preference. The Waterfall Test reveals that if the business sells for ₹15 crore, the investor takes ₹10 crore off the top, leaving ₹5 crore for everyone else. The founder, who assumed she would get 75% of the proceeds, would in fact receive about a third. She negotiates a 1x non-participating preference and raises a smaller ₹3 crore round, alongside a working-capital loan secured on her receivables. Growth is a little slower. But her stake — and her share of any future sale — is far bigger. Understanding the terms changed the decision more than the headline valuation ever could. Frequently Asked Questions “Is bootstrapping better than raising money?” Neither is always better. Choose capital that suits your business model, growth needs and appetite for control. “What exactly is a liquidation preference?” A right for investors to receive a set amount from a sale or liquidation before ordinary shareholders receive anything. “When should founders take independent advice?” Before signing any term sheet. The clauses that matter most are frequently the ones discussed least. Where things stand (October 2026): In India, issuing shares involves Companies Act procedures (including private placement rules), valuation reports, filings with the Registrar and, for foreign investment, reporting under FEMA. The “angel tax” on share premiums was abolished from assessment year 2025-26 (Finance (No. 2) Act, 2024), but valuation and anti-abuse provisions remain — confirm the current position with advisers before each round. The toughest pushback — and a reply The pushback: “In winner-takes-most markets, speed is everything. If we don’t raise big and grow fast, a better-funded rival will take the market. Worrying about terms is a luxury.” That’s true in some markets — platforms with strong network effects, for instance. The reply: Even there, the winners are typically founders who raise enough to move swiftly on terms that still reward success. Speed bought with punishing preferences, uncontrolled dilution and a model that loses more with every sale is not speed; it’s accelerating towards a wall. Raise boldly if your market demands it — but run the Waterfall Test first and know exactly what you’re giving up. Common mistakes to avoid Raising because everyone else is. Signing a term sheet without modelling the waterfall. Accepting participating preferences or full-ratchet anti-dilution without understanding them. Letting due diligence uncover issues you could have fixed yourself. Treating a funding announcement as an achievement in itself. Try this today (30 minutes) If you have a term sheet — or expect one — build a straightforward spreadsheet: 1. List every shareholder, their shareholding and their preference terms. 2. Model sale prices at 0.5x, 1x and 2x the post-money valuation. 3. Work out what each person receives in each scenario. If you have no term sheet yet, answer these questions instead: Do we need this money, or just want it? Which specific milestones will it deliver? Which decisions will we no longer be able to take on our own? If we never raised again, could the company survive? Key Takeaways Funding is a wager on potential, not evidence that a model works — WeWork reveals how far apart the two can drift. Terms such as liquidation preferences, anti-dilution and veto rights frequently matter more than the headline valuation. Before signing, run the Waterfall Test at half, one and two times the valuation. Inflated valuations, unchecked dilution and investors on a different timetable can trap founders. Prepare for due diligence early, and weigh alternatives: bootstrapping, customer prepayments, revenue-based finance, strategic partners and debt. Raise money when you could manage without it — that’s when you hold the most power.

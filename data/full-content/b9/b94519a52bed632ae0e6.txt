} } Listen to This ArticleForeign-exchange intervention offers limited benefits beyond the near term and currencies should be allowed to adjust to global shocks, a top International Monetary Fund official said. “Countries which allow exchange rates to move have benefited more,” Krishna Srinivasan, director of the IMF’s Asia and Pacific Department, said at a press conference in Mumbai on Wednesday, responding to a question about Asian currencies. With economies facing a succession of global shocks, exchange rates should be allowed to absorb some of the pressure and act as the “first line of defense,” he said, without naming the Reserve Bank of India. Central banks across Asia are grappling with currency weakness as global shocks drive capital outflows and boost demand for dollars. Policymakers from Indonesia to Japan have deployed a mix of interest-rate increases and market intervention, testing how far authorities can resist depreciation without running down reserves or imposing greater costs on their economies. In India, the RBI is mounting its most forceful defense of the rupee since the 2013 taper tantrum. The currency has been among Asia’s worst performers this year, pressured by elevated oil prices and persistent foreign outflows from Indian stocks. The RBI this month rolled out a special window to meet dollar demand from state-run oil companies and a new tool requiring banks to park cash against certain foreign-exchange transactions. The moves followed its first interest-rate increase in nearly four years, as policymakers confront the risk that a weaker rupee and elevated crude could further stoke inflation.

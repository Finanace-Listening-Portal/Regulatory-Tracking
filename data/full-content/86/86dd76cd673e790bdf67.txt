Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); Runwal Enterprises Limited Vs ACIT (ITAT Mumbai) Summary: The Income Tax Appellate Tribunal, Mumbai Bench “D”, in Runwal Enterprises Limited Vs ACIT, decided five connected appeals for Assessment Years 2020-21 to 2024-25 arising from assessments and reassessments following a search conducted on the Runwal Group. The principal dispute concerned alleged receipt of unaccounted cash consideration, commonly described as “on-money”, from the sale of residential and commercial units. The Assessing Officer treated the entire alleged receipts as undisclosed business income, whereas the Commissioner of Income Tax (Appeals) restricted the additions to an estimated profit of 15%. The assessee challenged even the reduced additions, arguing that the alleged cash receipts were not independently established and that the estimation remained excessive. The Tribunal held that, on the facts and judicial precedents considered, the additions should be restricted to 8% of the alleged on-money receipts for Assessment Years 2021-22 to 2024-25. The assessee was engaged in construction and development of residential and commercial real estate projects in Mumbai. A search under Section 132 of the Income-tax Act, 1961 was conducted on 6 October 2023 at various premises connected with the Runwal Group. During the proceedings, the Department relied upon a blue-coloured diary recovered from the cabin of Smt. Lucy Roychoudhary, loose papers, digital communications including WhatsApp and Signal messages, and statements recorded from employees and other persons. The Assessing Officer interpreted this material as indicating a practice of collecting cash consideration over and above amounts recorded in property sale agreements. For AY 2021-22, the assessee had originally declared income of ₹31,50,29,390. Following the search, reassessment proceedings were initiated under Section 147, and the Assessing Officer added ₹4,00,000 as alleged on-money income, besides making a separate disallowance of ₹1,70,68,921 under Section 14A. The assessee disputed the evidentiary basis of the on-money additions. It submitted that no identified purchaser had confirmed making an unaccounted payment, no corresponding cash trail or purchaser bank withdrawal had been established, and no seized receipt directly acknowledged receipt of cash by the assessee. It also pointed out that certain statements relied upon by the Department had subsequently been retracted through affidavits. According to the assessee, uncorroborated diary entries and statements could not establish actual receipt of unaccounted consideration. The Revenue, however, maintained that the cumulative seized material, digital communications and statements demonstrated a systematic practice of collecting on-money. The Tribunal did not accept the assessee’s request for complete deletion, observing that the material, as examined by the lower authorities, indicated unaccounted receipts in some transactions. The Tribunal separately examined the validity of reassessment for AY 2021-22. The CIT(A) had upheld the reopening on the ground that the Assessing Officer possessed specific information arising from the search and had independently applied his mind before initiating proceedings. Before the Tribunal, the assessee did not establish any jurisdictional infirmity or procedural breach warranting interference. Consequently, the Tribunal dismissed the challenge to reopening under Section 147. This finding was distinct from its subsequent decision to reduce the quantum of the on-money addition. The Tribunal therefore did not hold that the reassessment itself was invalid merely because the addition required a lower profit estimate. On the substantive issue, the Tribunal emphasised the distinction between gross business receipts and taxable income. It reasoned that unaccounted consideration received in a real estate business does not necessarily represent income in its entirety, because corresponding unrecorded expenditure may also be incurred towards land procurement, project approvals, construction, liaisoning and other business requirements. Referring to the principle recognised in CIT Vs President Industries, (2002) 258 ITR 654 (Gujarat High Court), and other decisions concerning suppressed turnover and on-money receipts, the Tribunal held that only the profit component embedded in such receipts should ordinarily be assessed where the factual circumstances support that approach. It rejected the Revenue’s contention that the entire gross amount must be taxed merely because the assessee had not separately quantified every unrecorded outgoing. While the CIT(A) had estimated taxable profit at 15%, the Tribunal found that this rate was not supported by a specific comparable case, empirical basis or other convincing material. It considered decisions applying an 8% profit estimate to unaccounted receipts and took account of the assessee’s business model, the nature of the projects and the commercial realities of the real estate sector. Particular reliance was placed upon the coordinate Bench’s order dated 20 April 2026 in Horizon Projects Private Limited, a sister concern involving substantially similar search material and allegations. Following the same reasoning, the Tribunal directed the Assessing Officer to restrict additions relating to alleged on-money from flats, shops and other real estate units to 8% of the respective amounts considered in the assessment orders. The 8% rate was adopted as a fact-specific estimate, not as a universally applicable statutory rate. The Tribunal also addressed the Revenue’s objection to telescoping or adjustment of unaccounted cash expenditure against alleged on-money receipts. It held that where receipts and related expenditure formed part of the same undisclosed business circuit, taxing the gross receipts and again making a separate addition for corresponding cash outgoings would result in duplication unless an independent source of expenditure was demonstrated. No such distinct source had been established in the proceedings. Accordingly, once the profit embedded in alleged on-money was estimated at 8%, separate additions for corresponding expenditure could not ordinarily survive. The Tribunal’s reasoning recognised the need to consider the economic substance of the transactions rather than assess the same underlying business activity more than once. Applying its findings for AY 2021-22 to the subsequent years, the Tribunal restricted the on-money additions to 8% for AYs 2022-23, 2023-24 and 2024-25 as well. For AY 2024-25, the disputed transactions included alleged cash receipts relating to both a parking space and a flat. The assessee did not press its Section 14A ground for AY 2021-22, and that ground was dismissed as not pressed. The appeal for AY 2020-21 was voluntarily withdrawn and was accordingly dismissed as withdrawn. In the consolidated result, the appeals for AYs 2021-22 to 2024-25 were partly allowed, while the appeal for AY 2020-21 was dismissed as withdrawn. The ruling illustrates that, even where material supports an inference of unaccounted business receipts, the taxable amount must be determined on a reasonable and legally sustainable basis, having regard to the profit element and any related expenditure. Cases Discussed: Ekta Housing Pvt. Ltd. Vs DCIT (ITAT Mumbai) — Referred to in the Revenue’s submissions as a decision distinguished on facts. FULL TEXT OF THE ORDER OF ITAT MUMBAI The aforesaid captioned appeals have been preferred by the assessee, Runwal Enterprises Limited, for Assessment Years 2020-21 to 2024-25 against separate appellate orders passed by the learned Commissioner of Income Tax (Appeals), CIT(A)-52, Mumbai / NFAC, arising out of assessments framed under section 143(3) and section 147 of the Income Tax Act, 1961. 1.1. Since all these appeals emanate from common search proceedings conducted in the case of Runwal Group, and since the additions made by the Assessing Officer as well as the relief granted by the learned CIT(A) are substantially founded upon common seized material, common statements, identical factual background and overlapping legal issues, all these appeals were heard together and are being disposed of by this consolidated order for the sake of convenience, consistency and judicial propriety. The principal controversy which runs through these appeals pertains to alleged receipt of “on-money” in real estate transactions, the extent to which such alleged receipts can be brought to tax, and whether the entire alleged receipts or only the profit element embedded therein can be subjected to taxation. Since the appeal for Assessment Year 2021-22 in ITA No.8932/Mum/2025 contains the fuller factual foundation and more elaborate grounds, we first take up the said appeal as the lead matter, and our findings therein shall, wherever applicable, govern the other years mutatis mutandis. 2. The grounds raised by the assessee for Assessment Year 2021-22 read as under: “1. On the facts and circumstances of the case as well as in law, the Learned CIT(A) has erred in confirming the action of the Learned Assessing Officer in reopening the assessment proceeding u/s 147 of the Income Tax Act, 1961, without considering the fact and circumstances of the case. 2. On the facts and circumstances of the case as well as in law, the Learned CIT(A) has erred in confirming the action of the Learned Assessing Officer in making additions in the hands of the appellant without any tangible material / information / incriminating evidence found during the course of search, without considering the facts & circumstances of the case and position in law. 3. On the facts and circumstances of the case as well as in law, the Learned CIT(A) has erred in restricting the addition made by the Learned Assessing Officer to the extent of Rs.60,000/- being 15 percent of Rs.4,00,000/- as profit earned on alleged on-money received on sale of flat, without considering the facts and circumstances of the case. 4. On the facts and circumstances of the case as well as in law, the Learned CIT(A) as well as the Learned Assessing Officer has erred in not appreciating the fact that the appellant has not taken on-money on sale of flats and simply made the addition by relying on blue colour diary seized from cabin of Smt. Lucy Roychoudhary without any independent corroborative evidence to support these entries. 5. On the facts and circumstances of the case as well as in law, the Learned CIT(A) and the Learned Assessing Officer erred in making additions on the basis of uncorroborated statements which were later retracted, without considering the facts & circumstances of the case and settled position of law. 6. On the facts and circumstances of the case as well as in law, the Learned CIT(A) has erred in restricting the disallowance made by the Learned Assessing Officer to the extent of Rs.11,72,310/- u/s 14A of the Income Tax Act, 1961, without considering and appreciating the facts and circumstances of the case. 7. The appellant craves leave to add, amend, alter or delete the said ground of appeal.” 3. The assessee is a domestic company engaged in the business of construction and development of residential and commercial projects in Mumbai and is one of the entities forming part of Runwal Group. For the year under consideration, the assessee had filed its original return of income under section 139(1) of the Act on 10.03.2022 declaring total income of Rs.31,50,29,390/-. Subsequently, a search and seizure action under section 132 of the Act was carried out in the case of Runwal Group on 06.10.2023 covering various residential and business premises of the group, its key persons and various group concerns. The assessee company, earlier known as Runwal Apartments Private Limited / Propel Developers Private Limited, was also covered during the course of search proceedings. Consequent upon the search and on the basis of information and material gathered therefrom, proceedings under section 147 were initiated for the year under consideration and notice under section 148 was issued on 19.03.2024. In response to the said notice, the assessee filed its return of income on 04.06.2024 declaring the very same income of Rs.31,50,29,390/-. Thereafter, notice under section 143(2) was issued on 26.06.2024 and duly served upon the assessee. Further notices under section 142(1) were also issued calling for details and explanations in respect of various issues emerging from the search material and other aspects of assessment, and in response thereto, the assessee furnished details and explanations before the Assessing Officer. 4. During the course of assessment proceedings, the Assessing Officer made additions on account of alleged “on-money” receipts said to have been received by the assessee in connection with sale of flats and commercial units. The Assessing Officer primarily relied upon statements recorded during the course of search proceedings, certain loose papers and diary entries allegedly found during search, including a blue coloured diary seized from the cabin of Smt. Lucy Roychoudhary, and certain digital communications such as WhatsApp and Signal chats recovered from mobile phones of some employees of the group. On the basis of such material, the Assessing Officer came to the conclusion that the group was engaged in a systematic practice of receiving part of sale consideration in cash over and above the recorded sale value. Proceeding on such premise, the Assessing Officer made addition of Rs.4,00,000/- on account of alleged on-money receipts and treated the entire amount as undisclosed business income in the hands of the assessee under section 28 of the Act. Apart from this, the Assessing Officer also made disallowance of Rs.1,70,68,921/- under section 14A of the Act on the ground that the assessee had earned exempt income in the form of share of profit from partnership firm without making corresponding disallowance of expenditure relatable thereto. 5. Aggrieved by the assessment order, the assessee preferred appeal before the learned CIT(A). Before the first appellate authority, the assessee vehemently contested the additions and submitted that the additions were made merely on presumptive basis by placing reliance upon statements recorded during search proceedings and certain loose notings without any independent corroborative material demonstrating actual receipt of on-money by the assessee. It was specifically submitted that the statements relied upon by the Assessing Officer, particularly those of certain employees and other persons, had subsequently been retracted by way of sworn affidavits and, therefore, such statements could not by themselves constitute conclusive evidence against the assessee. It was further contended that no customer confirmation, no cash trail, no evidence of bank withdrawal by purchasers, no seized receipt acknowledging receipt of cash by the assessee, and no documentary evidence directly linking the assessee with actual receipt of alleged on-money had been brought on record. Thus, according to the assessee, the additions were based on suspicion, surmises and generalised inference, and not on legally sustainable material establishing actual receipt of unaccounted cash. 6. The learned CIT(A), after considering the assessment order, submissions of the assessee, seized material and judicial precedents placed before him, examined the issue in some detail. The appellate authority observed that the Assessing Officer had relied upon statements recorded during search and certain seized documents which, according to the department, indicated possibility of receipt of cash consideration in certain real estate transactions. The learned CIT(A) noted that the statements of certain employees and the entries appearing in seized documents suggested that there existed a practice within the group of accepting part consideration in cash in certain transactions. On this basis, the learned CIT(A) was of the view that the possibility of receipt of on-money in real estate transactions could not be completely ruled out. However, the learned CIT(A) also accepted the equally important principle that even where on-money receipts are presumed or established, the entire amount of such receipts cannot automatically be treated as taxable income in its entirety. According to the learned CIT(A), in real estate business, unaccounted receipts may also be utilised towards various project-related expenses and business outgoings which may not have been recorded in the regular books of account; and therefore, as held in several judicial precedents, only the profit element embedded in such receipts can be brought to tax. Accordingly, the learned CIT(A) restricted the addition to 15% of alleged on-money receipts and deleted the balance. The same principle was applied to other additions made by the Assessing Officer in respect of alleged on-money on sale of flats and shops. 7. Thus, the learned CIT(A) did not delete the additions in toto, but granted substantial relief by restricting taxation only to the estimated profit element at the rate of 15% of the alleged receipts. The assessee is aggrieved by sustenance of such addition even to the extent of 15%, whereas the Assessing Officer’s approach of taxing the entire gross receipts did not find favour with the first appellate authority. 8. The other issue in Assessment Year 2021-22 relates to disallowance under section 14A. The Assessing Officer alleged that the assessee had earned income exempt from tax in the form of share of profit from partnership firm and had not made any disallowance under section 14A. Before the learned CIT(A), the assessee submitted that share of profit from partnership firm cannot be treated as exempt income in the ordinary or conventional sense, because such income has already suffered tax in the hands of the partnership firm before distribution of profits. It was, therefore, argued that section 14A ought not to be invoked in respect of such share income. Reliance was placed upon CBDT Circular No.8/2014 [F.No.173/99/2013-ITA-I]. Without prejudice, it was submitted that even assuming section 14A to be applicable, disallowance cannot exceed exempt income earned during the relevant year, and since the assessee had received share of profit of Rs.11,72,310/-, disallowance could not be sustained beyond that amount. 9. The learned CIT(A), however, did not accept the principal contention of the assessee that section 14A would have no application in respect of exempt share of profit from partnership firm. Relying upon the decision of the three-member Bench of the Tribunal, Ahmedabad, the learned CIT(A) held that disallowance under section 14A is required to be computed even in respect of exempt income earned from partnership firm. At the same time, the learned CIT(A) accepted the alternate contention of the assessee that disallowance under section 14A cannot exceed the exempt income earned during the year. Relying upon the decision of the Hon’ble Madras High Court in Chettinad Logistics Pvt. Ltd. and that of the Hon’ble Bombay High Court in Nirved Traders, the learned CIT(A) directed the Assessing Officer to restrict the disallowance to the extent of exempt income received or accrued to the assessee during the year. 10. Ground No.1 raised by the assessee challenges the validity of reopening of assessment under section 147. The learned CIT(A) has dealt with this challenge in considerable detail and has upheld the reopening. The learned CIT(A) observed that reassessment proceedings were initiated on the basis of specific and tangible material unearthed during search conducted in Runwal Group. The information so received was not vague or general in nature, but was linked to certain transactions involving the assessee and was sufficient to form a prima facie belief that income chargeable to tax had escaped assessment. The learned CIT(A) further held that existence of fresh, relevant and credible material is sufficient to satisfy the jurisdictional requirement of “reason to believe” contemplated under section 147. The learned CIT(A) also rejected the assessee’s contention that reopening was based on borrowed satisfaction. It was held that the Assessing Officer had not acted mechanically on third-party information but had applied his independent mind to material available on record and thereafter formed a bona fide belief regarding escapement of income. The reasons recorded by the Assessing Officer were found to be self-contained, specific and demonstrative of due application of mind. 11. Before us, the learned Authorised Representative has not been able to controvert the findings recorded by the learned CIT(A) on the issue of reopening by bringing any new fact, jurisdictional infirmity or binding judicial precedent which would persuade us to take a different view. There is nothing on record to show that the Assessing Officer had assumed jurisdiction without material, without application of mind or in breach of mandatory procedural requirements. In these circumstances, we find no infirmity in the order of the learned CIT(A) in upholding reopening of assessment under section 147. Accordingly, Ground No.1 raised by the assessee is dismissed. 12. Ground Nos.2 to 5 relate to addition sustained by the learned CIT(A) at 15% of the alleged on-money receipts. Since all these grounds are interconnected and arise from the same set of facts, they are being adjudicated together. 13. The learned counsel for the assessee, reiterating the submissions advanced before the lower authorities, assailed the sustenance of even 15% of alleged receipts. She submitted that there was no direct and clinching evidence on record to show that the assessee had actually received on-money from any identified customer. According to her, there was neither any customer confirmation nor any cash trail nor any evidence of bank withdrawal nor any seized receipt directly evidencing receipt of cash by the assessee. She further submitted that the statements relied upon by the Assessing Officer had been retracted and in any event, settled law does not permit addition merely on the basis of uncorroborated statements. It was also emphasised that no unaccounted cash was found from the assessee during search. Without prejudice, she submitted that even if any estimation was required to be made, the rate of 15% adopted by the learned CIT(A) was excessive, particularly having regard to the nature of the assessee’s project, which was a mid-segment housing project catering to middle-class purchasers and not a luxury or high-end premium development yielding extraordinary margins. She thus submitted that, at the highest, a lower estimation in the range of 5% to 8% could be adopted. 14. Per contra, the learned CIT-DR strongly supported the assessment order and submitted that once existence of on-money transactions was evidenced by seized documents, digital records and statements recorded under section 132(4), the entire gross receipt became liable to tax. According to him, the learned CIT(A) erred in reducing the addition merely to the profit element. He submitted that there was no material brought by the assessee to show that 85% of such receipts had actually been expended for project purposes. He further submitted that the assessee cannot seek notional deduction of expenditure without demonstrating actual incurrence thereof. According to him, if such expenditure was incurred in cash, then it would attract rigour of section 40A(3), and in any case, no deduction can be granted merely on assumption. On the issue of telescoping, it was his submission that on-money receipts and unaccounted expenditure constitute separate heads of undisclosed income and unless clear nexus is established, benefit of set-off cannot be allowed. He also submitted that decisions relied upon by the learned CIT(A), including Ekta Housing Pvt. Ltd., were distinguishable on facts and could not be mechanically applied. 15. We have heard the rival submissions and perused the material placed on record. The controversy before us is not merely whether some material was found during search suggesting receipt of unaccounted consideration in real estate transactions, but more fundamentally, what is the extent of income embedded in such alleged receipts. The concept of “on-money” in the real estate sector is not unknown to tax jurisprudence. It generally refers to an unaccounted component of sale consideration allegedly received in cash over and above the agreement value recorded in the books. Such matters ordinarily surface during search or survey actions through diaries, loose sheets, parallel records, digital chats and statements of persons connected with the project or business affairs of the builder. However, even where existence of such receipts is inferred or established, a further and equally important question arises: whether the whole of such receipts can be taxed as income, or whether only the profit element embedded therein can be brought to tax. This issue, in our considered view, is no longer res integra. 16. The consistent thread running through judicial pronouncements is that unaccounted receipts or suppressed sale consideration do not automatically represent income in entirety. Such receipts are in the nature of business receipts or suppressed turnover. In ordinary business parlance and in tax jurisprudence, there is a clear distinction between “receipt” and “income”. A receipt may be gross inflow, but income is what remains after taking into account corresponding costs, outgoings or expenditure embedded in the business activity. In real estate business, where on-money is alleged, courts and tribunals have repeatedly taken note of the fact that there may also be unrecorded project-related expenditure, payments connected with approvals, land aggregation, liaisoning, construction-related outgoings and other business exigencies. Therefore, unless the department is able to demonstrate that the entire alleged receipt represents pure income without any outgoing component, it would be legally unsustainable and commercially unrealistic to tax the entire gross receipt as income. 17. We may first advert to the reasoning extracted in the order itself from the decision of the Ahmedabad Bench in JCIT v. Narayan Land Estate, wherein the Tribunal lucidly explained that when seized or impounded documents disclose not merely receipt of on-money but also indicate the reality of unaccounted project-related expenditure, then a holistic approach is warranted and the gross on-money cannot be taxed in entirety. The Tribunal further recognised, taking judicial notice of ground realities of the real estate sector, that cash is often collected and cash is also deployed for various approval-related and project-related purposes. Therefore, a reasonable profit estimate on such receipts, rather than taxation of gross amount, accords with both commercial realism and settled tax jurisprudence. The relevant observation, as noted in the draft, is reproduced hereunder: “8.5 The decisions of the jurisdictional ITAT and High Court are binding on the Commissioner (Appeals). However, the appellant in its submission has not given the working of profit on such on-money and has not given the justification as to how the profit of 15% (as mentioned in the submission during the appeal proceedings) is adequate and fair. No doubt, one approach is that where the seized/impounded document bear the testimony of on-money being charged and collected and the same pages or similar group pages also contain entries related to unaccounted expenses on in relation to the projects undertaken and in such a case, such expenses are to be necessary allowed for the holistic interpretation of the documents seized/impounded. The other approach is to take cognizance of the market/realty sector that while it is the practice of the real estate market that whereas cash (over and above the consideration in cheque) are collected from the customers, the developers have to incur various unaccounted expenses also in relation to procurement of land and approval of the projects by various authorities & etc and therefore, there is rational/ justification in charging/bringing to tax only an estimated profit on on-money/premium amount collected from the customers instead of adding the gross amount of on-money/premium to the total income.” 18. The above principle is also fortified by the judgment of the Hon’ble Gujarat High Court in CIT v. President Industries, 258 ITR 654, wherein it was held that entire undisclosed sale proceeds cannot be regarded as income. Similar principle has found acceptance in decisions of various High Courts and benches of the Tribunal in matters involving suppressed sales and alleged on-money receipts. In Guruprerna Enterprises, following the ratio in Abhishek Corporation v. DCIT, it was held that even where seized documents establish receipt of on-money or premium, the entire receipts cannot be assessed as undisclosed income and only net profit rate can be applied on such unaccounted receipts. The legal position which thus emerges is that on-money receipts are in the nature of suppressed business receipts or unrecorded sale consideration, and what is chargeable to tax is only the profit component embedded therein and not the whole receipt as such. 19. Equally instructive is the decision in ITO v. Anand Builders, where the Tribunal held that only 8% of unaccounted on-money receipts could be brought to tax and not the entire gross receipts, for the reason that against such unaccounted receipts there would ordinarily be corresponding unaccounted payments and only the residual income remaining in the hands of the assessee can be taxed. The said view, as noted in the draft order, ultimately found affirmation when the challenge by the department did not succeed. Likewise, in Hema Haresh Mehta v. DCIT, Mumbai Tribunal held that reasonable profit embedded in total unaccounted gross receipts deserves to be brought to tax and 8% was considered appropriate in the facts of that case, with the clear observation that once profit is estimated, all associated expenditure stands deemed to have been allowed. 20. Reference may also be made to Kishor Mohanlal Telwala, where the Tribunal regarded 8% as fair and reasonable by drawing broad guidance from section 44AD, though not directly applying the said provision. It is true that the present assessee’s turnover may be far beyond the threshold of presumptive taxation and section 44AD may not directly apply. However, such statutory benchmarks can provide a broad indicator while arriving at a fair estimate in situations where direct quantification is impossible and only reasonable approximation is called for. Estimation, in such cases, must not be founded either on conjecture or on punitive instinct, but on a balanced appreciation of the nature of trade, scale of activity, surrounding circumstances and margins ordinarily available in such line of business. 21. Tested on the aforesaid principles, we are unable to accept the Revenue’s contention that the entire gross alleged on-money receipts deserve to be taxed as income. Such approach ignores the basic distinction between gross receipt and taxable income. At the same time, we are also unable to accept the assessee’s plea for complete deletion, because the seized material and statements, as concurrently appreciated by the lower authorities, do point towards existence of unaccounted receipts in some transactions. The learned CIT(A), therefore, was justified in not deleting the addition in entirety. The only real question which survives for our consideration is the appropriate rate at which income embedded in such alleged receipts should be estimated. Having regard to the nature of the assessee’s business, the location and character of the project, judicial precedents placed before us and broad commercial realities of real estate sector, we are of the considered view that estimation at 15% as sustained by the learned CIT(A) is on the higher side and does not appear to be founded upon any specific comparable case, empirical basis or cogent material. A more reasonable and judicially balanced estimate, in our view, would be 8% of the alleged on-money receipts. We accordingly direct that additions on account of alleged on-money receipts in respect of sale of flats, shops or other such real estate units be restricted to 8% of the amounts considered by the Assessing Officer. 22. Once we hold that only profit element at 8% is liable to be taxed on alleged on-money receipts, the Revenue’s grievance against telescoping or set-off allowed by the learned CIT(A) in respect of alleged unaccounted cash expenditure loses substantial force. In cases of this nature, where alleged receipts and alleged expenditure form part of same undisclosed business circuit, separate taxation of gross receipts and again of corresponding cash outgoings would result in duplication, unless the department is able to establish that such expenditure arose from an altogether independent source. No such separate source has been demonstrated before us. On the contrary, the very case of the department is that the assessee or group concerns generated cash through on-money and utilised the same in project-linked unaccounted dealings. In such a factual setting, telescoping is not a concession but a necessary corollary of coherent taxation. Once profit on gross receipts is estimated, separate addition of corresponding expenditure cannot ordinarily survive unless some independent and distinct source of expenditure is brought on record. 23. During the course of hearing, the learned counsel for the assessee also brought to our notice that similar on-money additions were made in the case of assessee’s sister concern, Horizon Projects Private Limited. The Mumbai Bench of the Tribunal in the case of Horizon Projects Private Limited, after appreciating the arguments advanced by the learned Authorised Representative and after considering similar factual and legal aspects, restricted addition on account of on-money to 8% vide order dated 20.04.2026. The relevant extract relied upon before us is reproduced hereunder: “19. Equally instructive is the decision in ITO v. Anand Builders, where the Tribunal held that only 8% of the unaccounted on-money receipts could be brought to tax, and not the entire gross receipts, for the reason that against such unaccounted receipts there would ordinarily be corresponding unaccounted payments and only the residual income that remains in the hands of the assessee can be taxed. The said view, as noted in the draft order, ultimately found affirmation when the challenge by the department did not succeed. Likewise, in Hema Haresh Mehta v. DCIT, Mumbai Tribunal held that a reasonable profit embedded in total unaccounted gross receipts deserves to be brought to tax and 8% was considered an appropriate estimate in the facts of that case, with the clear observation that once profit is estimated, all associated expenditure stands deemed to have been allowed. 20. Reference may also be made to Kishor Mohanlal Telwala, where the Tribunal regarded 8% as a fair and reasonable figure by drawing guidance from section 44AD, albeit not directly applying the provision. True it is, the present assessee’s turnover may be far beyond the threshold of presumptive taxation and section 44AD may not directly apply. Yet, such statutory benchmarks can often provide a broad indicator for arriving at a fair estimate where direct quantification is impossible and only an approximation on rational basis is called for. What is important is that estimation must rest neither on conjecture nor on punitive instinct, but on a fair appreciation of the nature of trade, scale of activity, and margins ordinarily available. 21. Tested on the aforesaid principles, we are unable to persuade ourselves to accept the Revenue’s contention that the entire gross on-money receipts deserve to be taxed as income. That approach ignores the basic distinction between receipt and income. At the same time, we are also not inclined to accept the assessee’s plea for complete deletion, because the seized material and statements, as concurrently appreciated by the authorities below, do point towards existence of unaccounted receipts in some transactions, and the learned CIT(A) was justified in not effacing the addition in toto. The only real question then is the appropriate rate at which the income embedded in such receipts should be estimated. On an overall consideration of the nature of the assessee’s business, the location and character of the project, the judicial precedents placed before us, and the broad commercial realities of the real estate sector, we are of the considered view that estimation at 15% as sustained by the learned CIT(A) is on the higher side and does not appear to rest on any specific comparative or empirical basis. A more reasonable estimate, in our view, would be 8% of the alleged on-money receipts. We accordingly direct that the additions on account of on-money receipts in respect of sale of flats and shops be restricted to 8% of the amounts considered by the Assessing Officer.” 24. The aforesaid decision of the co-ordinate Bench in the case of Horizon Projects Private Limited, being a sister concern and arising out of substantially similar allegations, same group background and similar nature of search material, lends further support to the view that what can be exigible to tax is only the profit element embedded in alleged on-money receipts and not the entire gross receipts. Consistency of judicial approach in similarly placed cases is also an important facet of fairness and certainty in tax adjudication. Thus, respectfully following and applying the same rationale, we hold that additions sustained by the learned CIT(A) at 15% deserve to be reduced and restricted to 8% of alleged on-money receipts. 25. Consequently, Ground Nos.2 to 5 raised by the assessee challenging sustenance of addition at 15% of alleged on-money receipts are partly allowed. We hold that additions on account of alleged on-money receipts in Assessment Year 2021-22 shall stand restricted to 8% of the respective amounts considered by the Assessing Officer, and no separate addition on account of alleged unexplained cash expenditure shall survive beyond what is embedded in such estimated profit. 26. Ground No.6 relates to disallowance under section 14A. During the course of hearing, the learned counsel for the assessee submitted that this ground is not being pressed. In view of the statement made at Bar, Ground No.6 is dismissed as not pressed. 27. In the result, the appeal of the assessee for Assessment Year 2021-22 is partly allowed. 28.

Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); WABCO India Limited Vs Commissioner of GST and Central Excise (CESTAT Chennai) Summary: The Customs, Excise and Service Tax Appellate Tribunal (CESTAT), Chennai, in WABCO India Limited Vs Commissioner of GST and Central Excise, allowed four appeals challenging demands aggregating ₹51,60,791 for alleged non-reversal of CENVAT credit on inputs and capital goods transferred from the appellant’s Domestic Tariff Area (DTA) manufacturing facility to its own Special Economic Zone (SEZ) unit. The dispute concerned October 2012 to June 2015 and arose under Rule 3(5) of the CENVAT Credit Rules, 2004. The Tribunal held that such clearances constituted exports under Section 2(m) of the Special Economic Zones Act, 2005, attracted the exemption under Section 26(1)(c), and enjoyed statutory protection under Section 51, which overrides inconsistent provisions of other laws. The appellant manufactured hose assemblies, compressors and valves at its Ambattur facility and maintained an SEZ unit at Mahindra World City, Chengalpet. The Department contended that inputs and capital goods removed as such attracted an obligation to pay an amount equivalent to the credit originally availed. Four proceedings resulted in demands of ₹4,61,551, ₹3,85,635, ₹3,10,159 and ₹40,03,446. The last three amounts had already been paid under protest, but the adjudicating authority appropriated those payments, confirmed the outstanding demand, imposed interest and levied a penalty of ₹1 lakh. The Commissioner (Appeals) upheld these actions. The Tribunal examined Sections 2(m), 26(1)(c) and 51 of the SEZ Act alongside Rules 3(5) and 6(6)(i) of the CENVAT Credit Rules. It found that the statutory definition of exports includes supplies from the DTA to an SEZ unit without restricting the benefit to goods manufactured by the supplier. The exemption for goods supplied for authorised SEZ operations therefore extended to inputs and capital goods transferred as such. Since Rule 3(5) is subordinate legislation, it could not override the substantive exemption created by the SEZ Act. The Tribunal also relied upon decisions of the Kerala, Andhra Pradesh and Chhattisgarh High Courts supporting the treatment of DTA-to-SEZ supplies as exports. The Bench rejected the Department’s reliance on Sundaram Brake Linings Ltd. and the earlier Larger Bench decision in Lakshmi Automatic Loom Works Ltd. It observed that the Larger Bench decision had subsequently been set aside by the Madras High Court and that the matter, on remand, had been decided in favour of the assessee. The Tribunal distinguished the circumstances of Sundaram Brake Linings and preferred the interpretation supported by subsequent High Court decisions. It also noted that the Department had never alleged diversion of goods or their non-use in authorised SEZ operations. On the procedural objection concerning the absence of ARE-1 documentation, the Tribunal held that the show cause notices had not alleged non-compliance with export procedures or failure of the goods to reach the SEZ unit. Consequently, the Department could not introduce such allegations at the adjudication stage to sustain demands founded exclusively on Rule 3(5). Relying on Supreme Court precedents concerning the limits of show cause notices, the Bench emphasised that substantive statutory benefits could not be denied merely because of procedural deficiencies where eligibility itself was not disputed. A significant aspect of the judgment concerned judicial discipline and the binding effect of appellate decisions. The Tribunal had already decided the identical issue in WABCO’s favour through orders dated 13 July 2012 and 22 March 2016. Nevertheless, the adjudicating authority declined to follow the earlier order because the Department had not challenged it owing to monetary limits, while the Commissioner (Appeals) preferred a conflicting earlier Tribunal decision. Referring to Union of India Vs Kamlakshi Finance Corporation Ltd. and Section 35R of the Central Excise Act, the Bench held that non-filing of an appeal on monetary grounds does not deprive a Tribunal decision of its binding character. Subordinate authorities must follow an applicable appellate decision unless it has been stayed or overturned. The Tribunal particularly criticised the Commissioner (Appeals) for effectively questioning the correctness of an earlier Tribunal decision in the appellant’s own case. It reiterated that subordinate tax authorities cannot disregard binding appellate orders merely because they disagree with their reasoning or consider another decision preferable. The Bench observed that this disregard of judicial discipline had unnecessarily prolonged litigation over an issue already settled for the same assessee. Accordingly, the Tribunal set aside the common Order-in-Appeal dated 25 July 2017 and all four demands aggregating ₹51,60,791. The consequential interest and ₹1 lakh penalty were also quashed. The appropriation of ₹47,99,240 paid under protest was set aside, and the appellant was held entitled to consequential relief in accordance with law. The decision reinforces both the statutory priority of SEZ exemptions over inconsistent CENVAT provisions and the obligation of tax authorities to respect binding appellate precedents. By that order the Commissioner (Appeals) affirmed the common Order in Original No. 55 to 58/2016 dated 30.08.2016 of the Joint Commissioner of Central Excise, Chennai II Commissionerate, which had confirmed four demands of an amount equal to the CENVAT credit availed on inputs and capital goods that the appellant removed as such to its own unit in a Special Economic Zone. The four appeals arise from one order and turn on one question, and are therefore disposed of together. Relevant Facts 2. The appellant manufactures hose assemblies, compressors and valves falling under Chapters 40 and 84 of the First Schedule to the Central Excise Tariff Act, 1985 at its Plant I in the Ambattur Industrial Estate, Chennai. It avails CENVAT credit on inputs and capital goods under the CENVAT Credit Rules, 2004 (CCR). It also has a unit in the Special Economic Zone (SEZ) at Mahindra World City, Chengalpet. Some of the inputs and capital goods on which credit had been taken were sent as such from Plant I to the SEZ unit, without payment of an amount equal to that credit. The Department regarded each such removal as a breach of Rule 3(5) of the CCR. 3. The dispute is not new. Demands of the same kind were raised for September 2008 to September 2010. For the period before October 2009 the Commissioner (Appeals) dropped the demands, and this Tribunal dismissed the appeals of the Revenue by Final Order Nos. 709 and 801 of 2012 dated 13.07.2012. The Department did not carry that decision further. The adjudicating authority in the present proceedings recorded that the decision had been accepted by the Department only on monetary grounds and not on merits, and that the appellant’s contention that it had attained finality was therefore not correct. The demand for October 2009 to September 2010 was confirmed by Order in Original No. 03/2011 dated 21.03.2011 and upheld by the Commissioner (Appeals) in Order in Appeal No. 215/2013 (M-II) dated 12.11.2013. The appellant’s appeal against that Order in Appeal was allowed by this Tribunal by Final Order No. 40523/2016 dated 22.03.2016. 4. The present proceedings cover October 2012 to June 2015 and rest on four notices. Statement of Demand No. 14/2013 dated 07.11.2013 sought Rs.4,61,551/- for October 2012 to June 2013, with interest under Section 11AA of the Central Excise Act, 1944 (the Act) and a penalty under Rule 15(1) of the CCR. Show Cause Notice No. 11/2014 dated 06.08.2014 and Show Cause Notice No. 02/2015 dated 04.02.2015 sought Rs.3,85,635/- for July to December 2013 and Rs.3,10,159/- for January to June 2014. Statement of Demand No. 46/2015 dated 27.07.2015 sought Rs.40,03,446/-for July 2014 to June 2015. For these last three periods the notices record that the appellant had already paid the amounts under protest at the time of clearance. The notices proposed that the payments be held to be legally due, the protest be vacated and the amounts appropriated. Each notice invoked Rule 14 and Rule 3(5) of the CCR read with Section 11A of the Act. The four demands together come to Rs.51,60,791/-. The two Show Cause Notices founded the proposal to vacate the protest on Order in Appeal No. 215/2013 (M-II), which this Tribunal later set aside by Final Order No. 40523/2016, some five months before the adjudicating authority passed the Order in Original. Reply and the orders below 5. The appellant replied to the aforesaid Statements of Demand and Show Cause Notices by letters dated 10.09.2014, 10.03.2015 and 11.08.2015. It contended that a supply from the Domestic Tariff Area (DTA) to an SEZ unit is an export under Section 2(m) of the Special Economic Zones Act, 2005 (SEZ Act), that Section 26(1)(c) of that Act exempts such goods from excise duty, that Rule 30 of the Special Economic Zones Rules, 2006 (SEZ Rules) requires a DTA supplier to clear goods to an SEZ unit as in the case of exports, and that Section 51 gives the SEZ Act overriding effect. It relied, among other decisions, on the order of 2012 in its own case. 6. The Joint Commissioner (adjudicating authority) held that credit is available only on inputs used in the manufacture of dutiable goods and that inputs cleared as such to any unit, whether in the DTA or in an SEZ, attract Rule 3(5). He rejected the plea under Rule 30 of the SEZ Rules on the view that only manufactured goods may go to an SEZ unit without payment of duty. He added that the appellant had produced no ARE-1 or like document to show that the procedure for clearance to an SEZ had been followed. He declined to follow the order of 2012 in the appellant’s own case because, in his view, the Department had accepted it only on monetary grounds and not on merits, so that it had not attained finality. Following Commissioner of Central Excise, Chennai-II v. Sundaram Brake Linings Ltd., 2014 (299) E.L.T. 342 (Tri.-Chennai) and Lakshmi Automatic Loom Works Ltd. v. Commissioner of Central Excise, Trichy, 2008 (232) E.L.T. 428 (Tri.-LB), he confirmed the four demands under Section 11A(10) of the Act. On the first demand he ordered interest under Section 11AA and imposed a penalty of Rs.1,00,000/- under Rule 15(1) of the CCR. For the other three periods he vacated the protest and appropriated the amounts paid. 7. The Commissioner (Appeals) noticed the decision of this Tribunal in the appellant’s own case reported as Wabco TVS (India) Ltd. v. Commissioner of Central Excise, Chennai-II, 2016 (344) E.L.T. 1034 (Tri.-Chennai). He nonetheless preferred Sundaram Brake Linings on the reasoning that it had not been considered in the appellant’s case and that the CCR, being a complete code, do not envisage export of inputs as such. He relied on the Larger Bench in Lakshmi Automatic Loom Works, found the circulars of the Board to be beside the point, and held that Section 51 of the SEZ Act could not displace a provision as clear as Rule 3(5). He upheld the demands and the appropriations with interest, and found the penalty of Rs.1,00,000/- reasonable since the appellant had not reversed the credit for October 2012 to June 2013, adding that interest was a mandatory consequence. The appeals were rejected. Submissions on behalf of the appellant 8. Ms. Vamini J, learned Counsel for the appellant, submitted that Section 2(m) of the SEZ Act, inter-alia, treats a supply of goods from the DTA to an SEZ unit as an export and that Section 26(1)(c) exempts such goods from any duty of excise. Rule 30 of the SEZ Rules, she pointed out, obliges the DTA supplier to clear the goods as in the case of exports, under bond or under claim of rebate on ARE-1. By Section 51 these provisions prevail over anything inconsistent in any other law, Rule 3(5) of the CCR included. The adjudicating authority had not engaged with Rule 30 at all. Support was also drawn from Rule 19 of the Central Excise Rules, 2002, from the Board’s Circular No. 283/117/96-CX dated 31.12.1996 and letter F. No. 345/2/2000-TRU dated 29.08.2000, and from the levy of export duty on certain supplies to SEZs, which in Counsel’s submission shows that the Government itself treats such supplies as exports. 9. Counsel contended that the issue stands concluded in the pellant’s favour by the two decisions of this Tribunal in its own case, which bound both lower authorities in terms of Union of India v. Kamlakshi Finance Corporation Ltd., 1991 (55) E.L.T. 433 (S.C.). Why the Department chose not to appeal was immaterial. She placed reliance on FCI OEN Connectors Ltd. v. Commissioner of Central Excise, Customs and Service Tax, Cochin, 2019 (368) E.L.T. 840 (Ker.), which holds that inputs removed as such to an SEZ unit under ARE-1 are exported and need not suffer reversal under Rule 3(5), and on Sujana Metal Products Ltd. v. Commissioner of Central Excise, Hyderabad, 2011 (273) E.L.T. 112 (Tri.-Bang.). The reliance on Sundaram Brake Linings and on the Larger Bench in Lakshmi Automatic Loom Works was misplaced, she argued, as the latter was set aside by the High Court and the matter, on remand, was decided in favour of the assessee by this Tribunal in Final Order Nos. 42161-42164/2021 dated 30.08.2021. As no provision of the CCR had been contravened, neither interest nor penalty could follow. Submissions on behalf of the Revenue 10. O.M. Reena, learned Authorised Representative, reiterated the findings of the impugned order. Analysis 11. We have heard both sides and perused the record. The following issues arise for our determination: i.Whether the appellant was bound by Rule 3(5) of the CCR to pay an amount equal to the credit on inputs and capital goods removed as such to its own SEZ unit. ii. Whether the lower authorities could decline to follow the decisions of this Tribunal in the appellant’s own case. iii.Whether interest and penalty survive. They are dealt with in seriatim. (i) Liability under Rule 3(5) of the CCR 12. The facts are undisputed. The notices themselves record that the goods went to the appellant’s own unit in the SEZ at Mahindra World City. There is no allegation that any of the goods were diverted, or that the SEZ unit did not use them in its authorised operations. The only charge is that the amount prescribed by Rule 3(5) was not paid on their removal as such. 13. That very charge, against this appellant, for removals to the same SEZ unit, has twice been answered by this Tribunal. In Commissioner of Central Excise, Chennai-II v. Wabco TVS (India) Ltd., Final Order Nos. 709 and 801 of 2012 dated 13.07.2012, the Tribunal followed Sujana Metal Products and held that clearances of inputs and capital goods to an SEZ are exports of dutiable goods on which the credit need not be reversed. In Wabco TVS (India) Ltd. v. Commissioner of Central Excise, Chennai-II, vide Final Order No. 40523/2016 dated 22.03.2016 reported in 2016 (344) E.L.T. 1034 (Tri.-Chennai), the appellant’s appeal was allowed in these words. There is no case of Revenue that input cleared by respondent is diverted elsewhere without being used in the exported goods. Section 51 of SEZ Act has overriding effect on any other law for the time being in force dealing with exports. Accordingly, following the rationale of Section 51 of SEZ Act, appellant is not required to reverse the Cenvat credit availed on the input cleared to its SEZ unit for manufacture of exported goods. 14. Nothing placed before us suggests that either decision has been set aside or stayed, and the Authorised Representative did not contend otherwise. The periods now in dispute simply continue the earlier ones, and neither the law nor the facts have changed in between. The two decisions also accord with the view of every High Court that has examined the question. The Kerala High Court in FCI OEN Connectors, on inputs removed as such to an SEZ unit, set aside a demand under Rule 3(5) and reasoned thus. “Since the Legislature had intentionally included Section 51 to give an overriding effect to the SEZ Act, wherever it is inconsistent with any other law for the time being in force, the exemptions provided under Section 26(3) cannot be denied to a transfer of inputs in the DTA to a unit in the SEZ.” 15. The Andhra Pradesh High Court dismissed the appeal of the Revenue against Sujana Metal Products in Commissioner v. Sujana Metal Products Ltd., 2016 (342) E.L.T. A115 (A.P.), the Revenue conceding that the issue was covered by Union of India v. Steel Authority of India Ltd., 2013 (297) E.L.T. 166 (Chhattisgarh). There the Chhattisgarh High Court held that goods supplied from the DTA to an SEZ are exports by force of Section 2(m) of the SEZ Act and must carry the benefits that the law gives to exports. No contrary decision of any High Court has been cited before us. 16. The statutory reasoning is short. Section 2(m) of the SEZ Act includes within export, inter-alia, the supply of goods from the DTA to a unit, and the definition is not confined to goods the supplier has manufactured. Section 26(1)(c) exempts goods brought from the DTA to an SEZ unit for its authorised operations from any duty of excise under the Act or any other law for the time being in force. Section 51(1) gives the SEZ Act effect notwithstanding anything inconsistent in any other law. Rule 3(5), a provision of subordinate legislation, would recover from inputs entering an SEZ unit the very excise incidence which Section 26(1)(c) removes, and to that extent it must yield. The CCR themselves point the same way, since Rule 6(6)(i) relieves goods cleared to an SEZ unit for its authorised operations from the rigour of Rule 6. The contrary view of the adjudicating authority, that only manufactured goods may enter an SEZ free of duty, finds no support in the text of the SEZ Act. 17. Sundaram Brake Linings, 2014 (299) ELT 342 (Tri-Chennai) on which both the authorities below rested, does not persuade us to take a different course. It drew its principal support from the Larger Bench in Lakshmi Automatic Loom Works, 2008 (232) ELT 428 (Tri-LB) which concerned inputs cleared to a hundred per cent export oriented undertaking under Notification No. 1/95-CE in the regime of Rule 57F, long before the SEZ Act. That Larger Bench order has since been set aside by the jurisdictional High Court in Lakshmi Automatic Loom Works Ltd. v. CESTAT, Chennai, 2019 (367) E.L.T. 368 (Mad.), and the matter remitted for fresh consideration in the light of Commissioner of Central Excise, Bangalore-II v. Solectron Centum Electronics Ltd., 2014 (309) E.L.T. 479 (Kar.). On remand, this Tribunal in Lakshmi Automatic Loom Works Ltd. v. Commissioner of CGST and Central Excise, Coimbatore, Final Order Nos. 42161-42164/2021 dated 30.08.2021 – CESTAT Chennai held that the assessee was entitled to clear the inputs as such to the export oriented undertaking without reversing the credit. The authority on which Sundaram Brake Linings principally rested has thus been displaced, and the law has since moved the other way. Sundaram Brake Linings also turned on its own facts, namely that the inputs had gone on invoices without any export procedure and without the permission required under Rule 19(2) of the Central Excise Rules, 2002. It did not notice the order of 2012 in the appellant’s case. Both Sundaram Brake Linings and the decision of 2016 in the appellant’s case are orders of a single Member. Sitting as a Division Bench, we must choose between them, and we follow the view which the High Courts have since endorsed. 18. There remains the observation of the adjudicating authority, in paragraph 16 of the Order in Original, that the appellant had produced no ARE-1 or like document to show that the procedure for clearance to an SEZ had been followed. The observation did not arise of its own accord. In paragraphs 19 and 20 of its reply dated 10.09.2014 to Show Cause Notice No. 11/2014 the appellant had itself pleaded that Rule 19 of the Central Excise Rules, 2002, the Board’s Manual of instructions and Rule 30(1) of the SEZ Rules require a DTA supplier to clear goods to an SEZ unit as in the case of exports, under bond or under cover of ARE-1, and had drawn from this the conclusion that no reversal of credit was called for. Paragraph 16 of the Order in Original answers that plea in its first sentence, and the remark about ARE-1 follows from it. The submission of learned Counsel that the adjudicating authority did not deal with Rule 30 is therefore not borne out by the record. Its answer on Rule 30, that only manufactured goods may be cleared to an SEZ unit without payment of duty, is a different matter, and we have found it unsupported by the SEZ Act in paragraph 16 above. 19. The observation nevertheless cannot sustain the demand. None of the four notices alleged that the clearances were made outside the prescribed procedure, or that the goods did not reach the SEZ unit for its authorised operations. Each accepts that the goods were removed to the SEZ unit and alleges only that the amount under Rule 3(5) was not paid. A defence that is not fully made good may fail, but its failure does not furnish the Revenue with a fresh foundation for a demand that the notice never raised. The show cause notice marks the limits of the case the assessee is called upon to meet, as the Supreme Court held in Commissioner of Customs, Mumbai v. Toyo Engineering India Ltd., 2006 (201) E.L.T. 513 (S.C.) and Commissioner of Central Excise, Bhubaneswar-I v. Champdany Industries Ltd., 2009 (241) E.L.T. 481 (S.C.). Had the Department doubted that the goods reached the SEZ unit, it was for the Department to say so in the notices. It did not. The finding that the credit was itself wrongly availed is also untenable for the same reason. It is outside the notices and at odds with Rule 3(5), which presupposes credit validly taken on inputs later removed as such. The exemption under Section 26 of the SEZ Act is, moreover, a substantive benefit that a procedural lapse does not defeat where eligibility is not in doubt, as held in Eclerx Services Ltd. v. Commissioner of CGST and Central Excise, Navi Mumbai, 2023 (72) G.S.T.L. 99 (Tri.-Mumbai), affirmed in Commissioner of CGST, Navi Mumbai v. Eclerx Services Ltd., 2023 (72) G.S.T.L. 4 (S.C.), and in GMR Aerospace Engineering Ltd. v. Union of India, 2019 (31) G.S.T.L. 596 (A.P.), against which the special leave petition of the Revenue was dismissed in (2023) 6 Centax 155 (S.C.). 20. We therefore hold that the appellant was not liable to pay any amount under Rule 3(5) of the CCR on the inputs and capital goods removed as such to its SEZ unit, and that none of the four demands can stand. In this view the further contentions founded on Rule 19 of the Central Excise Rules, 2002, the circulars of the Board and the levy of export duty do not call for decision, and we do not express any opinion on them. (ii) The refusal of the lower authorities to follow this Tribunal 21. The manner in which the authorities below dealt with the decisions of this Tribunal calls for comment. The adjudicating authority, as noticed in paragraph 3, declined to follow the order of 2012 on the footing that the Department had accepted it only on monetary grounds and not on merits, so that it had not attained finality. That reasoning confuses two distinct questions. Whether an order has become final between the parties is one question. Whether it binds the authorities below as a precedent is quite another. The binding force of a decision of this Tribunal upon the authorities subordinate to it does not depend on the Department accepting it, or even on its having become final. The Supreme Court in Union of India v. Kamlakshi Finance Corporation Ltd., 1991 (55) E.L.T. 433 (S.C.) : 1992 Supp (1) SCC 443 held that a pending appeal is itself no ground for not following an appellate order unless its operation has been suspended by a competent Court. “The principles of judicial discipline require that the orders of the higher appellate authorities should be followed unreservedly by the subordinate authorities. The mere fact that the order of the appellate authority is not “acceptable” to the department – in itself an objectionable phrase – and is the subject-matter of an appeal can furnish no ground for not following it unless its operation has been suspended by a competent Court.” 22. If an order under challenge must be followed until it is stayed, an order which the Department has chosen not to challenge must be followed with greater reason. The statement recorded by the adjudicating authority, that the order of 2012 lacked binding force because it was accepted only on monetary grounds, is therefore impermissible in law, and a demand confirmed on its strength cannot stand. We hold accordingly. 23. Section 35R of the Act, in force from 20.10.2010, poses no impediment to our so holding. Sub-section (2) preserves the right of the Department to appeal in any other case involving the same issue. Sub­section (3) forbids a party from contending that the Department acquiesced in a decision by not appealing against it. Sub-section (4) directs the appellate forum to have regard to the circumstances in which an appeal was not filed. Not one of these provisions says that a decision left unchallenged on account of the monetary limit loses its force as a precedent, or that the authorities below may disregard it. Our conclusion does not rest on any acquiescence of the Department, nor on the finality of the order of 2012 between the parties. It rests on the binding character of a decision of this Tribunal upon its subordinate authorities, which Section 35R leaves untouched. Having regard to the circumstance in which no appeal was filed, we find it to have been the monetary limit alone, a reason unconnected with the correctness of the decision. The liberty that sub-section (2) gave the Department was to carry the issue in appeal in another case. It gave the adjudicating authority no licence to ignore the decision in the meantime. 24. The Principal Bench of this Tribunal has read Section 35R in the same way. In Prem Henna Pvt. Ltd. v. Commissioner of Central GST and Central Excise, Jodhpur, 2025 (1) TMI 664 – CESTAT New Delhi the Commissioner had invoked Section 35R and the Board’s Instruction F. No. 390/Misc./163/2010-JC dated 20.10.2010 to deny precedent value to two such orders. The Tribunal held that portion of the Instruction contrary both to Section 35R and to judicial discipline, and observed that unless a decision of the Tribunal is set aside by the High Court or the Supreme Court, it will continue to bind the revenue authorities empowered to decide matters under the provisions of the Central Excise Act. In Varun Beverages Ltd. v. Commissioner of Customs, GST and Central Excise, Jaipur, Final Order No. 50105/2022 dated 07.02.2022 : 2022 (2) TMI 731 – CESTAT New Delhi it had held that the Commissioner (Appeals) was bound by an order in the assessee’s own case accepted on monetary grounds, and would have been bound even had the Department appealed against it, so long as the order stood. This Bench took the same view in TVS Credit Services Ltd. v. Commissioner of GST and Central Excise, Chennai North, 2026 (9)TMI 657-Cestat Chennai, holding that a decision of this Tribunal does not lose its character as a precedent merely because it went unchallenged for reasons unconnected with its correctness. 25. The lapse of the Commissioner (Appeals) is the graver one. He had before him a decision of this Tribunal rendered more than a year earlier in the appellant’s own case, on the identical question, which had set aside the demand confirmed for the earlier period and, with it, Order in Appeal No. 215/2013 (M-II) on which two of the present notices were founded. He in effect sat in appeal over that decision, preferring an earlier single Member order on the ground that the later Bench had not considered it. Whether a decision of this Tribunal is per incuriam is not a question for an authority subordinate to it. In Godrej Sara Lee Ltd. v. Excise and Taxation Officer-cum-Assessing Authority, 2023 (384) E.L.T. 8 (S.C.) the Supreme Court said so in plain terms. “Unless the discipline of adhering to decisions made by the higher authorities is maintained, there would be utter chaos in administration of tax laws apart from undue harassment to assessees.” 26. The jurisdictional High Court has recently reaffirmed that an unstayed appellate order must be followed whatever the stand of the Department, in Principal Chief Commissioner of GST and Central Excise, Chennai v. Senior Regional Manager, Tamil Nadu Civil Supplies Corporation, Thanjavur, 2026 (10) TMI 123 – Madras High Court. Orders of the kind under appeal do the Revenue no service. They have kept alive for over a decade a question this Tribunal had settled for this very assessee, and they burden the appellate system with litigation that judicial discipline exists to prevent. We trust that the authorities concerned will bear this in mind. (iii) Interest and penalty 27. With the demands gone, the interest ordered under Section 11AA of the Act and the penalty of Rs.1,00,000/- imposed under Rule 15(1) of the CCR on the first demand cannot survive. The Commissioner (Appeals) found the penalty reasonable only because the credit for October 2012 to June 2013 had not been reversed, a reason that presupposes the very liability we have negatived. The appropriation of Rs.3,85,635/-, Rs.3,10,159/- and Rs.40,03,446/- paid under protest for July 2013 to June 2015 likewise loses its foundation. Conclusion 28. In sum, the clearances of inputs and capital goods to the appellant’s SEZ unit are exports within Section 2(m) of the SEZ Act, the exemption in Section 26(1)(c) attaches to them, and by Section 51 that exemption prevails over Rule 3(5) of the CCR. That is the view this Tribunal had twice taken for the appellant, and the High Courts have taken the same view. The authorities below were bound to follow it and erred in declining to do so. The impugned order cannot be sustained. 29. In light of our aforesaid discussions and analysis, the impugned Order in Appeal No. 13 to 16/2017 (CTA-I) dated 25.07.2017 is set aside. In consequence, the demands raised by Statement of Demand No. 14/2013 dated 07.11.2013, Show Cause Notice No. 11/2014 dated 06.08.2014, Show Cause Notice No. 02/2015 dated 04.02.2015 and Statement of Demand No. 46/2015 dated 27.07.2015, together with the interest and the penalty of Rs.1,00,000/-, stand set aside; the vacation of protest and the appropriation of Rs.3,85,635/-, Rs.3,10,159/- and Rs.40,03,446/- paid under protest stand set aside, and the appellant shall be entitled to consequential relief in accordance with law. The appeals are allowed in the above terms.

Articles 6 min read IRDAI commission curbs could push 15-40% of LIC agents out over 2-4 years, deter new recruits, warn industry veterans Former LIC Managing Director Vipin Anand estimates that 15% of LIC agents could exit over two years, while LIAFI Chairman N Gajapathi Rao warns that attrition could reach 40% over three to four years. Both flag risks to agent livelihoods, recruitment and insurance distribution as IRDAI proposes tighter commission caps and expense limits. Anushka Sengupta ETBFSI Updated On Oct 21, 2026 at 01:09 PM IST Read by 100 Professionals var news_msid = 135060330; console.log(news_msid); Select ETBFSI as Preferred Choice on Google Copy Link Share on WhatsApp Share on Linkedin Share on X Share on Telegram Share on Facebook The Insurance Regulatory and Development Authority of India's (IRDAI) proposed overhaul of insurance commissions and expenses has raised concerns over the long-term viability of the agency distribution model, with industry veterans warning that tighter payouts could discourage new entrants, accelerate agent attrition and complicate efforts to expand insurance penetration.Former LIC Managing Director Vipin Anand and N Gajapathi Rao, Chairman – Organisation, IRDAI & Political Affairs, LIAFI, said the impact of the proposed framework would extend beyond headline commission rates to the incentives, operating costs and earning potential that sustain insurance sales. While Anand estimates that around 15% of LIC agents could leave over two years, Rao warns that attrition could reach 40% over three to four years if the proposals are implemented. Advt "People who do not have their livelihood dependent on it, especially people who are doing this business part-time or even new agents, are likely to move away because it may not be attractive enough anymore," Anand told ETBFSI.Anand said the potential exits would largely involve part-time agents and those who earn relatively little from insurance distribution, rather than agents who rely on it as their primary livelihood. He estimated that agents who might leave could account for around 1-2% of LIC's business.Rao offered a more severe estimate, warning that the proposed changes could make it harder for LIC to retain its existing agents and attract younger people into the profession."People like us who have 30 or 40 years of service in LIC cannot leave till our death, because there is no alternative for us. But if stringent actions such as clawbacks are introduced, people will not be encouraged to join the profession, and people will definitely leave," Rao said.The two estimates are not directly comparable: Anand's assessment covers two years, while Rao's warning spans three to four years. Neither represents an official projection. However, both point to the risk that changes in agent remuneration could affect the industry's ability to recruit and retain the people responsible for selling long-term insurance products. Advt IRDAI's consultation paper proposes changes to commission structures and expense limits, alongside stronger safeguards against mis-selling. The regulator is seeking to rein in distribution costs and improve policyholder outcomes, but the industry veterans argue that the final framework must also account for the economics of selling and servicing insurance.Commission caps could hit incentives as well as earningsAnand argued that the impact of the proposals cannot be assessed by looking at headline commission rates alone. He said non-commission incentives, including gifts, travel benefits and other rewards, have contributed to agents' overall earnings, and restrictions on these payouts could affect the attractiveness of the profession. Advt "The impact is not on commission. The impact is going to fall very heavily on the incentives, because the figures given in the paper include both commission and non-commission incentives. The commission may look very healthy at 20% or 25%, but these figures include all kinds of incentives that were substantial, and agents will realise the impact over time," Anand said.He recommended allowing insurers to spend an additional 10% of premium on incentives and marketing expenses over and above the proposed commission limits. He also said the regulator should consider the time, travel and follow-up involved in converting a prospective customer into a policyholder. Advt "For selling one life insurance policy, you have to visit at least 10 customers, and the one customer who agrees to buy will also make you run around four times. All that time and money investment is, I feel, not properly taken care of," Anand said.Rao also argued that agents' earnings need to be assessed alongside the costs they incur to secure and service policies. He said a profession that relies on repeated customer visits and personal relationships could become difficult to sustain if remuneration is reduced without addressing these costs."An agent has to knock on the door of a particular individual five times, 10 times or even 20 times to get one policy. The apparent income may look high, but the practical expenditure is also very high. Instead of spending money on contests and rewards, insurers should focus on making the agency profession financially viable," Rao said.The debate is particularly relevant for life insurance, where products often require explanation and repeated engagement before a customer commits to a long-term premium obligation. The veterans argue that reducing distribution costs should not come at the expense of the human network that brings customers into the insurance system.LIC could gain market share, but recruitment remains a concernDespite the risks to agent retention, Anand believes the proposed changes could also create opportunities for LIC, particularly in bancassurance. He argued that differences in incentives offered by insurers can influence which products bank partners distribute, and that restrictions on such incentives could alter the competitive balance."Now, if the incentive gets pared, selling LIC policies becomes easier. Some of the business that was going to private companies from the same banks may start coming to LIC, because a lot of customers prefer LIC," Anand said.He expects LIC could benefit if the relative advantage enjoyed by some private insurers in bancassurance distribution narrows. However, any gains in market share would not automatically resolve the longer-term challenge of retaining agents and attracting new recruits.Rao warned that the agency channel could struggle to bring in younger people if the profession is perceived as offering uncertain earnings without sufficient recognition or career prospects."Insurance is not recognised as a profession by the Government of India in the way doctors, lawyers or chartered accountants are recognised. If the profession is given greater recognition and importance in society, people will have more confidence in agents and the business will also grow," Rao said.Rao also argued that the response to rising distribution costs should include a broader review of insurers' operating expenses and policyholder returns, rather than focusing primarily on commissions. He called for greater efficiency in LIC's branch network and better services for policyholders, alongside measures to improve bonuses and make the agency channel more sustainable.Both veterans also cautioned against treating commission controls as a complete solution to mis-selling. Anand called for a broader review of insurer conduct and accountability, while Rao said penalties should target those responsible for deliberate mis-selling rather than indiscriminately penalising agents when policyholders discontinue policies for financial reasons.The challenge for IRDAI will be to balance lower distribution costs and stronger consumer protection with the financial viability of insurance sales. For LIC, the proposed changes could offer opportunities in some distribution channels, but the differing attrition estimates from Anand and Rao underscore a shared concern: the final framework must preserve incentives to sell insurance while ensuring that policyholders receive better value and protection.

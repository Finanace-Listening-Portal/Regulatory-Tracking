Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); National Research Development Corporation Vs Hindustan Zinc Ltd (Supreme Court of India) Summary: The Supreme Court of India considered appeals filed by National Research Development Corporation (NRDC) against orders dated 24 January 2023 and 10 March 2023 passed by the Delhi High Court in proceedings relating to enforcement of an arbitral award involving Hindustan Zinc Ltd. The central question before the Supreme Court was not whether the arbitral award was legally correct on its merits, but how the High Court had handled NRDC’s objections to execution while a separate request to stay the operation or execution of the award remained pending. The Court granted leave, allowed the appeals and set aside both impugned High Court orders. Its reasoning, however, was carefully qualified: the Supreme Court expressly considered NRDC’s objections under Section 47 of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 to be without merit and beyond the scope and jurisdiction of a court executing a decree. Thus, the setting aside of the High Court orders did not amount to acceptance of NRDC’s challenge to the award. The dispute arose in the context of an arbitration award whose enforcement was being pursued before the High Court. NRDC, as the party resisting enforcement, had filed objections or an application invoking Section 47 of the Code of Civil Procedure. That provision ordinarily concerns questions to be determined by a court executing a decree. The Supreme Court’s order draws an important distinction between the role of an executing court and the statutory process for challenging or staying an arbitral award. An executing court is concerned with enforcement and does not thereby acquire general appellate or supervisory authority to reopen the merits of the award. The broader statutory framework for arbitration is contained in the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996, which separately regulates challenges to awards and their enforcement. The Supreme Court’s statement about Section 47 should therefore not be overlooked merely because the appeals ultimately succeeded. The Court found a different reason to intervene. In its view, the High Court’s order dated 24 January 2023 contained a number of comments and observations that should not operate as binding findings when the pending application for stay came to be decided. NRDC had already moved an application under the Arbitration and Conciliation Act seeking a stay of the operation or execution of the award, and that application was awaiting consideration by the learned Single Judge. The Supreme Court was concerned that statements made while dealing with the execution objections could influence or constrain the judge deciding the stay application. It therefore set aside the High Court orders to prevent those observations from acquiring prejudicial effect. This was a procedural intervention directed at preserving a fair and independent determination of the pending application, not a finding that NRDC had established grounds to invalidate the award. The Supreme Court also observed that the objections or application in the execution proceedings and the proceedings for enforcement of the award should have been listed before the same Judge who was dealing with the request for a stay. This observation reflects the practical relationship between enforcement proceedings and an application seeking to suspend enforcement. Although the two involve different legal questions, inconsistent or overlapping directions can arise if they are considered separately without coordination. The Court did not formulate a general rule requiring every arbitration-related application to be heard by one judge in all circumstances. Rather, it addressed the procedural arrangement in this particular dispute and identified a preferable course given the pendency of NRDC’s stay application. The direction should be understood in that factual and procedural context. The distinction between an application challenging an arbitral award and an application for a stay of its enforcement is particularly important. The post-2015 framework for Section 36 generally separates the filing of a challenge under Section 34 from the grant of a stay: filing a challenge does not, by itself, automatically prevent enforcement. A party seeking protection ordinarily must obtain a stay order in accordance with the applicable statutory requirements. The Supreme Court’s order in the present case did not decide whether NRDC satisfied those requirements. Instead, it specifically preserved the High Court’s authority to consider the pending stay request on its own merits. The existence of a pending application was relevant to the Supreme Court’s concern about prejudicial observations, but was not treated as proof that enforcement must be stayed. The Court also dealt with an interim monetary direction made during the appeals. By an order dated 13 April 2023, the Supreme Court had stayed operation of the impugned High Court judgments or orders and required NRDC to deposit ₹3 crore in the Supreme Court within four weeks from 13 April 2023. In the final order supplied, the Court partly modified that arrangement. It directed NRDC to deposit ₹3 crore in the Delhi High Court instead, within five weeks from 13 April 2023. The wording is significant because the five-week period was expressly calculated from the earlier date, rather than from an unspecified later date. The Court did not waive the deposit, reduce its amount or declare that NRDC was entitled to recover the sum. It altered the forum for the deposit and the stipulated time while maintaining the underlying requirement to provide the money. The Supreme Court further directed that the deposited amount be converted into a fixed deposit for one year with an automatic renewal clause. It stated that the deposit would abide by the directions or orders of the High Court. This arrangement preserved the funds while leaving the High Court responsible for subsequent orders affecting them. The deposit was therefore an interim protective measure rather than a final determination of the parties’ substantive monetary entitlements. Nothing in the supplied order establishes that the amount was paid over to Hindustan Zinc Ltd., forfeited by NRDC, or finally allocated between the parties. The order also does not specify the bank, the rate of interest or any final disposition of accrued interest. Those matters should not be inferred from the fixed-deposit direction. As regards the pending stay application, the Supreme Court expressly stated that the learned Single Judge could decide NRDC’s application for stay of operation or execution of the arbitral award as expeditiously as possible. The order records that the application was stated to be fixed for hearing on 30 May 2023. That hearing date was mentioned as part of the procedural status before the Supreme Court; it was not a finding that the application was actually decided on that date or that any particular relief was granted. The Supreme Court left the substantive question of a stay open for the High Court. The scope of that enquiry, and any conditions that might be imposed, would be governed by the applicable arbitration provisions, including the rules concerning stay of arbitral awards under Section 36, to the extent relevant to the case. The Court’s express reservation on the merits is a defining feature of the decision. It clarified that it had made no comments or observations on the merits of the case, including the pending application or applications for stay under the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996. This qualification prevents the appellate disposal from being misunderstood as an endorsement of NRDC’s substantive position or of Hindustan Zinc Ltd.’s response to it. The Supreme Court accepted that the Section 47 objections lacked merit, yet still found sufficient procedural reason to set aside the High Court orders. Those propositions are not contradictory. One concerns the limited jurisdiction of an executing court; the other concerns the possible effect of judicial observations on a different application that had not yet been adjudicated. For parties involved in arbitration enforcement, the order illustrates the need to distinguish between attacking the award itself, requesting a stay of enforcement and raising objections to the mechanics of execution. Commentary on the execution of arbitral awards for recovery of money discusses these distinct procedural stages. The present Supreme Court order is narrower than a comprehensive ruling on all permissible execution objections. It states that the particular Section 47 objections filed by NRDC were without merit and beyond the executing court’s jurisdiction, but does not reproduce or separately decide every legal proposition discussed in the earlier High Court judgment. Accordingly, it would be inaccurate to describe this order as holding that all questions connected with execution are outside Section 47, or conversely as recognising a fresh opportunity to challenge an award on its merits through execution proceedings. The effect on the earlier Delhi High Court decision must likewise be described with precision. The Supreme Court set aside the orders dated 24 January 2023 and 10 March 2023. As a result, the impugned orders could not continue to operate in their earlier form, and the Supreme Court specifically sought to prevent comments in the 24 January order from being treated as binding findings when the stay application was heard. At the same time, the Supreme Court’s own observation that NRDC’s Section 47 objections were meritless remains an express part of its order. Readers should not confuse the formal setting aside of the High Court orders with a substantive rejection of the legal limits on collateral challenges to arbitral awards. The decision is best understood as separating the proper route for seeking a stay from the impermissible attempt to reopen the award through an execution objection. Ultimately, the Supreme Court allowed and disposed of the appeals in the terms stated, directed the ₹3 crore deposit in the High Court with fixed-deposit protection, and left the stay application for expeditious consideration by the learned Single Judge. Pending applications before the Supreme Court, if any, were also disposed of. The supplied text repeats the concluding disposition and the direction regarding pending applications; that repetition does not change the operative effect. No final adjudication of the arbitral award’s validity, the merits of the underlying commercial dispute or the ultimate outcome of the High Court stay proceedings appears in this order. The principal takeaway is therefore twofold: Section 47 execution objections could not be used by NRDC to secure a merits review of the arbitral award, but the High Court’s orders were nonetheless set aside to ensure that the separately pending stay application could be considered without being constrained by the observations made in the execution proceedings. We are inclined to set aside the impugned judgments/orders dated 24.01.2023 and 10.03.2023 passed by the High Court, even when in our view, the objections/application under Section 47 of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 filed by the present appellant – National Research Development Corporation, was without merit, being beyond the scope and jurisdiction of the court executing a decree. The reason why we have set aside the impugned judgment/order dated 24.01.2023 is that a number of comments and observations have been made in the order, which should not operate as a binding finding, as it is to be noted that the application for stay of operation/execution of the award filed by the appellant – National Research Development Corporation under the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996, is pending consideration of the learned Single Judge. In our opinion, the objections/application and the execution proceedings for enforcement of the award should have been listed before the same Judge. By order dated 13.04.2023, while staying the operation of the impugned judgments/orders, we directed the appellant – National Research Development Corporation to deposit Rs.3 crores in this Court within a period of four weeks from 13.04.2023. We partly modify this order with the direction that Rs.3 crores would be deposited in the High Court by the appellant – National Research Development Corporation within a period of five weeks from 13.04.2023. The deposit would be converted into a Fixed Deposit for a period of one year with an auto renewal clause. The deposit would abide by the direction/order of the High Court. In the meanwhile, it will be open to the learned Single Judge to decide the application for stay of the operation/execution of the arbitration award filed by the appellant – National Research Development Corporation, which is stated to be fixed for hearing on 30.05.2023, as expeditiously as possible. Recording the aforesaid, the appeals are allowed and disposed of in the above terms. We clarify that we have not made any comments and observations on the merits of the case including the application (s for stay under the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996, which are pending before the learned Single Judge. Pending application(s), if any, shall stand disposed of. The appeals are allowed and disposed of in terms of the signed order. Pending application(s), if any, shall stand disposed of.

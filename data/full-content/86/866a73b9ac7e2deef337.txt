Summary: The NVIDIA leadership controversy highlights the importance of workplace dignity, corporate governance, leadership accountability and effective implementation of the Sexual Harassment of Women at Workplace (Prevention, Prohibition and Redressal) Act, 2013 (POSH Act). Reported developments involving senior leadership and personal relationships raise important questions about professional boundaries, conflicts of interest and the potential misuse of organisational authority. However, consensual relationships or differences in professional power do not automatically constitute sexual harassment, and the reported circumstances do not establish any POSH violation. Under Section 3 of the POSH Act, unwelcome sexual conduct and circumstances involving preferential treatment, threats to employment or a hostile work environment may attract statutory protection. The article emphasises that POSH compliance must extend beyond maintaining policies, constituting Internal Committees and completing statutory formalities. Organisations must ensure independent complaint-handling mechanisms, leadership sensitisation, accessible reporting channels, protection against retaliation and effective board-level oversight. Section 6 provides an important safeguard by prescribing the Local Committee as the forum for complaints against the employer. The discussion particularly highlights the need for accountability when allegations involve CEOs, Managing Directors or other influential executives. A strong preventive framework helps organisations protect employees, minimise workplace risks and preserve institutional credibility. The central message is that POSH should be treated not merely as a legal obligation but as an essential element of responsible corporate governance, where prevention takes priority over damage control and workplace dignity remains non-negotiable. Is Corporate India Listening? The corporate world has recently witnessed developments surrounding NVIDIA and its former CEO, Jensen Huang, that have brought leadership conduct, personal relationships and corporate accountability into international focus. In November 2026, media reports raised questions about Huang’s personal relationships, including a reported relationship with a senior NVIDIA executive who allegedly reported directly to him. The reported relationship was not confirmed by the individuals concerned or NVIDIA. Huang also acknowledged infidelity in a message to employees. On November 12, 2026, NVIDIA officially announced his resignation as CEO and board member, effective immediately. These developments attracted international attention and brought the organisation’s leadership and governance arrangements under scrutiny. It is important to distinguish between reported personal relationships and established misconduct. The reported circumstances do not establish a violation of sexual harassment law, and there is no established finding that NVIDIA failed to comply with POSH requirements. Yet, this episode presents an important opportunity for corporate India to ask a much bigger question: Are we building organisations that are merely successful, or are we building organisations that are safe, accountable and respectful? Because corporate reputation is not determined by market capitalisation alone. It is also determined by the values an organisation practises when nobody is watching. 1. The Most Expensive Mistake Is to Treat Prevention As a Formality Organisations spend enormous resources protecting their financial assets, intellectual property, technology, business interests and market reputation. But how seriously do they protect their workplace culture? A POSH policy is often treated as a statutory document. An Internal Committee is constituted, awareness programmes are conducted, notices are displayed and annual returns are filed. Compliance may appear complete on paper. But is the workplace actually safe? Does every employee know how to report inappropriate conduct? Can an employee raise a concern against a senior executive? Does the leadership understand the consequences of misusing professional authority? Can the Internal Committee function independently when a powerful individual is involved? These are the questions that distinguish genuine compliance from paperwork. “A policy in a file cannot protect an employee. A policy in practice can protect an entire organisation.” 2. Why the NVIDIA Controversy Makes POSH Relevant The reported relationship involving a senior executive with a direct reporting connection to the CEO brings attention to a critical workplace issue: the intersection of personal relationships and professional authority. Consider an organisation where the CEO directly or indirectly influences an employee’s: Promotion and career progression. Performance appraisal. Salary and incentives. Professional opportunities. Employment continuity. Now consider what happens when a personal relationship develops or subsequently deteriorates. Can the subordinate exercise complete professional independence? Can she disagree without apprehension? Can she withdraw from a relationship without fearing adverse professional consequences? These questions are particularly important where there is a substantial difference in organisational authority. However, one legal distinction must be clearly understood: A consensual personal relationship is not automatically sexual harassment. A power imbalance, by itself, does not establish a POSH violation. The POSH Act, 2013 becomes relevant when the facts disclose unwelcome sexual conduct or circumstances falling within its statutory definition. Section 3 of the Act specifically recognises circumstances such as promises of preferential treatment, threats of detrimental treatment, threats to employment and the creation of an intimidating, offensive or hostile work environment. Therefore, the focus must be on the actual conduct, the circumstances surrounding it and whether the statutory requirements are satisfied. “Consent cannot be presumed. Authority cannot be misused. And professional opportunity must never become a bargaining instrument.” 3. POSH: Prevention Is the First Line of Protection The very name of the legislation tells us its purpose: Prevention. Prohibition. Redressal. Unfortunately, many organisations focus more on redressal than prevention. They wait for an incident, a complaint or an uncomfortable situation before reviewing their policies. That approach defeats the larger objective of workplace protection. An effective POSH framework should work before a complaint arises. It should ensure: POSH training and leadership sensitisation” target=”_blank” rel=”noopener noreferrer”>Leadership Sensitisation: Every CEO, director, manager and senior executive must understand professional boundaries, workplace power dynamics and accountability. Independent Internal Committee: The Internal Committee must be properly constituted, trained and empowered to discharge its statutory functions without interference. Accessible Reporting Mechanism: Employees should be able to report concerns without fear of retaliation, humiliation or career disadvantage. Professional Boundaries: Organisations should establish appropriate standards of conduct and address actual or perceived conflicts of interest. Continuous Awareness: POSH awareness should not be a once-a-year exercise. It must become part of organisational culture. POSH compliance” target=”_blank” rel=”noopener noreferrer”>Board-Level Oversight: The Board must ensure that POSH compliance is effective in practice and not merely reflected in statutory records. The objective is not to monitor or prohibit every personal relationship between employees. It is to prevent sexual harassment, coercion, retaliation and misuse of workplace authority. 4. The Real Test of POSH: When the Person Accused Is the CEO This is where the true strength of an organisation’s POSH framework is tested. Imagine an employee raising a sexual harassment complaint against the Managing Director or CEO. Will the employee feel safe enough to complain? Will the Internal Committee be able to function without pressure? Will the person holding the highest office be treated in accordance with the same legal standards as every other employee? The Indian POSH Act provides a specific mechanism for such situations. Under Section 6, where a complaint is against the employer himself, the Local Committee is the prescribed forum for receiving the complaint. This is a critical statutory safeguard against the concentration of authority in the complaint-handling process. “A CEO may occupy the highest chair in the organisation, but that chair does not place anyone above the law.” The credibility of a POSH framework is demonstrated by its ability to protect an employee even when the respondent is powerful, influential or indispensable to the business. 5. The Cost of Damage Control Is Greater Than the Investment in Prevention When a corporate controversy reaches international headlines, the consequences may extend well beyond the immediate circumstances. Leadership credibility may be questioned. Employees may lose confidence. Stakeholders may examine governance arrangements. The Board may face difficult questions. The organisation may have to spend considerable time rebuilding trust. This is why POSH must be viewed as an essential component of corporate risk management. An effective preventive framework cannot guarantee that controversies will never arise. It cannot regulate every personal decision or eliminate every reputational risk. But it can establish clear standards, create independent reporting mechanisms, discourage inappropriate conduct and provide a lawful process for addressing complaints. Compare the effort required to conduct meaningful POSH training, strengthen the Internal Committee and sensitise senior management with the potential human and organisational consequences of failing to address workplace risks. Prevention is not an expense. It is an investment in people, reputation and institutional credibility. 6. A Message to Every Boardroom in India The NVIDIA developments should encourage every Indian organisation to undertake an honest review of its workplace governance. Ask yourself: 1. Is our POSH policy genuinely implemented or merely documented? 2. Can an employee raise a complaint against the CEO without fear? 3. Is our Internal Committee independent and adequately trained? 4. Do senior management personnel understand that the law applies equally to them? 5. Are professional decisions protected from conflicts of interest and inappropriate influence? 6. Does our organisation encourage employees to speak up, or does hierarchy silence them? If these questions are uncomfortable, they are precisely the questions that need to be asked. A strong organisation does not wait for a crisis to discover the weaknesses in its compliance framework. It identifies and addresses those weaknesses before they become organisational failures. Conclusion: Don’t Wait for the Damage to Become Visible The NVIDIA controversy is a reminder that professional success, global recognition and business leadership must be accompanied by responsible conduct and institutional accountability. The purpose of POSH is much greater than avoiding litigation or completing annual statutory formalities. It is about ensuring that every individual can work without fear of sexual harassment, coercion, humiliation or retaliation. It is about ensuring that power is exercised responsibly. It is about ensuring that the law protects an employee regardless of the designation of the person involved. And above all, it is about creating a culture where dignity is not negotiable. “By the time a workplace controversy becomes a global headline, the consequences may already have travelled far beyond the workplace.” A Final Message to Corporate India Don’t wait for a complaint to start prevention. Don’t wait for a crisis to introduce accountability. Don’t wait for reputational damage to recognise the value of POSH. Because the true measure of leadership is not how much power one commands, but how responsibly that power is exercised. Beyond compliance. Beyond designation. Beyond power. Workplace dignity begins where accountability becomes non-negotiable. Precaution is better than cure. Prevention is better than damage control. And dignity must always come before designation. ****** About the Author: CS Sonal Jain | Company Secretary | POSH Compliance Professional Disclaimer: This article uses publicly reported developments concerning NVIDIA as a corporate governance discussion point.

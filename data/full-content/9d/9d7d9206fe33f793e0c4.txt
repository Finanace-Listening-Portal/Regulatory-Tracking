(Supreme Court of India) Dissenting Creditors Cannot Be Paid Through Debentures: Supreme Court Upholds Reconsideration of Videocon Resolution Plan Introduction The Supreme Court, in Twinstar Technologies Limited v. Bank of Maharashtra & Ors., 2026 INSC 1214, decided on 16 November 2026, has clarified the protection available to dissenting financial creditors under the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016. The Court held that their statutory entitlement cannot be discharged through non-convertible debentures redeemable over several years. The judgment also explains why tribunals cannot rewrite resolution plans, when the Committee of Creditors (CoC) may reconsider an approved plan, and why a subsidiary’s assets cannot automatically become part of its holding company’s insolvency estate. Background of the Dispute The proceedings concerned the consolidated insolvency resolution of Videocon Industries Limited and twelve group companies. Twinstar’s resolution plan, involving a financial outlay of ₹2,962.02 crore, received approval from 95.09% of the CoC’s voting share. Against admitted claims of ₹64,838.63 crore, the plan involved a haircut recorded by the NCLT at 95.85%. Financial creditors were offered ₹200 crore upfront and non-convertible debentures having an aggregate face value of ₹2,700 crore, carrying a 6.65% annual coupon. Redemption would commence 25 months after closing and extend to the sixth year. Certain financial creditors dissented. The NCLT nevertheless approved the plan, directing payment of their liquidation entitlement upfront in cash, before payments to assenting financial creditors. The NCLAT set aside that approval and remitted the matter to the CoC. Debentures Do Not Satisfy the Statutory Payment Requirement The Supreme Court examined Section 30(2)(b), which protects financial creditors who do not vote in favour of a resolution plan. Their payment cannot be less than the amount payable to them under Section 53(1) in a hypothetical liquidation. Relying on Jaypee Kensington Boulevard Apartments Welfare Association v. NBCC (India) Ltd., (2022) 1 SCC 401, the Court reiterated that the contemplated payment must be in money. A creditor choosing to dissent cannot be compelled to continue its financial association with the corporate debtor through securities. The plan did not expressly separate dissenting creditors from the debenture structure. Consequently, debentures redeemable over six years did not constitute the payment required by Section 30(2)(b). The plan also failed to secure their payment priority under Regulation 38(1)(b). A General Compliance Clause Is Insufficient The plan contained a clause stating that dissenting creditors would, in all circumstances, be paid according to the Code and applicable regulations. The Court held that such a general assurance of legal compliance cannot replace specific provisions identifying how, when and in what form payment will occur. Compliance must emerge from the operative terms of the plan itself. This finding has practical significance. A broadly drafted overriding clause cannot rescue a payment mechanism that fails to satisfy mandatory statutory safeguards. NCLT Cannot Rewrite the Payment Structure The NCLT sought to cure the defect by directing upfront cash payment to dissenting creditors. However, the available upfront amount had been earmarked for financial creditors collectively. The direction therefore altered both the payment structure and distribution arrangement approved by the CoC. Such modification fell outside the NCLT’s jurisdiction. Following K. Sashidhar v. Indian Overseas Bank, (2019) 12 SCC 150, and Committee of Creditors of Essar Steel India Ltd. v. Satish Kumar Gupta, (2020) 8 SCC 531, the Court reaffirmed that tribunals examine legality, while commercial decisions belong to the CoC. A defective plan requiring commercial modification must be remitted for reconsideration. Reconsideration Is Permissible Where Approval Is Legally Defective The Court rejected the contention that the CoC necessarily becomes functus officio immediately upon approving a plan. Its continuing statutory role enables it to act where approval is stayed or set aside in appellate proceedings. However, the ruling does not confer an unrestricted right to abandon an otherwise compliant plan. The Court distinguished Ebix Singapore (P) Ltd. v. Committee of Creditors of Educomp Solutions Ltd., (2022) 2 SCC 401, and Bank of India v. S.M. Kamal Pasha, 2026 SCC OnLine SC 2242. Here, reconsideration followed a finding of statutory non-compliance, rather than a commercial change of mind. The Court also declined to restrict reconsideration exclusively to Twinstar’s existing plan. Subsidiary Assets Remain Legally Distinct The connected appeals sought inclusion of foreign oil and gas assets held through subsidiaries in Videocon Industries’ insolvency estate. Applying Explanation (b) to Section 18 and Section 36(4)(d), the Court held that assets of Indian or foreign subsidiaries are excluded from the holding company’s relevant asset pool. Following Vodafone International Holdings B.V. v. Union of India, (2012) 6 SCC 613, it emphasised that ownership of shares does not amount to ownership of the subsidiary’s underlying assets. General allegations of fraud could not overcome this statutory distinction. Author’s Comments The judgment reinforces that commercial wisdom operates within statutory boundaries. Neither overwhelming creditor approval nor a general compliance clause can dispense with dissenting creditors’ mandatory protection. Equally, remand must remain exceptional, legally justified and subject to strict timelines. The decision should therefore be understood as permitting correction of an unlawful plan, without creating a general licence to reopen concluded commercial bargains. The Supreme Court dismissed the appeals and upheld the NCLAT’s remittal. Cases Discussed Vodafone International Holdings B.V. v. Union of India, (2012) 6 SCC 613 K. Sashidhar v.

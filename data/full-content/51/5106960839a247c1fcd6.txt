Summary: DSP Midcap Fund is an open-ended equity scheme predominantly investing in mid-cap stocks, managed by DSP Asset Managers Private Limited under DSP Mutual Fund. Its stated objective is long-term capital appreciation from a portfolio substantially constituted of equity and equity-related securities of mid-cap companies, without any assurance that the objective will be achieved. Official DSP disclosures show total AUM of ₹18,852.7 crore at 31 October 2026, with the displayed portfolio comprising 95.1% Indian equity and 4.9% cash and equivalents; the equity allocation shown on the scheme page was 67.6% mid cap, 16.0% large cap and 11.4% small cap. This article explains what the mandate permits, how current holdings and costs can change, the difference between Direct and Regular plans, the one-year exit-load rule, transaction timelines and current equity-oriented fund taxation. It is a factual scheme review, not a recommendation or return forecast. The regular plan traces the scheme’s allotment to 14 November 2006, while the Direct plan began on 1 January 2013. These dates distinguish the underlying scheme history from the later Direct plan history. The benchmark displayed by the AMC is the Nifty Midcap 150 Total Return Index. The official objective seeks long-term capital appreciation from a portfolio substantially constituted of equity and equity-related securities of mid-cap companies and allows other equity exposure for portfolio construction. The scheme expressly gives no assurance that the objective will be achieved. The 65% mid-cap floor is a category rule, not a fixed portfolio weight A mid-cap fund must ordinarily keep at least 65% of assets in mid-cap companies under the applicable categorisation framework. The remaining allocation can include other equities and permitted instruments within the scheme document. Market movements, purchases, sales, corporate actions and cash management cause the reported percentages to change. A single monthly portfolio is therefore a dated snapshot, not a promise that the same companies or capitalisation mix will remain. Direct, Regular, Growth and IDCW Options Are Not Interchangeable The scheme offers Direct and Regular plans and Growth and IDCW options. The underlying portfolio is common at the scheme level, but plan expenses and therefore NAVs and investor returns differ. A Regular plan includes distributor-related expenses or commissions permitted under the expense structure; a Direct plan excludes that distribution route. Growth retains distributable surplus within the NAV, while IDCW can make distributions when declared. IDCW is neither guaranteed nor additional return because the NAV falls to the extent of the payout and applicable levies. A lower expense ratio does not make a plan automatically appropriate The choice between Direct and Regular involves the investor’s service arrangement and ability to complete suitability, execution and ongoing review. This article does not decide that trade-off. An investor should compare the correct plan and option in all documents, because an application using a distributor code or a different option can produce a different folio classification, NAV series and cost experience. Expense Ratios: Current Numbers and the Need for Date Labels Expense ratios are charged to scheme assets and reduce NAV; they are not separately invoiced to each unitholder. The AMC’s live pages showed Direct-Growth total expense ratio of 0.83% as of 11 November 2026 and Regular-Growth total expense ratio of 1.73% as of 9 November 2026. The same pages showed base expense ratios of 0.65% for Direct and 1.41% for Regular on those dates. Total and base expense ratio are different measures, and the rate can change after the verification date. GST and trading-related costs are reflected within regulated expense reporting The TER display separates base expenses, brokerage, transaction costs and statutory levies, including GST, under the current disclosure format. An investor should use the AMC’s latest daily TER record rather than an old factsheet or third-party screen. A small annual percentage difference compounds through NAV over time, but the realised difference also depends on actual holding period, portfolio returns, flows and future expense changes. For ordinary purchase transactions, applicable NAV depends on a valid request, receipt and realisation of funds before the regulatory cut-off, not merely on clicking submit. The one-year exit-load clock applies to each allotment The current scheme page states an exit load of 1% for units redeemed or switched out up to 12 months from allotment, with no stated load after that period. Each purchase or SIP instalment creates its own allotment date, so an investor can hold some units outside the load period and others within it. Redemption ordinarily follows the scheme’s unit-allocation method and the applicable rules; account statements should be reviewed before assuming which units will be processed. Redemption processing and bank credit are separate steps The AMC page indicates same-day NAV for a valid redemption request before the cut-off, T+1 processing and bank credit by T+2 business days. A non-business day, bank rejection, lien, transmission issue, incomplete mandate or compliance hold can affect timing. A switch-out is also a redemption for load and tax analysis even though proceeds move into another scheme rather than the investor’s bank account. Mid-Cap Risk, Liquidity and Behavioural Pressure The scheme carries a Very High risk label. Mid-cap businesses may have narrower product lines, smaller balance sheets, lower analyst coverage and less liquid shares than established large companies. During a broad fall, portfolio securities can decline together, redemption requests can force liquidity management, and the fund can trail its benchmark or category for long periods. Active management also creates stock-selection, sector-allocation and execution risk. A long holding-period label is not a capital guarantee The AMC displays an ideal holding period of ten years or more, but that is an educational suitability cue rather than a guaranteed recovery schedule. A ten-year holding can still produce an unsatisfactory result. Investors also face sequencing risk: the NAV at the time of a large cash need may be depressed even if the long-run return later improves. SIPs spread purchase dates but do not prevent loss or ensure profit. Market-cap reclassification can move companies into or out of the mid-cap universe. Valuation compression can reduce NAV even when company earnings grow. Sector and stock decisions can cause sustained benchmark underperformance. A large AUM base can affect the ease of entering and exiting less-liquid holdings. IDCW declarations are discretionary and reduce NAV; they are not interest payments. Operational, cyber, registrar, banking and KYC failures can delay transactions. Indian Tax Treatment of DSP Midcap Fund Units DSP Midcap Fund is presented by the AMC as an equity-oriented fund for tax purposes. For a transfer on or after 23 July 2024 that satisfies the statutory conditions, units held for 12 months or less generally produce short-term capital gains taxed at 20% under the special equity provision. Units held for more than 12 months generally produce long-term capital gains taxed at 12.5% on the aggregate qualifying gain above ₹1.25 lakh in the financial year. Surcharge and health and education cess can apply, and the taxpayer’s status and transaction facts matter. Switches, STPs and redemptions can all be taxable transfers A switch from Direct to Regular, Growth to IDCW or one scheme to another is generally processed as redemption of one set of units and purchase of another. An STP instalment similarly involves a switch-out. Capital gain must be computed lot by lot using acquisition cost, redemption value and holding period. Exit load reduces the redemption proceeds under transaction mechanics and should be reflected consistently in the tax working and statement reconciliation. IDCW is taxed separately from capital gains IDCW received by an investor is generally taxable as income at the applicable rate, with tax deduction at source where statutory conditions and thresholds apply. Because IDCW reduces NAV, it should not be compared with a bank-deposit coupon. Non-residents, business holders, minors, trusts and investors using units as trading assets can have different withholding, treaty, characterisation or return-reporting issues. The current law and the investor’s records should be checked for the relevant year. Is DSP Midcap Fund an index fund? No. It is an actively managed open-ended mid-cap equity scheme. Nifty Midcap 150 TRI is its benchmark, not a portfolio that the scheme must mechanically replicate. 2. Must the scheme always hold exactly 65% in mid-cap stocks? No. The category requires a minimum mid-cap exposure, while the actual percentage can be higher and changes with trades and market values. The AMC showed 67.6% mid-cap exposure on 31 October 2026. 3. What were the latest verified Direct and Regular TERs? The AMC pages showed 0.83% for Direct-Growth on 11 November 2026 and 1.73% for Regular-Growth on 9 November 2026. Expense ratios can change, so these are dated figures. 4. Does the 1% exit load disappear for the entire folio after one year? The holding period is tested for each allotment. Later SIP instalments or purchases may still be within 12 months even if older units are outside the load period. 5. Is an IDCW payout an extra return? No. IDCW is declared subject to available distributable surplus and causes NAV to fall to the extent of the payout and applicable levies. It is also taxable in the investor’s hands under current rules. 6. How are redemptions taxed for a resident individual? For qualifying equity-oriented fund units, a holding period of 12 months or less generally attracts 20% short-term capital-gains tax; longer holdings generally attract 12.5% tax on aggregate qualifying gains above ₹1.25 lakh, plus applicable surcharge and cess. 7. Is a switch from Regular to Direct tax-free? Generally no. A switch is processed as redemption of the old units and purchase of new units, so exit load and capital-gains consequences can arise. 8. Does the Very High riskometer mean every investor will lose money? No. It signals the level of market and portfolio risk, not a forecast. Actual gains or losses depend on NAV movements, cash-flow dates, costs, taxes and holding period.

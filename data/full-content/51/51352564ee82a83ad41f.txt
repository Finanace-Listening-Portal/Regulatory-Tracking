Navigational Analysis of Registration Thresholds (Gross Sale Value vs. Profit Margin) under the CGST Act, 2017 for the dealers/traders of Second Hand Vehicles. Summary: A significant statutory ambiguity exists under Goods and Services Tax (GST) law regarding whether a second-hand car dealer must compute “Aggregate Turnover” for registration thresholds (₹40 lakh / ₹20 lakh / ₹10 lakh under Section 22) using the gross selling price of the vehicles or solely the profit margin (value addition) determined under the Margin Scheme. Neither the Central Board of Indirect Taxes and Customs (CBIC) nor the CGST Act explicitly reconciles the valuation mechanism of Rule 32(5) with the registration threshold definition under Section 2(6). However, a strict statutory reading—supported by judicial and departmental interpretations—indicates that counting only the margin amount for calculation of threshold turnover for registration carries severe litigation and penalty risks, and dealers should evaluate registration based on gross sale value unless operating purely as commission agents. The term Aggregate Turnover has been defined in Section 2(6) as “the aggregate value of all taxable supplies (excluding the value of inward supplies on which tax is payable by a person on reverse charge basis), exempt supplies, exports of goods or services or both and inter-State supplies of persons having the same Permanent Account Number…”. Further, the Valuation for the purposes of tax liability is governed by Section 15(1), which stipulates that the value of a supply of goods or services shall be the transaction value, which is the price actually paid or payable for the said supply. However, for the special and peculiar reasons related to the trading of the second hand vehicles, where most of the vehicles come from the individuals and unorganised sector not registered with the GST Department and thus not able to pass-on the input tax credit, special Valuation for Second-Hand Goods has been provided under a scheme known as “Margin Money Scheme”. As per Rule 32(5), where a taxable supply is made by a person dealing in buying and selling second-hand goods (and no Input Tax Credit has been availed on the purchase), “the value of supply shall be the difference between the selling price and the purchase price and where the value of such supply is negative, it shall be ignored.” Why and Where the Ambiguity Exists Section 2(6) relies on the phrase “aggregate value of all taxable supplies”, while Rule 32(5) explicitly defines “the value of supply” for second-hand goods as the margin (which is the actual profit earned by the trader/dealer). Because CBIC circulars have focused on tax payment mechanics and input tax credit eligibility on refurbishment/overheads (such as Circular No. 265/11/2026-GST) without addressing pre-registration threshold math, two competing legal interpretations have emerged. Competing Interpretations: Margin vs Gross Value One view is that the aggregate turnover is gross valuation of the vehicles. As per this argument, the value is governed by Section 2(6), which aggregates the “value of taxable supplies.” Under Section 15(5), the Government is empowered to notify special valuation rules. The other view and counter argument is that as per Rule 32(5), the margin money legally substitutes the transaction value with the “value of supply.” Therefore, the margin is the statutory value to be aggregated. The first view of aggregate price of car points out that Rule 32(5) is a procedural valuation rule solely meant to prevent double taxation when discharging tax liability by a registered person. For an unregistered dealer, the supply of a car is a transfer of title in goods whose commercial value under Section 15(1) is the gross consideration received. If a car is sold at a loss, Rule 32(5) states the value is “ignored” (treated as Nil). Selling 20 cars at a loss would result in ₹0 aggregate turnover despite crores in bank receipts. Even if a vehicle is sold at a loss, a supply of goods for consideration has occurred. Furthermore, Notification No. 10/2017-CT(R) only exempts inward supplies from unregistered sellers to registered dealers, not outward sales. However, the version of “margin money” for purposes of calculation of the gross value for registration turnover is not accepted by the Departmental authorities during assessment and audit. This often leads to show-cause notices under Section 73/74 for non-registration. The Revenue Department and Authorities for Advance Ruling (e.g., Maharashtra AAR) consistently hold that Rule 32(5) only determines the quantum on which tax is discharged, whereas Aggregate Turnover under Section 2(6) must be computed on the gross selling price. Strategic Advisory and Navigation Roadmap for Dealers Because selling even 6 to 8 mid-sized used cars in a year easily crosses ₹40 lakh in gross receipts—while yielding only ₹3–₹5 lakh in net profit—adopting the wrong position exposes a dealer to retrospective tax demands, interest, and penalties. Therefore, the dealers are supposed to be very careful to determine their liability well in time and they should structure their compliance using the following four-step framework: Distinguish Between Principal-to-Principal and Agency Models 1. Buy-and-Sell Model (Principal) If the dealer buys the vehicle, takes ownership (or holds possession with authority to transfer title), and sells it to a buyer, the transaction is a supply of goods. Due to strict departmental scrutiny and bank turnover mismatches (Income Tax Form 26AS / AIS reflects gross sale receipts, triggering automated GST notices), dealers operating on a principal basis are strongly advised to calculate Aggregate Turnover on Gross Selling Price and obtain registration once gross sales cross ₹40 lakh (or ₹20 lakh if the dealer also earns service income from repairs, financing commissions, or insurance). In many cases, the dealers purchase the second hand vehicle and get possession thereof by getting the blank papers signed for transfer from the Transport authorities. However, the payment is made by the dealer to the seller and later on when the vehicle is sold to a third person, the payment is received by the dealer, even though the vehicle is never transferred in the name of the dealer in the transport authority’s records. However, this is a trap and a dealer can be misled that he has never been owner of the said vehicle on record. But the transaction proves otherwise; the transfer of title of the second hand car is governed by the provisions of Sale of Goods Act, 1930 and the title is deemed to have been passed as provided in the said Act. In the case of second hand cars, the delivery of the possession along with the signed documents (even without specifying the identity of the buyer) amounts to transfer of title of the second hand car to the dealer, which is ultimately transferred to the further buyer on delivery of the car with the said papers signed by the original seller. Hence, in such case, the aggregate value of the second hand car has to be taken into consideration for aggregate turnover calculation. The non-transfer of the ownership in the RTA records does not amount to non-transfer of the title from the original seller to the dealer as per the provisions of Sale of Goods Act. Section 19 of the Sale of Goods Act clearly lays down that the property (simply put the “title”) in the goods (here the reference is to second hand car) is deemed to have been passed as intended by the parties. The delivery of the possession of second hand car, payment to the seller and delivery of signed documents by the seller to the dealer is proof of intention of the parties to pass on the property (title) of the second hand car to the dealer. 2. Brokerage / Commission Model (Agent) If the dealer never takes title to the car (means that there is no outright purchase from the owner) and facilitates a direct sale between the individual seller and individual buyer, and raises an invoice solely for a facilitation fee/commission, the supply is a service. Here, the turnover is strictly the commission earned, and registration is triggered only when total commission/service receipts exceed ₹20 lakh (₹10 lakh in special category states). For the distinction between trading on one’s own account and facilitating another person’s sale, see GST Impact on Supply of Used Vehicles. 3. Mixed Model Where the dealer is transacting the trading business in his own right as a principal and also working on the commission basis, the aggregate value of the second hand cars sold as a principal and the amount of commission have to be added up to reach the aggregate turnover. Leverage Voluntary GST Registration as a Commercial Advantage Unlike regular traders dealing in the other taxable goods, a second-hand car dealer does not necessarily suffer a tax disadvantage by registering early. Tax Liability Remains Low due to the reasons that once registered, the registered dealer may opt for Rule 32(5) (margin money scheme) read with Notification No. 08/2018-CT(Rate) (and subsequent rate rationalisations applying a uniform 18% GST solely on the positive profit margin). The customer does not pay 18% on the whole car, preserving price competitiveness against unregistered peers. Unlocking Blocked Working Capital Through Input Tax Credit As clarified by CBIC (Circular No. 265/11/2026-GST), while a margin-scheme dealer cannot claim ITC on the purchase of the used car itself, ITC is allowed on eligible business overheads—including showroom rent, advertising, refurbishment spare parts, workshop repairs, and software, subject to the applicable provisions of the CGST Act. Registration allows the dealer to offset the GST payable on their car margins against the GST paid on showroom rent and refurbishment costs, to the extent legally permissible. Avoid the Composition Scheme Trap Dealers with gross turnover up to ₹1.5 crore might be tempted to opt for the Composition Levy (1% tax on turnover). This can be financially detrimental for used car dealers. Under the Composition Scheme, the 1% tax applies to the gross selling price of the car without margin scheme benefits. Paying 1% on a ₹10 lakh car (₹10,000) often exceeds paying 18% on a ₹40,000 profit margin (₹7,200), while also forfeiting ITC on showroom rent and repairs. Strict Documentation and Invoicing Hygiene Upon Registration To defend margin-based tax computation during audits, registered dealers must: 1. Maintain a Vehicle-Wise Margin Register linking purchase agreements/KYC of the seller, refurbishment bills, and final sale invoices. 2. Issue an appropriate invoice showing the consolidated sale price and complying with the applicable GST invoicing requirements, ensuring buyers cannot erroneously claim ITC. 3. Ensure Income Tax Act Section 32 WDV rules are applied when buying depreciated business vehicles from registered corporate sellers, wherever the relevant depreciation-based valuation provision applies. Summary of Recommendations While a theoretical legal argument exists to compute registration thresholds on the net margin under Rule 32(5), it is commercially unwise and legally hazardous to rely on it without examining the applicable law and facts. Because GST liability under the Margin Scheme is restricted to the eligible positive margin—and registration unlocks valuable ITC on rent, marketing, and refurbishment—second-hand car dealers should conservatively assess the ₹40 lakh / ₹20 lakh threshold against gross vehicle sales, obtain registration where required, and discharge GST on the positive margin where the prescribed conditions are satisfied, after adjustment of eligible input tax credit as permissible under the law.

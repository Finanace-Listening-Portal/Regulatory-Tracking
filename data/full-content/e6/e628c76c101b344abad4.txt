(Delhi High Court) Summary: The Delhi High Court upheld Section 132 of the Companies Act, 2013 and the National Financial Reporting Authority Rules, 2018. It nevertheless quashed the show-cause notices and final orders identified in the common judgment because the NFRA failed to separate audit review from the decision to initiate disciplinary proceedings. Snehal N. Muzoomdar’s petition, W.P.(C) 5842/2023, was heard with Deloitte Haskins & Sells LLP’s lead petition, W.P.(C) 1065/2021, and the connected matters. Justices Yashwant Varma and Dharmesh Sharma reserved judgment on 22 July 2024 and pronounced it on 7 February 2025. Read SC Judgment in this case: Supreme Court Confines NFRA Interim Protection to Parties in 11 Writ Petitions Muzoomdar’s case arose from the review of the FY 2017–18 statutory audit of Dewan Housing Finance Corporation Limited (DHFL) by Chaturvedi & Shah LLP. NFRA told the Court that 33 engagement partners or branch auditors had signed reports for nearly 250 branches without valid appointments. The statutory auditor had referred to those reports. NFRA disclosed that it had prepared no separate Audit Quality Review Report (AQRR) for Muzoomdar. Its Executive Body obtained and examined his audit file, approved the show-cause notice issued on 21 November 2022, heard him and signed the final order dated 29 September 2023. The Court expressly quashed both instruments. Individual chartered accountants and audit firms questioned Section 132(4) and Rules 3, 8, 10 and 11. They sought to invalidate the provisions or exclude audits completed before 1 October 2018. They also challenged the notices and, where already made, orders imposing penalties or debarment. The Court traced the disciplinary framework under the Chartered Accountants Act, 1949, including the procedure under the 1988 Regulations and the 2007 Misconduct Rules. It placed Section 132 in the context of legislative efforts to strengthen independent oversight of statutory auditing and protect confidence in financial reporting. Rule 3 defines the classes of companies and bodies corporate within NFRA’s remit. The Court examined the distinct functions of monitoring and enforcing auditing standards, overseeing audit quality, investigating misconduct and taking disciplinary action. Section 132(4) empowers NFRA to investigate on a Central Government reference or on its own motion. The petitioners argued that treating an audit firm and its partners as jointly accountable imposed unconstitutional vicarious liability. The Court rejected that challenge, explaining that the firm accepts the engagement and performs it through its members. The shared obligations of supervision, diligence and quality control justified statutory accountability of the firm. The Court began its temporal analysis with the presumption that statutes operate prospectively. It examined whether applying the NFRA framework to earlier audits affected a vested right. An expectation that the previous disciplinary framework would continue did not confer immunity from scrutiny for professional misconduct. Section 132(4) adopts the meaning of professional or other misconduct assigned by Section 22 of the Chartered Accountants Act. The Court held that earlier audits could be examined against the duties and standards applicable when they were performed. The provision did not create a new category of misconduct for those audits. The Court rejected the Article 20(1) challenge because the constitutional protection against ex post facto punishment concerns criminal offences and punishments. The NFRA proceedings examined in the case were disciplinary. The appropriate standard of proof was preponderance of probabilities. The petitioners argued that Rule 11’s summary procedure, the absence of routine cross-examination and the reliance on the audit file undermined procedural fairness. The Court distinguished NFRA’s supervisory and record-based process from a complaint-driven proceeding that may turn on oral testimony. Rule 11 requires adherence to natural justice and provides for a personal hearing where appropriate. The notice must identify the allegations, evidence, provisions or standards said to be breached, and proposed action. The authority must supply the relied-upon documents and relevant extracts from investigation reports or other records. The Court treated the audit file as the contemporaneous record supporting the auditor’s conclusions. The requirement for a comprehensive audit file constrains NFRA as well as the auditor. The auditor may still refer to relevant standards or material supporting the conclusions recorded in the audit report. The Court read Section 132(3)(a) and (b) with the Rules as requiring separate functions within NFRA. Its Executive Body could not be treated as a statutory division simply because a division may be headed by the Chairperson or a full-time member. The disciplinary decision required independent consideration. NFRA’s disclosures showed how the Executive Body examined audit material, approved notices and, in several cases, heard respondents and took final decisions. In the matters involving AQRRs, the same members who recorded findings of audit failures relied on their own findings to decide whether disciplinary action should begin. The Court found that the wording of the reports and notices often conveyed conclusions on guilt with the appearance of finality. A show-cause notice must give the person charged a genuine opportunity to answer an allegation. An authority that has already expressed a concluded view risks making that opportunity ineffective. Drawing on Institute of Chartered Accountants of India v. L.K. Ratna and Ratan Lal Sharma v. Managing Committee, the Court assessed the reasonable likelihood of bias from the perspective of an informed observer. It doubted that an appeal or later hearing would necessarily cure a defect arising from the same members assuming overlapping roles. The Court rejected the challenge to Rule 3’s classification because the covered entities involve substantial public interest and financial exposure. It also recorded NFRA’s decision to permit charged auditors and audit firms to obtain legal assistance. The Court rejected the doctrine of necessity because NFRA could constitute separate units. The Court upheld the statutory framework and rejected the challenges based on vicarious liability, retroactive operation and procedural invalidity. It granted case-specific relief by quashing the notices listed for 11 writ petitions in paragraph 341, together with the final orders listed against four of those petitions. The Court left NFRA free to commence fresh proceedings from the notice stage. Where an AQRR exists, its findings must be treated as prima facie views. Members uninvolved in the audit review and preparation of the AQRR must independently decide whether disciplinary action should begin. Subsequent Supreme Court Proceedings In SLP (C) No. 4139/2025, the Supreme Court’s interim order dated 17 February 2025 permitted proceedings to continue where neither an AQRR nor a final order existed, while barring final orders in those cases and directing that orders already passed be kept in abeyance. On 16 November 2026, the Supreme Court confirmed that the interim directions apply only to the parties to the 11 writ petitions covered by the impugned judgment.

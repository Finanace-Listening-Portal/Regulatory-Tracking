Advt The surge in FCNR(B) deposits could put near term pressure on banks' net interest margins in the September quarter while simultaneously lowering their dependence on relatively expensive domestic wholesale funding. The initial margin drag is likely to ease as banks deploy the additional liquidity into higher yielding loans or use it to replace costlier funding.FCNR(B) mobilisation reached $132.98 billion, while total inflows including overseas foreign currency borrowings and external commercial borrowings stood at $143.596 billion. The FCNR(B) mobilisation window closed on August 31, after banks attracted a substantial volume of foreign currency deposits under the Reserve Bank of India's special swap facility.The immediate margin pressure stems from the deployment of the funds. Banks initially parked a significant portion of the additional liquidity in government securities and money market instruments, which generate lower returns than loans. This creates a temporary mismatch between the cost of the deposits and the yield earned on the funds. S&P Global Ratings expects the margin pressure to be most pronounced during the initial months before easing as banks redeploy the funds.The impact will vary across banks depending on the amount of FCNR(B) deposits mobilised, their existing funding mix and the pace at which the funds are deployed into loans. Banks with larger inflows relative to their deposit base have greater exposure to the initial margin drag. Advt The additional liquidity has already reduced banks' dependence on short term wholesale funding. Three month certificate of deposit rates fell to 5.86% on September 3 from 7.16% on June 8, while six month rates declined to 6.46% from 7.45%. The lower wholesale funding rates provide a counterweight to the initial pressure on margins.The effective cost of FCNR(B) deposits also needs to include the cost of hedging the interest payable on the deposits. The RBI's concessional swap arrangement covers the principal component, while banks have to separately hedge the interest liability. The additional cost has been estimated at 15 to 20 basis points.Funding cost effect Advt The margin effect will therefore depend on how banks use the funds. If liquidity remains in low yielding securities and money market instruments, the drag on NIMs can persist. If banks use it to replace expensive wholesale funding or increase loan deployment, the benefit from lower funding costs can increasingly offset the initial pressure.Market estimates have put potential near term NIM compression from the FCNR(B) inflows at different levels. A Kotak Institutional Equities estimate cited in September placed the potential impact at 10 to 30 basis points, but the actual effect will differ across banks based on their funding and deployment mix. Advt The Reserve Bank's liquidity operations are another factor. The banking system's liquidity surplus had risen to Rs 11.16 lakh crore in September before RBI used foreign exchange operations, bond sales and variable rate reverse repo operations to absorb surplus liquidity. By September 29, these measures had removed nearly $20 billion equivalent of rupee liquidity.The RBI absorption means that the amount of FCNR(B)-related liquidity available for deployment is lower than the gross inflow. It could also limit the extent to which surplus liquidity weighs on banks' margins.For banks, the FCNR(B) inflow is therefore a double-edged development for Q2 margins. It can initially dilute NIMs because of the time required to deploy the funds, while the resulting liquidity surplus can lower wholesale funding costs and provide scope to replace more expensive liabilities. The balance will depend on individual banks' mobilisation, hedging costs and deployment of the funds.

Summary: The GST treatment of discounts has undergone a significant change from 1 November 2026 following amendments to Sections 15(3)(b) and 34 of the CGST Act, 2017, removing the earlier requirements of a pre-existing agreement and specific invoice-wise linkage for post-supply discounts. Under the revised framework, a post-supply discount reduces taxable value where the supplier issues a credit note under Section 34 and the recipient reverses the attributable input tax credit (ITC). This article examines the GST implications of trade discounts, cash discounts, year-end turnover incentives, price protection, financial credit notes, manufacturer-funded secondary discounts, dealer promotional services, free samples and buy-one-get-one offers. It analyses CBIC Circulars 251/08/2025-GST, 253/10/2025-GST and 92/11/2019-GST, including the distinction between genuine price reductions and consideration for separately taxable services. Particular attention is given to the recipient’s ITC entitlement, credit-note adjustments through the Invoice Management System (IMS), the Section 34(2) reporting deadline and the treatment of financial credit notes without GST adjustment. The article also discusses relevant judicial decisions, including Shivam Steels, Hindustan Unilever, Southern Motors and Madras Rubber Factory, alongside comparative tables and practical calculations. It highlights transitional uncertainty for supplies made before November 2026 and emphasises the importance of proper documentation, timely credit-note reporting, recipient ITC reconciliation and compliance with the amended statutory conditions. Conclusion Show all 24 sections Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); Abstract and introduction Under GST, a discount reduces the taxable value only if it meets the conditions in Section 15(3) of the CGST Act, 2017. If it does not, GST is payable on the full price even though the buyer paid less. This paper explains the statutory test, the CBIC circulars and the recent amendments. It also covers the input tax credit position of the recipient, key rulings, worked illustrations and a compliance checklist. Discounts matter because they appear in almost every B2B supply chain: trade discounts on the invoice, cash discounts for early payment, year-end turnover and target incentives, and dealer schemes funded by manufacturers. The tax result turns on two questions: When was the discount agreed? Is it linked to specific invoices, with the recipient’s input tax credit reversed? Scope: Indian GST law (CGST Act, 2017 and the matching State GST Acts), as amended up to November 2026. Statutory framework Section 15(1) of the CGST Act makes the transaction value the starting point for valuation. Section 15(3) then decides which discounts come off that value, Section 34 provides the credit note mechanism, and Section 16 governs the recipient’s input tax credit. Section 15(3)(a): discounts given before or at the time of supply. The discount is excluded if it is “duly recorded in the invoice issued in respect of such supply”. Discounts of this kind include trade discounts and quantity discounts shown on the face of the invoice. Section 15(3)(b): discounts given after the supply. The test changed with effect from 1 November 2026. Element Up to 31-10-26 From 01-11-26 Agreement Discount “established in terms of an agreement entered into at or before the time of such supply” Requirement removed Invoice linkage Discount “specifically linked to relevant invoices” Requirement removed Document No express credit note requirement (a credit note was used in practice) “a credit note has been issued by the supplier” under Section 34 Recipient ITC ITC attributable to the discount “reversed by the recipient” Same condition, “in accordance with the provisions of section 34” Section 34: credit notes. Section 34(1) lets a supplier issue credit notes where the invoice overstated value or tax, goods are returned, or supplies are deficient. From 1 November 2026, Section 154 of the Finance Act, 2026 also allows credit notes for post-supply discounts under Section 15(3)(b). Under Section 34(2), the credit note must be declared in the return no later than 30 November after the end of the financial year, or the date of filing the annual return if earlier. Section 34(2) proviso, in force from 01-10-25. This proviso was inserted by Section 126 of the Finance Act, 2025 and notified by Notification 16/2025-Central Tax. It bars the supplier from reducing its output tax unless the recipient has reversed the ITC attributable to the credit note. Where the recipient is unregistered, the bar applies if the incidence of tax has been passed on. Section 17(5)(h). ITC is blocked on goods lost, stolen, destroyed, written off, or disposed of by way of gift or free samples. This governs free supplies made under promotional schemes. Types of discount and their GST treatment The label on a discount does not decide its treatment. What matters is when it is given, how it is documented, and whether it is really a price reduction or payment for something else. The tax charged on the invoice remains fully paid. Post-sale discount is not consideration for a service.A discount a manufacturer gives a dealer is not payment for a separate service by the dealer to the manufacturer. Dealers may use the discount for their own promotion. Secondary discounts passed to end customers.Where the manufacturer has no arrangement with the end customer, the discount is only a price reduction. It is not extra consideration for the dealer’s onward supply. Manufacturer-agreed end-customer price.Where the manufacturer agrees a reduced price with the end customer and compensates the dealer by credit note, that amount is consideration for the dealer’s supply. It forms part of the value of the dealer’s supply to the customer. Contracted promotional services.Where the dealer contractually undertakes specific activities (advertising, co-branding, displays, campaigns) for a stated consideration, the dealer must charge GST on that service. Circular 92/11/2019-GST: points still relevant. Free samples and gifts are not a supply unless covered by Schedule I, and ITC on them is blocked. A buy-one-get-one offer is a single supply at the price charged, and ITC on it is available. Discounts known and recorded at the time of supply reduce the value. Recent changes (2024 to November 2026) From 1 November 2026, a post-sale discount no longer needs a prior agreement or invoice-wise linkage. It needs a Section 34 credit note and the recipient’s ITC reversal. The amended test applies from 1 November 2026. For credit notes issued on or after that date for supplies made earlier, the amended text appears to govern, but the notification gives no express transitional rule. Where the old conditions were also met, document both positions on file. The Section 34(2) time limit still applies: credit notes for FY 2025-26 supplies must be declared by 30 November 2026, or by the date the annual return is filed if earlier. What did not change. The word “discount” is still used, so an amount that is really payment for a service (for example, a contracted promotion fee) still falls outside Sec 15(3)(b). The recipient’s ITC reversal is still a condition, now tracked through the Invoice Management System (IMS). Input tax credit implications for the recipient The recipient reverses ITC only when the supplier reduces its tax through a GST credit note. A financial credit note (no GST adjustment) leaves the recipient’s ITC intact. A credit note uploaded by the supplier appears in the recipient’s IMS. The recipient can accept it, reject it, or keep it pending for one tax period. After that it is deemed accepted. On acceptance, the recipient can declare the exact ITC to reverse. This matters where the original ITC was taken only in part, or not at all. If the recipient rejects the credit note, the supplier’s reduced liability is added back in the next period. Section 16 points. Circular 251/08/2025 states that no reversal is needed when the supplier’s tax stays unchanged. Officers have sometimes argued that paying less than the invoice value triggers the 180-day reversal under the second proviso to Sec 16(2). Keep the financial credit note and the ledger set-off on file to show the invoice was fully settled. The Madras High Court set aside an ITC reversal demand on financial credit notes in Shivam Steels (2024). Judicial decisions and advance rulings The courts have treated post-sale discounts as price reductions, not payment for services. The pre-GST rulings on whether a discount was “known at the time of removal” explain why the old Sec 15(3)(b) conditions existed. The manufacturer pays the dealer 50,000 under a written agreement for in-store branding. This is a separate service. The dealer issues a tax invoice for 50,000 plus GST of 9,000, and the manufacturer may claim ITC of 9,000. Conclusion The 1 November 2026 amendment ends most of the litigation over “prior agreement” and “linked to invoices”. A post-sale discount now reduces value when two things happen: the supplier issues a Section 34 credit note, and the recipient reverses the matching ITC through IMS. The open questions are now about substance (a discount or payment for a service) and timing (the Sec 34(2) deadline and supplies made before November 2026). Circular 251/08/2025 settles most of the secondary-discount issues. Open point: the official Gazette text of Notification 03/2026-CT and the Finance Act, 2026 should be checked for any transitional provision before applying the new test to pre-November 2026 supplies.

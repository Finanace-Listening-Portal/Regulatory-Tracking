Summary: The GST Appellate Tribunal, Agra Bench, in Ashok Kumar Singh v. M/s Safecon Lifesciences Private Limited,, dismissed the Revenue’s appeal and upheld input tax credit (ITC) relief granted to a purchaser accused of obtaining invoices from an allegedly bogus supplier. The dispute concerned purchases worth ₹21,30,000 made during November 2019, involving ITC of ₹3,83,400. Based on a supplier-side investigation, the Department initiated proceedings under Section 74, resulting in a tax demand, equivalent penalty and interest of ₹53,676. The Tribunal examined the documentary evidence produced by the taxpayer, including purchase orders, tax invoices, e-way bills, banking records, stock registers, consignment documents, vehicle particulars and supplier-related GST records. It accepted that the evidence supported actual purchase, transportation and receipt of goods, satisfying the relevant factual requirements for ITC eligibility. The Tribunal also considered the subsequent restoration of the supplier’s GST registration and distinguished the judicial precedents relied upon by the Revenue on the facts of the case. The Department’s objection concerning admission of additional evidence was rejected because the adjudicating authority had been given an opportunity to examine the documents and submit its report. The ruling highlights that allegations against a supplier must be evaluated alongside transaction-specific evidence produced by the purchaser. However, it does not establish that invoices, banking payments or restoration of registration alone conclusively prove ITC eligibility. The decision underscores the importance of maintaining comprehensive documentary evidence to establish genuine purchases and actual receipt of goods. The buyer may have received the goods, recorded them in the stock register and paid the consideration through banking channels, yet face proceedings for denial of input tax credit on the strength of an investigation conducted at the supplier’s end. The decision of the GST Appellate Tribunal, Agra Bench, in Ashok Kumar Singh v. M/s Safecon Lifesciences Private Limited, Appeal No. APL/24/AGR/2026, dated 16 October 2026, addresses this situation. The Tribunal dismissed the departmental appeal and upheld the taxpayer’s entitlement to input tax credit after considering the evidence of purchase, transportation and receipt of goods, the supplier’s tax records and the subsequent restoration of its registration. Its reasoning recognises an important distinction: an allegation against a supplier cannot be examined in isolation from the evidence relating to the purchaser’s actual transactions. Background of the Dispute Safecon Lifesciences Private Limited had purchased goods worth ₹21,30,000 from M/s Neon Pharma Chem, a supplier registered in Maharashtra, against two tax invoices during November 2019. It availed input tax credit of ₹3,83,400 on those purchases. The proceedings originated from departmental communications referring to an investigation against the supplier. According to the information received, the supplier was allegedly fictitious and engaged in issuing invoices without undertaking actual purchases or sales. The Revenue relied substantially on the investigation report and a statement reportedly made by a person present during the investigation. A notice under Section 74 was issued to the purchaser. As no reply was submitted, the adjudicating authority raised a tax demand of ₹3,83,400, imposed an equivalent penalty and levied interest of ₹53,676. The first appellate authority granted relief to the taxpayer. The Revenue then challenged that relief before the Tribunal. Issues Before the Tribunal The dispute raised three connected questions: Whether an allegation that the supplier was bogus justified denial of the purchaser’s input tax credit despite the documents produced to establish genuine purchases. Whether the evidence sufficiently established actual transportation and receipt of goods for the purposes of Section 16(2)(b). Whether the first appellate authority had accepted additional evidence without giving the adjudicating authority an opportunity to examine it. Revenue’s Objection: Invoices and Bank Payments Were Not Enough The Revenue contended that the taxpayer had claimed credit merely on the strength of invoices issued by a bogus supplier, without establishing actual receipt of goods. Relying on Malik Traders v. State of Uttar Pradesh and State of Karnataka v. M/s Ecom Gill Coffee Trading Private Limited, the departmental representative argued that invoices, consignment documents and payments through banking channels did not, by themselves, conclusively prove a genuine transaction. The Revenue sought vehicle particulars, transportation records and evidence concerning freight payments. It also objected to the admission of additional evidence at the first appellate stage. The central controversy was therefore not simply whether invoices existed. It was whether those invoices represented goods that had actually moved and been received. Why the Taxpayer Succeeded Evidence extended beyond invoices The taxpayer’s case rested on a collection of contemporaneous records rather than a bare assertion that payment had been made. The documents referred to in the order included: Purchase orders and tax invoices; E-way bills; Banking and payment records; Entries in the books of account and stock register; Consignment documents and vehicle particulars; and The supplier’s GST returns and records relating to tax payment. The Tribunal accepted that the material supported the actual purchase, transportation and receipt of goods. It therefore rejected the premise that the taxpayer had merely procured invoices without receiving the underlying supplies. This factual finding was central to the decision. Supplier-related allegations required transaction-specific examination The Tribunal observed that the allegation against the supplier could not be considered in isolation from the recipient’s evidence. Eligibility for credit had to be examined with reference to the applicable statutory conditions and the facts of the particular transactions. This is the principal value of the ruling for bona fide buyers. The Tribunal did not treat an adverse report against the supplier as a substitute for examining the purchaser’s records. It considered whether the evidence in the purchaser’s case supported the allegation of an invoice-only transaction. On the material before it, the allegation did not justify denial of credit. Restoration of the supplier’s registration supported the defence The supplier’s registration, which had allegedly been cancelled following the investigation, was subsequently restored by its jurisdictional authority. The restoration order had been placed before the first appellate authority, and the Revenue did not dispute that restoration before the Tribunal. Paragraph 26 contains a broadly worded observation that restoration nullified the allegation that the supplier was bogus or non-existent. That observation should be read with the surrounding findings. The decision also rests on the documentary evidence of actual purchases and movement of goods. It should not be presented as a ruling that restoration of registration, by itself, proves every transaction entered into by a supplier. The department had an opportunity to examine the evidence The Revenue’s procedural objection also failed. The first appellate authority had forwarded the taxpayer’s documents to the adjudicating authority and called for a report. The adjudicating authority submitted its report on 12 October 2021, and that report was considered in the appellate proceedings. The Tribunal consequently found no sufficient basis to interfere on the ground that the department had been denied an opportunity to examine the additional evidence. Significance for Bona Fide Buyers The ruling supports a carefully defined proposition: where the buyer produces evidence establishing genuine purchases, transportation and receipt of goods, an allegation concerning the supplier must be assessed against that evidence rather than treated as conclusive. Equally, the decision does not support the proposition that an invoice and a bank payment are invariably sufficient. Safecon succeeded because its evidence went further. The Tribunal referred to transport records, vehicle particulars, stock entries and supplier-related tax records, in addition to invoices and payment details. It distinguished the authorities relied upon by the Revenue on the facts before it; it did not dispense with the requirement to establish actual receipt of goods. For taxpayers, the importance of the case lies in this distinction between claiming to be a bona fide purchaser and demonstrating the genuineness of the purchase through records. The protection recognised in the order followed the evidence. Conclusion The Safecon Lifesciences decision places the purchaser’s actual transactions at the centre of the enquiry. A supplier-side investigation may give rise to suspicion, but the Tribunal held that the purchaser’s evidence could not be disregarded merely because such suspicion existed. Having accepted the records supporting genuine purchases and receipt of goods, and having noted the restoration of the supplier’s registration and the department’s opportunity to examine the evidence, the Tribunal dismissed the Revenue’s appeal and upheld the taxpayer’s relief. For bona fide buyers, the ruling is significant not as an unconditional shield against ITC disputes, but as recognition that a properly supported transaction must be judged on its own evidence. The decisive feature was not the purchaser’s assertion of good faith; it was the documentary foundation supporting that assertion.

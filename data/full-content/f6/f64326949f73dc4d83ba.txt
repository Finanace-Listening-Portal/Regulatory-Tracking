Summary: A contract change does not automatically create a new contract under Ind AS 115. First, the parties must approve a change that creates or modifies enforceable rights and obligations. The entity then tests whether added goods or services are distinct and priced at their standalone selling prices. If both conditions are met, the addition is a separate contract. If not, the accounting depends on whether the remaining goods or services are distinct from those already transferred: distinct remaining items are treated prospectively as termination of the old contract and creation of a new one, while a modification to a partially satisfied single performance obligation is recognised through a cumulative catch-up adjustment. This guide converts that sequence into a reviewable decision process with documentation, journal-entry logic and disclosure controls. Key Takeaways Show all 19 sections Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); Identify an approved modification before choosing the accounting model Paragraph 18 of Ind AS 115 defines a contract modification as an approved change in scope, price or both that creates new enforceable rights and obligations or changes existing ones. Approval may be written, oral or implied by customary business practice. A draft change order, an internal forecast or a supplier’s cost overrun is not enough unless the parties have created enforceable changes under the contract and applicable law. The amount of additional consideration need not always be finally agreed when scope is approved. Paragraph 19 requires the entity to consider all relevant facts where price is disputed or not yet determined. If scope has been approved but the price remains open, the entity estimates the related transaction-price change using the variable-consideration requirements and constraint. That judgment should be separated from the later classification of the modification. Gateway question Evidence Consequence Has scope or price changed? Approval chronology Determines the date of prospective or catch-up accounting A separate contract requires both distinct additions and appropriate pricing Condition one: added goods or services are distinct The scope must increase through additional promised goods or services that are distinct under paragraphs 26–30. A promised item is capable of being distinct when the customer can benefit from it on its own or with other readily available resources, and it is distinct within the contract when the promise is separately identifiable rather than an input to a combined output. A new unit of an already delivered standard product often passes; added work that significantly integrates into a single customised asset may not. Condition two: price reflects standalone selling prices The contract price must increase by consideration reflecting the standalone selling prices of the added distinct goods or services, including an appropriate adjustment for the circumstances. An adjustment can be justified, for example, because selling costs for an existing customer differ from those for a new customer. The file should show the observable or estimated standalone price and explain the adjustment. A negotiated amount is not automatically a standalone selling price merely because both parties accepted it. Only when both conditions are present is the modification accounted for as a separate contract. Revenue recognised for the original contract is then unaffected by the new contract. This is an accounting construct rather than a legal cancellation. Revenue recognised before the modification is not reversed merely because the price for the remaining goods changes. The amount allocated to remaining performance obligations is the consideration from the original contract that was included in the transaction price but not yet recognised, plus the consideration promised as part of the modification. The entity allocates that combined amount to the remaining distinct goods or services using the applicable allocation principles. The method is prospective because the revised economics affect future transfers. Worked logic for repeat product deliveries Assume an entity agrees to deliver 100 identical distinct units for ₹100 each. After 60 units are transferred, the parties add 30 units for ₹80 each, and ₹80 does not reflect the appropriate standalone selling price. Because the price condition fails, the addition is not a separate contract. If the remaining 40 original units and 30 added units are distinct from units already transferred, the remaining original consideration of ₹4,000 and modification consideration of ₹2,400 are combined and allocated prospectively across the 70 remaining units. This simplified example demonstrates the mechanism; actual allocations may differ where standalone selling prices vary or discounts relate to specified obligations. Non-distinct remaining work requires a cumulative catch-up adjustment Paragraph 21(b) applies when the remaining goods or services are not distinct and form part of a single performance obligation that is partially satisfied at the modification date. The modification becomes part of the existing contract. The entity updates the transaction price and its measure of progress, then recognises the resulting adjustment to revenue immediately on a cumulative catch-up basis. The adjustment can increase or reduce current-period revenue. This outcome is common in contracts for a single combined asset or integrated service where additional work is not separately identifiable from the output already under construction. The preparer should recompute total expected consideration, enforceable variable amounts, costs or inputs used for progress, and any constraint. The journal entry must reconcile revenue recognised to date before and after the revised measure; simply spreading the additional price over the remaining calendar period may be wrong. Mixed modifications need a consistent split Paragraph 21(c) addresses a combination: some remaining goods are distinct while others form part of a partially satisfied performance obligation. The entity accounts for the effects on unsatisfied and partially unsatisfied obligations consistently with paragraph 21’s objectives. A defensible approach identifies the components, applies prospective treatment to distinct remaining items and cumulative catch-up logic to the non-distinct portion, supported by a rational allocation of modification consideration. Separate a modification from a later transaction-price change After contract inception, transaction price can change because uncertainty resolves without the parties changing enforceable scope or price. Paragraphs 87–90 govern those changes. A price change arising from the modification follows paragraphs 18–21; later changes are allocated based on whether they relate to pre-modification variable consideration and how the modification was accounted for. The chronology matters because labelling every estimate update a modification can produce an incorrect reset of allocation. The contract memo should therefore distinguish the approval date, the modification consideration, variable amounts existing before modification, and subsequent estimate changes. It should also record any reversal of constrained consideration, change in standalone selling prices used only for new allocations, and the contract-asset or contract-liability effect. Standalone selling prices are not generally reallocated retrospectively merely because current market prices changed. Close controls should make the paragraph 18–21 decision reproducible A complete file includes the original contract, all amendments and correspondence, enforceability assessment, promised-goods inventory, distinctness memo, standalone-price evidence, transaction-price computation, allocation schedule, progress calculation, journal entries and disclosure assessment. The approver should be able to follow one dated decision tree from contractual change to accounting outcome without relying on the project manager’s shorthand description. Frequently Asked Questions on Ind AS 115 modifications Is every signed change order a separate contract? No. It is a separate contract only when it adds distinct goods or services and the added price reflects their standalone selling prices with appropriate adjustments. Can a modification exist before the final price is agreed? Yes. If enforceable scope is approved, the entity may need to estimate the price change as variable consideration and apply the constraint. When is accounting prospective? When the modification is not a separate contract and the remaining goods or services are distinct from those already transferred, paragraph 21(a) applies prospectively. When is a cumulative catch-up recorded? When remaining work is not distinct and forms part of a partially satisfied single performance obligation, the modification updates the existing contract and revenue to date. Does prospective accounting reverse revenue already recognised? Normally no. The remaining unrecognised original consideration and modification consideration are allocated to remaining distinct goods or services. How is a modification with both distinct and non-distinct elements handled? Apply paragraph 21 consistently to each component, using prospective logic for distinct remaining items and catch-up logic for the non-distinct partially satisfied obligation. Is a change in estimated variable consideration always a contract modification? No. A modification changes enforceable scope or price through party approval; an estimate change may instead be a transaction-price change under paragraphs 87–90. Distinct remaining goods produce prospective termination-and-new-contract accounting. Non-distinct remaining work in a partially satisfied obligation produces cumulative catch-up.

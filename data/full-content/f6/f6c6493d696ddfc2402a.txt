(Supreme Court of India) Summary: The Supreme Court of India, in its judgment dated 09.09.2021, examined the limits of judicial interference with arbitral awards under Sections 34 and 37 of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996, while adjudicating the dispute between Delhi Airport Metro Express Private Limited (DAMEPL) and Delhi Metro Rail Corporation Limited (DMRC). The principal issue was whether the Division Bench of the Delhi High Court had exceeded its statutory jurisdiction by partly setting aside an arbitral award dated 11.05.2017 in favour of DAMEPL. The dispute arose out of a public-private partnership for the development and operation of the approximately 22.7-kilometre Airport Metro Express Line connecting New Delhi Railway Station with Dwarka Sector 21 through Indira Gandhi International Airport. Under the Concession Agreement dated 25.08.2008, DMRC was responsible for basic civil construction, land acquisition and related approvals, while DAMEPL undertook the design, procurement, installation, commissioning, operation and maintenance of railway systems. Commercial operations commenced on 23.02.2011. Subsequently, serious disputes arose regarding defects in the civil structure, particularly cracks in girders, defective bearings, structural deformations and associated safety concerns. DAMEPL contended that DMRC had failed to remedy defects within the contractual cure period and consequently terminated the agreement. The arbitral tribunal upheld the termination and awarded substantial compensation. Although the Delhi High Court’s Single Judge declined to interfere, its Division Bench partly annulled the award. The Supreme Court, applying the principles of limited judicial intervention, patent illegality and permissible contractual interpretation, restored the award in its 2021 decision. That decision was subsequently set aside by the Supreme Court in curative proceedings on 10.04.2024. The factual controversy originated when DAMEPL raised concerns about defects in the viaduct and bearings through communications dated 22.03.2012 and 23.05.2012. DMRC initially reported that no bearings were damaged but acknowledged deterioration of grouting material and advised appropriate speed restrictions. Following a meeting convened by the Ministry of Urban Development on 02.07.2012, a Joint Inspection Committee examined the structural defects. DAMEPL discontinued operations on 08.07.2012 and issued a formal notice on 09.07.2012 requiring DMRC to cure the defects within 90 days. DAMEPL thereafter issued a termination notice dated 08.10.2012, alleging failure to remedy the defects or take effective corrective measures under Article 29.5.1(i) of the Concession Agreement. DMRC invoked arbitration on 23.10.2012, disputing the validity of termination and maintaining that repair measures had been undertaken. Following certification by the Commissioner of Metro Railway Safety (CMRS) on 18.01.2013, operations resumed on 22.01.2013 subject to conditions, including speed restrictions. DAMEPL maintained that its temporary operation of the line was undertaken in the public interest and did not amount to abandonment of its termination rights. It ultimately stopped operations on 30.06.2013 and handed over the line to DMRC. Before the arbitral tribunal, DMRC sought specific performance of the agreement or, alternatively, compensation of approximately Rs. 3,173 crore with interest at 18% per annum and additional monetary relief. DAMEPL filed a counterclaim seeking Rs. 3,470 crore as termination payment, together with interest and other contractual amounts. The dispute principally required interpretation of the contractual termination mechanism, the 90-day cure period, the meaning of material adverse effect and the consequences of DMRC’s alleged failure to fulfil its obligations. The three-member arbitral tribunal, comprising technically qualified engineers, examined extensive evidence relating to the structural condition of the Airport Metro Express Line. It recorded that approximately 1,551 cracks existed in 367 girders, affecting about 72% of the girders, and that several defects had implications for the structural integrity of the project. The tribunal also considered approximately 80 girders showing twists ranging between 10 mm and 20 mm, besides gaps between shear keys and girders that had not been adequately rectified. The tribunal found that DMRC had neither cured the material defects nor undertaken effective remedial measures within the stipulated 90-day period following the notice dated 09.07.2012. It consequently upheld DAMEPL’s termination notice dated 08.10.2012. The tribunal distinguished the statutory safety clearance issued by the CMRS from contractual compliance with the obligation to cure defects within the prescribed period. Although the Commissioner permitted resumption of operations, restrictions limiting train speed to 50 kilometres per hour and requirements for continued monitoring were considered relevant to the unresolved structural issues and the purpose of the high-speed airport connection. On the financial claim, the tribunal examined the definitions of Equity, Adjusted Equity, Debt Due and Subordinated Debt contained in the Concession Agreement. Out of approximately Rs. 685 crore contributed by DAMEPL’s promoter, it treated Rs. 611.95 crore as Equity for contractual purposes, calculated Adjusted Equity at Rs. 983.02 crore and awarded Rs. 2,782.33 crore as termination payment, together with further interest. The tribunal rejected DMRC’s claims dependent upon invalidity of the termination notice and declined to require DAMEPL to resume operations. DMRC challenged the arbitral award under Section 34 of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act. The learned Single Judge of the Delhi High Court, by judgment dated 06.03.2018, dismissed the challenge, holding that the tribunal’s findings concerning structural defects, contractual interpretation and computation of compensation were supported by a plausible interpretation of the evidence and agreement. However, the Division Bench, exercising appellate jurisdiction under Section 37, reversed the Single Judge’s decision on 15.01.2019 and partly set aside the arbitral award. The Division Bench considered the tribunal’s treatment of termination dates inconsistent and questioned the conclusion that DMRC had failed to cure the defects despite the subsequent CMRS clearance and continued operation of the line. It also held that the tribunal had adopted an erroneous contractual interpretation while including Rs. 611.95 crore in Equity, particularly because the amount had been converted into subordinated debt through a board resolution dated 16.03.2011. According to the Division Bench, these findings were perverse, patently illegal and contrary to the contractual provisions. Before the Supreme Court, DAMEPL argued that the Division Bench had undertaken an impermissible reassessment of evidence and substituted its preferred contractual interpretation for a possible view taken by expert arbitrators. DMRC, on the other hand, contended that the CMRS certificate was conclusive of the line’s safety, that effective remedial steps had been taken, that the contractual cure period had been misunderstood and that the tribunal’s treatment of subordinated debt as Equity was contrary to the agreement. The competing arguments raised the distinction between correcting genuine patent illegality and conducting an impermissible appeal on factual and contractual merits. In its 2021 judgment, the Supreme Court extensively examined the legislative objective of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996, particularly Section 5, which restricts judicial intervention, and the statutory grounds for setting aside awards under Section 34. The Court traced the effect of the Arbitration and Conciliation (Amendment) Act, 2015, including the introduction of Section 34(2A), which permits domestic arbitral awards to be set aside for patent illegality appearing on the face of the award. Referring to Associate Builders v. Delhi Development Authority, (2015) 3 SCC 49, and Ssangyong Engineering and Construction Company Limited v. National Highways Authority of India, (2019) 15 SCC 131, the Court explained that patent illegality must go to the root of the matter and cannot be founded merely upon erroneous application of law or reappreciation of evidence. It recognised that awards may be interfered with where the arbitrator adopts an interpretation that no reasonable person could accept, disregards vital evidence, makes findings without evidentiary support, fails to furnish reasons or decides matters beyond contractual authority. However, courts are not entitled to reconsider the quality or quantity of evidence merely because a different conclusion appears preferable. The Court also discussed Renusagar Power Co. Ltd. v. General Electric Co., 1994 Supp (1) SCC 644, concerning the fundamental policy of Indian law, and referred to Uttarakhand Purv Sainik Kalyan Nigam Ltd. v. Northern Coal Field Ltd., (2020) 2 SCC 455, Bhaven Construction v. Executive Engineer, Sardar Sarovar Narmada Nigam Ltd., 2021 SCC OnLine SC 8, and Rashtriya Ispat Nigam Ltd. v. Dewan Chand Ram Saran, (2012) 5 SCC 306, while explaining the statutory limits of judicial supervision. The Court cautioned against routinely describing arbitral findings as perverse or patently illegal after independently reassessing the factual record. This approach, it held, would undermine the object of finality and minimal court intervention in arbitration proceedings. Applying those principles, the Supreme Court held in 2021 that the arbitral tribunal had adopted a possible interpretation of Article 29.5.1(i) concerning the 90-day cure period and the termination mechanism. The tribunal had distinguished between the notice dated 09.07.2012 requiring rectification of defects, the termination notice dated 08.10.2012 and the effective termination date of 07.01.2013. The Court rejected the Division Bench’s conclusion that these dates demonstrated inconsistency or confusion in the award. It further held that the statutory competence of the CMRS to certify railway safety did not conclusively establish that DMRC had complied with the separate contractual requirement to cure the identified defects within the relevant period. The CMRS certificate dated 18.01.2013 was issued after expiry of the contractual cure period and contained operating restrictions and monitoring conditions. The question of public safety for restarting commercial operations was therefore distinguishable from the contractual question whether the defaults identified in the cure notice had been remedied within time. The Court emphasised that the tribunal’s technically qualified members had examined the relevant evidence and that their factual conclusions could not be displaced by a fresh judicial appreciation of the record. The subsequent operation of the line without adverse incidents did not, by itself, invalidate the earlier contractual findings. On Adjusted Equity, the Supreme Court held that the tribunal’s interpretation of the express contractual definitions was reasonable and possible, notwithstanding the treatment of promoter contributions as subordinated debt in DAMEPL’s financial records. The Court noted that contractual definitions, rather than the ordinary treatment of equity share capital under company law, governed computation of the termination payment. Accordingly, it rejected the Division Bench’s interference with the inclusion of Rs. 611.95 crore in Equity and restored the tribunal’s computation. The approach reflected the principle that a plausible interpretation of contractual terms by an arbitrator ordinarily cannot be replaced by the reviewing court. On the remaining issues, the Supreme Court accepted the tribunal’s finding that DAMEPL had not waived its termination rights merely by participating in reconciliation meetings or temporarily operating the Airport Metro Express Line following the termination notice. The Court observed that DAMEPL’s participation was undertaken without prejudice to its contractual position and that the Division Bench had correctly declined to interfere with the tribunal’s conclusion on waiver. The Court also declined to revisit DMRC’s request for specific performance because the relevant findings had not been appropriately challenged before the High Court. Regarding interest, it upheld the arbitral determination made under Articles 29.8 and 36.2.6.1 of the Concession Agreement and rejected DMRC’s objection that the interest award resulted in unjust enrichment. Consequently, by judgment dated 09.09.2021, the Supreme Court allowed DAMEPL’s appeal, set aside the Delhi High Court Division Bench judgment and restored the arbitral award of Rs. 2,782.33 crore with contractual interest. DMRC’s connected appeal was dismissed. The 2021 judgment thus emphasised restraint in judicial review of arbitral findings, the contractual autonomy of parties and the limits of revisiting technical and financial determinations made by arbitrators. However, this outcome did not remain final: by its subsequent judgment dated 10.04.2024 in Curative Petition (C) Nos. 108–109 of 2022, the Supreme Court set aside the 2021 decision, holding that the arbitral award suffered from patent illegality and that its earlier restoration had resulted in a miscarriage of justice. Accordingly, the operative legal position must be understood in light of the 2024 curative judgment rather than treating the 2021 restoration as continuing to govern the dispute. Cases Discussed 1. Renusagar Power Co. Ltd. v. General Electric Co. (Supreme Court); 1994 Supp (1) SCC 644. The Supreme Court referred to this decision while examining the meaning of the fundamental policy of Indian law as a ground for challenging arbitral awards. The judgment was relevant to interpreting the narrowed public-policy standard following amendments to the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996. 2. Rashtriya Ispat Nigam Ltd. v. Dewan Chand Ram Saran (Supreme Court); (2012) 5 SCC 306. The decision was referred to in support of judicial restraint in arbitration matters and the principle that courts should not ordinarily substitute their own interpretation of contractual terms for a reasonable interpretation adopted by an arbitrator. 3. Associate Builders v. Delhi Development Authority (Supreme Court); (2015) 3 SCC 49. The judgment discussed the principles governing perversity, patent illegality, contractual interpretation and public policy in challenges to arbitral awards. The Supreme Court explained that the principles recognised in Associate Builders must be understood in the light of subsequent statutory amendments and the decision in Ssangyong Engineering. 4. Ssangyong Engineering and Construction Company Limited v. National Highways Authority of India (Supreme Court); (2019) 15 SCC 131. The Court relied extensively upon this decision for the restricted interpretation of public policy and patent illegality after the 2015 amendments. It reaffirmed that reappreciation of evidence is impermissible and that contractual interpretation generally remains within the arbitrator’s domain, subject to the recognised grounds for setting aside an award. 5. Uttarakhand Purv Sainik Kalyan Nigam Ltd. v. Northern Coal Field Ltd. (Supreme Court); (2020) 2 SCC 455. The judgment was cited while explaining the statutory framework of arbitral proceedings and the restricted role assigned to courts under the Arbitration and Conciliation Act. 6. Bhaven Construction Through Authorised Signatory Premjibhai K. Shah v. Executive Engineer, Sardar Sarovar Narmada Nigam Ltd. (Supreme Court); 2021 SCC OnLine SC 8. The decision was cited to support limited judicial intervention in arbitration and adherence to the statutory mechanisms prescribed by the Arbitration and Conciliation Act. 7. Delhi Airport Metro Express Pvt. Ltd. v. Delhi Metro Rail Corporation Ltd. (Supreme Court); Civil Appeal Nos. 5627–5628 of 2021; 09/09/2021; (2022) 1 SCC 131. The present decision held that a court cannot set aside a reasoned arbitral award merely by substituting its own interpretation of evidence and contractual provisions for a plausible view taken by the tribunal. The judgment was subsequently set aside by the Supreme Court in its curative decision dated 10.04.2024.

Articles 5 min read Banks flag gaps in ECL implementation, seek RBI clarity on existing provisions, loan classification The Reserve Bank of India is set to meet banks on November 12 to discuss the implementation of the expected credit loss (ECL) framework, with lenders looking to flag concerns over the treatment of excess provisions held under existing norms, crop-cycle-based agricultural loans and fair valuation of investment portfolios. Banks are seeking clarity on these issues as they assess the provisioning impact of the transition. Advt One of the key concerns raised by public sector banks relates to agriculture lending, where repayment and recovery are dependent on crop cycles rather than conventional monthly repayment schedules."Typically, agriculture loans do not follow 30-day or 60-day DPD norms in the same manner because cash flows are dependent on the crop cycle. There is a sowing period, followed by harvesting and marketing, and recovery happens only after the farmer sells the produce. In some cases, it could take 18 months from sowing to marketing," a senior public sector bank official told ETBFSI.The banker said applying conventional days-past-due (DPD) parameters without accounting for these cycles could result in agriculture loan accounts moving into Stage 2 under the ECL framework, even when the delay is attributable to the underlying crop cycle rather than a deterioration in the borrower's repayment capacity.Banks are awaiting clarity from the regulator on whether crop-cycle-based repayment patterns can be accommodated while determining the staging of such exposures.HTM portfolio, staff loans add to provisioning concernsAnother area where banks are seeking clarity is the treatment of their Held-to-Maturity investment portfolios.Under the existing accounting treatment, investments classified under the HTM category are generally not subjected to regular fair valuation. Banks, however, are concerned about the provisioning implications if such portfolios are required to be fair-valued under the new framework. Advt "HTM portfolios are very large for banks. If you are required to fair-value these investments and provide for the difference, that could become a substantial amount, particularly when interest rates are moving up," the banker said.Banks are also seeking clarity on the treatment of concessional staff loans, particularly housing loans extended to employees at rates lower than those offered to the general public.The concern is that applying fair valuation to such loans could result in an additional provisioning requirement because of the difference between the concessional interest rate and the prevailing market rate. Advt A banker said employee loans could have a meaningful provisioning impact because of the size of such portfolios at some lenders.There are also questions around the treatment of security available against employee loans, including the bank's potential recourse to employees' provident fund, gratuity and other entitlements.Banks seek clarity on excess IRAC provisioningPerhaps one of the more consequential clarifications being sought by lenders relates to the treatment of provisions already made over and above the minimum requirements under the existing IRAC framework.A banker explained that if a lender is required to maintain 60 per cent provisioning on an account under the existing framework but has already provided 100 per cent, the additional 40 per cent has been provided over and above the prescribed requirement. Advt If the ECL-based provisioning requirement for the same exposure works out to 70 per cent, banks want clarity on whether the excess 30 per cent provision can be reversed or whether it would have to be retained.The difference in interpretation could have a substantial bearing on the final provisioning requirement."These are some of the clarifications we are expecting. If the excess provision is allowed to be reversed, the requirement will be one amount. If it is not allowed to be reversed and has to be continued, the requirement could effectively double," another banker told ETBFSI.The banker said the issue is among the accounting-related aspects that lenders expect to discuss with the RBI, along with inconsistencies in the implementation of the ECL framework and the existing IRAC provisions.Apart from SBI, Canara Bank has estimated an ECL transition requirement of Rs 12,000-15,000 crore, while Bank of Baroda has pegged its impact at around Rs 12,500 crore. Punjab National Bank has estimated an impact of around Rs 8,000 crore, Union Bank of India Rs 5,500 crore, Indian Bank Rs 3,500 crore and Indian Overseas Bank around Rs 3,000 crore.A public sector bank official, speaking to ETBFSI, put the lender's estimated provisioning requirement at around Rs 13,000 crore, while another banker indicated a figure of around Rs 12,500 crore.These estimates underline the potential capital implications of the transition, although the final numbers could change depending on regulatory clarifications and the assumptions used by individual banks.The RBI has issued the final ECL directions, but discussions between the regulator and lenders are continuing on implementation-related aspects.Setty had said that a working group is engaging with the RBI to fine-tune certain aspects, while the regulator is also keen to understand banks' technology preparedness, the functioning of their models and the further changes required before implementation.Banks are also expecting a broader interaction with the regulator on November 12, involving senior bank management and directors, to discuss implementation preparedness and areas requiring clarification.The ECL framework will replace the existing incurred-loss-based provisioning approach with a forward-looking model under which banks will recognise expected credit losses based on changes in the credit risk of an exposure.Under the three-stage framework, Stage 1 exposures will attract provisions for 12-month expected credit losses, while Stage 2 and Stage 3 exposures will require provisions based on lifetime expected credit losses.The RBI has provided a four-year transition mechanism to help banks absorb the initial capital impact, allowing a portion of the transition-related adjustment to be added back to Common Equity Tier 1 (CET1) capital. The permitted add-back will progressively decline over the transition period.

Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); Panipat Jalandhar NH-1 Tollway Private Limited Vs National Highways Authority of India (Supreme Court) Summary: The Supreme Court in Panipat Jalandhar NH-1 Tollway Private Limited Vs National Highways Authority of India examined whether an arbitral tribunal comprising an arbitrator who had been appointed on multiple occasions by the same party could adjudicate further disputes arising from the same contract, having regard to Section 12(1)(b) read with Entry 22 of the Fifth Schedule to the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996. The dispute arose from a concession agreement dated 9 May 2008 between the petitioner and the National Highways Authority of India for six-laning approximately 291.10 kilometres of National Highway-1 between Panipat and Jalandhar. Multiple disputes subsequently arose concerning project execution, delinking of a road stretch, suspension of toll collection and termination of the concession agreement. The petitioner claimed losses exceeding ₹2,000 crore and sought separate arbitral references for distinct disputes arising under the agreement. The Delhi High Court had earlier constituted a second arbitral tribunal consisting of retired Supreme Court judges, including Justice G.P. Mathur as the respondent’s nominee. When further disputes arose, NHAI proposed referring them to the same tribunal to avoid duplication of proceedings and save time. The petitioner objected, contending that Justice G.P. Mathur had already received multiple appointments involving NHAI and that another nomination would attract Entry 22 of the Fifth Schedule. By its judgment dated 17 January 2022, the Delhi High Court directed that the additional disputes should be adjudicated by the already constituted second arbitral tribunal. The petitioner challenged this direction before the Supreme Court. The Supreme Court examined the statutory framework governing independence, impartiality and eligibility of arbitrators. It distinguished the Fifth Schedule, which identifies circumstances giving rise to justifiable doubts regarding independence or impartiality, from the Seventh Schedule, which specifies circumstances resulting in statutory ineligibility under Section 12(5). Referring to Voestalpine Schienen GmbH v. Delhi Metro Rail Corporation Ltd., (2017) 4 SCC 665, and HRD Corporation (Marcus Oil and Chemical Division) v. GAIL (India) Limited, (2018) 12 SCC 471, the Court explained that the determination of possible bias requires an objective assessment from the perspective of a neutral third person. Repeated appointments under Entry 22 do not necessarily establish actual bias or impose an absolute statutory disqualification. Rather, the concern is whether successive appointments reflect a special affinity or relationship capable of compromising an arbitrator’s impartiality. Applying these principles, the Supreme Court held that Entry 22 should not operate as an automatic prohibition against appointing the same arbitral tribunal to resolve multiple disputes arising between the same parties under the same contract. The Court distinguished such situations from repeated appointments in unrelated contractual disputes, where the possibility of special affinity between the appointing party and the arbitrator may warrant closer scrutiny. In the present case, Justice G.P. Mathur had initially been nominated during proceedings before the Delhi High Court, had submitted the prescribed disclosures, and was one member of a three-member tribunal consisting of experienced retired Supreme Court judges. No relationship falling within the Seventh Schedule was alleged or established. The Court also observed that the petitioner had itself previously proposed that disputes be referred to an existing arbitral tribunal. Consolidating disputes under the same agreement would ordinarily promote procedural efficiency, avoid repetitive arguments and reduce expenditure, provided that consolidation was feasible and did not prejudice the proceedings. The Supreme Court found no basis to question the independence or impartiality of the tribunal or interfere with the Delhi High Court’s decision under Article 136 of the Constitution. Accordingly, the Special Leave Petition was dismissed. The judgment clarifies that repeated arbitral appointments require a contextual assessment of impartiality, rather than mechanical application of a numerical threshold, particularly where the disputes arise from the same contractual relationship. The short question involved in this Special Leave Petition is, whether the constitution of Arbitral Tribunals consisting of the same Arbitrator and/or Arbitrators in two or more arbitrations to adjudicate disputes arising out of the same contract is barred under Section 12(1)(b) read with Entry 22 of the Fifth Schedule of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996 (hereinafter referred to as “the Arbitration Act”). 2. The Petitioner, Panipat Jalandhar NH-1 Tollway Private Limited entered into a Concession Agreement dated 9th May 2008 with the Respondent, National Highways Authority of India (NHAI) for Six-Laning of 291.10 km of the Panipat-Jalandhar Section of NH-1 (National Highway-1) from 96.00 km to 387.10 km in the States of Haryana and Punjab. The duration of the Concession Agreement was 15 years from 11th May 2009. 3. The relevant provisions of the Concession Agreement are as follows:- “44.1 Dispute resolution 44.1.1. Section 12 of the Arbitration Act, as amended with effect from 23rd October 2015 requires that when a person is approached in connection with his possible appointment as an Arbitrator, he should make a disclosure in writing, stating the existence of any direct or indirect, past or present relationship or interest in any of the parties or in relation to the subject-matter in dispute, which is likely to give justifiable doubts as to his independence or impartiality. He is also to disclose whether he can devote sufficient time to the arbitration, so that he can complete the arbitration within a period of twelve months. The disclosure under Section 12 is required to be made in the form specified in the Sixth Schedule. 23. In determining whether any circumstances exist which give rise to justifiable doubts as to the independence or impartiality of an Arbitrator/Arbitral Tribunal, the grounds set forth in the Fifth Schedule are to serve as a guide. 24. Under Section 12 read with the Fifth Schedule to the Arbitration Act, and in particular, Entry 22 to the said Schedule, the fact that an Arbitrator had, within the past three years, been appointed as Arbitrator on two or more occasions, by any one of the parties or an affiliate of that party, could give rise to justifiable doubts as to the independence or impartiality of the Arbitrator. A person whose relationship with the parties or Counsel or subject matter of the dispute falls in any of the categories specified in the Seventh Schedule is, however ineligible to be appointed Arbitrator, notwithstanding any prior agreement to the contrary. 25. As held by this Court in Voestalpine Schienen GmbH v. Delhi Metro Rail Corporation Ltd.1, independence and impartiality of the Arbitrator are the hallmarks of any arbitration proceedings. Rule against bias is one of the fundamental principles of natural justice, which applies to all judicial and quasi-judicial proceedings. 26. As observed by this Court in Voestalpine Schienen GmbH (supra):- “25. Section 12 has been amended with the objective to induce neutrality of arbitrators viz. their independence and impartiality. The amended provision is enacted to identify the “circumstances” which give rise to “justifiable doubts” about the independence or impartiality of the arbitrator. If any of those circumstances as mentioned therein exists, it will give rise to justifiable apprehension of bias. The Fifth Schedule to the Act enumerates the grounds which may give rise to justifiable doubts of this nature. Likewise, the Seventh Schedule mentions those circumstances which would attract the provisions of sub-section (5) of Section 12 and nullify any prior agreement to the contrary. In the context of this case, it is relevant to mention that only if an arbitrator is an employee, a consultant, an advisor or has any past or present business relationship with a party, he is rendered ineligible to act as an arbitrator. Likewise, that person is treated as incompetent to perform the role of arbitrator, who is a manager, director or part of the management or has a single controlling influence in an affiliate of one of the parties if the affiliate is directly involved in the matters in dispute in the arbitration. Likewise, persons who regularly advised the appointing party or affiliate of the appointing party are incapacitated. A comprehensive list is enumerated in Schedule 5 and Schedule 7 and admittedly the persons empanelled by the respondent are not covered by any of the items in the said list.” 27. As observed by this Court in HRD Corporation (Marcus Oil and Chemical Division) v. GAIL (India) Limited (Formerly Gas Authority of India Limited2, a broad commonsensical approach must be taken to the items stated in Fifth and Seventh Schedule. This approach requires a fair construction of the words used therein, neither enlarging or restricting them unduly. 28. The issue of reasonable likelihood of bias must be dealt with objectively from the point of view of a neutral third person. The question to be posed is, whether a neutral third person would perceive the appointment of the Arbitrator as unfair. In other words, the Court has to address to itself the core question of whether the Arbitrator has such interest in the Arbitration, or any of the parties thereto, that could colour his objectivity and sway his decision in any particular way, keeping in mind the grounds specified in the Fifth Schedule. However, a person whose relationship with the parties or counsel or the subject matter of the dispute falls is any of the categories specified in the Seventh Schedule, would outright be ineligible to be appointed as an Arbitrator. 29. It is nobody’s case that the relationship of Justice G.P. Mathur (Retd.) with the parties or counsel or the subject matter of the dispute falls under any of the categories specified in the Seventh Schedule. The grounds enumerated in the Fifth Schedule do not disqualify a person from being appointed Arbitrator. The Court would only examine whether such person has any such interest in the Arbitration that could affect his objectivity and impartiality. 30. As observed by this Court in HRD Corporation (Marcus Oil and Chemical Division) v. Gail (India) Limited (supra):- “14. The enumeration of grounds given in the Fifth and Seventh Schedules have been taken from the IBA Guidelines, particularly from the Red and Orange Lists thereof. The aforesaid guidelines consist of three lists. The Red List, consisting of non-waivable and waivable guidelines, covers situations which are “more serious” and “serious”, the “more serious” objections being non-waivable. The Orange List, on the other hand, is a list of situations that may give rise to doubts as to the arbitrator’s impartiality or independence, as a consequence of which the arbitrator has a duty to disclose such situations. The Green List is a list of situations where no actual conflict of interest exists from an objective point of view, as a result of which the arbitrator has no duty of disclosure. These Guidelines were first introduced in the year 2004 and have thereafter been amended, after seeing the experience of arbitration worldwide. In Part 1 thereof, general standards regarding impartiality, independence and disclosure are set out.” 31. Entry 22 corresponds to the Orange List where the Arbitrator has a duty to make a disclosure, which Justice G.P. Mathur (Retd.) has done. This Court has to consider if there are justifiable doubts as to the independence or impartiality of Justice G.P. Mathur (Retd.). 32. The object of Entry 22 of the Fifth Schedule read with Section 12(1) of the Arbitration Act and in particular Explanation (1) to Section 12(1) is to see that disputes are not referred to an Arbitrator who has successively been nominated by any of the parties, or their associates, in different arbitrations because of some special affinity with the Arbitrator. 33. In this context, it may be pertinent to take judicial notice of the practice prevailing prior to the amendment of Section 12 of the Arbitration Act with effect from 25th October 2015, when the same Arbitrator, often an associate advocate of a law firm dealing with the cases of a particular party, was appointed Arbitrator by that party by virtue of an Arbitration Clause which empowered that party to appoint an Arbitrator. This was a common practice in case of some financing and hire purchase agreements. 34. Section 12 read with Entry 22 of the Fifth Schedule to the Arbitration Act frowns upon the appointment of an Arbitrator in successive arbitrations by virtue of some special affinity and/or connection with that Arbitrator. 35. The question which is to be addressed by this Court is the possible reason why the Arbitrator should have been appointed on two or more occasions in the past three years. Is there possibility of such special affinity between that Arbitrator and the party concerned that could make the Arbitrator lean favourably towards that party? Does the same Arbitrator being nominated again and again because of any special affinity with the party making the nomination, expect any favourable treatment from the Arbitrator? 36. It is immaterial whether the Arbitrator is actually biased in favour of the party making the nomination. Justice should not only be done, but manifestly be seen to have been done. The nomination of the same Arbitrator in successive arbitrations relating to different contracts may give rise to justifiable apprehension that the Arbitrator may be favourably disposed towards the party making the nomination. 37. In the instant case, the Respondent had not nominated any Arbitrator till 4th May 2021, when the application filed by the Petitioner under Section 11(6) of the Arbitration Act was taken up for hearing before the Delhi High Court. In Court, Counsel had, after taking instructions from the representative of the Respondent, suggested the name of Justice G.P. Mathur (Retd.) as the Respondent’s nominee. This is not a case where different arbitration references relating to different contracts are being made to the same Arbitrator with any ulterior intent. 38. This Court is of the view that Entry 22 would not apply as a rule to appointment of the same Arbitral Tribunal to adjudicate multiple disputes between the same parties arising out of the same contract, to avoid duplication of arguments and save time. 39. In the considered view of this Court, Entry 22 of the Fifth Schedule would only be attracted in case of multiple references by a party, of disputes relating to different contracts, to the same Arbitrator, in excess of the cap of Entry 22. Reference of a dispute to the same Arbitrator conducting different arbitrations concerning any party attracts the Fifth Schedule. Where the disputes pertain to the same contract and a subsequent reference is made to the same Arbitral Tribunal only for the sake of convenience, it cannot be said that circumstances exist, which raise reasonable doubts as to the impartiality or independence of the Arbitral Tribunal. 40. The High Court has very rightly held that there is no embargo to appointment of Arbitrator in cases more than the number stipulated in Entry 22 of the Fifth Schedule. The Court is not debarred from taking a just decision. 41. The declaration made by Justice G.P. Mathur (Retd.) by his communication dated 25th May 2021 and the second declaration made on 5th August 2021 have been set out in the judgment and order impugned. Justice G.P. Mathur is not ineligible to be appointed Arbitrator under Section 12(5) of the Arbitration Act, or otherwise. As held by the High Court, a conjoint reading of both the declarations clearly show that neither the learned Arbitrator nor any member of his family had any relationship with the Respondent or any associate or affiliate of the Respondent which could cast any doubts on the propriety of his appointment. He had categorically declared that he had been appointed Arbitrator in three other matters, the last of which was by virtue of the order dated 4th May 2021 of the Delhi High Court as stated above. 42. Significantly the Petitioner itself had earlier, long after the amendment of 2015, by its letter dated 11th December 2020 suggested reference of all disputes between the Petitioner and the Respondent to the first Arbitral Tribunal adjudicating the first reference since 2013, or alternatively to any other Arbitral Tribunal that might be constituted. It is difficult to comprehend why objection should now be raised to reference of the same disputes covered by the letter dated 11th December 2020 to the second Arbitral Tribunal, which is already in seisin of dispute relating to the same project and the same contract. 43. The second Arbitral Tribunal is comprised of highly experienced legal luminaries who had been Judges of the highest Court in this country. There is no reason to suppose that the Arbitral Tribunal will not be independent or impartial. Moreover, Justice G.P. Mathur (Retd.) is one of the Arbitrators in an Arbitral Tribunal comprising two others. Even if he had earlier been nominated by the Respondent, that was because of his legal acumen and vast experience. 44. This Court also agrees that an arbitration clause is incorporated in an agreement and/or contract with the object of speedy resolution of disputes. Multiple disputes arising out of the same agreement and/or contract should, wherever possible, be consolidated and referred to the same Arbitral Tribunal, unless reference to the same Arbitral Tribunal is not expedient or feasible. To give an example, reference of a subsequent dispute to an Arbitral Tribunal deciding an arbitration which is at an advanced stage of arguments, or where proceedings have been concluded and award awaited, may not be feasible. The example is only illustrative. 45. This Court finds no infirmity in the impugned judgment and order of the High Court, which warrants interference under Article 136 of the Constitution of India. 46. The Special Leave Petition is, accordingly, dismissed. The special leave petition is dismissed in terms of the signed non-reportable judgment.

Advt “What they came up with is that yes, junior workforce gets affected but overall senior employment picks up and total employment picks up etc. This is their paper which is good news at one level that the massive labour shock that we anticipated isn't materialising yet,” Nageswaran said.“But from the Indian standpoint, given the demographic dividend, we have to be mindful that junior-level jobs are indeed slower than what was there pre-AI because a young country with a narrowing door has to worry. It is exposed where it hurts at the junior level and this will create inequality widening,” he said.Per Nageswaran a bad start or a delayed start will be a scar for many years in terms of earnings and returns flow to capital and seniority while the young are told to wait. That has got social ramifications as well.He said India should not focus only on the impact of AI on IT-sector jobs.“And if you look at the aspiration gap, more graduates and fewer first jobs, educated youth already face higher unemployment than the unskilled. Therefore, what we need to do in order to make sure it does not become a systemic social risk is we should not focus only on the IT sector and what it does to IT sector jobs,” Nageswaran said. Advt “We need to focus on the next two blocks here: creating jobs that use AI and jobs that are AI insulated.”AI could push India towards labour-intensive growth“In some sense, paradoxically, AI will force India to look at its endowment, labour. We inherited a capital-intensive model of development because that is what legacy industrial revolution from the West gave us. We had no choice. But AI is going to force us to look at labour-intensive growth, labour-intensive manufacturing,” Nageswaran said.He said the trade agreements India has concluded with the UK and European Union, as well as those under negotiation, are providing an opportunity to create labour-intensive manufacturing and services. Advt “And the trade agreements we have concluded with the UK, European Union and the many that are under negotiation are giving us the opportunity to create labour-intensive manufacturing and also labour-intensive services whether it is in elder care, whether it is in special needs children, hospitality, tourism etc.,” he said.Nageswaran said India would also need to invest more in making trade skills fashionable.“We will have to invest a lot more in making trade skills fashionable. It will not happen easily because it's a social phenomenon. Social phenomena take time but therefore we have to start today whether it is through using, you know, brand champions or leaders or social influencers, whatever it is, at all levels,” he said. Advt He also stressed the need for greater private-sector participation in curriculum development.“And more importantly, making sure that the private sector participates in the curriculum development. The good news is, as Secretary, Ministry of Skill Development, and I wrote in an article in Mint last month, the new PMU initiative involves the private sector in curriculum development and course development on skilling and that is very important for creating AI-insulated jobs,” Nageswaran said.When asked whether the government should consider a PLI scheme for AI-insulated jobs, Nageswaran said free trade agreements could serve as the catalyst.“I think in some sense the free trade agreements serve as the catalyst. What PLI did for other advanced manufacturing areas. These recent UK agreement which is now active and the EU agreement which should become active soon. They kind of serve the purpose of providing the incentive and the motivation for production to increase in these areas,” he said.“And I think we should wait and see what happens as a result of that before we try to come up with incentive because I think the market will provide the right incentives,” Nageswaran said.

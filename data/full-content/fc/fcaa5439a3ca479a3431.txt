Summary : Business partnerships often fail not because of financial losses but because partners leave essential questions unanswered when their relationship is still strong. The experience of Adolf and Rudolf Dassler, who built a successful shoe business before personal conflict led them to establish Adidas and Puma separately, illustrates how a partnership can break down even when the underlying business has potential. Drawing on founder research, historical commercial practices and practical business examples, this article explains why written agreements are essential for protecting both business interests and personal relationships. It introduces the SPLIT-UP framework, covering Shares and vesting, Power and decision-making, Labour and contributions, Intellectual property, Termination, Unexpected events and Profits. These seven areas help partners define ownership, allocate responsibilities, establish approval procedures, protect business assets, agree exit and valuation mechanisms, prepare for death or disability, and determine how profits will be retained or distributed. The article also examines the documents that may be needed, including partnership deeds, limited liability partnership agreements, shareholders’ agreements, founders’ agreements and articles of association. It highlights warning signs, common mistakes, practical steps for different partners and questions to discuss before disagreements arise. For Indian businesses, the applicable legal framework depends on the business structure and relevant state requirements. The central lesson is that a carefully drafted, consistent and regularly reviewed agreement can turn future disagreements into established procedures, helping preserve trust, business continuity and the relationships on which a partnership was built. Together they built a thriving business. By 1948, years of bitter personal conflict had driven them apart. Adolf created Adidas; Rudolf created Puma. Locals said the town itself divided along brand lines for generations. The company had not failed. The partnership had. Partners survive losses. They rarely survive ambiguity It nearly always begins with a handshake. Two friends. Two siblings. A former colleague who feels “like family.” One brings the idea; the other brings the money — or the contacts, or the technical know-how. No one wants to mention lawyers. It feels cold. “We trust each other.” “We’ll work it out when we need to.” That moment always arrives. When the business is struggling, partners typically pull together; survival gives them a shared enemy. The real tests tend to come later: When one partner puts in twelve-hour days and the other reveals up twice a week. When profits finally arrive and the partners disagree on what to do with them. When one wants to expand and the other wants dividends. When a spouse, sibling or investor joins the picture. When one partner decides to leave. None of these situations is unusual, and every one is foreseeable. That’s the hard truth: nearly every partnership dispute concerns a question that could have been settled on the first day, while everyone was still on good terms. An agreement is cheapest to write when you don’t need it — and priceless when you do. What the research says Noam Wasserman of Harvard Business School studied thousands of founders for The Founder’s Dilemmas (2012). One of his clearest conclusions was that many founding teams split equity swiftly and evenly — frequently in the first month, before they know what each person will actually contribute — and that these early, unexamined choices frequently become sources of friction later. The pattern is global. It reveals up in Silicon Valley startups, Indian family firms, African partnerships and Gulf trading houses alike. What history teaches: the partnership contract is ancient Partnership quarrels are as old as commerce. Merchants in ancient Mesopotamia recorded their partnership terms on clay tablets. Medieval Islamic and Italian traders developed detailed partnership structures — the mudaraba and the commenda — setting out who supplied capital, who supplied labour and how profits and losses would be divided. Those merchants did not write things down because they distrusted one another. They wrote them down because they had seen what happens when nothing is written. The Seven Hard Questions Before money and emotions get involved, every partnership should answer seven questions in writing. Remember them as SPLIT-UP: S — Shares: who owns what, and how is it earned? An equal split feels fair on day one and frequently feels unfair later. If one founder leaves after eight months, should they keep half of a company the other builds over ten years? Fix: Use vesting — ownership earned over time. A common startup approach is four years with a one-year “cliff,” so nothing vests until the first year is complete. In partnership firms and LLPs, tie profit share to defined roles and capital, and revisit it at fixed intervals. P — Power: who decides what? “We’ll decide together” works until the two of you disagree. Fix: Spell out decision rights. Which decisions need everyone’s consent — borrowing, admitting partners, selling the business, giving guarantees? Which can each partner take alone in their own area? And what happens in a deadlock — a mediator, a casting vote or a buy-sell mechanism? L — Labour and contribution: what does each partner bring? Capital, time, skills, relationships, intellectual property. Fix: Record capital contributions, the expected time commitment, roles and responsibilities. If one partner takes a salary and the other does not, put that in writing. I — Intellectual property: who owns the assets? Code written before the company existed. A brand registered in one partner’s name. A customer list sitting on someone’s personal laptop. Fix: Assign all business IP to the company or firm in writing, and register trademarks in the entity’s name. T — Termination: how does someone leave? The clause partners avoid most — and need most. Fix: Define “good leaver” and “bad leaver” terms. Agree the valuation method in advance. Give the remaining partners a right of first refusal. Add reasonable non-compete and non-solicitation terms, within what local law permits. U — Unexpected events: death, disability, divorce, dispute Unpleasant to discuss, but inevitable over a long enough period. Fix: Decide whether shares pass to heirs or must be bought back. Consider partnership or key-person insurance to fund a buyout. Include a dispute-resolution clause — mediation first, then arbitration. P — Profits: how will the money be used? Reinvesting versus distributing is among the commonest causes of conflict. Fix: Agree a default rule — for instance, retain a set percentage for working capital and growth, and distribute only after statutory dues and loan commitments are met. Quick recap The short version: settle SPLIT-UP in writing while you’re still friends, and most future disputes become procedures rather than battles. The documents that protect the relationship Depending on the structure and the country, the answers belong in a partnership deed, an LLP agreement, a shareholders’ agreement, a founders’ agreement or the company’s articles of association. What the document is called matters far less than what it says. An internet template beats nothing — but only just. A template can’t know that one partner is contributing land, another runs a side business, or a relative holds shares for someone else. Your agreement must describe your partnership, not a generic one. Ensure the documents are consistent, too. A shareholders’ agreement that conflicts with the articles of association — or a will that conflicts with both — can cause litigation rather than prevent it. Signs of progress A good partnership agreement reveals itself through calm, not paperwork. Look for these signs: Disagreements end in decisions, not in silence or delay. Money questions — drawings, salaries, reinvestment — are settled by the agreed formula rather than renegotiated each month. Every partner can describe the exit process in the same words. Review dates are honoured — the agreement is revisited each year or after any major change. The decade ahead More and more businesses are founded by partners who live in different cities or countries, work on the venture part-time or bring in investors early. That makes unwritten understandings even more fragile. Digital signatures and shared online documents make writing things down easier than ever — and make it harder to claim nobody did. Tomorrow’s partnerships will last because of clarity, not closeness. Next steps for each role Each partner: privately write down what you believe the arrangement on ownership, roles and exit actually is. Then compare notes. The finance partner: list every payment made to partners over the past 12 months. The managing partner: arrange a meeting with a lawyer to turn the answers into a signed agreement. All partners: fix one date each year to review the agreement together. A smaller-scale example Take two hypothetical friends who open a café, each investing the same amount. One works there full-time; the other keeps a job and helps out at weekends. Nothing is written down. Two years later, the café is profitable. The full-time partner feels she should be paid a salary before profits are shared. The other believes profits should be split evenly because the investment was even. Both views are reasonable. Neither is recorded. A two-hour session with an adviser produces a one-page agreement: a market salary for the working partner, profits split equally after that, a buy-out formula based on a multiple of average profit, and a rule that any spending above ₹2 lakh needs both signatures. The friendship survives because the money questions finally have answers. Frequently asked questions “Do family partnerships need written agreements as well?” More than any other kind. Family ties make disputes more painful and harder to settle without clear terms. “What should a partnership agreement cover at a minimum?” Ownership, capital, roles, decision rights, profit sharing and drawings, admitting new partners, and exit and valuation rules. “Can we change the agreement later?” Yes, by mutual consent. Review it at a minimum yearly and after any significant change in the business. Where things stand (October 2026): In India, partnership firms are governed by the Indian Partnership Act, 1932; LLPs by the Limited Liability Partnership Act, 2008 (under which the LLP agreement must be filed); and firms by the Companies Act, 2013, where shareholder rights should be reflected in the articles of association to be reliably enforceable. Stamp duty, registration and enforceability rules differ by state and country — take local legal advice. The toughest pushback — and a reply The pushback: “Bringing these questions up reveals we don’t trust each other. It will damage the relationship before we even begin.” The reply: It works the other way. A partner who refuses to write down fair terms is telling you something significant — early, when it’s still cheap to discover. And partners who agree clear terms can trust each other more, because the hardest conversations are already behind them. Think of a seatbelt: wearing one doesn’t mean you expect to crash. It means you care about the people in the car. What partners should avoid Holding shares or partnership interests in a relative’s name for convenience. It creates legal, tax and inheritance issues — and in India can raise issues under benami property law. Spoken side deals — “you take a bigger salary and I’ll take a bigger dividend.” Mixing personal loans between partners with business capital. Letting one partner sign bank papers, guarantees and contracts without the others knowing. Signing an agreement and then ignoring it. A document that doesn’t match how the business truly runs protects nobody. Try this today (30 minutes) Sit down with your partner — somewhere calm, away from the office — and each answer these questions separately, on paper: If one of us wanted out tomorrow, what should happen? What would we do if we strongly disagreed on a major decision? Is our ownership still fair given what each of us now contributes? Who legally owns our brand, domain, software and customer data? Do our spouses or families understand our arrangement? Then compare. Every difference is a future dispute you can settle today, at no cost. Key takeaways Partnerships seldom end because of losses; they end because of questions nobody settled at the start. Work through SPLIT-UP: Shares and vesting, Power and decisions, Labour and contributions, Intellectual property, Termination, Unexpected events and Profits. Keep your documents specific, consistent with one another and aligned with how the business actually operates. Avoid nominee shareholdings, verbal side arrangements and one-person signing authority. Writing things down doesn’t signal distrust. It protects trust. Clear terms are not suspicion. They are respect — for your partner, for the business and for the friendship that began it.

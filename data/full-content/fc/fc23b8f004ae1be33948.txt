Summary: Investors earning profits from the sale of IPO shares shortly after listing may face different income-tax consequences depending on whether the shares are sold through a recognised stock exchange or through a genuine off-market transaction. Under Section 111A of the Income-tax Act, 1961, qualifying short-term capital gains on listed equity shares are taxable at 20% for transfers made on or after 23 July 2024, subject to the prescribed Securities Transaction Tax (STT) conditions. A genuine off-market sale that is not chargeable to STT may fall outside Section 111A, potentially resulting in taxation at normal slab rates. This distinction is particularly relevant under the new tax regime for Assessment Year 2026-27, where eligible resident individuals with total income not exceeding ₹12 lakh may claim a rebate under Section 87A, limited to ₹60,000 and the tax payable at normal slab rates. Tax on gains chargeable under Section 111A is not eligible for this rebate. The article examines an illustration involving ₹4 lakh of other taxable income and ₹5 lakh of IPO listing gains, comparing the potential tax consequences of exchange-based and genuine off-market sales. It also highlights that an off-market transfer must be legally permissible, commercially genuine and properly documented. Investors should consider applicable STT provisions, transfer restrictions, stamp duty, depository charges and other relevant tax rules before selecting a transaction method. The possibility of a lower tax liability does not, by itself, establish that an off-market sale is suitable or permissible in every case. Short-term capital gains arising from specified transactions in equity shares are covered by Section 111A of the Income-tax Act, 1961. For transfers made on or after 23 July 2024, the applicable rate under Section 111A is 20%, provided the prescribed conditions are satisfied. For a retail investor, this may appear straightforward: sell listed shares at a profit, calculate the gain and pay tax at the prescribed rate. But there is an important statutory condition that deserves attention. Section 111A applies to specified transactions where the sale of equity shares is chargeable to Securities Transaction Tax (STT). This raises an interesting question: Does every sale of listed shares necessarily attract STT? The answer is no. Is Every Sale of Listed Shares an Exchange Transaction? Listed shares can be transferred through different mechanisms. A sale executed through a recognized stock exchange is generally routed through the exchange and its clearing mechanism. Such transactions may attract STT, subject to the applicable provisions. An off-market transaction, on the other hand, involves the transfer of securities from one demat account to another without routing the transaction through a stock exchange and its clearing mechanism. Such transfers are carried out through the depository system and are subject to prescribed procedures and regulatory safeguards. Off-market transfers may take place for various reasons, including genuine private sales and permitted transfers between family members. However, one distinction is essential. An offline transaction is not necessarily an off-market transaction. If an investor places an order through a telephone call or another offline mode, but the sale is executed through a recognized stock exchange, it remains an exchange transaction. The tax treatment depends on the actual nature of the transaction—not the method used to place the order. The Crucial Question: Does Section 111A Apply to an Off-Market Sale? This is where the discussion becomes particularly interesting. Section 111A requires the transaction of sale of the equity share to be chargeable to STT. A genuine off-market sale that is not chargeable to STT may therefore fall outside the scope of Section 111A. If the special-rate provision does not apply, the short-term capital gain may instead be taxable at the normal applicable rates. This distinction could be significant for investors under the new tax regime, particularly those whose total income falls within the lower tax brackets. However, the conclusion must be based on the actual transaction and the statutory conditions. The mere fact that STT was not paid should not, by itself, be considered conclusive. Could the Section 87A Rebate Change the Tax Outcome? The discussion does not end with the applicable tax rate. For eligible resident individuals under the new tax regime, Section 87A provides a rebate subject to specified conditions. For AY 2026–27, the rebate may be available where total income does not exceed ₹12 lakh, subject to the prescribed requirements. But there is an important distinction between capital gains taxed at normal slab rates and gains taxed at special rates. Tax on short-term capital gains chargeable under Section 111A is excluded from the Section 87A rebate. Therefore, if a genuine off-market transaction results in capital gains being taxed at normal slab rates rather than under Section 111A, the possibility of claiming the rebate may arise. This is the point that makes the subject particularly relevant to investors with modest taxable income. A difference in the applicable tax provision could potentially make a meaningful difference to the final tax liability. A Simple Illustration of IPO Listing Gains Taxation Consider a resident individual under the new tax regime with the following income: Particulars Amount Other taxable income ₹4,00,000 IPO listing gain ₹5,00,000 Total income ₹9,00,000 Assume the IPO shares are sold shortly after listing. Situation A: Sale Through a Stock Exchange If the transaction satisfies the conditions of Section 111A, the short-term capital gain of ₹5 lakh would be taxable at the special rate of 20%. The tax on this special-rate gain is calculated separately. The Section 87A rebate cannot be used to eliminate the tax payable on the Section 111A gain. Situation B: Genuine Off-Market Sale Suppose the shares are sold through a genuine off-market transaction that is not chargeable to STT. If the gain falls outside Section 111A and is taxable at normal slab rates, the investor may potentially become eligible for the Section 87A rebate, subject to the prescribed conditions. The final tax liability would depend on the applicable slab rates, rebate eligibility and other relevant provisions. The illustration demonstrates why the mode of transfer may deserve careful examination before an investor decides how to sell IPO shares. Is Tax Planning Through an Off-Market Sale Always Advisable? A possible tax advantage should not automatically be treated as a recommendation to undertake an off-market transaction. Before considering such a sale, investors should examine: Whether the proposed transfer is legally permissible. Whether the IPO shares are freely transferable and subject to any lock-in or other restrictions. Whether the transaction is genuinely chargeable to STT. Whether the consideration is genuine and properly documented. Applicable stamp duty and depository charges. Whether any other tax provisions apply, particularly where shares are transferred to family members. The legal validity and commercial substance of the transaction are as important as its tax consequences. Tax planning should be based on genuine transactions and a proper understanding of the law—not merely on the absence of STT. A genuine off-market sale that is not chargeable to STT may fall outside the scope of this provision and result in taxation at normal slab rates. For an eligible resident individual, this may also raise the possibility of claiming the Section 87A rebate. However, the tax consequences must be examined carefully before drawing any conclusion or undertaking a transaction.

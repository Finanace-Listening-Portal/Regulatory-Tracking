(Supreme Court of India) Summary: The Supreme Court of India, in Power Grid Corporation of India Limited Vs Madhya Pradesh Power Transmission Company Limited & Ors., examined the scope of the Central Electricity Regulatory Commission’s (CERC) powers under the Electricity Act, 2003, particularly its authority to regulate inter-State electricity transmission, determine transmission tariffs and permit recovery of compensation arising from delays in commissioning transmission infrastructure. The principal controversy concerned whether CERC could exercise regulatory powers under Section 79(1) in the absence of specific provisions in regulations framed under Section 178. The Court also considered whether the Madhya Pradesh High Court was justified in entertaining writ petitions under Article 226 despite the availability of a statutory appellate remedy under Section 111 before the Appellate Tribunal for Electricity (APTEL). The dispute originated from the implementation of the Western Region System Strengthening Scheme XIV (WRSS-XIV) and Western Region Strengthening Scheme XVI (WRSS-XVI). Power Grid Corporation of India Limited, a central transmission utility, had undertaken construction of transmission assets at the Indore substation pursuant to arrangements approved during meetings of the Standing Committee on Power System Planning. Madhya Pradesh Power Transmission Company Limited (MPPTCL), the State transmission utility, was responsible for constructing and commissioning the corresponding downstream intra-State transmission lines. The projects required coordinated commissioning so that the transmission infrastructure could operate effectively. However, delays in the completion of downstream facilities resulted in a dispute concerning the financial consequences of transmission assets remaining unavailable for their intended use. Power Grid approached CERC through Petition Nos. 311/TT/2018 and 266/TT/2018 seeking approval of the commercial operation dates and determination of transmission tariffs under the Central Electricity Regulatory Commission (Terms and Conditions of Tariff) Regulations, 2014. By orders dated 21 January 2020 and 27 January 2020, CERC considered the commissioning delays and approved the relevant commercial operation dates under Regulation 4(3)(ii). Although CERC did not condone the entire time overrun, it permitted Power Grid to claim specified compensation relating to the period preceding the approved commercial operation dates and directed that transmission charges for the subsequent period until the downstream system was charged would be borne by MPPTCL. Power Grid subsequently raised a bill of approximately ₹6.18 crore, which MPPTCL challenged. MPPTCL filed writ petitions before the Madhya Pradesh High Court contending that neither the 2014 Tariff Regulations nor the arrangements between the parties authorised CERC to impose compensatory transmission charges. It argued that CERC had exceeded its statutory jurisdiction and effectively introduced contractual obligations that the parties had never agreed upon. MPPTCL further maintained that CERC had granted relief beyond the prayers made before it and had violated principles of natural justice. Relying on judicial precedents concerning exceptions to the alternative-remedy rule, MPPTCL submitted that a writ petition was maintainable because the impugned regulatory orders were allegedly without jurisdiction. The High Court accepted the maintainability argument and admitted the writ petitions notwithstanding the appellate remedy under Section 111. Power Grid challenged the High Court’s decision before the Supreme Court, contending that the Electricity Act, 2003 establishes a specialised regulatory and appellate framework for electricity-sector disputes. It argued that CERC possessed express authority under Sections 62 and 79 to regulate inter-State transmission and determine the associated tariff. According to Power Grid, any disagreement regarding the correctness, legality or propriety of CERC’s orders could be examined by APTEL under Section 111. The appellant emphasised that MPPTCL had not challenged the constitutional validity of any statutory provision or regulation. Instead, the challenge concerned particular orders passed in the exercise of CERC’s recognised jurisdiction. Power Grid also highlighted the possibility of conflicting decisions if different High Courts independently entertained disputes involving the same inter-State transmission system. The Supreme Court examined the relationship between Sections 79 and 178 of the Electricity Act, 2003, with particular reference to its earlier Constitution Bench judgment in PTC India Limited v. Central Electricity Regulatory Commission, (2010) 4 SCC 603. It explained that CERC performs distinct functions under the two provisions. Section 79 confers administrative, regulatory and adjudicatory powers, while Section 178 authorises the framing of statutory regulations through delegated legislation. Although decisions under Section 79 must conform to regulations framed under Section 178 wherever such regulations apply, the absence of a specific regulation does not prevent CERC from exercising its statutory functions. The Court emphasised that making regulations is different from regulating a particular activity or resolving a specific regulatory situation through an order. The Court further distinguished between regulatory and adjudicatory functions under Section 79. It observed that the authority to regulate inter-State transmission under Section 79(1)(c) and determine transmission tariffs under Section 79(1)(d) necessarily includes addressing situations affecting the commercial operation and financial viability of transmission infrastructure. Where existing regulations do not expressly address the consequences of delayed commissioning, CERC may exercise its general regulatory authority to address that gap, provided its decision remains consistent with the Electricity Act and applicable regulations. Accordingly, the absence of an express provision in the 2014 Tariff Regulations dealing with compensation for such delays did not automatically deprive CERC of jurisdiction to make a regulatory order concerning transmission charges. On the question of natural justice, the Supreme Court held that CERC’s decision permitting Power Grid to claim compensation was regulatory in character rather than a final adjudication of liability between the parties. The Commission had addressed the consequences of delayed commissioning within its statutory regulatory jurisdiction. Therefore, a separate prayer seeking such regulatory relief was not indispensable, nor was an additional opportunity of hearing required at that particular stage. The Court clarified that a concrete dispute concerning liability for transmission charges would arise upon the raising of bills and could be examined through the statutory appellate process. Consequently, the High Court had erred in treating the regulatory order as a jurisdictional defect or violation of natural justice sufficient to bypass the alternative remedy. The Supreme Court also clarified the distinction between challenging delegated legislation and challenging an individual regulatory order. A regulation framed under Section 178 has legislative character and its validity cannot be examined by APTEL through an ordinary statutory appeal. However, an order made under Section 79(1), even when it exercises regulatory authority, operates in relation to particular parties and circumstances and remains appealable under Section 111. The Court held that the exceptions recognised in Whirlpool Corporation v. Registrar of Trademarks, (1998) 8 SCC 1, were not attracted. Merely alleging that CERC had exceeded its authority did not establish the kind of jurisdictional defect necessary to justify bypassing the specialised statutory remedy. Allowing both civil appeals, the Supreme Court set aside the Madhya Pradesh High Court’s judgment dated 25 February 2021 and dismissed the writ petitions. Importantly, the Court expressly limited its decision to CERC’s jurisdiction and the maintainability of the writ proceedings. It did not determine whether MPPTCL was actually liable to pay the disputed transmission charges in the particular circumstances. That question was left open for examination by APTEL if MPPTCL elected to pursue an appeal under Section 111. The judgment reinforces the distinction between statutory rule-making and case-specific regulatory powers, recognises CERC’s authority to address regulatory gaps, and emphasises the importance of using the specialised appellate mechanism established under the Electricity Act, 2003.

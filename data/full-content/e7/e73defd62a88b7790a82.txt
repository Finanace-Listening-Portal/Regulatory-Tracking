Summary: The question of whether interest is payable on every GST debit note issued following a price escalation requires examination of the original contractual arrangement, the nature of additional consideration and the statutory date on which differential tax became payable. A debit note may record a retrospective enhancement of an existing price, or it may reflect additional consideration arising from a subsequent agreement, approval or settlement. These situations can have different implications under GST law, particularly when applying Sections 12, 13, 15, 34 and 50 of the Central Goods and Services Tax Act, 2017. The issue has gained significance following the GST Appellate Tribunal, Bengaluru Bench decision in Gopinath TM v. Mukand Limited, Appeal No. APL/258/BUR/2026, dated 7 October 2026. The dispute concerned retrospective price revisions relating to supplies made before the introduction of GST. Supplementary invoices and debit notes were issued after GST implementation, and differential tax of ₹54,44,190 was paid. The Tribunal restored an interest demand of ₹13,85,623 while maintaining deletion of penalty. Its reasoning relied on the transitional treatment of upward price revisions under Section 142(2)(a) and the Supreme Court’s approach to retrospective escalation in Steel Authority of India Ltd. v. Commissioner of Central Excise. The central legal distinction is between an existing contractual liability quantified at a later date and a new liability created by a subsequent event. Where the original contract expressly provides for retrospective price escalation, the Revenue may contend that additional consideration relates back to the original supply. Conversely, where the original price was final and additional consideration became enforceable only after fresh negotiations or substantive approval, the taxpayer may dispute interest for an earlier period. Neither the date of the debit note nor uncertainty over the amount is necessarily conclusive. Interest on GST Debit Notes: Does Every Price Escalation Attract Interest? Introduction Price escalation is common in long-term commercial contracts involving manufacturing, infrastructure, construction and supply of goods. The consideration originally agreed between parties may subsequently increase because of changes in input costs, contractual escalation clauses, revised price negotiations or approvals granted by the recipient. Such adjustments are ordinarily documented through debit notes.While payment of differential GST on an upward price revision may not be disputed, the more complicated question concerns interest. Does every debit note attract interest from the original invoice date, even where the additional consideration was neither determined nor legally payable at that time? This question assumes significance following the decision of the GST Appellate Tribunal, Bengaluru Bench, in Gopinath TM v. Mukand Limited, Appeal No. APL/258/BUR/2026, dated 7 October 2026. The Tribunal upheld an interest demand arising from retrospective price escalation relating to pre-GST supplies.The decision invites a broader examination of the distinction between an existing liability quantified subsequently and an additional liability created by a later contractual event. This distinction is particularly important when applying Sections 12, 13, 15, 34 and 50 of the CGST Act to transactions occurring entirely under GST. 2. Background of the GSTAT Bengaluru Decision The dispute in Mukand Limited concerned goods supplied between April and June 2017, before GST was introduced. The original transaction values were subject to upward revision under contractual price-escalation arrangements. Following the introduction of GST, supplementary invoices and debit notes were issued on 29 September 2018 and 15 October 2018. Differential tax amounting to ₹54,44,190 was discharged through the GST mechanism. The department contended that the revised prices related back to the original supplies and demanded interest of ₹13,85,623, along with penalties. Although the First Appellate Authority deleted the interest and penalty, the Revenue challenged that decision before GSTAT. The Tribunal held that the retrospective price revision enhanced the value attributable to the original supplies. It concluded that Section 142(2)(a), which provides a transitional mechanism for upward price revisions, did not eliminate the corresponding interest liability. The interest demand was consequently restored, while the deletion of penalty was maintained. Importantly, the case concerned pre-GST supplies and a retrospective contractual revision. It did not directly determine the treatment of every debit note issued for transactions taking place entirely under GST. 3. Section 142(2)(a): Transitional Treatment of Price Revisions Section 142(2)(a) addresses upward price revisions under contracts entered into before the appointed day. Where prices are revised upwards after the introduction of GST, the provision requires issuance of a supplementary invoice or debit note within the prescribed period and provides for its treatment under the GST framework. The taxpayer argued that the transitional provision created a fresh tax point and that payment through returns relating to the debit-note period should be regarded as timely. The Tribunal rejected this interpretation. It treated the supplementary document as a mechanism for reporting and discharging tax, rather than a provision extinguishing interest arising from retrospective enhancement of the original transaction value. This distinction matters because Section 142 deals specifically with the transition between tax regimes. For supplies made entirely under GST, the liability must be examined under the ordinary valuation, time-of-supply and interest provisions. 4. The Supreme Court’s Ruling in Steel Authority of India The Tribunal relied substantially on Steel Authority of India Ltd. v. Commissioner of Central Excise, decided by the Supreme Court in 2019. That case involved goods cleared under the Central Excise regime at prices subsequently increased with retrospective effect. The assessee paid differential duty but disputed interest for the intervening period. The Supreme Court held that retrospective price escalation could give rise to interest on differential duty. The reasoning was that the original price was variable under the contractual arrangement, and the subsequently finalised price represented the value attributable to the original clearance. The Court considered earlier decisions, including CCE v. SKF India Ltd. and CCE v. International Auto Ltd. The significant distinction is between a price that was already variable under the original agreement and a price that was final when the supply occurred. The existence of a subsequent debit note does not, without examining the contractual arrangement, establish that additional tax was overdue from the original invoice date. 5. Section 15: Valuation and Additional Consideration Section 15(1) generally identifies the transaction value as the price actually paid or payable for a supply, subject to the prescribed conditions. The expression “price actually paid or payable” becomes relevant where the original consideration is subsequently increased. If the contract provides for retrospective price adjustment, the additional consideration may represent part of the value attributable to the original supply. However, a different question arises where the original price was final and additional consideration becomes payable only because of a subsequent agreement, approval or independent event. Section 15 determines valuation principles. It does not, by itself, conclusively establish the date from which interest becomes payable on every subsequent adjustment. The valuation of a supply and the commencement of interest liability must therefore be examined as separate statutory questions. 6. Section 34: Does a Debit Note Create a Fresh Tax Liability? Section 34(3) requires issuance of a debit note where the taxable value or tax charged in an invoice is found to be lower than the taxable value or tax payable in respect of the supply. Section 34(4) provides for declaration of the debit note in the return for the month in which it is issued and adjustment of the tax liability in the prescribed manner. However, Section 34 does not expressly provide that interest must invariably be calculated from the original invoice date. Equally, it does not establish that interest can never arise for a period preceding the debit note. Where the document records a retrospective adjustment to an existing contractual liability, the Revenue may contend that differential tax relates back to the original supply.Conversely, where additional consideration becomes payable only upon a later event, the taxpayer may argue that the underlying liability did not exist earlier. The issuance and reporting of a debit note must therefore be distinguished from the determination of when the differential tax became legally due. 7. Sections 12 and 13: Time of Supply Sections 12 and 13 govern the time of supply of goods and services respectively. Their application depends upon the nature of the transaction, invoicing requirements, payment arrangements and applicable statutory provisions. Consider goods supplied in January for ₹10 lakh, followed by additional consideration of ₹2 lakh determined in September. The original time of supply does not, by itself, resolve whether differential tax on the additional amount was overdue from January.If the original contract provided for retrospective escalation, the Revenue may argue that the enhanced consideration formed part of the original supply value. However, if the additional amount arose under a fresh agreement concluded in September, the taxpayer may contend that no corresponding entitlement existed in January.Similar questions arise in service contracts where additional payments depend upon certification, measurement, completion of milestones or approval of escalation claims. The relevant inquiry is whether the later event merely quantified an existing entitlement or created an enforceable entitlement for the first time. 8. Section 50: When Does Interest Actually Arise? Section 50(1) provides for interest where a person liable to pay tax fails to pay it within the prescribed period. Section 50(2) governs calculation of interest from the day succeeding the date on which the tax was due, subject to the applicable statutory provisions. The essential inquiry therefore involves two questions: When did the differential tax become legally payable? Did that amount remain unpaid beyond the applicable due date? In cases of retrospective price escalation, the department may contend that the enhanced value relates to the original supply and that differential tax remained unpaid from the earlier due date. However, where the contractual entitlement itself arose only upon a subsequent event, the taxpayer may dispute the existence of any earlier default. The argument is not that uncertainty automatically eliminates interest. Rather, interest requires identification of a legally established tax liability and the date on which its payment became due. 9. Scenario I: Retrospective Escalation Under the Original Contract Suppose a manufacturer supplies goods worth ₹1 crore in January under a contract expressly providing for retrospective price revision linked to steel prices. In September, the agreed formula results in an additional consideration of ₹15 lakh. A debit note is issued and differential GST is paid. Here, the price-adjustment mechanism already existed when the original supply occurred. Although the final amount was determined later, the contract contemplated a variable price. This situation closely resembles the principle examined in Steel Authority of India. The Revenue may argue that the revised consideration represents the final value attributable to the original supply. Consequently, interest exposure may arise for the intervening period, subject to establishing the applicable due date under GST law. 10. Scenario II: Liability Existed but the Amount Was Uncertain Consider a contract providing for additional consideration based on a future cost index. The supplier’s contractual entitlement exists from the beginning, but the precise amount cannot be calculated until the relevant index is published. The taxpayer may argue that differential tax could not have been paid before the amount became ascertainable. The Revenue may respond that subsequent quantification merely determined the value attributable to the earlier supply. The Supreme Court’s reasoning in Steel Authority of India makes it difficult to maintain that uncertainty of quantum, by itself, necessarily prevents interest. The more significant question is whether the underlying liability already existed or whether it arose only upon a subsequent event. 11. Scenario III: Liability Depended on Future Approval A materially different situation arises where a contractor may claim additional compensation for extraordinary cost increases, but the contract requires substantive approval before any entitlement arises. At the time of the original invoice, the contractor has no unconditional right to additional consideration. A claim is subsequently submitted and approved after examination. If approval is a genuine contractual condition precedent, the taxpayer may contend that the additional entitlement arose only when that condition was satisfied. However, if approval merely verifies an entitlement that already existed, the Revenue may argue that the liability arose earlier. The distinction depends on the contractual language and the legal effect of the approval process. A mere possibility of additional consideration should not automatically be equated with an existing enforceable liability. 12. Scenario IV: A Fresh Agreement Revises a Final Price Suppose goods are supplied for ₹10 lakh under a contract containing no price-escalation clause. The original transaction is completed at a final price. Several months later, the parties independently negotiate an additional payment of ₹2 lakh because of unforeseen commercial circumstances. The taxpayer may contend that the additional entitlement arose only under the subsequent agreement and that differential tax could not have been overdue before that event. However, the actual substance of the arrangement remains important. A later agreement may genuinely create a new obligation, or it may merely document a retrospective revision already contemplated between the parties. The tax treatment must therefore follow the contractual and factual character of the additional payment rather than the debit-note date alone. 13. Where Neither Liability Nor Quantum Was Established A particularly important situation arises where both the existence and amount of additional consideration depend upon future negotiations. For example, an infrastructure contractor may claim ₹50 lakh for unexpected site conditions. The recipient disputes the claim, and the parties eventually settle for ₹20 lakh. If the settlement creates an enforceable obligation for the first time, the taxpayer may argue that the additional consideration was not legally payable before settlement. This differs from a contractual escalation formula under which an existing liability is merely quantified later. Nevertheless, the settlement terms must be examined. If the agreement retrospectively revises the original price, the Revenue may contend that the enhanced value relates back to the earlier supply. The decisive factor is the legal nature of the entitlement, not simply the date of settlement or issuance of the debit note. 14. What Evidence Matters in Litigation? The original contract is often more important than the debit note itself. The relevant evidence includes the original pricing terms, escalation clauses, subsequent agreements, correspondence, approval requirements, cost calculations, settlement documents, invoices, debit notes, GST returns and payment records. Particular attention should be given to whether the original price was final or provisional, whether additional consideration was contractually predetermined, and whether a later approval merely verified an existing claim or created a fresh entitlement.The effective date of any retrospective revision is equally important. These documents help establish whether the debit note records an earlier liability or reflects an obligation that arose subsequently. They also assist in determining the correct period, if any, for calculation of interest. 15. Can the Mukand Judgment Apply to Every GST Debit Note? The Mukand Limited decision must be understood within its factual and statutory context. It concerned supplies made before GST, retrospective contractual price escalation and the transitional provisions of Section 142(2)(a). The Tribunal applied the reasoning of Steel Authority of India to uphold interest on differential tax arising from the retrospective revision. The decision therefore provides support to the Revenue where a subsequent price increase enhances the value attributable to an earlier supply under an existing contractual arrangement. However, it does not conclusively determine every situation involving debit notes issued for supplies made entirely under GST. Fresh contractual arrangements, contingent escalation claims, disputed settlements and additional consideration arising only upon satisfaction of substantive conditions require separate examination. The distinction does not guarantee exclusion of interest. It identifies the questions that must be resolved before an interest demand can be sustained. 16. Conclusion The liability to interest on debit notes cannot be determined merely by comparing the original invoice date with the date on which differential GST was paid. The first question is whether the additional consideration represents a retrospective revision of an existing contractual price or an entitlement that arose subsequently. Where the original agreement provides for retrospective escalation, the principles recognised in Steel Authority of India and applied by GSTAT Bengaluru in Mukand Limited may support an interest demand. However, where additional consideration depends upon future negotiations, substantive approval or a fresh contractual obligation, the taxpayer may have grounds to dispute interest for the period preceding the creation of that liability. The distinction between an existing liability quantified later and a liability created later is central to the analysis. Ultimately, Section 50 requires determination of when differential tax became legally due and whether it remained unpaid beyond that date. The mere issuance of a debit note cannot replace this statutory examination.

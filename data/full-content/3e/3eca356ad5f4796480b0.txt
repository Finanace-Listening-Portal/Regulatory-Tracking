Summary: The Gauhati High Court’s interim order inG.M. Coke Industries v. Union of India and 5 Others, WP(C)/6028/2026, dated 4 November 2026, highlights the statutory restriction against imposing multiple GST penalties on the same person for the same act or omission. The petitioner, an Assam-based coke manufacturer, challenged a demand-cum-show-cause notice dated 30 September 2026 alleging wrongful availment of input tax credit of ₹16,21,12,454 on invoices issued by 17 suppliers without actual receipt of goods. The notice also proposed penalties under Section 122. Relying on Section 75(13) of the CGST Act, 2017 and CBIC Circular No. 171/03/2022-GST dated 6 July 2022, the petitioner argued that an additional Section 122 penalty could not be imposed for the same fraudulent availment or utilisation of ITC already attracting penal action under Section 74. The Court considered the apparent conflict with the circular sufficiently serious to warrant examination, issued notice and restrained further action under the impugned show-cause notice until the next returnable date, 9 December 2026. Importantly, the Court neither finally quashed the demand nor decided whether the disputed ITC was admissible. The article examines the distinction between recovery of wrongful ITC and overlapping penalties, the different fake-invoice scenarios addressed by the circular, the statutory requirement of the same person and same act, the application of Section 74A from FY 2024–25 onwards, and practical steps for preparing a GST show-cause notice reply. It also identifies the need to verify the notice’s reference to Section 122(1)(viii), since wrongful ITC without receipt of goods is addressed in clause (vii). Table of Contents▸ 1. Coke Industries and Its Importance to Taxpayers A taxpayer receiving a GST show-cause notice should not examine only the amount of tax demanded. The penalty proposals deserve equal attention. A notice may refer to several provisions, but the mere availability of different penalty sections does not mean that every section can be applied cumulatively to the same act. This is particularly relevant in disputes concerning input tax credit allegedly taken without receipt of goods. The department may propose recovery of the disputed credit, interest and penalty under Section 74 and, in addition, invoke Section 122 on the same allegation. The question is not whether wrongful credit can be recovered. The question is whether the same person can be subjected to an additional penalty under another provision for the very act already attracting penalty under the demand provision. Section 75(13) and CBIC Circular No. 171/03/2022-GST dated 6 July 2022 address this issue. The interim order of the Gauhati High Court in M/s G.M. Coke Industries v. Union of India and 5 Others, WP(C)/6028/2026, dated 4 November 2026, brings its practical importance into focus. 2. Facts of the Case According to the order supplied, G.M. Coke Industries is a partnership firm engaged in the manufacture of coke classifiable under HSN 2704, with its principal place of business in Assam. The firm challenged a demand-cum-show-cause notice dated 30 September 2026 issued by the Joint Director, Directorate General of GST Intelligence, Guwahati Zonal Unit. The principal allegation was that the firm had availed ITC on invoices and corresponding e-way bills issued by 17 suppliers without actually receiving the goods. The disputed credit proposed to be recovered was ₹16,21,12,454. The order also records that the notice proposed penalties under Section 122(1)(viii) and Section 122(2)(b), together with the corresponding State GST provisions read with the IGST Act. These were allegations and proposals in the notice, not established findings of wrongful conduct. 3. What Was the Taxpayer’s Objection? The petitioner relied on CBIC Circular No. 171/03/2022-GST dated 6 July 2022. Its submission was that the circular permits demand and recovery, with applicable interest and penal action under Section 74, in the circumstances covered by the clarification. However, it does not permit another penalty under Section 122 for the same fraudulent availment or utilisation of ITC. The petitioner also relied on Commissioner of Customs v. Indian Oil Corporation Ltd., reported in (2004) 267 ITR 272 (SC), on the binding nature of Board circulars upon departmental authorities. The objection was therefore directed at the legality of the notice and its penalty proposals. It was not a contention that the circular legalises credit taken without receipt of goods. 4. What Did the High Court Actually Order? The High Court observed that the proposal to impose penalty under Section 122 conflicted with the circular referred to before it. The Court considered that the matter required examination and issued notice to the respondents. On the interim prayer, the Court directed: “Till the next returnable date, the respondents will not initiate any further action in terms of the show cause notice.” The interim prayer was made returnable on 9 December 2026. The operative result must be understood within these limits: Point Position under the supplied order Was the writ petition finally decided? No. The Court issued notice and sought the respondents’ response. Was the show-cause notice finally quashed? No. Was the disputed ITC held to be admissible? No. Was interim protection granted? Yes. Further action under the impugned notice was restrained until the next returnable date. Were the allegations against the taxpayer proved? The supplied order does not decide that question. These distinctions follow from paragraphs 15 to 19 of the order. It would therefore be incorrect to describe the case as a final ruling cancelling the entire demand or abolishing penalty in fake-invoice cases. 5. The Statutory Protection: Section 75(13) The protection against overlapping penalties comes from the Act itself. It is not merely a concession given through a circular. Section 75(13), in its amended form, provides: “Where any penalty is imposed under section 73 or section 74 or section 74A, no penalty for the same act or omission shall be imposed on the same person under any other provision of this Act.” Three elements require attention: A penalty is imposed under the relevant demand provision. The other penalty concerns the same act or omission. Both penalties are sought to be imposed on the same person. The section does not say that every other contravention committed by that person disappears. Nor does it automatically protect another person who may have independently committed an offence. Its purpose is specific: an act already penalised under the demand provision cannot be made the basis of another penalty against the same person under a different provision of the Act. For this reason, a reply should not merely say “double penalty is not permissible.” It should identify the precise duplication—who is being penalised, for which act, and under which provisions. 6. What the CBIC Circular Clarifies Circular No. 171/03/2022-GST was issued under Section 168(1) to clarify demand and penalty consequences in transactions involving fake invoices. The situation relevant here is the second illustration in the circular: “A” issues an invoice to “B” without an underlying supply. “B” takes ITC on that invoice without receiving goods or services. “B” subsequently makes an actual outward supply and uses that credit to discharge the output tax liability. The circular states that “B” is liable to demand and recovery of the credit, together with penal action under Section 74 and applicable interest under Section 50. It then expressly clarifies that, where penal action for that fraudulent availment or utilisation is taken under Section 74, no penalty for the same act can be imposed on “B” under another provision of the CGST Act, including Section 122. The distinction is therefore between: the penalty forming part of the proceedings under Section 74; and an additional penalty under Section 122 for the same fraudulent availment or utilisation. The circular restricts the second. It does not remove the first. 7. Why This Matters to Taxpayers An Overlapping Penalty Can Substantially Increase the Proposed Liability Consider a notice demanding recovery of credit and proposing a penalty equal to that credit under Section 74. If another equivalent penalty is added under Section 122 for precisely the same act, the taxpayer faces an additional financial exposure requiring independent statutory justification. Section 75(13) is therefore not a technical objection without monetary consequence. In a substantial ITC dispute, the duplicated penalty may itself be a large amount. Penalty Proposals Must Be Examined Separately from the Tax Demand A taxpayer may dispute receipt-related allegations on the strength of transport records, stock entries and manufacturing records. Independently, the taxpayer may also object that the proposed penalties overlap. These are different grounds. Failure to establish the factual defence on ITC does not, by itself, make an otherwise prohibited additional penalty lawful. The circular itself recognises recovery and Section 74 penal action while excluding another penalty for the same act. The Department Must Address Its Own Applicable Clarification The Gauhati High Court treated the alleged departure from the circular as a matter requiring judicial examination. This gives the order practical relevance where a notice proposes the very combination of penalties restricted by the circular. Nevertheless, the protection granted in that case belongs to that proceeding. Another taxpayer cannot treat the supplied order as an automatic stay of a separate notice. 8. Illustration: Recovery and Penalty Are Not the Same Question Assume that a manufacturer is alleged to have fraudulently availed and utilised ITC of ₹20 lakh without receiving goods. Assume also that the case concerns a period governed by Section 74 and that, after adjudication, the allegations are established. For illustration, the notice proposes: Component Proposed amount Recovery of wrongful ITC ₹20 lakh Interest As legally applicable Full penalty under Section 74 ₹20 lakh Additional penalty under Section 122 for the same availment and utilisation ₹20 lakh The objection concerns the last line. Where the Section 74 penalty is imposed on that manufacturer for the fraudulent availment or utilisation, Section 75(13), read with the circular, bars another penalty under Section 122 on the same manufacturer for the same act. The disputed credit, legally payable interest and applicable Section 74 penalty do not disappear merely because the additional penalty is unsustainable. This illustration assumes a full Section 74 penalty solely to explain the duplication. The actual liability must be determined with reference to the applicable statutory conditions and the facts of the particular proceeding. 9. The Circular Does Not Give the Same Result in Every Fake-Invoice Case It is unsafe to cite only the favourable portion of the circular without first identifying the transaction covered by it. The circular distinguishes between different situations: Transaction described in the circular Broad treatment explained by CBIC A person issues an invoice without any underlying supply Section 122(1)(ii) penalty may apply; the circular explains why demand under Sections 73/74 does not arise for that purported outward supply in the stated situation. A recipient takes fraudulent ITC and uses it to pay tax on actual outward supplies Recovery, applicable interest and Section 74 penal action; no additional penalty for the same act under Section 122. An intermediary takes credit without receipt and passes it onwards through invoices without actual outward supply The circular separately explains penalties under Sections 122(1)(ii) and 122(1)(vii), rather than applying the second illustration mechanically. The treatment depends on what the person actually did. Invoice issuance, wrongful credit, utilisation against genuine output liability and onward passing of credit are not interchangeable descriptions. 10. Illustration: The Supplier and Recipient Are Different Persons Suppose “A” issues an invoice without supplying goods. “B” takes credit on that invoice and uses it against tax on actual outward supplies. If “B” is penalised under Section 74 for its fraudulent availment or utilisation, “B” can invoke the restriction against an additional penalty for that same act. However, that does not automatically protect “A” from penalty for issuing the invoice without supply. “A” is a different person, and its conduct is separately dealt with in the circular. The statutory expression “same person” is therefore as important as “same act or omission.” 11. An Important Citation Point in the Supplied Order The supplied order repeatedly refers to Section 122(1)(viii). This deserves careful verification before preparing a reply or publishing a case note. Under the statutory text, Section 122(1)(vii) concerns taking or utilising ITC without actual receipt of goods or services, whereas Section 122(1)(viii) concerns fraudulently obtaining a refund. The reference in the order should not be silently changed. Equally, it should not be reproduced as though clause (viii) is the provision dealing with non-receipt-based ITC. The correct professional approach is to state what the order records and verify the original show-cause notice. Without that notice, it is not possible to determine whether the reference arose in the notice itself, in its reproduction, or from some additional allegation not fully set out in the interim order. A wrong clause also should not be assumed, without further examination, to invalidate the entire proceeding. The nature of the allegation and the resulting prejudice require separate consideration. 12. Section 74A: Check the Financial Year Before Applying the Analysis A current article on this issue must also recognise the change in the demand provisions. Sections 73 and 74 continue to govern the relevant demands for periods up to FY 2023–24. Section 74A governs determination for FY 2024–25 onwards. The amended Section 75(13) expressly includes Section 74A within its restriction against another penalty for the same act or omission on the same person. The practical consequence is straightforward: the provision applicable to a notice must be checked against the financial year involved, not merely the date on which the notice was issued. The 2022 circular explains the then-existing Sections 73 and 74 framework. For a Section 74A proceeding, the corresponding statutory protection should be placed directly on the amended Section 75(13), with the circular used carefully for its explanation of the transaction and the prohibition against duplication. The supplied G.M. Coke Industries order does not clearly identify the disputed tax periods. No conclusion about the correct period-specific demand provision should therefore be drawn merely from the notice being dated in 2026. 13. How the Issue Should Be Raised in a Reply A useful objection should connect the law to the actual penalty paragraphs of the notice. The taxpayer or representative should: Identify each proposed penalty and its statutory provision. Record the act or omission alleged under each provision. Check whether the proposals concern the same person. Explain whether the Section 122 proposal repeats the conduct already covered by the demand-linked penalty. Refer to Section 75(13) and the relevant illustration in Circular No. 171/03/2022-GST. Ask the authority to identify any separate conduct said to justify an independent penalty. This approach follows the statutory requirements and the transaction-specific explanation in the circular. It is stronger than a general submission that no penalty can be imposed under GST. A suitable paragraph, after adapting it to the facts, may read: Without prejudice to the noticee’s objections to the proposed demand, the additional penalty proposed under Section 122 is independently objected to. The notice relies upon the same alleged availment and utilisation of input tax credit to propose penalty under Section 74 and again under Section 122. No separate act or omission attributable to the noticee has been identified for the additional penalty. Section 75(13), read with Serial No. 2 of Circular No. 171/03/2022-GST dated 6 July 2022, prohibits another penalty on the same person for the same act where penalty is imposed under Section 74. The additional penalty proposal therefore requires to be dropped. This paragraph is appropriate only where the notice actually contains the stated overlap. A Section 74A case requires corresponding changes in the statutory references. 14. Do Not Allow the Penalty Objection to Replace the Defence on Facts Where the allegation is non-receipt of goods, the taxpayer’s factual records remain central. The penalty objection does not establish that the goods were received. As a matter of case preparation, the reply should bring together the available evidence of the transaction, such as: purchase orders and invoices; transport and delivery records; weighbridge slips and inward registers; stock entries; consumption and production records; payment records; and correspondence explaining shortages, returns or delivery discrepancies. The documents should be linked invoice-wise to the allegation rather than merely enclosed in bulk. The legal and factual submissions should remain separate: the taxpayer may deny wrongful credit altogether and, without prejudice, challenge the additional penalty. That distinction is consistent with the circular’s separate treatment of recovery and penal consequences. 15. What Should Not Be Claimed on the Strength of This Order? The following statements would overstate the supplied order: “The High Court has finally quashed the ₹16.21 crore demand.” “No penalty is possible where goods have not been received.” “Section 122 cannot apply in any fake-invoice transaction.” “Every taxpayer with a similar notice is protected from further proceedings.” The Court granted interim protection pending further examination. The circular preserves Section 74 penal action in the relevant situation and separately recognises other penalty consequences in other transaction patterns. It also notes that Section 132 may be invokable, subject to its conditions and the facts of the case. The professional value of this order lies in using it accurately, not in giving it a wider effect than its operative directions support. 16. Conclusion The G.M. Coke Industries interim order is important because it draws attention to a point that can be overlooked when taxpayers concentrate only on the principal ITC demand: every penalty proposal must have its own lawful basis. The department is entitled to examine wrongful ITC and proceed in accordance with law. But the existence of a disputed tax liability does not authorise overlapping penalties against the same person for the same act. Section 75(13) expressly restricts such duplication, and CBIC’s circular explains its application to the specified ITC transaction. For taxpayers, the practical lesson is to examine the notice provision by provision. Contest the allegation on evidence. Examine the demand under the provision applicable to the relevant financial year. Independently test every penalty against the restriction in Section 75(13). Finally, the Gauhati High Court’s order dated 4 November 2026 must presently be described for what the supplied document shows: an interim order restraining further action under the impugned notice until the next returnable date, not a final cancellation of the demand.

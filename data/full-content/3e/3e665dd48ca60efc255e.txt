(NCLAT Delhi) Summary : The National Company Law Appellate Tribunal, Principal Bench, New Delhi, dismissed five connected appeals challenging approval of the resolution plan for Mastana Foods Private Limited and rejection of objections to that plan. The appeals were filed by Amba Grains Private Limited and others, suspended director and personal guarantor Anil Khurana, and prospective resolution applicant, suspended director and personal guarantor Krishan Mohan Mendiratta. The Bench examined objections concerning an unsolicited higher bid after the e-challenge process, subsequent clarification of the successful applicant’s treatment of secured tax dues, eligibility verification under Section 29A of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, valuation of land and buildings, continuation of the resolution professional after disciplinary suspension, the consortium’s proposed implementation and shareholding structure, and a one-time settlement proposal. The corporate debtor entered CIRP on 18 September 2019 following State Bank of India’s Section 7 application. SBI constituted the Committee of Creditors with 100% voting share. Earlier invitations and a previously approved bid were followed by further proceedings concerning State tax claims. A fresh Form G was published on 7 December 2024, and eight applicants submitted plans in the ensuing process. After presentations and an initial round of improved financial proposals, the CoC conducted online e-bidding on 15 April 2025. The consortium of HR Commercials Private Limited and Crown Steels emerged as the highest bidder at approximately ₹131.91 crore; Amba’s final e-bid was approximately ₹91.91 crore, and Mendiratta’s was approximately ₹77.91 crore. SBI subsequently approved the successful consortium’s revised plan and addendum with its entire voting share. Amba incorporated its ₹91.91 crore e-bid in a plan submitted on 21 April 2025 but then unilaterally submitted another plan on 22 April 2025 increasing its offer to ₹133.91 crore. It argued that the enhanced offer deserved consideration, particularly because the successful applicant was subsequently permitted to revise its plan. The resolution professional, SBI and the successful applicant relied on the RFRP, the 8 April 2025 process communication and the applicants’ undertakings. These required revised plans to incorporate the final e-bidding amounts and prohibited unilateral deviation while reserving the CoC’s powers to negotiate. SBI also submitted that, despite Amba’s higher aggregate second offer, the successful applicant’s proposal provided more to secured financial creditors. Anil Khurana disputed the comparative NPV and maintained that lower recovery directly prejudiced him as personal guarantor. The Tribunal held that extension of the deadline for submitting revised plan documents to 22 April 2025 did not reopen the financial bidding process. Under the accepted process terms, an applicant could not substitute another amount for its final e-bid unless called upon by the CoC or resolution professional. Amba’s enhanced plan and correspondence had, in fact, been placed before the CoC, which consciously approved the successful consortium’s plan. The CoC could assess feasibility, viability and other plan provisions and was not bound to accept a proposal merely because it stated a higher amount. The RFRP also permitted negotiation with the highest e-bidder. The Tribunal treated Amba’s unilateral enhancement as unsolicited and rejected the contention that its non-selection established discrimination. The appellants separately challenged the additional provision for Haryana VAT/excise dues, alleging that the successful applicant’s original plan did not comply with State Tax Officer Vs Rainbow Papers Limited. The Tribunal found that clarification of the tax department’s treatment had been sought from all applicants. Further queries were sent only to applicants whose replies were vague. Against the department’s admitted claim of ₹12,05,07,027, the successful applicant had initially provided ₹4,06,10,868 and subsequently provided a further ₹3,32,27,872, taking that allocation to ₹7,38,38,740. The Tribunal accepted this compliance-driven clarification and addendum, finding that all applicants had the opportunity to clarify their treatment of the claim and that the successful plan was compliant when considered for approval. On presentation of allegedly non-compliant plans, the Tribunal considered Sections 30(2) and 30(3), Regulation 39 and the 26 May 2025 amendment to the CIRP Regulations, together with IBBI’s press release dated 30 May 2025. The amended framework required presentation of all received plans, including details of non-compliant plans, to the CoC. It was already in force when the CoC considered the plans in July 2025. The Tribunal distinguished presentation and scrutiny from approval of a compliant plan and found no violation in obtaining clarifications. It also rejected the Section 29A objection: the resolution professional submitted that the due diligence report confirming applicants’ eligibility had been shared with the CoC on 10 April 2025, before e-bidding. The Tribunal found no basis for Amba to use its stated uncertainty over eligibility to justify an enhanced bid after the auction. The suspended directors and guarantors challenged the valuation of approximately 34.30 acres of land at Kaithal, Haryana, alleging that agricultural circle rates had been used despite conversion to industrial use. They relied on a privately obtained valuation and the Enforcement Directorate’s earlier valuation. The Tribunal recognised that suspended directors who were also personal guarantors could challenge approval under Section 61, but rejected their valuation objections on the record. Two IBBI-registered valuers had assessed the property, considered its industrial use, agricultural planning zone, surrounding land, access and market conditions. Market indications of ₹1.80–₹2.20 crore per acre led to a base rate of ₹2 crore, adjusted to ₹1.40 crore per acre, and a land valuation of approximately ₹48.02 crore. The Tribunal found no glaring illegality, noted that the valuers had not differed significantly, and declined to reopen the accepted valuation merely on competing assertions. The Tribunal also upheld continuation of the resolution professional. The disciplinary suspension order dated 24 July 2025 itself gave the CoC liberty to decide whether he should continue. Given the advanced stage of CIRP, the CoC consciously retained him, and the Tribunal concurred with the adjudicating authority’s conclusion. On the proposed implementation structure, it interpreted the RFRP’s statement that the consortium “may” identify or incorporate a special purpose company as an option. Incorporation of such a company was therefore not mandatory. However, because HR Commercials and Crown Steels remained responsible for implementing the approved plan within the stipulated 270 days from its effective date, the Tribunal expressly directed the consortium to retain more than 50% shareholding in the corporate debtor until full implementation. The one-time settlement argument also failed. The Tribunal found no compulsion on SBI to accept the suspended director’s proposal and declined to issue such a direction after approval of the resolution plan. It noted the adjudicating authority’s findings on the proposal, its rejection and the asserted routing of funds. Any dispute over SBI’s retention of an upfront amount before CIRP could be raised before the appropriate forum. Reiterating that statutory review under Sections 30(2), 31, 32 and 61(3) does not permit the adjudicating or appellate authority to substitute its commercial assessment for that of the CoC, the Tribunal dismissed all five appeals, made no order as to costs and disposed of pending applications, subject to its direction requiring the consortium’s majority shareholding until implementation.

Chandrasekaran's current term is due to end in February 2027 and recent reports have said he has decided not to seek reappointment, adding another governance issue to the company's response to the RBI decision. Advt The immediate regulatory issue is clear. The RBI rejected Tata Sons' March 2024 application to voluntarily surrender its registration and asked the company to take necessary action for full compliance with the rules applicable to NBFC-Upper Layer entities. Tata Sons was classified in the upper layer in 2022 and its original three-year listing deadline expired in September 2025.The governance implications are more complicated because Tata Sons is controlled by Tata Trusts, which hold about 66% of its equity. The Shapoorji Pallonji Group holds 18.37%, while Tata group companies collectively hold about 13%. The Trusts' dominant ownership is accompanied by special rights under Tata Sons' Articles of Association over board appointments and certain strategic decisions.Those rights could become a key issue if Tata Sons moves towards a public listing. Articles 104B and 121 give Tata Trust nominees significant affirmative rights over specified corporate decisions. How those provisions would operate under the governance requirements applicable to a listed company would have to be examined as part of the listing process.The issue is particularly sensitive because there are differences among Trust representatives over whether Tata Sons should remain private or pursue a listing. Recent reports have said Noel Tata is opposed to a listing, while Venu Srinivasan supports one. Such differences could complicate shareholder and board-level decisions as Tata Sons works out its response to the RBI. Advt The impactThe succession question adds another layer. Chandrasekaran's decision not to seek reappointment comes as the Tata Sons board deals with the RBI's decision and differences among the Trusts. Recent reports have said the company's Nomination and Remuneration Committee may ask him to reconsider, making the September 17 board meeting important for both the leadership transition and the response to the RBI.The RBI rejection also leaves Tata Sons with fewer options to preserve its existing private-company structure. The company can seek clarity from the RBI on the compliance process and could explore legal remedies against the decision, although there has been no confirmed court challenge. The RBI's latest communication has not specified a fresh deadline for compliance with the listing requirement. Advt For the Shapoorji Pallonji Group, which owns 18.37% of Tata Sons, a listing could make the holding easier to value and potentially provide greater liquidity. Tata group companies that collectively own about 13% could similarly obtain a publicly quoted value for their stakes. These outcomes, however, would depend on the eventual structure of any listing.The regulatory backdrop has also strengthened the case for continued upper-layer classification. The RBI's revised June 2026 framework uses Rs 1 lakh crore in assets as one of the criteria for identifying NBFCs for the upper layer. Tata Sons had total assets of about Rs 2.01 lakh crore as of March 31, 2026, according to recent reports. Advt The RBI's decision therefore does more than revive the listing issue. It puts pressure on Tata Sons to reconcile a regulatory framework designed for an upper-layer NBFC with a governance structure built around concentrated Trust ownership and special shareholder rights, while the company simultaneously deals with a leadership transition.The September 17 board meeting is likely to offer the first indication of how Tata Sons intends to navigate these overlapping regulatory, governance and succession issues. If the RBI decision stands, the company's challenge will be to move towards compliance without creating a governance structure that conflicts with the rights and arrangements embedded in its existing Articles.

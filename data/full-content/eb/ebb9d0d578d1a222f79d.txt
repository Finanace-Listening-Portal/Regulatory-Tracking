Summary: Company incorporation and private placement are two essential processes under the Companies Act, 2013, enabling businesses to establish a separate legal identity and raise capital from selected investors. Company incorporation is governed primarily by Sections 3, 4, 7, 9 and 10A, along with the Companies (Incorporation) Rules, 2014. The process involves selecting the appropriate company structure, obtaining Digital Signature Certificates, reserving a company name, preparing the Memorandum and Articles of Association, and completing registration through the MCA’s SPICe+ system. Companies covered by Section 10A must also file the commencement-of-business declaration in Form INC-20A within 180 days of incorporation. Private placement, governed principally by Section 42 of the Companies Act, 2013, allows companies to issue securities to identified investors without making a public offer. Depending on the transaction, Section 62(1)(c) and the preferential allotment rules may also apply. The procedure includes obtaining necessary approvals, identifying eligible investors, issuing Form PAS-4, receiving subscription money through prescribed banking channels and maintaining statutory records. Securities must generally be allotted within 60 days of receiving application money, failing which the amount must be refunded within the following 15 days. Delayed refunds attract interest at 12% per annum from the expiry of the initial 60-day period. Importantly, Form PAS-3 must be filed within 15 days of allotment in a private placement under Section 42(8). The article explains the applicable procedures, documentation, statutory deadlines, differences between private placement and preferential allotment, and consequences of non-compliance, helping promoters, investors and professionals understand the regulatory requirements for company formation and fundraising in India. The business must be formed as a legal entity, meet the applicable statutory requirements and, where necessary, raise capital through a legally recognised method. In India, company incorporation establishes the company, while private placement provides a route for an existing company to raise funds by offering securities to selected investors. Both processes are principally governed by the Companies Act, 2013 and the rules made under it. Incorporation gives a company a legal identity separate from its members. Private placement is a method of raising funds through an offer of securities to identified persons rather than the public at large. Although the procedures are distinct, they are often connected in practice, especially for start-ups and growing businesses that seek investment after incorporation. For founders, investors and professionals dealing with company-law matters, it is important to understand the applicable statutory provisions, procedural steps, documents and time limits. This article explains the legal framework and key compliance requirements for incorporation and private placement in India. Company Incorporation in India Meaning and Legal Framework of Company Incorporation Incorporation is the formal legal process through which a company is registered and obtains an identity separate from its members. It is an important step in establishing a company to carry on business in India Section 7 of the Companies Act, 2013 requires prescribed documents, information and declarations to be filed with the Registrar of Companies (ROC) for incorporation. Once the application is accepted, the ROC issues a certificate of incorporation as evidence that the company has been legally formed. Section 9 of the Companies Act, 2013 explains the effect of registration. From the date of incorporation, the company becomes a body corporate with the ability to hold property, enter into contracts, and sue or be sued in its own name. The principal provisions relating to incorporation include Sections 3, 4, 7, 9, and 10A of the Companies Act, 2013, the Companies (Incorporation) Rules, 2014, and the relevant provisions of the Companies (Registration Offices and Fees) Rules, 2014. The Ministry of Corporate Affairs (MCA) provides an electronic portal for filing incorporation applications and supporting documents. Authorities: Companies Act, 2013, §§ 3–4, 7, 9–10A; Companies (Incorporation) Rules, 2014; Ministry of Corporate Affairs, “FAQs on Incorporation and Allied Matters” (SPICe+ process). Step-by-Step Procedure for Company Incorporation Step 1: Decide the type of company. Promoters should select the form of company that suits the proposed business and its ownership structure. Options include a private company, a public company and a One Person Company, subject to the requirements of the law. Each form has different requirements concerning membership, directors and share capital. Start-ups may choose a private company where they intend to raise funds from a selected group of investors. Step 2: Obtain Digital Signature Certificates (DSCs). Persons who are required to sign electronic incorporation documents must obtain Digital Signature Certificates through the prescribed process. These certificates are used to authenticate electronic filings on the MCA portal. The time taken depends on the documents submitted and the issuing agency’s process. Step 3: Apply for the company name. Section 4 of the Companies Act, 2013 and the Companies (Incorporation) Rules, 2014 govern the proposed company name. The name must comply with the legal requirements and should not be prohibited or impermissibly similar to an existing company name or trademark. The application is generally made through the MCA’s online process, including SPICe+ Part A where applicable. Step 4: Prepare the constitutional documents. The promoters must prepare the Memorandum of Association (MoA) and Articles of Association (AoA). The MoA sets out essential details about the company, including its name, objects and capital structure, as applicable. The AoA contains rules for the company’s internal management, including matters relating to shares, directors and meetings. These documents establish the company’s legal and operational framework. Step 5: File the incorporation application. The application is filed electronically through the MCA’s integrated SPICe+ process. Depending on the company’s circumstances, the filing may include SPICe+ Part B, e-MoA, e-AoA, AGILE-PRO-S and other applicable forms, documents and declarations. The application contains details of subscribers, proposed directors, the registered office and share capital, where relevant. Applications for Director Identification Numbers (DINs) may also be made through the integrated process where permitted. Step 6: Verification by the Registrar of Companies. The ROC examines the application and supporting documents. If the requirements are satisfied, the ROC proceeds with incorporation. If there are omissions, inconsistencies or other deficiencies, the applicant may be asked to provide further information or resubmit the documents. Promoters should ensure that identity proofs, the MoA and AoA, shareholding details, subscriber particulars and director details are accurate and consistent throughout the filing. Step 7: Issue of the certificate of incorporation. Once the application is approved, the ROC issues the certificate of incorporation and assigns the company a Corporate Identity Number (CIN). PAN and TAN applications are processed through the integrated system in accordance with the applicable procedure. Incorporation is not the end of the compliance process; the company must also complete any applicable post-incorporation filings and formalities. Documents Required for Company Incorporation The documents required depend on the type of company and the identity and address of its subscribers, directors and proposed registered office. The documents and information commonly required include the following: Identity and address proof of subscribers and proposed directors, such as PAN or passport, where applicable. Address proof of subscribers and directors, where required. Digital Signature Certificates of the persons signing the electronic forms. Memorandum of Association and Articles of Association in the applicable form or e-form. Details of directors, DINs where available, shareholding particulars and the required declarations. Proof of the proposed registered office, such as a recent utility bill and prescribed proof of ownership or tenancy, together with the property owner’s no-objection certificate where applicable. Prescribed forms, declarations, subscriber details and other information required under Section 7 and the applicable rules. Documents relevant to corporate or foreign subscribers and directors, including authorisation and authenticated or apostilled documents where necessary. This list is indicative and not every item is mandatory in every case. Promoters should consult the current MCA instructions and applicable statutory requirements for the particular application before filing. Authorities: Companies Act, 2013, § 7; Companies (Incorporation) Rules, 2014; Ministry of Corporate Affairs, SPICe+ incorporation FAQs. Timeline and Post-Incorporation Compliance The Companies Act, 2013 does not prescribe one fixed period for completing every incorporation application. The timeline depends on factors such as name availability, completeness of documents, payment of the required fees and stamp duty, and any queries raised by the ROC. As a general estimate, the process may take several working days or extend to a few weeks. Promoters must also consider Section 10A of the Companies Act, 2013. A company having share capital and incorporated after the commencement of the Companies (Amendment) Act, 2019 must file a declaration in Form INC-20A within 180 days of incorporation, confirming that every subscriber has paid the value of the shares agreed to be taken. Until this declaration is filed with the ROC, the company cannot commence business or exercise borrowing powers. Authorities: Companies Act, 2013, § 10A; Companies (Incorporation) Rules, 2014; Ministry of Corporate Affairs, Form INC-20A guidance. Private Placement of Shares in India Meaning and Legal Framework Under Section 42 Private placement is a method of raising funds in which a company offers its securities to identified persons without inviting the public to subscribe. It is commonly used by start-ups, closely held companies and businesses seeking funds from a selected group of investors. Section 42 of the Companies Act, 2013 is the principal provision governing private placement, supplemented by the Companies (Prospectus and Allotment of Securities) Rules, 2014. Where the transaction involves a preferential allotment of securities, Section 62(1)(c) and Rule 13 of the Companies (Share Capital and Debentures) Rules, 2014 may also apply. The company’s constitutional documents and other applicable requirements should also be considered. Private placement is a formal legal process. Depending on the issue, it may require Board and shareholder approvals, an offer to identified persons, prescribed documents, compliance with banking requirements, allotment within the statutory period and filings with the ROC. Step-by-Step Procedure for Private Placement Step 1: Determine the issue and identify the proposed investors. The company should decide the class and number of securities, the issue price, the amount to be raised and the purpose for which the funds will be used. It must identify the persons to whom the offer will be made and comply with the statutory limits. Under the applicable rules, the general limit is 200 persons in aggregate in a financial year for each kind of security, subject to categories excluded by law, such as eligible institutional buyers and qualified employee stock option offerees. Step 2: Obtain Board approval. The Board should consider the proposed issue, including its terms, class and number of securities, and the identified persons to whom the offer is to be made, where applicable. The issue must be authorised under the company’s constitutional documents and satisfy the relevant capital and procedural requirements. The Board’s resolutions should be properly recorded and applicable filing requirements fulfilled at the appropriate stage. Authorities: Companies Act, 2013, §§ 42, 62(1)(c); Companies (Prospectus and Allotment of Securities) Rules, 2014, r. 14; Companies (Share Capital and Debentures) Rules, 2014, r. 13. Step 3: Obtain shareholders’ approval where required. Section 42 and Rule 14 of the Companies (Prospectus and Allotment of Securities) Rules, 2014 set out relevant approval requirements. Where a special resolution is required, it must be passed at a duly convened general meeting. The explanatory statement should disclose the prescribed details, including the type of securities, issue price and its justification, valuation where applicable, amount to be raised, objects of the issue and other material terms. Specific rules or exemptions may apply to certain issues, including some non-convertible debentures. Where the transaction is a preferential issue of shares, Section 62(1)(c) and Rule 13 of the Companies (Share Capital and Debentures) Rules, 2014 should also be considered. Step 4: Prepare and send the private placement offer. The company must prepare Form PAS-4, the prescribed private placement offer-cum-application letter, and address it to the identified persons. The offer must not amount to a public offer, and the application is intended for the person to whom it is addressed. The company must follow the prescribed method of sending the offer and maintain the required records. The applicable rules and current MCA instructions should be checked for the precise requirements and timing. Step 5: Receive subscription money through the prescribed banking channel. The applicant must remit the subscription amount from their own bank account, subject to any applicable exception. The company should maintain proper records of the money received. Cash payments are not permitted except where a specific legal exception applies. The money must be kept in the separate bank account required under Section 42 and the applicable rules until it can be used in accordance with the statutory conditions. Companies Act, 2013, § 42 (India); Companies (Prospectus and Allotment of Securities) Rules, 2014. Step 6: Allot securities within the statutory period. Section 42(6) requires the company to allot the securities within 60 days from the date of receipt of the application money. If the securities are not allotted within that period, the company must refund the money within 15 days after the expiry of the 60-day period. If the company fails to refund the amount within those 15 days, interest at 12% per annum becomes payable from the expiry of the 60-day period, in accordance with the Act. Step 7: File the return of allotment. The company must file Form PAS-3 with the ROC within 15 days of allotment, along with the prescribed particulars of allottees and other required documents. The company should also update its statutory registers and accounting records and arrange for share certificates or dematerialised records, as applicable. Failure to follow the prescribed allotment and filing procedure may result in legal and compliance consequences. Documents Required for Private Placement The documents commonly required for a private placement of shares include the following: The company’s MoA and AoA, showing authority for the proposed issue. Board resolution approving the proposed offer, its terms and the identified persons, as applicable. Notice of the general meeting and explanatory statement for shareholder approval, where required. Special resolution and evidence of the relevant filing, where required. Valuation report, where required under the applicable provisions. Form PAS-4 containing the prescribed offer-cum-application details and addressed to the specific recipient. Form PAS-5 or the applicable record of private placement, as required under the rules in force. Investors’ application forms and relevant identity, address and subscription details. Bank records showing receipt of subscription money and compliance with the applicable banking requirements. Form PAS-3 with the prescribed list of allottees and supporting documents. Updated register of members, share certificates or applicable dematerialized records, and other statutory records. Additional documents may be required depending on whether the consideration is paid in cash, whether the issue is preferential, and whether sector-specific or securities-law requirements apply. Statutory Timelines for Allotment, Refund and ROC Filing The main statutory time limits to consider in a private placement are as follows: Authorities: Companies Act, 2013, § 42(6), (8); Companies (Prospectus and Allotment of Securities) Rules, 2014, rr. 12, 14. Approvals: The necessary Board and shareholder approvals must be obtained at the appropriate stage, where required. Allotment: The securities must be allotted within 60 days from the date of receipt of the application money. Refund and interest: If allotment is not completed within 60 days, the company must refund the money within the following 15 days. If it fails to refund within that period, interest at 12% per annum is payable from the expiry of the 60-day period. Return of allotment: Form PAS-3 must be filed with the ROC within 15 days of allotment in a private placement. The relevant statutory text, rules and current MCA instructions should be consulted when calculating deadlines. Each period must be calculated from the event specified by law, such as receipt of application money or allotment, rather than from the date the company first considered raising funds. Authorities: Companies Act, 2013, § 42(6), (8); Companies (Prospectus and Allotment of Securities) Rules, 2014, rr. 12, 14. Compliance Issues and Practical Considerations Common Compliance Issues During Incorporation Incorporation and private placement are document-driven legal processes. In incorporation, inconsistencies between forms, identity proofs, addresses, subscriber details, shareholding information and constitutional documents may lead to queries or resubmission. Reviewing all documents carefully before filing can help reduce delays. Private Placement Restrictions and Approval Requirements In private placement, compliance problems may arise when a company approaches investors without following the required approval process, restrictions on eligible offerees, offer-document requirements or statutory limits. The company should identify the intended investors and ensure that the prescribed procedure is followed before proceeding with the offer. Difference Between Private Placement and Preferential Allotment A further consideration is the distinction between private placement and preferential allotment. These concepts are related but are not identical in every situation. Depending on the structure of the transaction, a company may need to comply with Section 42 as well as Section 62(1)(c) and the relevant rules. The applicable requirements should be assessed before the issue is undertaken. Consequences of Non-Compliance Under the Companies Act, 2013 Failure to comply with the statutory requirements may lead to penalties or other legal consequences for the company and the persons responsible. Depending on the breach, the company may also be required to refund the money raised with applicable interest. Errors in incorporation filings can delay registration or create further compliance difficulties. Compliance should not be treated as a formality to be completed only after a business decision has been made. It should be considered during planning, because errors, omissions, and missed deadlines can affect both the formation of the company and its fundraising process. Conclusion Company incorporation and private placement are two distinct but connected aspects of establishing and financing a business in India. Incorporation creates a separate legal entity, while private placement enables a company to raise funds from selected investors through a regulated process. Both require careful preparation of documents, compliance with statutory provisions, appropriate approvals and attention to prescribed timelines. A sound understanding of the Companies Act, 2013, the applicable rules, required documents and statutory deadlines can help promoters and investors avoid unnecessary delays and compliance problems. The precise requirements should be checked against the current law and MCA instructions before filing an application or undertaking an issue of securities. Treating legal compliance as part of the planning process helps establish a proper foundation for the company and its future fundraising.

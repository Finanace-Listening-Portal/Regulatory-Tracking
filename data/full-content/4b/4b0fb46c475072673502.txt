Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); Quantum Petro Chem India Vs Commissioner of Customs (CESTAT Ahmedabad) Summary :The Customs, Excise and Service Tax Appellate Tribunal (CESTAT), Ahmedabad, in Quantum Petro Chem India Vs Commissioner of Customs, Kandla, held that the Customs Department was not justified in insisting upon a bank guarantee equivalent to 10% of the assessable value of imported Distillate Marine Oil when laboratory reports did not conclusively establish that the goods were restricted High Flash High Speed Diesel (HFHSD). The appellant had imported 2,165.28 MT of Distillate Marine Oil through Kandla Port under four Bills of Entry dated 6 September 2025, declaring the goods under Customs Tariff Item 2710 19 61 for industrial use. The Department suspected misclassification under Customs Tariff Item 2710 19 49 and seized the goods under Section 110 of the Customs Act, 1962. Subsequently, the Commissioner permitted provisional release under Section 110A subject to several conditions, including execution of a bond covering 100% of the assessable value, furnishing end-use undertakings and providing a 10% bank guarantee amounting to ₹95,53,000. The importer furnished the guarantee under protest but challenged its continuation as arbitrary and disproportionate. The dispute principally arose from the CRCL, Visakhapatnam test report dated 24 September 2025, which described the imported petroleum product as closely resembling HFHSD under IS 16861. However, the sample had a flash point of 50°C against the minimum requirement of 66°C for HFHSD, while several prescribed parameters had not been tested. Subsequent clarifications from CRCL, New Delhi indicated that HFHSD, automotive diesel, gas oil and distillate marine fuel belong to the broader category of diesel fractions. The laboratory could not establish deliberate blending with lower hydrocarbon fractions. It also clarified that cloud point, relied upon by the Department to question compliance with IS 16731, depends upon the intended use, climatic conditions and voyage requirements. The Commissioner himself had recorded that the tested parameters were more consistent with DMX-type Distillate Marine Fuel than HFHSD and that the balance of convenience favoured the importer. The appellant relied upon Gaurav Electronics Vs Additional Commissioner of Customs, Gastrade International Vs Commissioner of Customs, Kandla, Aparajita Energy Private Limited Vs Union of India, Noya Infrastructure LLP Vs Union of India, and Prajakt Agro Industries Pvt. Ltd. Vs Commissioner of Customs, Kandla to contend that incomplete laboratory testing and inconclusive classification findings could not justify onerous provisional-release conditions. The Tribunal particularly considered the Gujarat High Court decisions concerning similar distillate oil imports and its earlier decision in Prajakt Agro Industries Pvt. Ltd., where the bank guarantee condition had been dispensed with. It found that the insistence on a bank guarantee in the present case was inconsistent with those decisions. The Tribunal accordingly set aside the bank guarantee requirement and directed its return upon compliance with the bond and end-use certificate requirements. It recorded that end-use certificates were already available as Exhibit-J at pages 86–99 of the appeal record and directed the importer to furnish any outstanding certificates. The decision concerns the conditions for provisional release and does not constitute a final adjudication of tariff classification or confiscation. Quantum Petro Chem India (hereinafter referred to as “the Appellant”), prefers the present appeal challenging the Provisional Release Order bearing File No. CUS/SIIB/INT/751/2025-SIIB-O/o Commr-Cus-Kandla dated 09.11.2025 passed by the Respondent (hereinafter referred to as “the impugned order”), under Section 110A read with Section 110 of the Customs Act, 1962 (hereinafter referred to as “the Act”) whereby the Respondent has provisionally released the goods imported by the Appellant on certain terms and conditions including execution of bond of 100% of assessable value, Bank Guarantee of 10% of the assessable value and furnishing of end use bond and undertaking. The present challenge is limited to the condition requiring the Appellant to furnish a Bank Guarantee equivalent to 10% of the assessable value of the seized goods, and consequentially to the continuance of Bank Guarantee No. 0155NDDG00022226 dated 15.11.2025 for INR 95,53,000/- furnished under protest. The Appellant also seeks direction for provisional release of goods at the earliest even though the Appellant has fulfilled all the conditions mentioned in the impugned order. 2. RR Global FZC, UAE, and were laden from Sohar, Oman. The total quantity imported under the disputed BOEs was 2165.28 MT. The goods were declared under Customs Tariff Item 2710 19 61 as Distillate Marine Oil, and the Appellant declared the goods for industrial use. The details of the Bills of Entry and quantities are as under: Sr. No. Bill of Entry No. Date Quantity of Goods Descriptio n Declared Classification Declared 1. 4347583 06.09.2025 540 MT Distillate Marine Oil 2710 19 61 2. 4347689 06.09.2025 540 MT Distillate Marine Oil 2710 19 61 3. 4347687 06.09.2025 545.28 MT Distillate Marine Oil 2710 19 61 4. 4347586 06.09.2025 540 MT Distillate Marine Oil 2710 19 61 2.2 A Letter bearing no. F. The Department alleged that the imported goods, though declared as Distillate Marine Oil under Customs Tariff Item 2710 19 61, were allegedly closely resembling or most akin to High Flash High Speed Diesel (“HFHSD”) under IS 16861. The report described the sample as a pale-yellow coloured, free-flowing, clear and bright oily liquid, mainly composed of petroleum hydrocarbons, having mineral oil content more than 70% by weight. The report recorded the following broad characteristics: Sr. No. Parameter Result 1 Acid Number Nil 2 Ash Content Nil 3 Flash Point (PMCC)° C 50°C 4 Density at 15°C 0.8224 g/ml 5 Kinematic Viscosity at 40°C 2.25 cSt 6 Sulphur Content 0.15% by wt. 7 Distillation- IBP, °C 159.1°C Distillation -50% distilled at °C 253.1°C 8 % Recovery at 350°C More than 85% by volume, including losses 9 % Recovery at 370°C More than 95% by volume, including losses 10 Cloud Point, °C -11°C 3.2 The CRCL, Visakhapatnam report further recorded that the sample was other than light oils and preparations, other than solvents, other than kerosene, other than vacuum gas oil, other than gas oil, other than light diesel oil, other than automotive diesel fuel not containing bio-diesel, other than diesel fuel blend / bio-diesel, other than aviation gasoline, other than motor spirit / gasoline, other than automotive diesel fuel containing bio-diesel, other than transformer oil, and other than waste / used / hazardous oil. However, while dealing with fuel class and IS 16731 compliances which is for Distilled Marine Oil, the report stated that the sample did not comply with IS 16731 in respect of cloud point. 3.3 The said report also contained a specific comment that the sample confirms the stringent parameters of HFHSD under IS 16861 in respect of the parameters tested, except flash point, which was found to be 50°C, i.e. 16°C lower than the minimum limit of 66°C. The report further suggested that this may be due to blending / mixing of petroleum fraction below 170°C and above 150°C and that the instance of mixing / blending of lower fraction may be examined by the Customs authorities. The report recorded that the sample closely resembles or is most akin to HFHSD under IS 16861. 3.4 The Appellant submits that the Department’s entire action of seizure and imposition of onerous provisional release conditions rests substantially on the aforesaid report and, in particular, on two aspects: first, the purported cloud point deviation from IS 16731, and secondly, the observation that the sample has a carbon chain similar to diesel fraction and allegedly closely resembles or is most akin to HFHSD. 4. REPRESENTATION BY THE APPELLANT 4.1 Upon being informed of the CRCL, Visakhapatnam report, the Appellant submitted a representation dated 30.09.2025 disputing the conclusions drawn in the report and seeking appropriate clarification / referral to the competent laboratory authority. The Appellant categorically stated that the goods imported were Distillate Marine Oil / Distillate Oil and not HFHSD. The Appellant also pointed out that the report itself did not establish that the goods were HFHSD, since the flash point was 50°C, which is below the minimum flash point of 66°C required for HFHSD under IS 16861. The Appellant further submitted that the standard applicable to HFHSD could not be applied to the goods declared as Distillate Marine Oil. 4.2 The Appellant also submitted that cloud point is not a determinative classification parameter in the manner assumed by the Department. The Appellant specifically pointed out that cloud point depends upon the buyer, the end-use, the intended voyage, and the climatic conditions in which the product is proposed to be used. The Appellant also submitted that the goods were meant for industrial use and that the cloud point parameter could not, by itself, render the goods restricted HFHSD or justify seizure or harsh provisional release conditions. 5. No show cause notice adjudicating classification, confiscation, penalty or fine had been decided at the stage of seizure or at the stage of the impugned provisional release order. The Appellant craves leave to refer to and rely upon the seizure memo at the time of hearing which has been incorporated in the impugned provisional order. 6. CLARIFICATION SOUGHT FROM CRCL NEW DELHI 6.1 Further, the Department sought clarification from CRCL, New Delhi. It issued clarification dated 08.10.2025 and stated as under: HFHSD, Automotive Diesel Fuel, Gas Oil, Distillate Oils, etc. are all middle distillates obtained by refining crude oil through fractional distillation with boiling range typically between 160°C and 370°C, and that after initial distillation, the middle distillate fraction may undergo further treatment such as hydrodesulphurization and additive treatment to meet specific quality standards; Based on the CRCL, Visakhapatnam test reports, the products under reference may be considered as diesel fraction. That it was not possible to ascertain whether the product was deliberately mixed with lower fraction of hydrocarbons. CRCL, New Delhi also clarified that the end-use of the product may be ascertained by the Customs authorities. Further, in relation to the intended use of Distillate Oil as per IS 16731, CRCL, New Delhi stated that IS 16731 is intended for marine diesel engines and that for Indian industries, fuel oil required for diesel generating sets in captive power plants is governed by a separate specification. The clarification thus did not conclude that the imported goods were HFHSD. At the highest, it described the product as diesel fraction, a broad middle-distillate category within which several petroleum products fall. 6.2 In the meanwhile, the Appellant requested for provisional release of the imported goods vide various emails dated 29.10.2025, 14.10.2025, 13.10.2025. The Department again sought clarification from CRCL, New Delhi in relation to the applicability and significance of cloud point. CRCL, New Delhi issued clarification dated 28.10.2025. The said clarification referred to IS 16731 and stated that the standard specifies requirements for fuels for use in marine diesel engines and boilers prior to conventional onboard treatment, and that the specifications can also be applied to fuels used in stationary diesel engines of the same or similar type as those used for marine purposes. Most importantly, CRCL, New Delhi clarified that the purchaser should ensure that cold-flow characteristics, including pour point, cloud point and cold filter plugging point, are suitable for the ship’s design and intended voyage. CRCL, New Delhi further referred to the CIMAC Guideline on Cold Flow Properties of Marine Fuel Oils, and clarified that if the intended use of the sample is as marine fuel in ships operating in colder weather conditions, cloud point becomes significant; for other usage, cloud point is not a significant parameter. It was further stated that the end-use of the sample may be ascertained by the Customs authorities. 6.3 In a similar matter involving M/s. Prajakt Agro Industries Pvt. Ltd., the Customs authorities sought further clarification from CRCL, New Delhi on the “most akin” aspect in light of the judgment of the Hon’ble Supreme Court in Gastrade International. CRCL, New Delhi issued clarification dated 04.11.2025 wherein it was clarified that the term “diesel fraction” is a middle distillate oil which includes HFHSD under IS 16861, Automotive Diesel Fuel under IS 1460, Gas Oil under IS 17789, Distillate Marine Fuel under IS 16731, and Light Diesel Oil under IS 15770. It further clarified that whenever a sample does not meet the requirement of any of the aforesaid products under the respective Indian Standards, it may be because of deviation from one or more parameters. However, such sample can still be categorised as diesel fraction based on carbon chain, distillation range and other parameters. CRCL, New Delhi expressly stated that whether the sample is more akin to HFHSD under IS 16861, Distillate Marine Fuel under IS 16731, or any other distillate oil, is of no consequence because all these products are diesel fraction. The Appellant craves leave to refer to and rely upon the clarification issued by CRCL, New Delhi at the time of hearing which has been incorporated in the impugned provisional order. 7. In terms of parameters which were not conducted, it is pertinent to note that carbon residue, copper strip corrosion, cetane index, pour point, oxidation stability and lubricity are important parameters to ascertain classification of goods. Carbon residue (ISO 10370, micro method) controls coking tendency and “heavy ends”, Since HFHSD is used in compression-ignition engines that may run for long hours, excess “heavy ends” or trace residua accelerate nozzle fouling, sticking rings, and after treatment fouling. The standard therefore both limits carbon residue to 0.3 % m/m and separately says “This fuel shall not contain any residuum oil.” Copper strip corrosion (ISO 2160) guards against reactive sulfur/acid attack. Diesel handling systems use copper- and brass-containing parts (pumps, injectors, heat-exchanger tubes, gauge elements). Fuels with reactive sulfur species, organic acids or peroxides corrode these metals, leading to leaks, stuck precision parts, and costly downtime at sea. IS0 2160 is the industry screen for that risk and without passing it, a fuel cannot be relied upon in marine service even if other properties look fine. Cetane index (S0 4264) assures ignition quality and stable combustion. HFHSD must combine a high flash point with reliable ignition in CI engines. Low cetane index fuels cause hard starting, rough running, increased white smoke, and higher NOx/HC — particularly in cold starts. Testing and meeting 1SO 4264 > 45 is therefore part of the identity of HFHSD. Pour point (ISO 3016) ensures the fuel will flow and be pump able in service. IS 16861 sets 3 ◦C (winter) and 15 ◦C (summer) maxima. HFHSD is widely stored and used in vehicles across climates. The Hon’ble Supreme Court held that where expert material does not cover all relevant parameters and the opinion is not definitive, conclusions averse to the importer cannot be sustained. The principle squarely applies here. After examining the issue in detail and after noting that all the parameters prescribed under IS 1460:2005 are not tested and non‑conformity of flash point and distillation range, the Hon’ble Supreme Court held as under:- “However, this analysis and conclusions arrived at by the High Court are problematic for the following reasons: i. There was no expert opinion at all that the samples which were tested were indeed of HSD. ii. The opinion as contained in the test results was merely mentioning about conformity of the samples with certain specifications of IS 1460:2005 and not about conformity with all the specifications. iii. Once the rule making authority had clearly delineated the requisite parameters for ascertaining the nature of the goods/substance, compliance/conformity with the stated parameters would be the requirement. iv. There are 21 parameters laid down under IS 1460:2005 and none of the tests have shown compliance with all these parameters. The last and third test have reported compliance with 14 parameters, though as discussed above in respect of 2 of the aforesaid 14 parameters, namely, flash point and distillation range, the same are not in conformity. Thus, it cannot be said there is substantial compliance with the parameters of IS 1460:2005. v. Flash point, though may not be the most important parameter, yet, its importance in determining the nature of the Automotive oil cannot be ignored. Flash point being a very important criterion to classify petroleum products, non­compliance of the samples on this parameter would make the classification doubtful. We are of the view that non-examination of any product/article/goods on all the parameters laid down by the customs authority will always lead to uncertainty and doubt, which are required to be removed when dealing with confiscatory proceedings. The genesis of the prolonged litigation lies in the non-availability of adequate facilities for testing all the parameters provided under Bureau of Indian Standard Specifications. Such a dispute could have been avoided had the testing facilities for all the parameters been available. Since the Authorities themselves had laid down the specific parameters for classification of goods, as in the present case by referring to classification under IS 1460:2005, it is incumbent upon the Authorities to ensure that necessary facilities are made available for testing of any disputed article on all these parameters as otherwise, laying down such parameters would be meaningless.” 13. The Department seeks to rely upon a laboratory report which does not conclusively state that the goods are HFHSD, does not establish conformity with all HFHSD parameters, and itself records non-conformity on flash point. Such a report cannot justify an onerous Bank Guarantee condition. 13.1 The principle laid down in Gastrade International (supra) is particularly significant in petroleum classification disputes because products falling under Chapter 27 often share overlapping parameters. Where the applicable standard prescribes specific parameters, the Department cannot rely upon selective conformity with some parameters while disregarding non­conformity with others. The “most akin” test must be applied on the basis of proper scientific evidence and not by conjecture, suspicion or partial resemblance. In that matter also, the Department relied upon test reports that allegedly showed conformity with some parameters of a different petroleum product, while the reports were incomplete and did not test all essential parameters. Herein also the Department has treated an inconclusive test report as sufficient to impose adverse consequences, although the report itself is qualified, incomplete and contradicted by subsequent CRCL, New Delhi clarifications. 13.2 The Respondent has therefore erred in imposing the Bank Guarantee condition notwithstanding absence of conclusive expert evidence. If the Department, with access to CRCL and other Government laboratories, has not obtained a complete and definitive report establishing that the goods are HFHSD, the importer cannot be burdened with a harsh financial condition merely because the goods share certain middle-distillate characteristics with other petroleum products. THE PRESENT CASE IS SQUARELY COVERED BY THE IDENTICAL DECISION OF THE HON’BLE CESTAT, AHMEDABAD IN PRAJAKT AGRO INDUSTRIES PVT. LTD. 14. This Hon’ble CESTAT, Ahmedabad in Prajakt Agro Industries Pvt. Ltd. v. Commissioner of Customs, Kandla in Customs Appeal 11585 of 2025 vide Final Order No. 10024/2026 dated 19.01.2026, dealt with an identical provisional release dispute. In that case also, the importer had imported Distillate Marine Oil / Distillate Oil, the Department alleged that the goods were diesel / HFHSD, CRCL, Visakhapatnam test reports were relied upon while CRCL, New Delhi clarified that the goods were diesel fraction and that whether they were more akin to HFHSD or Distillate Marine Fuel was of no consequence. The Respondent in the present case imposed provisional release conditions including a Bank Guarantee for 10% of assessable value. 14.1 This Hon’ble Tribunal in Prajakt Agro (supra) held that the issue was whether the conditions imposed by the Commissioner in the provisional release order were proper and sustainable or whether they were inconsistent with the judgments of the Hon’ble Gujarat High Court in similar matters. The Tribunal noted that the Commissioner himself had held that the samples were not identical to HFHSD for the limited purpose of provisional release and that, for tested parameters, the samples matched fully with DMX-type Distillate Marine Fuel and only partially with HFHSD. The Tribunal further noted that the balance of convenience lay in favour of the importer. 14.3 The Hon’ble Tribunal in Prajakt Agro (supra) specifically held that the conditions imposed for provisional release were not sustainable, that the Bank Guarantees furnished by the importer were liable to be discharged, and that other conditions of provisional release were liable to be modified as per the decision of the Hon’ble Gujarat High Court in Noya Infrastructure. The said decision is binding and squarely applicable to the present case. This Hon’ble Tribunal in Prajakt Agro (supra) vide Final Order No. 10024/2026 dated 19.01.2026 held as under: “4.1 It is pertinent to mention here that Ld. Commissioner vide impugned order has not agreed with the allegations of investigating agencies and he has observed that the finding of CRCL Visakhapatnam that samples failed to meet IS 16731 requirements on cloud point and water content for DMX grade are not sustainable. He further held that for the tested parameters, both samples match fully with DMX-type Distillate Marine Fuel (IS 16731) under Customs TI 2710 19 61, and only partially with HFHSD (IS 16861) under Customs TI 2710 19 49 and therefore, the balance of convenience lies in favour of the importer. Ld. Commissioner of customs also held that the sample of the subject goods is “most akin” to the DMX-type Distillate Marine Fuel (IS 16731) on the parameters tested by CRCL, Visakhapatnam.” 15. The present Appellant stands on an even stronger footing because the impugned order in the present case contains findings materially identical to those considered in Prajakt Agro. The Respondent has recorded that the goods are not identical to HFHSD, that CRCL, New Delhi did not endorse “most akin to HFHSD”, that deliberate mixing could not be ascertained, that cloud point is not determinative, and that the tested parameters favour DMX-type Distillate Marine Fuel over HFHSD. Therefore, on parity with Prajakt Agro, the Bank Guarantee condition must be set aside and the Bank Guarantee furnished by the Appellant must be discharged. THE IMPUGNED CONDITION IS CONTRARY TO THE JUDGMENT OF THE HON’BLE GUJARAT HIGH COURT IN NOYA INFRASTRUCTURE LLP 16. The Hon’ble Gujarat High Court in Noya Infrastructure LLP v. Union of India reported in 2025 (12) TMI 1026 – Gujarat High Court considered the same class of goods, namely Distillate Oil, and held that the test report was not definitive that the Distillate Oil was in fact diesel or HFHSD. The Hon’ble Gujarat High Court recognised the CRCL, New Delhi clarification that diesel fraction is a broad category including HFHSD, Automotive Diesel Fuel, Gas Oil, Distillate Marine Fuel and Light Diesel Oil. 16.1 The Hon’ble High Court further held that cloud point cannot be used as the sole basis to seize the cargo because it is relevant only at the place, vessel and time of use and depends upon end-user requirements. The Hon’ble Gujarat High Court quashed the seizure memos and directed release subject to filing of end-use certificate. The said judgment was applied by the Hon’ble CESTAT in Prajakt Agro to delete the Bank Guarantee condition. The Respondent’s insistence on Bank Guarantee is therefore contrary to binding jurisdictional High Court precedent. A copy of the decision in the case of Noya Infrastructure LLP v. Union of India reported in 2025 (12) TMI 1026 – Gujarat High Court is attached herewith and marked as Annexure L. FINDINGS 17. The Learned AR confronted with the position of Hon’ble Gujarat High Court in the matter of Aparajita Energy Private Limited Vs. UOI, reported in 2025 (12) TMI 1025-Gujarat High Court, as well as the decision of Division Bench in the case of Prajakt Agro Industries Pvt Ltd. Vs. Commissioner of Customs, Kandla, delivered vide Final Order No. 10024/2024 dated 19.01.2026 in which under similar circumstances, test reports relied upon by the Department and same having been clarified by CRCL, New Delhi, were considered at the stage of provisional release. As also the decision of Hon’ble Gujarat High Court in Noya Infrastructure LLP vs. UOI, reported in 2025 (12) TMI 2026 – Gujarat High Court, in which the Hon’ble High Court, after considering the same class of goods, namely, distillate oil, and also that the test reports were not definitive as to whether the distillate oil was, in fact, diesel or HFHSD, the Hon’ble Gujarat High Court recognized the CRCL clarification that diesel fraction is a broad category including HFHSD and other distillate oils that cloud point cannot be used as sole basis to seize of the cargo and directed the vacation of seizure and release of the goods, subject to filing of end-use certificate. Which decision was also followed by this CESTAT in Prajakt Agro industries Pvt. Ltd. cited (Supra) to do away with bank guarantee condition. This court finds that the decision to seek bank guarantee in the instant case is also against the directives of Hon’ble Gujarat High Court in Noya Infrastructure LLP vs UOI, reported in 2025 (12) TMI 2026, as well as the decision in Prajakt Agro Industries Pvt. Ltd. cited (supra). The condition of furnishing a bank guarantee, therefore, in the special circumstances of this matter, is liable to be set aside. 17.1 The learned AR has sought that as was directed by the Hon’ble Gujarat High Court party, should produce an end-use certificate. 17.2 From the records, this court finds that same has already beenpro duced on record as Exhibit-J at page No. 86 to 99. However, if any, end-use certificate still remains party shall produce, the same before the authorities along with the certificates already on record. On same having been produced, in totality and a bond having been given the bank guarantee shall be liable to be returned. 18. The appeal is allowed subject to the above condition.

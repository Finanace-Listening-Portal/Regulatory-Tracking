Start-up Speed: Can the Articles of a Private Company Provide for 24 Hours’ Notice of General Meetings? Summary: Start-ups need to approve fundraising, option pools and changes to their articles quickly. Section 101 of the Companies Act, 2013 requires twenty-one clear days’ notice of a general meeting, and allows shorter notice with the consent of members. A separate exemption gives private companies a second route: section 101 applies to them only unless their articles provide otherwise. This article examines how the two routes differ, why the second suits start-ups, how far a clause in the articles can go (including a clause of 24 hours), what the 2017 amendments add by way of conditions, and why, for an annual general meeting, the financial statements under section 136 remain a separate hurdle. A bridge round has to close before the investor’s offer lapses, an option pool has to be approved before the next hire joins, and the articles have to be changed to fit a new investor’s rights. Each of these needs the approval of shareholders, and shareholders in a young company are often spread across cities and time zones. Under section 101 of the Companies Act, 2013 (the Act), a general meeting needs twenty-one clear days’ notice. For a company moving at start-up speed, that is a long wait. The law offers two ways to shorten it. The first is general: the proviso to section 101(1) allows shorter notice with the consent of members. The second is special to private companies: under the exemption notification of 5 June 2015, section 101 applies to a private company unless its articles provide otherwise. This raises a practical question that every founder and company secretary should be able to answer. Can a private company write a very short notice period, even 24 hours, into its articles, and what stays in place when it does? This article answers that question in stages. It sets the two routes side by side, shows why the second route suits start-ups, examines the limits on what the articles can say, explains how to adopt such a clause, and then turns to a point that is easily missed: the circulation of financial statements under section 136, which follows its own rules. 2. Route 1: The Statutory Proviso to Section 101(1) Section 101(1) requires not less than twenty-one clear days’ notice, in writing or through electronic mode. Following the Companies (Amendment) Act, 2017, the proviso permits a general meeting to be called on shorter notice if consent, in writing or by electronic mode, is given: in the case of an annual general meeting, by not less than ninety-five per cent of the members entitled to vote at it; and in the case of any other general meeting, by a majority in number of the members entitled to vote who also hold not less than ninety-five per cent of the voting paid-up capital (or, for a company without share capital, by members having not less than ninety-five per cent of the total voting power). A further proviso counts members only on the resolutions on which they are entitled to vote. The distinction between an AGM and other meetings is easy to overlook, and it matters. The 95% threshold in the first limb is a headcount test, whereas the second limb combines a headcount majority with a capital test. Route 1 is also meeting-specific: the consent must be obtained for each meeting, and a defective consent exposes the resolutions passed at the meeting to challenge. 4. Route 2: The Exemption for Private Companies By G.S.R. 464(E) dated 5 June 2015, issued under section 462, the Central Government directed that sections 101 to 107 and section 109 shall apply to a private company “unless otherwise specified in respective sections or the articles of the company provide otherwise” (item 7 of the Table). The above-mentioned proviso covers more than notice. The same entry applies to quorum, proxies, voting and demand for a poll. The reasoning in this article extends to those provisions, although they are not examined here. It is silent on the length of the period. The notification states no minimum, no maximum and no consent percentage. The idea of a 24-hour clause is an application of the exemption, not something the notification itself states. Paragraph 2 of the notification adds that private companies, while complying with the exceptions and modifications, “shall ensure that the interests of their shareholders are protected.” The notification does not say whether this is a condition of the exemption or a general direction. That is a point on which reasonable readers could differ, and the better course for a company is to treat it as a real constraint on how short the period is made and how it is used. The Exemption Is Conditional: Paragraph 2A The flexibility is not available to every private company. By a notification of 13 June 2017, the Ministry inserted paragraph 2A, which provides that the exceptions, modifications and adaptations in the Table apply only to a private company that has not committed a default in filing its financial statements under section 137 or its annual return under section 92. The notification does not define how the test operates. It is arguable that a late filing on payment of additional fees under section 403 does not erase a default already committed. Section 403(2), as substituted by the 2017 Amendment Act, preserves liability to penalty notwithstanding late filing, and the notification, unlike the amended section 73(2)(e), prescribes no cure period. On that reading, a company that has ever filed late is outside paragraph 2A. A more lenient reading would treat the company as no longer in default once it has filed and paid the fee. The text leans towards the stricter reading, but the point is arguable. A company in doubt should therefore keep Route 1 available as a safeguard, since the Route 1 proviso does not depend on paragraph 2A. Paragraph 2A names only sections 137 and 92. Whether a late filing of the special resolution altering the articles (section 117) affects the position is not addressed in the notification, and this article does not treat it as settled. 5. Why This Matters for Start-ups One point should be clear at the outset. The exemption in item 7 is not a start-up concession. It is available to every private company. Start-ups are simply the companies that gain the most from it, for many reasons. Speed at the moments that matter. The decisive events in a start-up’s life (an investment round, an option pool, an amendment to the articles) each need a shareholder resolution. A company that can convene a meeting at short notice can act when the deal needs it, instead of when the notice period allows. It replaces repeated paperwork with a one-time step. Without a clause, a start-up must collect consents again for every meeting called at short notice, and must keep the evidence to defend each one. A single alteration of the articles replaces that routine for all future meetings. The Conditions Bite Hardest on Start-ups The benefits above come with two cautions. First, paragraph 2A makes every exemption in the Table depend on the company’s filing record. Young companies often file late while their compliance functions are still being built, and on the stricter reading in Section 4, a single late filing can matter long after it is cured. Second, once a start-up takes institutional money, the shareholders’ agreement usually sets its own rules on notice, quorum and reserved matters, and investors commonly hold a veto over changes to the articles. A clause that suits the founders may not survive the term sheet. A prudent start-up therefore treats the clause as a tool for the speed of its own decisions, and not as a way around the investors it has agreed to protect. 6. Can the Articles Override the Act? The articles of association are the company’s internal rulebook, and they remain subordinate to the Act. Section 6 gives the Act overriding effect over anything to the contrary in the memorandum or articles. Where an article conflicts with a mandatory provision, the conflicting part is void to that extent. The term “ultra vires” is sometimes used for this situation, but it is imprecise. It traditionally describes acts beyond the company’s objects or powers. A clause in the articles that conflicts with the Act is better described as void for inconsistency with the statute, with section 6 as the anchor. The real question under Route 2 is different. The exemption itself authorises the articles to provide otherwise for sections 101 to 107 and 109. A shorter notice period is therefore not inconsistent with section 101, because section 101, as it applies to the company, is subject to the articles. What the articles cannot do is purport to override the rest of the Act. This is why drafting matters. A clause such as “the company may convene any general meeting on 24 hours’ notice, notwithstanding anything contained in the Companies Act, 2013” is unnecessarily broad and invites the objection that it attempts to contract out of the statute. A safer formulation is: “Subject to the provisions of the Companies Act, 2013, the rules made thereunder, and applicable exemptions or notifications, a general meeting of the company may be convened at such shorter notice as may be provided in these Articles.” A clause drafted this way records that the company is using a flexibility the statute gives, and does not claim a power the statute withholds. 7. Adopting the Clause: Section 14 A private company seeking to introduce such a clause must alter its articles under section 14. An alteration requires a special resolution, and the company should ensure that: the proposed alteration is placed before the members through a properly convened meeting or consents given as the Act permits; the special resolution is passed by the requisite majority; the resolution and the altered articles are filed with the Registrar within the time prescribed under sections 14 and 117; the altered articles are recorded in the company’s records and circulated to members; and the amended articles are thereafter followed consistently. A practical point follows. The meeting at which the clause is adopted cannot itself rely on the clause, since the clause takes effect only on adoption. That meeting must be convened on twenty-one clear days’ notice, or on shorter notice under Route 1. 8. How Short Can the Period Be? Neither the Act nor the exemption notification sets a minimum period for a private company using Route 2. A 24-hour clause is therefore not prohibited. That is the legal position. The practical position is more cautious, for three reasons. First, the exemption is expressed to operate with the shareholders’ interests protected. A period so short that a member cannot realistically attend invites the argument that the clause is being used to defeat participation. Second, a clause that is valid on its face can still be used oppressively. Where the majority uses a very short period to pass resolutions without effective participation by a minority, the minority may allege oppression or mismanagement, and the tribunal will look at how the clause was used, not only at its text. Third, several points are not settled, and they should be recognised as open; section 101 applies except where the articles provide otherwise, so only the matter the articles actually address is displaced. This is an argument, not a settled rule. 9. Section 136: The Financial Statements A short notice period in the articles solves only half of the problem for an AGM. Section 136(1) separately requires that the financial statements, the auditor’s report and the other prescribed documents reach members at least twenty-one days before the meeting. The 2015 exemption notification does not mention section 136 at all, and neither does the 2017 amendment. Nothing in either relaxes that requirement. The proviso to section 136(1), as substituted by section 38 of the Companies (Amendment) Act, 2017, deems documents sent less than twenty-one days before the meeting to be duly sent if members agree who are a majority in number and who hold not less than ninety-five per cent of the voting paid-up capital (or, for a company without share capital, not less than ninety-five per cent of the total voting power). This test differs from the one in section 101 for an AGM, which asks for not less than ninety-five per cent of the members entitled to vote. In a company with three members holding 50%, 45% and 5%, for example, consent under section 101 requires all three members, whereas consent under section 136 can be given by the two larger holders. A consent obtained for one section therefore cannot be assumed to satisfy the other. A company whose articles provide for 24 hours’ notice may convene a general meeting on that notice, but for an AGM it should also obtain the consent contemplated by the proviso to section 136(1). Otherwise, it risks the financial statements being treated as not duly sent. 10. Practical Takeaways Use Route 2 for standing flexibility and adopt the clause by special resolution under section 14, with the required filings. Draft the clause “subject to” the Act, the rules and applicable exemptions. Avoid “notwithstanding anything contained in the Companies Act”. Before relying on the clause, confirm that the company has not defaulted in its filings under sections 137 and 92, and treat a past late filing as a reason for caution. For an AGM, obtain a separate consent under the proviso to section 136(1), in addition to relying on the articles for the notice. Keep Route 1 consents available as a safeguard for important meetings, and for meetings of companies that may fall outside paragraph 2A. Choose the period with the members’ actual ability to attend in mind. A very short period suits a closely held company in which every member is reachable, and is harder to defend where there are minority holders. Keep a record of the consents and of the basis on which each short-notice meeting was convened. 11. Conclusion A private company can, within limits, set its own notice period for general meetings through its articles, and that period can be as short as 24 hours. For a start-up, the value is concrete: it can close a round, approve an option pool or amend its articles on the timetable the business needs, and no single shareholder can stall the process by withholding consent. But the flexibility is neither unconditional nor complete. It depends on a clean filing record, on a validly adopted and carefully drafted clause, on the shareholders’ interests remaining protected, and, for an AGM, on a separate consent under section 136. Founders who understand these limits can use the exemption to move faster with confidence. Those who treat a 24-hour clause as a licence to ignore them risk challenge to the resolutions they pass, and may find the clause renegotiated at the next funding round.

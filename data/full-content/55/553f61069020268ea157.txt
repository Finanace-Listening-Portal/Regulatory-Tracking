Summary: The Goods and Services Tax Appellate Tribunal (GSTAT), Lucknow, in Vilasa Developers v. Himanshu Verma, JC (IT) & Others, APL/329/LCK/2026, dated 3 November 2026, set aside orders cancelling the taxpayer’s GST registration and restored the registration. The dispute arose from allegations of inadmissible input tax credit on purchases from suppliers whose registrations had been cancelled retrospectively. The cancellation notice referred to Section 16(2) but failed to identify the suppliers, GSTINs, relevant tax period or disputed credit, preventing the taxpayer from preparing an effective defence. The Tribunal emphasised that a general allegation cannot substitute for a specific show-cause notice disclosing the factual basis and statutory ground for cancellation. It distinguished tax determination proceedings under Section 74A from registration cancellation proceedings governed by Section 29 and Rule 22. The Tribunal also examined the appellate authority’s reliance on inquiry material not disclosed in the original proceedings and the procedural safeguards governing additional evidence under Rule 112. Oral departmental communications could not independently provide sufficient grounds for cancellation. While restoring registration, the Tribunal expressly left the merits of the disputed ITC to be determined separately in accordance with law. The decision reinforces that cancellation proceedings must independently satisfy the applicable statutory requirements, that an appellate order cannot retrospectively repair fundamental defects in the original notice, and that taxpayers must receive a definite and meaningful opportunity to answer allegations before their registration is cancelled. GST Registration Cannot Be Cancelled on General Allegations: The Vilasa Developers Decision A notice must disclose the case against the taxpayer; an appellate order cannot supply the missing grounds Cancellation of GST registration has consequences beyond the taxpayer’s account on the portal. It affects the ability to conduct taxable business, issue invoices and maintain commercial relationships. A power carrying such consequences must be exercised on identified statutory grounds and through a procedure that gives the taxpayer a meaningful opportunity to answer the allegations. A statement that a taxpayer has “wrongfully availed input tax credit” does not, by itself, explain why registration should be cancelled. The taxpayer must be informed of the transactions questioned, the relevant period, the alleged contravention and the legal basis for the proposed action. The decision of the GST Appellate Tribunal, Lucknow, in Vilasa Developers v. Himanshu Verma, JC (IT) & Others, APL/329/LCK/2026, dated 3 November 2026, addresses this distinction. The Tribunal set aside the original and appellate orders concerning cancellation and restored the appellant’s registration. The decision emphasises the need for specificity in the notice and for observance of the statutory procedure governing cancellation. An intimation under Section 74A preceded a notice proposing cancellation of the appellant’s registration. The cancellation notice alleged a violation of Section 16(2), but did not identify the suppliers, their GSTINs, the relevant period or the disputed credit. The registration was cancelled on 1 April 2026. The appellant’s application for revocation was rejected on 15 May 2026, and the first appellate authority upheld the adverse orders on 25 September 2026. The appellant then approached the Tribunal. The taxpayer maintained that its purchases were genuine and supported by invoices, e-way bills and banking transactions. It also challenged the absence of particulars in the notice and the reliance, during appellate proceedings, on inquiry material that had not formed part of the original allegations. The Tribunal allowed the appeal, set aside the cancellation and appellate orders, and restored the registration. Importantly, it confined its decision to cancellation and did not determine the merits of the separate proceedings under Section 74A. A notice must contain an allegation that can be answered A show-cause notice is not merely a procedural step preceding an order. It tells the taxpayer what must be explained and identifies the case the authority proposes to establish. An allegation of wrongful ITC availment may involve several different issues. The Department may question receipt of goods, the nature of the supply, compliance with a particular condition of Section 16 or the supplier’s conduct. Unless the notice identifies the actual objection, the taxpayer cannot know which documents or explanation are required. In Vilasa Developers, the absence of supplier particulars, the relevant period and the disputed credit was material. The Tribunal treated the deficiency as affecting the taxpayer’s ability to answer the case, rather than as a minor defect in drafting. The practical difficulty is apparent. A taxpayer cannot prepare a transaction-wise defence against an unidentified set of transactions. Nor can it explain the effect of a supplier’s cancellation when the notice does not identify the supplier or the relevant cancellation particulars. The opportunity to reply becomes meaningful only when the allegation is sufficiently definite. Reproducing a statutory expression is not enough A notice may refer to Section 16(2) or use an expression such as “ITC availed in violation of the Act”. Such language identifies a broad area of concern; it does not necessarily disclose the facts constituting the alleged violation. There is a difference between naming a provision and explaining its alleged breach. For example, if the objection concerns purchases from a supplier whose registration was cancelled, the taxpayer needs to know which supplier and which invoices are in dispute. If the objection concerns non-receipt of goods, that allegation must be stated. If a particular period is under scrutiny, the notice must identify it. The reported reasoning in Vilasa Developers recognises that the notice itself must carry the particulars necessary to sustain the proposed action. Its deficiencies cannot be cured merely by supplying a fuller explanation at a later stage. This is not a demand for elaborate drafting in every notice. It is a requirement that the taxpayer should be able to understand and answer the case before suffering an adverse order. ITC adjudication and registration cancellation are separate proceedings A central feature of the decision is the distinction between proceedings concerning disputed ITC and proceedings for cancellation of registration. Section 74A concerns determination of tax and related consequences. Cancellation is governed by a separate statutory framework under Section 29 read with Rule 22. The Tribunal held that proceedings initiated under Section 74A could not automatically be treated as valid cancellation proceedings. The distinction is substantive. An inquiry into credit eligibility asks whether a particular amount was lawfully availed. A cancellation proceeding asks whether a statutory ground exists for terminating the taxpayer’s registration. Although the underlying facts may overlap, the two questions are not identical. The Department must therefore identify the ground authorising cancellation and explain how the facts satisfy that ground. The existence of an ITC dispute cannot substitute for that exercise. In Vilasa Developers, the reported decision records that reliance on Section 29 at the appellate stage could not cure the failure of the notice and cancellation order to invoke the relevant cancellation framework. An appellate order cannot reconstruct the original case An appeal permits scrutiny of an order. It should not become the occasion for constructing a materially different case against the taxpayer without a proper opportunity to respond. In the present matter, the first appellate authority relied on an inquiry report that had not been referred to in the original notice. The Tribunal examined that reliance in the context of Rule 112 and the requirement to allow an adequate opportunity of rebuttal. The concern is straightforward. A taxpayer may answer every allegation disclosed in the notice and still lose the appeal because of additional adverse material considered later. Unless that material is disclosed and an opportunity to answer it is provided, the original opportunity to reply does not address the case ultimately decided. Where further material is considered necessary, the authority must follow the applicable procedure. It cannot assume that a general hearing is sufficient when the taxpayer has not been informed of the particular evidence being relied upon. Oral information cannot replace recorded grounds The reported facts also refer to an oral communication from another departmental officer. The Tribunal held that an oral intimation could not provide a sufficient basis for cancellation in the circumstances before it. Information received from another officer may justify an inquiry. It does not remove the need to formulate the allegation, identify the relevant material and communicate the proposed grounds to the taxpayer. The taxpayer is entitled to answer the case recorded against it—not an undisclosed departmental understanding. This distinction is particularly important where action originates from intelligence inputs, internal reports or inter-office communications. Such information must be examined and translated into a legally intelligible notice before it is used to support cancellation. Retrospective cancellation of a supplier does not dispense with inquiry Purchases from a supplier whose registration was cancelled retrospectively formed part of the factual background. The taxpayer disputed the adverse inference and relied on documents supporting the genuineness of its purchases. The Tribunal’s decision should not be read as declaring that every such purchase necessarily qualifies for ITC. That was not the issue finally decided. The relevant point is procedural: a supplier’s retrospective cancellation does not excuse the Department from identifying the disputed transactions and following the legal procedure applicable to the action proposed against the recipient. A recipient must be given an opportunity to address the particular allegation. A general reference to dealings with cancelled suppliers is not an adequate substitute for a properly formulated case. Restoration of registration is not a finding allowing the disputed ITC The scope of relief requires careful attention. The Tribunal restored the appellant’s registration. It did not, by doing so, pronounce upon the admissibility of every disputed credit or terminate the separate Section 74A proceedings. The reported decision expressly confines itself to the cancellation dispute. Two propositions must therefore be kept separate: The cancellation proceedings were legally defective. The disputed ITC was admissible on merits. The first does not automatically establish the second. This limitation does not diminish the importance of the decision. A taxpayer may succeed against cancellation while still being required to answer a properly constituted tax demand. Equally, an unresolved tax demand does not validate a defective cancellation order. Each proceeding must stand on its own statutory basis. Practical significance for taxpayers and representatives The decision provides a useful framework for reviewing cancellation proceedings. The first task should be to examine the notice itself, before turning to the merits of the underlying transactions. The relevant questions include: Does the notice identify the factual allegation? Does it specify the transactions and period in dispute? Does it disclose the statutory ground for cancellation? Has the material relied upon been made available? Does the final order remain within the allegations communicated? Has additional adverse material been introduced during appeal? Was a reasonable opportunity given to answer that material? Have tax-demand proceedings and cancellation proceedings been kept distinct? Where the notice is deficient, the objection should be raised specifically. A taxpayer may request the missing particulars and supporting material while also furnishing the available explanation and documents, without conceding that the notice is valid. A procedural objection is stronger when it explains the prejudice caused. Rather than merely describing a notice as “vague”, the reply should identify what is missing and why the omission prevents an effective response. For instance: “The notice does not identify the suppliers, invoices, relevant tax period or amount of credit alleged to have been wrongly availed. In the absence of these particulars, the appellant is unable to correlate the allegation with its books and supporting records or furnish a transaction-wise explanation.” That submission directs attention to the actual defect. The responsibility of the adjudicating authority A cancellation order should demonstrate an independent application of mind to the taxpayer’s explanation and the statutory ground invoked. If the allegation concerns a particular supplier, the order should address the explanation relating to that supplier. If documents have been produced to establish receipt of goods or payment, their relevance should be examined. If cancellation is considered justified despite that explanation, the reasons should be stated. A finding cannot be replaced by a repeated allegation. The Vilasa Developers decision is significant because it treats these requirements as part of lawful decision-making, rather than as formalities that can be supplied after cancellation. Its treatment of the deficient notice, undisclosed inquiry material and distinct statutory proceedings reflects that approach. Conclusion The decision in Vilasa Developers establishes an important procedural limit on the exercise of the cancellation power. A general allegation of wrongful ITC availment does not dispense with the need for a specific notice, disclosure of the case against the taxpayer and adherence to the statutory framework governing cancellation. The taxpayer must be told what transaction is questioned, what contravention is alleged and why that contravention is said to justify cancellation. An appellate authority cannot repair the absence of those foundations merely by adding further reasons or relying on undisclosed material. The Department remains entitled to investigate disputed credit and determine liability through lawful proceedings. What it cannot do is treat an unresolved allegation as a substitute for the grounds and procedure required to cancel registration. The essential principle is simple: before a taxpayer is required to defend its registration, it must be given a definite case to answer.

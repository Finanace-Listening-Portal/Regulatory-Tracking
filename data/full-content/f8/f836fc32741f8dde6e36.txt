Summary: The dismissal of a Special Leave Petition by the Supreme Court on 20 November 2026 has brought renewed attention to the legality of system-generated notices issued for cancellation of GST registration without identifying the issuing officer. In M/s M Y Ent Bhatta v. State of U.P., the Allahabad High Court held that a show cause notice containing neither the name nor the office of the issuing authority was contrary to the statutory framework under Section 29(2) of the CGST Act, 2017 and the applicable GST Rules. The Court emphasised that the power to initiate cancellation proceedings belongs to the proper officer and not to the GST portal or its automated system. Consequently, it quashed both the defective notice and the subsequent cancellation order, while permitting fresh proceedings in accordance with law. The principle was subsequently followed in M/s Gupta Enterprises v. Central Board of Indirect Taxes and Customs, where the Allahabad High Court similarly invalidated a system-generated notice and the resulting cancellation order. The Central Board challenged that judgment before the Supreme Court through SLP (Civil) Diary No. 56046 of 2026, which was dismissed on 20 November 2026 after the Court declined to interfere. However, applying the principles laid down in Kunhayammed v. State of Kerala and Khoday Distilleries Ltd. v. Sri Mahadeshwara Sahakara Sakkare Karkhane Ltd., dismissal of a non-speaking SLP does not amount to affirmation of the High Court’s reasoning or declaration of law under Article 141 of the Constitution. Nevertheless, the Allahabad High Court’s ruling continues to bind GST authorities within Uttar Pradesh, reinforcing that cancellation proceedings must originate from an identifiable statutory officer and comply with the prescribed procedural safeguards, including the opportunity of hearing. INTRODUCTION Over the last few years a very large number of GST registrations have been suspended and then cancelled on the strength of notices that carry no name, no designation and no office of any officer. The notice simply reads “Jurisdictional Officer System Generated Notice, Contact Jurisdiction Office for further process”. The dealer is told that his registration is proposed to be cancelled, but he is not told who proposes it. The cancellation order that follows is then passed, often without any reply having been possible, by an officer who appears for the first time at the final stage. The Allahabad High Court has held that such a notice is not a notice known to the Act. The view was taken by the Chief Justice’s Bench in M/s M Y Ent Bhatta, followed by another Division Bench in M/s Gupta Enterprises, and the Special Leave Petition filed by the Central Board of Indirect Taxes and Customs against the latter judgment has now been dismissed by the Supreme Court on 20 November 2026. This article examines the statutory basis of the principle, the reasoning of the High Court, and the precise legal effect of the order of the Supreme Court. 2. THE STATUTORY SCHEME Section 29(2) of the Central Goods and Services Tax Act, 2017 and the corresponding provision of the Uttar Pradesh Goods and Services Tax Act, 2017 confer the power to cancel registration on “the proper officer”. The proviso to the sub-section forbids cancellation without giving the person an opportunity of being heard. Section 2(91) defines the proper officer as the Commissioner or the officer of the tax administration who is assigned that function by the Commissioner. The power is, therefore, personal to an identified officer. Rule 21A provides for suspension of registration during the pendency of cancellation proceedings. Rule 22(1) requires the proper officer, where he has reasons to believe that the registration is liable to be cancelled, to issue a notice in FORM GST REG-17, or an intimation in FORM GST REG-31 where suspension is involved, calling upon the person to show cause. Rule 22(3) then requires the order of cancellation in FORM GST REG-19. Rule 26(3) further provides that the notices, certificates and orders under the Chapter on registration shall be issued electronically by the proper officer, or by an officer authorised to issue them, through a digital signature certificate or through e-signature, or verified by such other mode as may be notified. Read together, these provisions make one thing plain. The formation of reasons to believe, the issue of the show cause notice, the hearing and the order are all acts of an officer. The statute nowhere vests any of these functions in the common portal or in the Goods and Services Tax Network. The portal is a medium through which the officer acts; it is not the authority that acts. 3. M/S M Y ENT BHATTA V. STATE OF U.P. The petitioner in M/s M Y Ent Bhatta v. State of U.P., Writ Tax No. 546 of 2025, Neutral Citation No. 2025:AHC:78209-DB, decided by Arun Bhansali, C.J. and Kshitij Shailendra, J. on 13 May 2025, was served a show cause notice dated 14.11.2023 proposing cancellation for failure to furnish returns for a continuous period of six months. Registration was suspended with effect from the same date, and an order dated 05.01.2024, issued by the Assistant Commissioner, cancelled it. The notice bore only the legend that it was a system generated notice and that the dealer should contact the jurisdiction office. By its order dated 08.04.2025 the Court called for instructions. The State placed on record an explanation of the Goods and Services Tax Network referring to an “Advisory on issuance of Notices/Orders without digital signatures of the issuing authorities”. The stand was that since the officer logs into the portal by means of his digital signature, the notices and orders generated thereafter need not themselves bear a digital signature. The Court rejected the explanation. It held that the advisory might be a valid explanation where the name and office of the issuing authority are indicated and only the digital signature is missing. In the case before it, however, the notice disclosed neither the name nor the office of the issuing authority. The Court said: “As the power lies with the officers and not with the system, the issuance of notice which is system generated and does not bear the name/office, cannot be justified based on the explanation sought to be produced.” Holding that the notice was dehors the Act and the Rules, the Court quashed both the notice and the cancellation order, leaving the respondents free to issue a fresh notice in accordance with law, if the occasion arose. Two features of the judgment deserve notice. First, the final order in that case was in fact passed by a named officer, the Assistant Commissioner. The Court nevertheless set it aside, because an order built upon a notice that is no notice in law cannot stand on its own. A defect in the foundation is not cured by a signature at the end. Second, the reasoning ties the requirement of a name and office directly to the hearing guaranteed by the proviso to Section 29(2). A dealer who does not know which officer proposes to cancel his registration cannot know before whom he is to reply or appear. A notice that asks him to “contact jurisdiction office” places on the noticee the burden of discovering his own adjudicator, which is the reverse of what the statute contemplates. 4. M/S GUPTA ENTERPRISES V. CENTRAL BOARD OF INDIRECT TAXES AND CUSTOMS In M/s Gupta Enterprises v. Central Board of Indirect Taxes and Customs and another, Writ Tax No. 1501 of 2026, decided on 11 March 2026, a Division Bench of Saumitra Dayal Singh, J. and Vivek Saran, J. was dealing with a cancellation order dated 09.10.2025 preceded by a system generated notice dated 08.09.2025. Noticing that the matter was covered by M/s M Y Ent Bhatta, the Bench extracted its reasoning and, for the self same reasons, set aside both the notice and the order, with liberty to the revenue authorities to issue a fresh notice in accordance with law. The respondents in Gupta Enterprises were the Central Board of Indirect Taxes and Customs and another. The principle is therefore not confined to the State tax administration. Since Section 29 and Rules 21A, 22 and 26 are common to the Central and State enactments, the same reasoning governs a notice issued in the name of a Central tax range as much as one issued in the name of a State tax sector. 5. THE SUPREME COURT ORDER OF 20 NOVEMBER 2026 The Board carried the judgment in Gupta Enterprises to the Supreme Court in Special Leave Petition (Civil) Diary No. 56046 of 2026. On 20 November 2026 a Bench of Dipankar Datta, J. and Sheel Nagu, J., after hearing the Additional Solicitor General for the petitioners, condoned the delay in filing, observed that it was not inclined to interfere with the impugned judgment and order of the High Court, and dismissed the Special Leave Petition. The legal effect of such an order is settled. In Kunhayammed v. State of Kerala, (2000) 6 SCC 359, the Supreme Court held that an order refusing special leave, whether or not it gives reasons, does not attract the doctrine of merger, because the Court has not exercised its appellate jurisdiction at all. An order without reasons declares no law within the meaning of Article 141. The position was reaffirmed by a three Judge Bench in Khoday Distilleries Ltd. v. Sri Mahadeshwara Sahakara Sakkare Karkhane Ltd., (2019) 4 SCC 376. It would, therefore, be inaccurate to say that the Supreme Court has affirmed or approved the reasoning in M/s M Y Ent Bhatta. What the order does is equally important. The judgment in Gupta Enterprises has attained finality between the parties. The law declared by the Division Bench in M/s M Y Ent Bhatta continues to hold the field in the State, and under the principle stated in East India Commercial Co. Ltd. v. Collector of Customs, AIR 1962 SC 1893, the law declared by a High Court binds every authority and tribunal functioning within its territorial jurisdiction. The tax officers of the State and the Central tax officers posted in Uttar Pradesh are thus bound to follow it. The Union, through its senior law officer, chose to test the question before the Supreme Court and the Court declined to interfere. For practical purposes the question can no longer be treated as an open one in this State. 6. THE SCOPE AND LIMITS OF THE PRINCIPLE The principle has a definite boundary and it is worth stating it accurately. (a) The ratio attaches to a notice that bears neither the name nor the office of the issuing authority. The Court itself indicated that where the name and office are disclosed and only the digital signature is absent, the advisory of the Network may furnish a valid explanation. Whether a given notice falls within the principle is, therefore, a question to be answered from the face of that notice. (b) The relief granted in both cases was the setting aside of the notice and the order, with liberty to the department to issue a fresh notice in accordance with law. The principle does not confer immunity from cancellation. It requires that the power be exercised by an identified officer through a notice that the law recognises. Once the order is set aside, the registration stands revived, and the proper officer may begin afresh if grounds exist. (c) Neither judgment rests on Rule 26(3). That rule, which requires notices and orders under the Chapter on registration to be issued by the proper officer through a digital signature certificate or e-signature, independently supports the same conclusion. A notice that carries no signature of any kind and no name of any officer can hardly be described as issued by the proper officer through a digital signature. (d) The reasoning that the power lies with the officer and not with the system is not peculiar to registration. The Act vests the power to issue a show cause notice for demand, a scrutiny notice or an order of assessment in the proper officer as well. Whether the same logic will be extended to those notices is for the courts to decide in an appropriate case, but the foundation of the argument is the same. 7. CONCLUSION Automation was introduced into GST administration to make the officer’s work easier, not to replace the officer. The judgments in M/s M Y Ent Bhatta and M/s Gupta Enterprises restore the elementary rule of administrative law that a statutory power must be exercised by the authority on whom the statute confers it, and that the person affected is entitled to know who that authority is. The order of the Supreme Court dated 20 November 2026 does not declare the law for the country, but it leaves the Allahabad High Court’s view undisturbed and final. Until a larger Bench or the Supreme Court in an appropriate case holds otherwise, a cancellation founded on a nameless system generated notice cannot be sustained in Uttar Pradesh. ***** Disclaimer: The views expressed in this article are the author’s own thoughts.

Vs Commissioner of GST & Central Excise (CESTAT Chennai) Summary: The Customs, Excise and Service Tax Appellate Tribunal (CESTAT), Chennai, allowed 15 connected appeals arising from allegations of clandestine manufacture and removal of mild steel (MS) ingots, wrongful availment of CENVAT credit, and related penalties against Geetham Steels Pvt. Ltd. (GSPL), its directors, buyers, suppliers and associated manufacturers. The dispute originated from Order-in-Original No. 13/2014-C.Ex. dated 29 December 2014, passed by the Commissioner of Central Excise, Salem. Against GSPL, the adjudicating authority had confirmed a duty and credit demand aggregating to ₹1,70,97,731, together with an equivalent penalty. A separate duty demand of ₹4,60,967 was confirmed against Sri Kamalaganapathy Steel Rolling Mills Ltd. (SKSRM), while penalties were imposed on other appellants. The principal issues were whether the Department had established clandestine clearances through admissible and corroborated evidence, whether the CENVAT credit denial was justified, and whether the consequential penalties could survive. The investigation began with searches conducted by the Directorate General of Central Excise Intelligence on 28 September 2010 at GSPL’s factory and office, the premises of scrap merchants, the residence of managing director C. Natarajan and the residence of former accountant P. Kumar. Officers recovered production records, private documents, computer equipment, a hard disk and pen drives. The contents of the pen drives were examined at a nearby browsing centre and subsequently processed on several occasions. Further searches were conducted at the premises of buyers, including Balaji Steel Industry and SKSRM, in March 2012, and at GSPL’s premises in June 2012. In October 2013, an external cyber-forensic analyst participated in imaging electronic devices. The Department relied on the resulting printouts, statements recorded under Section 14 of the Central Excise Act, 1944, production reports, stock discrepancies, transport information and records recovered from third parties to allege that GSPL had manufactured and removed ingots without recording the transactions or paying excise duty. Following the investigation, a show cause notice dated 28 October 2013 proposed recovery of ₹1,19,66,174 towards duty on alleged unaccounted clearances between February 2010 and February 2012 and ₹51,31,557 towards CENVAT credit allegedly taken without actual receipt of inputs. The Department relied on electronic entries indicating unaccounted sales of MS ingots, differences between production sheets and RG-1 records, alleged stock shortages, and invoices said to have been issued without movement of scrap. It also alleged that SKSRM had manufactured and cleared TMT rods using ingots received without accounting or duty payment. GSPL disputed the accuracy and admissibility of the electronic records and explained that certain ingots were defective and had been transferred for remelting. It further argued that the Department had not established the necessary links between alleged excess production, raw-material consumption, transportation, receipt of consideration and actual clandestine removals. A substantial part of the Tribunal’s examination concerned cross-examination-witness-mandatory-terms-section-9d-excise-act.html” title=”Section 9D examination and cross-examination requirements” target=”_blank” rel=”noopener”>Section 9D of the Central Excise Act, 1944, governing the use of statements recorded during an excise investigation. The appellants submitted that statements obtained under Section 14 could not automatically be relied upon as substantive evidence without compliance with the statutory procedure for determining their admissibility. The Department responded that the appellants had not raised the objection or requested cross-examination before the adjudicating authority. The Tribunal considered whether this procedural objection could be raised at the appellate stage and the consequences of relying on statements without following Section 9D. It concluded that statutory requirements affecting the admissibility of evidence could not be bypassed merely because the statements had been recorded by authorised officers. The absence of proper examination and testing of relevant statements seriously impaired their evidentiary value, particularly where those statements formed an important foundation of the demands. The Tribunal also examined Section 36B of the Central Excise Act, 1944, dealing with computer-generated evidence. The Department’s case depended heavily on printouts taken from pen drives, hard disks and computers seized during investigation. The appellants challenged the handling, copying, imaging and printing of those devices and specifically pointed to the absence of a certificate satisfying Section 36B(4). The Department maintained that the data had been recovered through documented proceedings and that the conditions concerning ordinary use of computers were substantially fulfilled. However, the Tribunal found that the statutory safeguards for admitting electronic records had not been properly complied with. Referring to the Supreme Court’s ruling in Anvar P.V. v. P.K. Basheer and the principles governing certification of secondary electronic evidence, it held that the disputed printouts could not be treated as legally admissible evidence merely because they had been recovered during official searches or examined by forensic personnel. On the allegations of clandestine production and removal, the Tribunal examined the production sheets, RG-1 records, raw-material accounts, alleged stock shortages and the explanation concerning defective ingots. GSPL maintained that ingots failing quality checks were returned for remelting and that subsequent accounting records and returns reflected those transactions. The Tribunal also examined the Department’s allegations regarding unaccounted scrap purchases, sales to rolling mills, transport movements and cash receipts. It found important gaps in the investigation, including inadequate verification of the persons actually responsible for transportation, insufficient independent evidence concerning receipt and delivery of goods, and material inconsistencies in the reconstruction of production and transactions. The Tribunal observed that certain assumptions concerning the quantities of raw materials and finished products did not yield a reliable material balance. Private records and suspicious entries, without dependable corroboration, were insufficient on the facts to establish clandestine clearances. The Tribunal separately considered the positions of SKSRM and the other appellants against whom penalties had been imposed under the Central Excise Rules, 2002. SKSRM disputed the allegation that it had received unaccounted ingots and cleared TMT rods without duty payment, pointing out that the Department relied on limited and inadequately authenticated weighment slips and statements. Other appellants denied issuing invoices without supplying goods or receiving unaccounted materials. Some also raised questions concerning the applicability of Rule 26 penalties, the absence of investigations at their premises and relief sought under the Sabka Vishwas (Legacy Dispute Resolution) Scheme. The Tribunal considered these arguments alongside the common evidentiary defects affecting the principal case. It found that the Department had failed to establish the underlying transactions through reliable and sufficiently corroborated evidence. Accordingly, the associated duty demands and penalties could not be sustained merely by relying on the same disputed material. Allowing all 15 appeals, the Tribunal held that the findings of clandestine manufacture and clearance of MS ingots during February 2010 to February 2012 were untenable, that the denial of CENVAT credit for February to May 2010 was incorrect, and that the duty demand against SKSRM concerning TMT rods was unsustainable. The Tribunal observed that the investigation had failed to examine or properly pursue crucial contemporaneous evidence, that relevant transporters had not been adequately questioned, and that electronic evidence had been handled in a manner undermining its admissibility. Suspicion could not replace demonstrable, reliable and corroborative proof. It therefore set aside Order-in-Original No. 13/2014-C.Ex. dated 29 December 2014, with consequential relief as per law, and held that interest and penalties could not survive once the principal demands failed. The Tribunal also expressed concern about continuing non-compliance with established judicial requirements relating to electronic evidence and witness statements. Cases Discussed Anvar P.V. Vs P.K.

Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); Amit Agency Vs Commissioner (Patna High Court) Summary: The Patna High Court held that a show cause notice issued under Section 74 of the CGST Act, 2017 could not be invalidated on jurisdictional grounds where the proper officer had recorded specific statutory contraventions and circumstances supporting its invocation. The petitioner, Amit Agency, a partnership firm engaged in the cigarette business, challenged the final audit report dated 18.07.2023, show cause notice dated 30.08.2023, adjudication order dated 22.04.2024, corrigendum dated 27.08.2024 and subsequent order dated 18.10.2024 concerning Financial Years 2017-18, 2018-19 and 2019-20. The audit conducted under Section 65 of the CGST Act revealed several discrepancies, including short payment of GST, non-payment of interest, failure to discharge GST under the reverse charge mechanism, delayed filing of GSTR-1 and GSTR-3B, non-payment of late fees and unreconciled turnover. The audit also identified interest liability of Rs. 68,64,631 and short payment of tax amounting to Rs. 33,75,668 for Financial Years 2018-19 and 2019-20. The Monitoring Committee approved the audit findings on 07.07.2023 and directed recovery of the outstanding amounts within 15 days, failing which a show cause notice was to be issued. Subsequently, proceedings were initiated under Section 74(1), culminating in confirmation of the demand for tax, interest, penalty and late fees. The petitioner contended that the Department had mechanically invoked Section 74 merely on the basis of audit objections without establishing fraud, wilful misstatement or suppression of facts with an intention to evade tax. Reliance was placed on the Supreme Court decisions in M/s G.R. Infra Projects Limited Ratlam Vs State of Madhya Pradesh and M/s Tata Steel Limited Vs Union of India, which emphasised that the foundational facts justifying invocation of the extended limitation period must emerge from the notice itself. The petitioner further argued that substantial input tax credit was available in its electronic credit ledger and GSTR-2A, thereby neutralising the alleged tax liability and undermining the allegation of tax evasion. Other objections included clubbing of three financial years in a single notice and adjudication order, completion of the audit beyond the prescribed limitation period, failure to upload the prescribed summaries in Forms GST DRC-01 and DRC-07, absence of digital signatures under Rule 26(3), incorrect computation of interest and imposition of penalty through a corrigendum issued under Section 161. The respondents defended the proceedings, contending that the audit was completed within the statutory period, the petitioner had been afforded several opportunities of personal hearing, and the show cause notice contained sufficient reasons for invoking Section 74. They also raised a preliminary objection regarding the availability of an efficacious statutory appellate remedy under Section 107. The High Court examined the principles governing the exercise of extraordinary writ jurisdiction under Article 226 of the Constitution of India and referred to the Supreme Court judgments in CIT Vs Chhabil Dass Agarwal and United Bank of India Vs Satyawati Tandon. Although an effective alternative remedy was available, the Court proceeded to examine the limited jurisdictional objection raised against the show cause notice. On examining Sections 65 and 74 of the CGST Act, the Court observed that the proper officer was empowered to initiate appropriate proceedings based on discrepancies detected during an audit, subject to fulfilment of the statutory conditions governing the relevant provision. In the present case, the notice specifically described the instances of non-payment and short payment of GST, delayed filing of returns, non-payment of late fees and failure to discharge reverse charge liabilities. It also alleged that these statutory contraventions would have remained undetected had the audit not been conducted. The Court further found that paragraph 9(ix) of the show cause notice contained a proposal for recovery of penalty, contrary to the petitioner’s contention that no such proposal had been made. Distinguishing the Supreme Court decisions in G.R. Infra Projects Limited and Tata Steel Limited, the Court observed that those judgments dealt with notices lacking adequate foundational facts supporting allegations of fraud or suppression. Nevertheless, it reiterated that mere non-payment or short payment of GST for bona fide reasons could not justify invocation of the extended limitation period under Section 74 and that each case must be examined on its own facts. The High Court further observed that while exercising extraordinary writ jurisdiction, it was not ordinarily required to examine the sufficiency of the reasons underlying the proper officer’s satisfaction regarding fraud, wilful misstatement or suppression of facts. Drawing support from the Supreme Court judgment in Sanand Properties Private Limited Vs Joint Commissioner of Income Tax, the Court distinguished between examining the existence of relevant material supporting the formation of an opinion and evaluating the sufficiency of such material. In the circumstances of the present case, the Court found that the show cause notice did not suffer from non-application of mind or any jurisdictional error warranting interference under Article 226. Consequently, it declined to examine the adjudication order on merits, considering the availability of an effective statutory appellate remedy under Section 107 of the CGST Act. Taking note of the fact that a copy of the writ petition had been served upon the respondents on 21.08.2024, within four months of the adjudication order dated 22.04.2024, the Court granted liberty to the petitioner to file a statutory appeal within one month from the date of the judgment and directed that such appeal be considered on its own merits. All questions were expressly left open to the parties, and the Court clarified that its observations were confined to examining the validity of the show cause notice and did not constitute a final determination of the disputed tax, interest, penalty or other substantive issues. Accordingly, the writ petition was disposed of with liberty to pursue the statutory appellate remedy. Cases Discussed 2026 INSC 920 — M/s Tata Steel Limited Vs Union of India and Ors. It is an admitted position that the impugned order as contained in Annexure ‘P-6’ is an appealable order under Section 107 of the CGST/BGST Act, 2017. The appellate remedy is equally efficacious. It is well settled that a rule of alternative remedy is a rule of convenience. In catena of judgments, the Hon’ble Supreme Court has emphasized that where there is an alternative statutory remedy, which is equally efficacious, it would not be appropriate to entertain a writ application and exercise the extraordinary writ jurisdiction of this Court to examine the original order unless the impugned order suffers from jurisdictional error. We refer judgment of the Hon’ble Supreme Court in case of Commissioner of Income Tax and Others vs. Chhabil Dass Agarwal reported in (2014) 1 SCC 603 : (2013) 357 ITR 357. Paragraphs ‘15’, ‘16’ and ‘17’ are extracted hereunder for a ready reference:- “15. Thus, while it can be said that this Court has recognised some exceptions to the rule of alternative remedy i.e. where the statutory authority has not acted in accordance with the provisions of the enactment in question, or in defiance of the fundamental principles of judicial procedure, or has resorted to invoke the provisions which are repealed, or when an order has been passed in total violation of the principles of natural justice, the proposition laid down in Thansingh Nathmal case22, Titaghur Paper Mills case3 and other similar judgments that the High Court will not entertain a petition under Article 226 of the Constitution if an effective alternative remedy is available to the aggrieved person or the statute under which the action complained of has been taken itself contains a mechanism for redressal of grievance still holds the field. Therefore, when a statutory forum is 22 Thansingh Nathmal v. Supt. Of Taxes, [AIR 1964 SC 1419] 3 [Titaghur Paper Mills Co. Ltd. v. State of Orissa, (1983) 2 SCC 433 : 1983 SCC (Tax) 131] created by law for redressal of grievances, a writ petition should not be entertained ignoring the statutory dispensation. 16. In the instant case, the Act provides complete machinery for the assessment/reassessment of tax, imposition of penalty and for obtaining relief in respect of any improper orders passed by the Revenue Authorities, and the assessee could not be permitted to abandon that machinery and to invoke the jurisdiction of the High Court under Article 226 of the Constitution when he had adequate remedy open to him by an appeal to the Commissioner of Income Tax (Appeals). The remedy under the statute, however, must be effective and not a mere formality with no substantial relief. In Ram and Shyam Co. v. State of Haryana40 this Court has noticed that if an appeal is from “Caesar to Caesar’s wife” the existence of alternative remedy would be a mirage and an exercise in futility. 17. In the instant case, neither has the writ petitioner assessee described the available alternate remedy under the Act as ineffectual and non-efficacious while invoking the writ jurisdiction of the High Court nor has the High Court ascribed cogent and satisfactory reasons to have exercised its jurisdiction in the facts of the instant case. In light of the same, we are of the considered opinion that the writ court ought not to have entertained the writ petition filed by the assessee, wherein he has only questioned the correctness or otherwise of the notices issued under Section 148 of the Act, the reassessment orders passed and the consequential demand notices issued thereon.” 40 (1985) 3 SCC 267 26. In the case of United Bank of India vs. Satyawati Tandon and others reported in (2010) 8 SCC 110, the Hon’ble Supreme Court has reiterated this view. Paragraph ‘43’ and ‘44’ is reproduced hereinbelow:- “43. Unfortunately, the High Court overlooked the settled law that the High Court will ordinarily not entertain a petition under Article 226 of the Constitution if an effective remedy is available to the aggrieved person and that this rule applies with greater rigour in matters involving recovery of taxes, cess, fees, other types of public money and the dues of banks and other financial institutions. In our view, while dealing with the petitions involving challenge to the action taken for recovery of the public dues, etc. the High Court must keep in mind that the legislations enacted by Parliament and State Legislatures for recovery of such dues are a code unto themselves inasmuch as they not only contain comprehensive procedure for recovery of the dues but also envisage constitution of quasi- judicial bodies for redressal of the grievance of any aggrieved person. Therefore, in all such cases, the High Court must insist that before availing remedy under Article 226 of the Constitution, a person must exhaust the remedies available under the relevant statute. 44. While expressing the aforesaid view, we are conscious that the powers conferred upon the High Court under Article 226 of the Constitution to issue to any person or authority, including in appropriate cases, any Government, directions, orders or writs including the five prerogative writs for the enforcement of any of the rights conferred by Part III or for any other purpose are very wide and there is no express limitation on exercise of that power but, at the same time, we cannot be oblivious of the rules of self-imposed restraint evolved by this Court, which every High Court is bound to keep in view while exercising power under Article 226 of the Constitution.” 27. Keeping in view the aforesaid judicial pronouncements, we would have immediately relegated the petitioner to the alternative remedy of appeal but in view of the plea raised on behalf of the petitioner that the adjudicating authority who has issued the ‘SCN’ (Annexure ‘P4’) and passed the order in original (Annexure ‘6’) has committed a jurisdictional error, we have proceeded to examine the plea. (3) The registered person shall be informed by way of a notice not less than fifteen working days prior to the conduct of audit in such manneras may be prescribed. (4) The audit under sub-section (1) shall be completed within a period of three months from the date of commencement of the audit: Provided that where the Commissioner is satisfied that audit in respect of such registered person cannot be completed within three months, he may, for the reasons to be recorded in writing, extend the period by a further period not exceeding six months. Explanation.- For the purposes of this sub-section, the expression “commencement of audit” shall mean the date on which the records and other documents, called for by the tax authorities, are made available by the registered person or the actual institution of audit at the place of business, whichever is later. (5) During the course of audit, the authorised officer may require the registered person, – 1. Enforced w.e.f. 1-7-2017 vide Noti. No.9/2017-Central Tax, dt. 28-6-2017 (i) to afford him the necessary facility to verify the books of account or other documents as he may require; (ii) to furnish such information as he may require and render assistance for timely completion of the audit. (6) On conclusion of audit, the proper officer shall, within thirty days, inform the registered person, whose records are audited, about the findings, his rights and obligations and the reasons for such findings. (7) Where the audit conducted under sub-section (1) results in detection of tax not paid or short paid or erroneously refunded, or input tax credit wrongly availed or utilised, the proper officer may initiate action under section 73 or section 74 1[or section 74A].” 34. On a bare reading of sub-section (7) of Section 65, it appears that where the audit results in detection of tax not paid or short paid or erroneously refunded, or input tax credit wrongly availed or utilized, the proper officer has been conferred with power to initiate action under Section 73 or Section 74 or Section 74A. It is, thus, the proper officer who has to take a view that in the facts of the case, under which provision action be initiated. 35. Section 74 of the CGST Act provides for determination of tax (pertaining to the period upto Financial Year 2023-24) not paid or short paid or erroneously refunded or input tax credit wrongly availed or utilized by reason of fraud or any willful misstatement or suppression of facts. Sub-section (1) of 1. Inserted by Finance (No.2) Act, 2024 (15 of 2024), dt. 16-8-2024, w.e.f. 1-11-2024 vide SO 4253(E), dt. 27-9-2024 Section 74 provides for service of a notice upon the person chargeable with tax. The ‘SCN’ as to why he should not pay the amount specified in the notice along with interest payable thereon under Section 50 and a penalty equivalent to the tax specified in the notice shall be issued under sub-section (1). Sub-section (2) provides for a time limit of at least six months prior to the time limit prescribed in sub-section (10) for issuance of the order. Sub- section (9) provides that the proper officer shall, after considering the representations, if any, made by the person chargeable with tax, determine the amount of tax, interest and penalty due from such person and issue an order. Sub-section (10) states that “the proper officer shall issue the order under sub-section (9) within a period of 5 years from the due date for furnishing of annual return for the financial year to which the tax not paid or short paid or input tax credit wrongly availed or utilized relates to or within 5 years from the date of erroneous refund.” It is important to take note of sub- section (5) of Section 74 which reads as under:- “(5) The person chargeable with tax may, before service of notice under sub-section (1), pay the amount of tax along with interest payable under section 50 and a penalty equivalent to fifteen per cent. of such tax on the basis of his own ascertainment of such tax or the tax as ascertained by the proper officer and inform the proper officer in writing of such payment.” 36. On a complete reading of the scheme of Section 74, in the facts of the present case, it is evident that after the final audit report was approved by the MCM and the same was communicated to the petitioner, the petitioner was liable to deposit the tax, interest and penalty as the case may be as per the final audit within the given period of 15 days or as per sub-section (5) before service of the notice under sub-section (1), in such circumstance, his case would have been covered under sub-section (5) of Section 74. In this case, since the case of the petitioner was not covered by sub-section (5) of Section 74, the respondent no.3 proceeded to issue notice under sub-section (1) of Section 74. As regards the reasons behind invocation of sub-section (1) of Section 74 of the CGST Act, 2017, the respondents have made statements in paragraph ‘7’ onwards in the ‘SCN’. From paragraph ‘7’ onwards, in the ‘SCN’, it has been pointed out that had the audit not been conducted of the records/registers of the noticee, this non-payment/short payment of GST, late fee for delayed filing of GSTR-1, delayed payment of tax by way of delayed payment of GSTR-3B, GST under RCM, etc. as given in detail would not have been noticed. 37. It is stated that under Section 155 of the CGST/SGST Act, 2017, where any person claims that he is eligible for input tax credit under the CGST/SGST Act, 2017, the burden of proving such claim shall be on such person. The government has placed full trust on the taxpayers from the very beginning and accordingly a measure like self-assessment etc. have been provided. All these operate on the basis of honesty of the taxpayers, therefore, the governing statutory provisions create an absolute liability when any provision is contravened or there is a breach of trust by the taxpayers. To this Court, thus it appears that the principle behind issuance of ‘SCN’ under Section 74(1) of the CGST/BGST Act, 2017 is the breach of the principle of ‘utmost good faith’. 38. This Court finds that the respondents have shown in paragraph ‘8’ that how in this case there is a non-payment/short payment of GST, non-payment/short payment of late fee for delayed filing of GSTR-1, non-payment of GST under RCM in terms of Section 9(4) and non-payment of the late fee for delayed filing. The ‘SCN’ called upon the petitioner to submit his response with all evidences and proposed to recover the non-paid/short-paid amounts on various heads, applicable interest on the amount mentioned in (i), (ii), (iv) to (vii) under Section 50 of the CGST/BGST Act and penalty on the amount mentioned at (ii), (iv), (v), (vi) and (vii). This is in consonance with the principle of natural justice. The proposed demand are fully enumerated under paragraph ‘9’ of the ‘SCN’. At this stage, we find that a summary of the ‘SCN’ in GST DRC-01 dated 31.01.2024 uploaded on the GST portal is enclosed and regarding this the respondents have given an explanation. The plea taken with regard to non- compliance with sub-rule (3) of Rule 26 of the GST Rules has been duly addressed in the counter affidavit and in supplementary counter affidavit. It is evident on perusal of the ‘SCN’ (Annexure ‘P-5’) that under paragraph ‘9(ix)’, there was a proposed demand for recovery of penalty, therefore, the contention of learned Senior counsel that there was no proposal for recovery of the penalty is not a correct submission. 39. At this stage, we take note of the judgment of the Hon’ble Supreme Court in the case of M/s G.R. Infra Projects Limited Ratlam (supra). In the said case, the ‘SCN’ dated 13.06.2025 was issued under Section 74 of the CGST Act, 2017. On going through the said ‘SCN’, the Hon’ble Supreme Court observed in paragraph ‘10’ of the judgment as under:- “10. A bare reading of the notice would indicate that but for a bland statement of ‘fraud or concealment of facts’ nothing is stated as to how fraud was inferred or concealment of facts were detected. In fact, the ‘or’ employed indicates that even the assessing officer was not sure that the assessment was proceeded by reason of fraud or on the ground of concealment of facts. What is required for the extended time to be applied are the allegations, which lead to the inference of a fraud or the concealment as attempted by the assessee resulting in suppression of facts, should emanate from the notice itself. It cannot be a mechanical use of the words ‘fraud, willful misstatement or suppression of facts’ without listing out the aspects which persuades the assessing officer to conclude that there has been employed either of these surreptitious devices by the assessee.” 40. It is important to take note that in case of M/s G.R. Infra Projects Limited Ratlam (supra), the Hon’ble Supreme Court categorically held that whether a notice or order is valid is to be judged from the notice or order itself. Paragraph ‘5’ of the judgment is extracted hereunder for a ready reference:- “5. We refused to look at the counter affidavit on the trite principle that when an authority has issued a notice or an order, the requirements to make the notice or order valid should be contained in such notice or order and cannot be supplanted by a counter affidavit in Court, where the notice or order is alleged to be invalid for reason to non-application of mind, the requirements having not met and so on and so forth.” 41. It is evident that in case of M/s G.R. Infra Projects Limited Ratlam (supra), the Hon’ble Supreme Court was concerned as to whether the notice under Section 74 in the said case can be one deemed to have been issued under Section 74 of the CGST Act, 2017 read with Madhya Pradesh Goods and Services Tax, 2017 (MPGST Act). 42. In the case of M/s Tata Steel Limited (supra), the Hon’ble Supreme Court was concerned with the ‘SCN’ issued for three financial years 2018-19 to 2020-21, purportedly in pursuance of an objection raised on an audit by the office of the Comptroller and Auditor General of India. The ‘SCN’ was termed to be one under Section 74 of the CGST Act. It was contended before the Hon’ble Supreme Court that there was no allegation of fraud, willful misstatement or suppression of facts, bereft of which there can be no invocation of Section 74, where there is an extended period of five years, as distinguished from a period of three years available under Section 73. 43. While giving a consideration to the submissions with regard to invocation of Section 74 and the essential conditions of the said provision, the Hon’ble Supreme Court in M/s Tata Steel Limited (supra) took notice of the facts of the case and dealt with this issue from paragraph ‘9’ to ‘15’ of the judgment which we reproduce hereunder:- “9. That allegation, if available, the Department can seek to sustain the notice under Section 74, which is what they attempted to do. The proceedings which led to the SCN commenced with Annexure P1 dated 27.05.2024; issued with respect to audit observations regarding mismatch of input tax credit (ITC) for the three financial years and for the short payment of tax for the financial year 2019-2020. Replies were submitted and on 27.06.2024 by Annexure P5, documents were sought in support of the explanations offered. Communications were exchanged between the assessee and the 7th respondent and eventually the SCN was issued on 13.06.2025 produced as Annexure P11. Subsequently, the Additional Commissioner, respondent No.5 intimated, by Annexure P12 dated 27.06.2025, that the SCN has been transferred to ‘call book’ meaning thereby ‘kept in abeyance’. It was also intimated that the Department has contested the issue raised as an audit objection, before the Public Accounts Committee. 10. Later, a fresh notice was issued on 01.07.2025, reviving the earlier notice and proposing a protective demand since the proceedings are time bound under the GST law, there being no such measure of protective assessment statutorily permitted under the GST Act. 11. At the outset we have to notice that the proceedings under Section 73/74 can be initiated only on the satisfaction of the Assessing Officer. Even if observations/objections are made on audit, the Assessing Officer should enter his satisfaction before a notice is issued. Insofar as a notice under Section 74, the satisfaction should be not only of mismatch of ITC and short payment of tax having occurred, as is alleged in this case, the Officer should be satisfied that either fraud/willful misrepresentation/suppression had led to such mismatch or short payment of tax. 12. We are unable to countenance the argument of the learned ASG that Explanation 2 to Section 74 applies, since even according to him it stood omitted with effect from 01.11.2024. Equally fallacious is the argument that the proceedings were initiated prior to the expiry of limitation under Section 73. The limitation under Section 73 (10) is also to issue the order under Section 73 (9) and not to issue a notice and sub-section (2) of Section 73 provides for a notice, at least three months before the time limit under Section 73 (10). 13. The fact that the Department had taken up the objections on audit with the Public Accounts Committee itself indicates that there was no satisfaction at the end of the Department, meaning the Assessing Officer; as to the mismatch or the short fall of payment of tax having occurred, not to say anything about the suppression alleged. Further, but for a bland statement of availing of ITC for the three years ‘without documentary evidence and suppress the facts’ (sic), the SCN did not contain any foundational facts to validate such allegation of suppression. 14. It is not mere lip service to the provisions that is intended when an extended limitation period is provided for recovering an excess benefit availed, short payment or excess refund, from the assessee, especially when the allegation is of fraud/willful misrepresentation/suppression. The foundational facts which led to the inference arrived at of fraud/willful misrepresentation/suppression should be evident from the notice itself. The mere employment of such words will not indicate an application of mind, upon which alone the satisfaction can be arrived at. The words are not to be mechanically recited in the notice to enable recovery outside the normal limitation provided under the statute 15. In the facts of the above case, we do not find any such factual facts having been stated to make out a case of a deliberate device employed to evade tax or avail excess ITC, as coming out from the SCN. The bland statement made at some places of suppression of facts, merely to avail the extended period of limitation would barely suffice and puts to peril the notice under Section 74. The SCN on the above reasoning, cannot be sustained and the consequential Order-in-Original dated 26.12.2025 too is put in peril and both are set aside. However, the extended period of two years is not yet over, for all the three years since the three- year limitation expired only on 28.02.2025 for all the subject financial years. It would be open for the Department, if it is thought fit, to initiate an appropriate proceeding under Section 74, but with the foundational facts, coming out from the notice itself and an order being passed before 28.02.2027.” 44. A bare reading of paragraph ‘9’ of the judgment in M/s Tata Steel Limited (supra) would show that in the said case, the ‘SCN’ was issued with respect to audit observations regarding mismatch of input tax credit (ITC) for three financial years and for the short payment of tax for the Financial Year 2019-20. The appellant before the Hon’ble Supreme Court had filed replies vide Annexure ‘P-5’ whereafter documents were sought in support of the explanations offered and communications were exchanged between the assessee and the seventh respondent. In the said case, the facts would reveal that after the ‘SCN’ was issued on 13.06.2025 (Annexure ‘P-11’), subsequently the Additional Commissioner (Respondent No.5) intimated by Annexure ‘P-12’ dated 27.06.2025 that the ‘SCN’ has been transferred to ‘call book’, thus it was kept in abeyance. It was intimated to the appellant that the department has contested the issue raised as an audit objection, before the Public Accounts Committee. It is in this background of the fact the Hon’ble Supreme Court observed in paragraph ‘11’ that even if observations/objections are made on audit, the assessing officer should enter his satisfaction before a notice is issued. The officers should be satisfied that either fraud/willful misrepresentation/suppression had led to the mismatch of ITC or short payment of tax. In paragraph ‘13’, the Hon’ble Supreme Court has taken note of the fact that the department had taken up the objections on audit with the Public Accounts Committee and that itself indicates that there was no satisfaction at the end of the department meaning the assessing officer; as to the mismatch or the shortfall of payment of tax having occurred, not to say anything about the suppression alleged. It is in this background, the judgment of the Hon’ble Supreme Court came in case of M/s Tata Steel Limited (supra). The Hon’ble Supreme Court did not find any factual facts having been stated to make out a case of a deliberate device employed to evade tax or avail excess ITC, as coming out from the ‘SCN’. 45. In the facts of the present case, we have noticed that the final audit report together with copy of the decision of the MCM (Annexure ‘P-4’) was served upon the petitioner. There is no statement that the petitioner ever contested this final audit report. In this background, we find that the ‘SCN’ (Annexure ‘P5’) was issued, it categorically states about non-payment of GST amount, non-payment of late fee, short payment of tax and interest. It further provides in paragraph ‘7’ had the audit not been conducted of the records/registers of the noticee, the fact that these were an outright act in defiance of the law, made by the noticee, would have gone unnoticed. It is a case where it has been detected that there is a non-payment/short payment of GST in Rs.32,75,632/- and these facts have been revealed from the records in possession of the petitioner. The allegation in the ‘SCN’ is of blatant violation and defiance of the law by the petitioner in not paying the GST. 46. To this Court, it appears that where the audit report has found huge amounts of non-payment/short payment in contravention of law where the assessee is aware of delayed filing of GSTR-1, but does not pay the late fee and where he is unable to satisfy the proper officer with his bonafides in not making payment of such huge amount, the proper officer would be fully competent within the scope of sub-section (1) of Section 74 in issuing ‘SCN’ to the assessee. While it is true that in a simple case of non-payment/short payment of the GST for the bonafide reasons, a case cannot be brought within the purview of the extended period of limitation by invoking sub-section (1) of Section 74 read with sub-section (10) of Section 74, every case has to be looked into in its own facts. Where the proper officer has categorically stated about the communications made in this regard to the noticee/assessee, but the assessee does not comply with the final audit report within the prescribed period, the proper officer, being satisfied that it is a case of willful suppression and concealment, would be competent to take recourse to sub-section 1 of Section 74. 47. This Court is of the considered opinion that sitting in its extraordinary writ jurisdiction, this Court is not supposed to go into the sufficiency of the reasons for forming an opinion by the proper officer that any tax has not been paid or short paid or erroneously refunded or the ITC has been wrongly availed or utilized by reason of fraud or any willful statement or suppression of facts to evade tax. The opening line of sub-section (1) of Section 74 states “where it appears to the proper officer …..”. Dealing with the cases of reopening of an assessment under Section 147 read with Section 148 of the Income Tax Act, 1961 (in short ‘IT Act’), the Hon’ble Supreme Court had occasion to consider the scope and ambit of interference by the High Court in the notice issued under Section 148 of the IT Act. The Hon’ble Supreme Court held in the case of Sanand Properties Private Limited vs. Joint Commissioner of Income Tax and Ors. reported in (2026) 488 ITR 337 : 2026 SCC OnLine SC 851 inter alia that “… Since the belief is that of the Income-tax Officer, the sufficiency of reasons for forming the belief, is not for the court to judge but it is open to an assessee to establish that there in fact existed no belief or that the belief was not at all a bona fide one or was based on vague, irrelevant and non-specific information. To that limited extent, the court may look into the conclusion arrived at by the Income-tax Officer and examine whether there was any material available on the record from which the requisite belief could be formed by the Income-tax Officer and further whether that material had any rational connection or a live link for the formation of the requisite belief. It would be immaterial whether the Income-tax Officer at the time of making the original assessment, could or, could not have found by further enquiry or investigation, whether the transaction was genuine or not….” 48. In our opinion, in the facts of the present case, no fault may be found in the matter of issuance of ‘SCN’ under subsection (1) of Section 74 of the CGST Act. 49. To this Court, it appears that the ‘SCN’ (Annexure ‘P-4’) does not suffer from the vice of non-application of mind, much less any jurisdictional error. 50. Having reached to the aforesaid conclusion, this Court is of the considered opinion that the order in original (Annexure ‘P-6’), being an appealable order, this Court would not exercise its discretionary jurisdiction under Article 226 of the Constitution of India to entertain a challenge to the order in original (Annexure ‘P-6’). The petitioner may seek his remedy in accordance with law before the appellate authority or as may be advised to him in accordance with law. 51. At this stage, we place on record that learned ASG has informed this Court and it is also evident from the records that copy of the writ application was served upon his office on 21.08.2024. In such circumstance, the impugned order (Annexure ‘P-6’) being dated 22.04.2024, the service of copy took place well within a period of four months from the date of the order in original (Annexure ‘P-6’). Section 107 of the CGST Act provides a period of limitation of three months for filing an appeal. It further provides a window of one month for presenting an appeal and for this purpose, the appellate authority would be competent to allow the petitioner to present the appeal within a further period of one month. We, therefore, observe that if the petitioner prefers an appeal within a period of one month from today, the appellate authority shall consider the same on its own merit. 52. This writ application stands disposed of with liberty as aforesaid. 53. All questions are left open to the parties. 54.

Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); JMV Enterprise Vs Commissioner of Customs (CESTAT Ahmedabad) Summary : The Customs, Excise and Service Tax Appellate Tribunal (CESTAT), Ahmedabad, in JMV Enterprise Vs Commissioner of Customs, Mundra, partly allowed 14 connected customs appeals arising from two adjudication orders concerning the classification of imported polyurethane-coated textile fabrics, applicability of anti-dumping duty, demands on earlier imports, confiscation and penalties. The Division Bench comprising Judicial Member Somesh Arora and Technical Member Satendra Vikram Singh delivered Final Order Nos. 10847–10860/2026 on 19 November 2026. The dispute arose from imports by JMV Enterprise, Om Enterprises and other connected persons through Mundra, where textile products were declared under Customs Tariff Headings 59119990 and 59050090. The Department alleged that the goods were actually polyurethane-coated fabrics classifiable under CTH 59032090, attracting differential basic customs duty and anti-dumping duty under Notification No. 14/2022-Customs (ADD) dated 20 May 2022. The central controversy was whether the laboratory findings justified reassessment of both available and earlier consignments and whether the resulting demands, confiscation and penalties could legally be sustained. Acting on intelligence, officers of the Directorate of Revenue Intelligence conducted searches at the premises of JMV Enterprise, Jai Maa Enterprises and associated traders, including premises at Chembur, Mumbai. Statements were recorded from importers, proprietors, freight-forwarding representatives, traders and other persons connected with the transactions. Representative samples were drawn from goods available in warehouses and commercial premises and forwarded to the Central Revenues Control Laboratory (CRCL), Vadodara. The test reports described several varieties of woven, knitted and non-woven fabrics coated with compounded polyurethane, with certain samples also showing PVC or other film lamination. Based on these findings and the recorded statements, the Department concluded that the descriptions of felt woven coated fabric, textile coated fabric and glitter fabric did not reflect the actual nature of the goods. It further alleged that earlier consignments imported under similar descriptions were also liable to reclassification and additional customs duty. Two show-cause notices dated 7 November 2024 proposed reclassification, differential customs duty, anti-dumping duty, confiscation and penalties under various provisions of the Customs Act, 1962. In the JMV Enterprise proceedings, the adjudicating authority confirmed differential duty demands of ₹1,24,12,428 and ₹3,12,724, besides confiscation, redemption fine and penalties. In the Om Enterprises proceedings, differential customs duty of ₹1,15,64,091 was confirmed for earlier consignments, along with other consequential directions. The aggregate differential duty demands relating to past consignments consequently amounted to ₹2,42,89,243. The authorities invoked Section 28(4) for recovery, Section 28AA for interest, Section 111(m) for confiscation and provisions including Sections 112, 114A, 114AA and 117 for penalties. The importers and other affected persons challenged these determinations before the Tribunal, disputing both the classification and the evidentiary basis for extending the demands to earlier imports. The appellants submitted that the imported goods were coated with compounded polyurethane containing acrylics, fillers, PVC, stabilisers and other additives, making them chemically and commercially different from the polyurethane leather covered by the anti-dumping notification. They argued that the product under consideration identified in the Directorate General of Trade Remedies investigation was polyurethane leather manufactured through a combination of coagulation or wet processing and transfer coating or dry processing. According to the appellants, their goods were manufactured through the dry process alone, as supported by certificates issued by overseas suppliers. They further contended that a special fiscal levy such as anti-dumping duty must be interpreted strictly and could not be extended to goods outside the notified description. Reliance was placed on judicial decisions dealing with strict interpretation of fiscal notifications, technical product characteristics and the limits of anti-dumping duty. The appellants also questioned the reliability and sufficiency of the CRCL reports for determining coverage under the anti-dumping notification. They maintained that descriptions such as fabric coated with compounded polyurethane did not conclusively establish that the goods were polyurethane leather or otherwise fell within the notified product category. They referred to decisions distinguishing coated fabrics from laminated fabrics and argued that the Department had failed to establish the manufacturing process, product characteristics and commercial identity required for the levy. A separate objection concerned the application of test reports from available consignments to past imports. According to the appellants, every Bill of Entry constituted a separate assessment, and the composition of goods imported earlier could not be presumed merely because later consignments or goods found at buyers’ premises had been tested. They further challenged reliance on statements recorded under Section 108 without satisfying the evidentiary requirements of Section 138B. Examining the classification issue, the Tribunal observed that Heading 5911 covered specified textile products and articles for technical uses, whereas Heading 5903 specifically covered textile fabrics impregnated, coated, covered or laminated with plastics, other than fabrics falling under Heading 5902. Within Heading 5903, the relevant tariff subdivision specifically addressed polyurethane-coated fabrics. The laboratory reports concerning the available consignments established that the imported fabrics were coated with compounded polyurethane. The Tribunal therefore found no justification for retaining their declared classification under CTH 59119990. It agreed with the adjudicating authorities that the relevant goods were correctly classifiable under CTH 59032090. The Tribunal consequently upheld the reassessment of available consignments and the differential customs duty arising from the corrected classification. On the question of compounded polyurethane, the Tribunal examined the overseas suppliers’ certificates and noted that polyurethane resin was mixed with acrylic emulsions, pigments, fillers, plasticisers, catalysts and other additives to obtain particular coating properties and finished-product characteristics. It found that such compounding was undertaken to achieve the desired quality, texture and performance of the material and did not, by itself, alter the essential polyurethane character of the coating. The appellants were also unable to produce technical literature sufficient to establish their proposed distinction. The Tribunal accordingly rejected the contention that the presence of additives automatically excluded the goods from Notification No. 14/2022-Customs (ADD). It further distinguished the authorities relied upon concerning incomplete machinery, parts of compact fluorescent lamps and challenges to anti-dumping findings, holding that those decisions did not displace the levy applicable to the goods involved in the present appeals. The Tribunal separately examined the argument that goods manufactured exclusively through the dry coating process were outside the scope of anti-dumping duty. Although the appellants relied on the description of the manufacturing process appearing in the DGTR investigation, the Tribunal referred to the Designated Authority’s final findings, which specifically considered requests for exclusion of products manufactured using only the dry technique. The Designated Authority had found that sufficient evidence had not been produced to establish a meaningful distinction between goods manufactured through the two processes and that the resulting products were commercially and functionally substitutable. It therefore declined to exclude products manufactured through the dry technique. The Tribunal also noted that the operative anti-dumping notification did not prescribe a particular manufacturing process as a condition for levy. Accordingly, it upheld the applicability of anti-dumping duty to the available consignments, together with their reassessment, confiscation under Section 111(m) and the redemption fines imposed in lieu of confiscation. The Tribunal, however, accepted the appellants’ objections concerning demands on past consignments. It held that laboratory reports relating to available consignments could not automatically establish the composition or classification of goods imported under earlier Bills of Entry. The Department had not produced independent test reports for the earlier imports or reliable evidence linking the goods recovered from buyers’ premises to the specific past consignments under dispute. Furthermore, the adjudicating authorities had relied on statements recorded during investigation without first determining their admissibility in accordance with Section 138B of the Customs Act. Relying particularly on Stonex India Pvt. Ltd. Vs Commissioner of Customs, Mundra, and the principles recognised in Shalimar Paints Ltd. and Marks Marketing Pvt. Ltd., the Tribunal held that the evidentiary foundation for the demands relating to past imports was inadequate. It therefore set aside the confiscation of earlier imported goods and the associated differential customs duty demands and interest, while preserving the Department’s action concerning the available consignments. In determining the consequential relief, the Tribunal observed that the adjudicating authorities had examined the respective roles of the importers and connected persons while imposing penalties. Nevertheless, because the duty demands and confiscation relating to past consignments had been set aside, the penalties required proportionate reduction. In the Om Enterprises proceedings, the Tribunal reduced the penalty on Om Enterprises to ₹6 lakh under Section 112(a)(ii), on proprietor Hari Kishan to ₹2 lakh under Section 117, and modified the penalties imposed on Arun Jyoti Mahajan, Kapil Kotiya and Sabu George. In the JMV Enterprise proceedings, it reduced the penalty on JMV Enterprise to ₹2 lakh, on proprietor Sanjeev Shekhar Malhotra to ₹1.5 lakh, and similarly reduced penalties on other concerned persons. It also directed that no penalty under Section 114A would survive against Jai Maa Enterprise and Skytex in the specified proceedings. The Tribunal thus partly allowed the appeals, establishing that correct classification and anti-dumping liability for tested consignments do not dispense with the Department’s obligation to establish independent and legally admissible evidence for demands relating to earlier imports.

Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); Stock And Bond Trading Company Vs DCIT (ITAT Mumbai) Section 68: Missing Lenders’ ITRs Cannot Sustain Loan Addition When Primary Evidence Is Filed and AO Makes No Enquiry Background of the Disputex The assessee, engaged in trading in shares and derivatives, filed its return declaring nil income. During scrutiny assessment under section 143(3), the Assessing Officer made additions towards unsecured loans under section 68, along with other adjustments. The dispute reached the Tribunal in an earlier round, when the matter was restored to the Commissioner of Income Tax (Appeals) for consideration of additional evidence furnished by the assessee. During the proceedings following that remand, the Commissioner called for a report from the Assessing Officer. After considering the report, the Commissioner granted partial relief but sustained an addition of ₹2,06,70,000 relating to loans from six lenders. The assessee challenged this surviving addition before the Tribunal. Remand Report Accepted Identity and Genuineness The assessee furnished documents concerning ten lenders, including PAN or assessment particulars, confirmations, ledger accounts and bank statements of the lenders and the assessee. The Assessing Officer expressly accepted the identity of all ten lenders and the genuineness of the loan transactions. He also accepted the creditworthiness of four lenders on the basis of documents such as return acknowledgements, an intimation under section 143(1), tax deduction certificates and financial statements. However, he considered creditworthiness unproved for the remaining six lenders because their income-tax returns or other satisfactory evidence of financial capacity had not been furnished. These six loans comprised ₹40 lakh from Anil A & Co., ₹15 lakh from Chavda Brothers, ₹25 lakh from Mihir Consultancy & Trading Co., ₹16.70 lakh from Manoj Mittal HUF, and ₹55 lakh each from Vinit Enterprises and Jay Shree Procon Pvt. Ltd. Why the Commissioner Sustained the Addition The Commissioner held that establishing identity and genuineness did not dispense with the requirement of proving creditworthiness. According to the Commissioner, routing transactions through banking channels did not, by itself, demonstrate the lenders’ financial capacity. Similarly, repayment in a subsequent year could not cure a failure to explain the credit satisfactorily in the year of receipt. The Commissioner also rejected reliance on relief granted in the assessee’s own case for assessment year 2014-15, observing that each assessment year required examination on its own facts. Accordingly, the addition concerning the six lenders was sustained. Assessee’s Arguments Before the Tribunal The assessee emphasised that it had furnished the lenders’ particulars, confirmations and bank statements, together with evidence of receipt and subsequent repayment through banking channels. Its principal objection was that the Assessing Officer had conducted no independent enquiry with the identified lenders, either during assessment or during the remand proceedings. The assessee argued that its primary burden stood discharged and that the Revenue could not sustain the addition merely because certain lenders’ returns were unavailable. It relied on decisions including Orissa Corporation, Rohini Builders and Ayachi Chandrasekhar Narsangji. It also submitted that the statutory requirement concerning explanation of the source of loans or borrowings introduced from assessment year 2023-24 could not be applied to the year under consideration. The Revenue supported the Commissioner’s order but alternatively requested another remand to examine creditworthiness, including through notices under sections 133(6) or 131. Tribunal’s Findings The Tribunal found that the assessee had furnished almost all primary particulars. The lenders’ identities were undisputed, the transactions had passed through banking channels, confirmations and bank statements were available, and the loans had subsequently been repaid. On the cumulative facts, the Tribunal held that the assessee had discharged its primary onus and that the burden had shifted to the Assessing Officer. It found the absence of lenders’ ITRs insufficient to sustain the addition in these circumstances. Despite having the lenders’ PANs, addresses and confirmations, the Assessing Officer had made no effort to verify the assessee’s explanation directly with them. The Tribunal also criticised the Commissioner’s approach. Having powers co-terminous with those of the Assessing Officer, the Commissioner could have conducted the necessary enquiries or directed the Assessing Officer to undertake them. Merely relying on the adverse conclusion in the remand report, without addressing the supporting evidence and subsequent repayments, was held unjustified. The Tribunal particularly noted that this was already the second round of litigation. Decision and Author’s Comments The Tribunal set aside the appellate order on the disputed issue and directed deletion of the addition of ₹2,06,70,000. The appeal was allowed; the matter was not sent back for another enquiry. The decision does not establish that PAN details, banking channels or repayment invariably prove every loan. Its significance lies in the combined evidentiary record and the Revenue’s failure to investigate an explanation supported by identifiable lenders and transaction documents. Creditworthiness remains relevant under section 68, but an adverse conclusion must engage with the evidence available. Here, the Department accepted identity and genuineness yet left its remaining concern untested despite opportunities in both the original and remand proceedings. The practical lesson is to maintain a complete lender-wise record, including financial capacity wherever available. Equally, once substantial primary evidence is furnished, the assessing and appellate authorities must examine it meaningfully before sustaining an addition. Cases Discussed CIT v. In law and in the facts and circumstances of the Appellant’s case, learned CIT(A) has erred in confirming the addition of Rs. 2,06,70,000/-under section 68 of the Income Tax Act. In the absence of such evidence, the requirement of establishing creditworthiness, which is a mandatory condition u/s 68, remained unfulfilled in respect of these six lenders. 5.4 In the subsequent hearings before him, the assessee reiterated that the unsecured loans were repaid in the subsequent assessment year through banking channels and, therefore, the transactions should be treated as genuine. The appellate authority stated that this contention could not be accepted as repayment of a loan in a subsequent year does not, by itself, cure the defect in the year of receipt if the assessee fails to establish the creditworthiness of the lenders at the time the loans were taken. Section 68 of the Act requires the assessee to satisfactorily explain the nature and source of the credit in the year of receipt. Besides, even before him, the assessee merely furnished Balance sheet schedules of certain lenders. However, no supporting bank statements, transaction-wise repayment details or documentary evidence evidencing the actual flow of funds had been placed on record. Such incomplete documentation did not discharge the onus of proving creditworthiness. 5.5 The appellate authority further noted that the assessee placed reliance on the appellate order passed in its own case for A.Y., 2014-15 contending that the issue was covered in its favour on the principle of consistency. This argument was not acceptable. Each assessment year is a separate and independent unit and the principle of res judicata does not apply to income-tax proceedings. The relief granted in A.Y. 2014-15 was based on the specific findings recorded in the remand proceedings for that year. In the present year, however, the remand report contained a clear finding that creditworthiness had not been established in respect of six lenders. Relief could not, therefore, be granted merely by placing reliance on the outcome of a different assessment year. 5.6 The assessee further argued that PAN details, ledger accounts and bank statements had been furnished for all lenders and that the transactions were routed through banking channels. While these documents may establish identity and genuineness, they did not by themselves, establish creditworthiness. Mere routing of funds through bank accounts does not demonstrate the financial capacity of the lenders to advance substantial sums. As noted in the remand report, the absence of income-tax returns or financial statements for six lenders leaves the issue of creditworthiness unproved. The reliance placed by on judicial precedents was misplaced. In the present case, the remand report contained a categorical finding that creditworthiness had not been proved for six lenders. In the result, the ground on this issue was partly allowed, by deleting the addition u/s 68 to the extent of Rs. 85,40,000/- and sustaining the balance addition of Rs. 2,06,70,00:1h 6. Before us, the ld.AR has reiterated the same contentions as made before the ld.CIT(A) emphasizing that the loans were repaid subsequently to the lenders. The transactions both for taking loan and repayment thereof in the subsequent had been through banking channels and the assessee had provided PAN, confirmation of each of the parties and the bank statement evidencing such payment and repayment of the loan. It also submitted the bank statement of the lenders when the loan was advanced. The AO had not carried out any independent inquiry with regard to any of the said six parties even while submitting the remand report. The ld.AR has further submitted that under section 68, onus to prove source of source of a loan or borrowing is mandatory w.e.f. 01.04.2023 i.e. from A.Y 2023-24.By submitting these documents, the initial onus of proof shifts completely on the Revenue as the assessee had provided the lender’s PAN card, confirmation letter, and bank summaries showing that transactions happened via proper banking channels. 6.1 Reliance has also been placed on various judicial decisions i.e. Orissa Corporation (159 ITR 78) (SC), CIT vs. Lovely Exports Pvt. ITO (ITAT Delhi)etc. It is claimed that the assessee fully discharged its initial legal burden under Section 68 by providing explicit identity particulars (names, addresses, PANs), loan account confirmations, and bank statements of the three lending entities. The ld.DR relied on the appellate order. However, he requested to remand the case back to the AO for making necessary enquiries as deemed fit for examining the creditworthiness of the lenders. The assessee be directed to submit the respective tax details, financials of the lenders etc. to show their credit worthiness. He would examine the lenders to find out the same as nothing prevented him from issuing notices u/s 133(6)/131 of the Act independently. 8. We have carefully considered the relevant facts of the case. As discussed above, it appears that the assessee has submitted almost all primary details in respect of all the lenders. The respective identity has not been doubted by the AO. The transactions have all been done through banking channels and the assessee has submitted the respective bank statements and confirmations. The said loans have also been repaid by the assessee. Therefore, in so far as the assessee is concerned, we are of the considered opinion that it has discharged its primary onus which now shifted to the AO who repeated the additions on the ground that ITRs were not filed. However, such a ground does not appear convincing enough for holding the assessee as a defaulter. We also find that despite such basic details made available by the assessee,the AO did not make any effort to cross verify the contentions of the assessee from respective lenders neither in the original nor in the remand proceedings, whose confirmations, PAN adderesses etc. were already with him. 8.1 It is noted that the ld. AR in support of the contention has relied upon the various judicial precedent which we have considered and taking into account the overall aspect of the case we are of the considered view that the assessee has discharged its initial burden to prove the transaction. Once, the assessee has placed on record all the details of the immediate source and the details of the source of the source and there is no reason to doubt that source when other material have been filed with supporting evidence. Even the ld.CIT(A) has failed to adjudicate the issue in judicious manner and solely depended on the Remand report of the assessee. Having co-terminous powers of an Assessing officer, he is duly empowered to make necessary enquiries himself or get it done by the AO. He has also not appreciated the fact that the amounts were repaid in the subsequent years. Thus, we are of the considered view that the ld. CIT(A) was not justified in upholding the addition made by the to the extent of Rs 2.06 cr. without realising that the proceedings before him was second round of litigation and therefore, it was all the more expected of him to adjudicate the issue in hand in a fair and judicious manner. 8.2 Considering the entirety of the matter, we hold that the appellate order is not sustainable. Accordingly, the same is set aside and the AO is directed to delete the impugned addition, thus allowing the ground of appeal in this regard. 9. In the result, the appeal of the assessee stands allowed. Order pronounced in the open court on 13/10/2026.

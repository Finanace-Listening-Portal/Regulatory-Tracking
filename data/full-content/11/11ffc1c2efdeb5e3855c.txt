Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); Supreme Petrochem Ltd. Vs Union of India (Delhi High Court) Summary: The Delhi High Court, in Supreme Petrochem Ltd. Vs Union of India and Anr., decided W.P.(C) 10289/2020 by judgment dated 17 November 2026, upholding the constitutional validity of Section 9A(1) of the Customs Tariff Act, 1975, read with the relevant provisions of the Anti-Dumping Rules, 1995. A Division Bench comprising Justice Anil Kshetarpal and Justice Harish Vaidyanathan Shankar held that the Central Government possesses statutory discretion to impose or decline to impose anti-dumping duty even where the Designated Authority has recorded affirmative findings concerning dumping, injury to domestic industry and the causal relationship between them. The Court rejected the contention that this discretion was arbitrary, excessive or unguided under Article 14 of the Constitution. It further held that the statutory mechanism permitting the Government to take the ultimate decision was consistent with India’s obligations under the WTO Anti-Dumping Agreement. The judgment clarifies the distinction between the technical determination undertaken by the Designated Authority and the broader public-interest assessment entrusted to the Central Government. The controversy arose from an investigation concerning imports of polystyrene originating in or exported from Iran, Malaysia, Singapore, Chinese Taipei, the United Arab Emirates and the United States of America. Supreme Petrochem Limited, a domestic producer, was among the applicants seeking anti-dumping protection against the imported goods. Following its investigation, the Designated Authority issued affirmative final findings dated 12 June 2020 under File No. 6/10/2019, recommending definitive anti-dumping duty on a country-wise and producer-wise basis. However, the Central Government, through Office Memorandum No. 354/88/2020-TRU dated 11 November 2020, decided not to impose the recommended duty. Aggrieved by the decision, the petitioner invoked Article 226 of the Constitution, seeking a declaration that the statutory provisions were unconstitutional, quashing of the Office Memorandum and a direction requiring implementation of the Designated Authority’s recommendations. The petitioner contended that Section 9A of the Customs Tariff Act, when interpreted in the light of Articles 9.1 and 9.5 of the WTO Anti-Dumping Agreement, contemplated an integrated decision-making process rather than two separate stages of technical recommendation and governmental discretion. According to the petitioner, the Designated Authority performed its functions on behalf of the Central Government, and its findings followed a quasi-judicial investigation involving disclosure of relevant information, consideration of evidence and an opportunity of hearing. It was argued that permitting the Government to disregard those findings without conducting a corresponding hearing would undermine natural justice and the statutory framework. Reliance was placed on Automotive Tyre Manufacturers Association v. Designated Authority, Reliance Industries Ltd. v. Designated Authority and Saurashtra Chemicals Ltd. v. Union of India. The petitioner also maintained that the impugned decision deprived it of an effective statutory appellate remedy under Section 9C. On the merits of the refusal to impose duty, the petitioner argued that the Central Government had improperly relied upon considerations concerning domestic production capacity and the demand-supply position of polystyrene. It submitted that the Designated Authority had already examined the relevant economic circumstances before recommending anti-dumping measures and that the Government could not effectively substitute its own technical assessment. The petitioner challenged the sufficiency of the material supporting the public-interest justification and relied upon Dai-Ichi Karkaria Ltd. v. Union of India. It further submitted that anti-dumping duty was a trade-remedial measure rather than an ordinary fiscal levy and that the decision to impose or refuse duty could not be treated as wholly immune from procedural fairness or judicial scrutiny. In the alternative, it argued that even a legislative or quasi-legislative exercise of statutory discretion remained reviewable where the decision was arbitrary, unreasonable or unsupported by relevant considerations. The Union of India defended the statutory scheme by emphasising the permissive language of Section 9A(1), Rule 18(1) and Article 9.1 of the WTO Anti-Dumping Agreement. It submitted that the Designated Authority’s function was to investigate dumping, determine injury and recommend appropriate measures, whereas the Central Government retained responsibility for deciding whether the recommended duty should actually be imposed. The Government argued that anti-dumping law was intended to address unfair international trade practices and protect competitive conditions, rather than guarantee domestic producers insulation from imports. It relied upon decisions including Commissioner of Customs v. G.M. Exports, Union of India v. Agricas LLP, S&S Enterprise v. Designated Authority, Alembic Ltd. v. Union of India and Eveready Industries Ltd. v. Union of India. The respondents maintained that the decision not to impose duty was taken in the wider public interest because domestic production was inadequate to meet demand and the levy could adversely affect downstream industries. Examining the statutory and international framework, the High Court held that Article 9.1 of the WTO Anti-Dumping Agreement expressly recognises the discretion of the importing Member’s authorities to decide whether anti-dumping duty should be imposed even after the necessary conditions have been satisfied. The provision does not require automatic imposition of duty following an affirmative determination of dumping and injury. Nor does it prescribe a single institutional mechanism through which the importing country must exercise its authority. The Court observed that Section 9A(1) uses the expression “may”, while Rule 17 characterises the Designated Authority’s conclusions concerning the appropriate duty as recommendations. These provisions establish a deliberate distinction between the specialised investigative function and the ultimate governmental decision. Accordingly, the two-stage mechanism under Indian law was neither inconsistent with the WTO Agreement nor unconstitutional merely because the Government could decline to act upon an affirmative recommendation. Addressing the challenge under Article 14, the Court rejected the argument that the Central Government enjoyed unrestricted or arbitrary discretion. It held that the statutory power must be understood in the context of the Designated Authority’s findings, the objectives of the anti-dumping regime and the wider public interest. The Government’s discretion was therefore not a licence to disregard relevant material or act for extraneous purposes. At the same time, the Court recognised that anti-dumping measures involve economic policy considerations extending beyond the injury suffered by individual domestic producers. It distinguished the technical determination of dumping margins, injury, non-injurious price and causal connection from the policy decision whether a duty should be imposed. The Court relied upon the statutory distinction recognised in Designated Authority v. Andhra Petrochemicals Ltd. and the broader principles governing economic legislation and judicial restraint. Consequently, the constitutional challenge to the impugned provisions failed. On the question of natural justice, the Court examined whether the Central Government was required to conduct an additional hearing before declining to impose duty. It distinguished the quasi-judicial proceedings conducted by the Designated Authority from the legislative or quasi-legislative character of the Government’s ultimate decision under Section 9A(1). The Court observed that the Government was not reopening or redetermining the technical findings recorded after investigation. Instead, it was exercising a separate statutory discretion concerning the desirability of imposing the recommended measure. The principles applicable to hearings before the Designated Authority could not automatically be extended to the Government’s subsequent policy decision. The Court also considered the petitioner’s reliance on Section 9C and the authorities addressing appellate remedies, but did not treat those arguments as grounds for invalidating the statutory scheme or the impugned Office Memorandum. Turning to the factual justification for the refusal, the High Court considered the material concerning the domestic availability of polystyrene, the requirements of downstream industries and the consequences of imposing additional import costs. The Government had referred to the inadequacy of domestic production and the effect that anti-dumping duty could have upon the demand-supply balance. A material development was the closure of one of the three domestic producers, leaving only two surviving producers in the relevant market. The Court considered the distinction between the Designated Authority’s injury analysis and the Government’s assessment of the broader consequences of restricting imports through an additional duty. It accepted that the effect on downstream users, domestic supply constraints and the risk of market concentration were legitimate public-interest considerations. The Court also examined the explanation placed before it regarding the Government’s decision and found a rational connection between the material considered and the refusal to impose anti-dumping duty. It declined to substitute its own economic assessment for that of the competent authority. In conclusion, the Delhi High Court held that neither the constitutional challenge to Section 9A(1) and the relevant Anti-Dumping Rules nor the challenge to the Office Memorandum dated 11 November 2020 had merit. The Central Government’s decision was legislative or quasi-legislative in character, was supported by relevant public-interest considerations and did not warrant interference under Article 226. Accordingly, W.P.(C) 10289/2020 was dismissed. The Court separately clarified that W.P.(C) 16640/2022 filed by Carborundum Universal Limited, W.P.(C) 16647/2022 filed by Grindwell Norton Limited and W.P.(C) 17186/2022 filed by Asahi India Glass Limited concerned different products and required independent consideration. The Registry was directed to list those petitions with W.P.(C) 9754/2026, Indrayani Sales Private Ltd. v. Union of India, before the appropriate Roster Bench on 8 January 2027. The judgment therefore establishes that affirmative anti-dumping findings do not create an automatic entitlement to duty, while preserving judicial review of governmental discretion for constitutional and legal infirmities.

Summary : Family businesses enjoy patient capital, loyal relationships, quick decisions and a powerful sense of legacy, but they also face distinctive risks when ownership, management, family finances and personal relationships overlap. The division of Reliance Industries between Mukesh Ambani and Anil Ambani following their father’s death without a will illustrates the consequences of leaving succession arrangements unresolved. Drawing on the three-circle model developed by Harvard researchers Renato Tagiuri and John Davis, this article presents the Four Separations framework for building resilient family enterprises: separate ownership from management, distinguish returns on ownership from compensation for work, keep family money separate from company funds, and conduct family conversations independently of business decisions. It explains how to begin succession planning five to ten years before a proposed handover, assess and develop successors, transfer decision-making authority gradually, and give the founder a meaningful future role. It also outlines practical governance mechanisms, including a family constitution, family council, independent directors, consistent wills and shareholders’ agreements, and professional management. Through a hypothetical textile trading business, practical indicators of progress, frequently asked questions and actionable next steps for family leaders, successors, finance heads and independent advisers, the article shows how written rules and clear responsibilities can prevent avoidable disputes. Family businesses that plan early and establish transparent governance are better positioned to preserve relationships, professionalise operations and transfer their legacy across generations. Separate family conversations from business decisions Quick recap Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); Introduction When Dhirubhai Ambani, the founder of Reliance Industries, died in July 2002, he left no will. Inside two years, a public rift between his sons Mukesh and Anil was front-page news. In 2005, after mediation by their mother, Kokilaben, the group was split between them. Extraordinary vision had built the business. Nobody had written down who would own and lead it next. Why this story matters to every family firm If it can happen in one of the world’s most successful business families, it can happen in any family business — in Guwahati, Jakarta, Lagos or São Paulo. In many family firms, the meetings that matter most don’t take place in the office. They take place over dinner, at weddings, in the car on the way to a relative’s home. Decisions are informal. Salaries are flexible. The business meets family needs, and the family props up the business when cash is short. For years, it works. Then the founder ages. The children grow up. Some join the business and some do not. Spouses arrive; cousins appear. And a question nobody has ever answered suddenly becomes urgent: Who owns what, who decides what, and who comes next? The strengths — and the hidden risks Family companies are the backbone of many economies, India’s included. They enjoy advantages that professionally run firms envy: Patient money and long-term thinking. Deep loyalty from staff, suppliers and customers. Quick decisions and personal relationships. A sense of legacy that goes beyond quarterly results. They also face risks most firms do not: Roles handed out by birth rather than ability. Family quarrels spilling into business decisions. No clear boundary between family money and company money. Succession left open until it becomes a crisis. Many cultures have a proverb that wealth is lost by the third generation — “shirtsleeves to shirtsleeves in three generations” in English, with versions in Chinese, Italian, Japanese and other languages. Research estimates of how many family firms reach the third generation vary widely by country and method, but the pattern is consistent enough to take seriously. Most family firms are not destroyed by competitors. They are divided at the dinner table. What history teaches: the three circles In the 1980s, Harvard researchers Renato Tagiuri and John Davis described the family business as three overlapping circles: family, ownership and business. A person may sit in one, two or all three — a relative who owns shares but doesn’t work in the company, a non-family manager, a family member who works there but owns nothing. Each position carries different expectations. Most family conflict arises from treating these roles as if they were identical — assuming that ownership guarantees a job, that a job guarantees ownership, or that being family guarantees both. The model has endured for four decades because it explains so much so simply. The Four Separations From the three circles comes a practical framework I call the Four Separations. Family businesses that last tend to keep apart four things that others mix together: 1. Separate ownership from management Not every child should run the company. Loving a child is not the same as trusting their leadership. Some successors excel; others would be happier and more effective elsewhere. Fix: A family member can be a proud owner without being managing director. Set entry criteria for family members: education, experience outside the firm, a role with measurable responsibilities and the same review process as every other employee. 2. Separate rewards for owning from rewards for working Parents want to treat their children equally. But giving equal shares to a child who works in the business and one who doesn’t can breed resentment on both sides. Fix: Pay family members who work in the business a market salary for their role, and reward ownership through dividends. Discuss the arrangement openly before it becomes a grievance. 3. Separate family money from company money Household bills paid by the company. Undocumented loans to relatives. Property bought in the company’s name and used by one branch of the family. Harmless while one person controls everything — explosive once there are several heirs. Fix: Document every transaction between the family and the business. Pay family members through proper payroll. Record loans with terms. Review related-party transactions with your accountant each year. 4. Separate family conversations from business decisions Many families avoid hard subjects to keep the peace. But silence is not peace; unspoken resentment builds. Fix: Hold regular family meetings — apart from business meetings — to discuss expectations, worries and plans, with a neutral facilitator if needed. Let the company be run in the boardroom, not at the dinner table. Quick recap The short version: family firms endure when ownership, rewards, money and conversations each stay in their own lane — and fail when the lanes blur. The succession question The founder’s reluctance is the largest succession risk of all. Many founders put succession off because the business is part of who they are, and stepping back feels like disappearing. Yet an unplanned succession — forced by sudden illness or death — is the most damaging kind. Fix: Begin planning five to ten years before the intended handover: 1. Assess candidates honestly — family and non-family alike. 2. Develop the successor through outside experience, mentoring and rotation across functions. 3. Share decision-making in stages, with growing authority. 4. Hand over formally, with a set date and a clear announcement. 5. Give the founder a meaningful next role — chair, mentor or leader of a new venture — so stepping back feels like a promotion rather than an exit. The structures that help families last Enduring family enterprises don’t rely on goodwill alone. They build governance: A family constitution or charter covering values, rules for employing family members, share transfers, dividend policy, conflict resolution and the role of in-laws. A family council to deal with family matters so they stay out of the boardroom. A board with independent members who can challenge assumptions family members may not feel free to question. Wills and shareholders’ agreements that are consistent. A will that conflicts with the company’s articles can lead to litigation. Professional managers wherever the family lacks expertise — given genuine authority. A clear legal and tax structure — holding firms, family trusts or other arrangements chosen with professional advice rather than inherited informally. Signs of progress Separation reveals itself in calmer decisions and cleaner figures: Pay for family members is tied to defined roles and market rates. Personal expenses no longer turn up in the company’s books. Family meetings and board meetings take place separately, with separate agendas. Disagreements are resolved through agreed processes, not seniority or emotion. Outside talent stays and grows, because promotion is not reserved for relatives. The decade ahead India’s family companies face one of the largest generational handovers in their history, as founders from the 1980s and 1990s pass control to children who have frequently studied abroad and hold different ambitions. Many of the next generation won’t want to join. Family firms that separate ownership from management, adopt a family constitution and build professional teams will come through the transition. Those that depend on unwritten loyalty may not. Next steps for each role Head of the family: set down on one page who should own, who should manage and who should govern the business in ten years’ time. Next generation: ask for a defined role, a job description and a performance review, like any other employee. Finance head: list every family-related payment made by the business in the past 12 months. Independent adviser or director: propose a family council meeting held separately from business meetings. A smaller-scale example Take a hypothetical textile trading firm run by two brothers and their sons. Everyone draws money as needed; nobody has a defined role; and both the elder brother’s son and the younger brother’s son expect to take charge. An adviser helps them make four changes: fixed salaries linked to roles; a monthly business meeting held apart from family dinners; a written succession plan naming one cousin managing director and the other head of a new export division; and an annual dividend for all family shareholders. The arguments don’t stop. But now they have a venue, a process and an end point. Frequently asked questions “Should family members receive market salaries?” Yes, tied to the roles they actually perform. Returns on ownership should come separately, through dividends. “What is a family constitution?” A written agreement among family members covering ownership, governance, roles, succession and how disputes will be resolved. “How do we deal with a family member who is underperforming?” Apply the same standards you would to any employee, with clear feedback and support — and keep the business conversation separate from the family relationship. Where things stand (October 2026): In India, succession to business assets depends on wills, the applicable personal succession law, company articles and any shareholders’ agreement. Family settlements, holding structures and private trusts each carry specific tax, stamp duty and regulatory consequences. Take advice from both a lawyer and a tax professional, and review arrangements whenever family circumstances change. The toughest pushback — and a reply The pushback: “We are a family, not a corporation. Constitutions, councils and independent directors will make our relationships cold and legalistic. We trust one another.” The reply: Structure doesn’t replace affection; it protects it. The families most likely to fall apart are those forced to improvise answers to painful questions in the middle of a crisis — after a death, a divorce or a business loss. A family that agreed the rules calmly in advance can disagree without fighting, because the rules already say what happens next. Writing things down is not mistrust. It’s a gift to the next generation. Common mistakes to avoid Placing heirs in senior roles without training or accountability. Keeping all shares in the founder’s name, with no will or succession plan. Treating the business as the family’s bank. Letting family disputes hold up business decisions. Giving non-family managers titles without authority — capable people will leave. Assuming “we’ll sort it out later.” Later is frequently a courtroom. Try this today (30 minutes) Draw the three circles — family, ownership and business — on a sheet of paper, and write each family member’s and key manager’s name in the right place. Then ask, for each person: What do they expect from the business over the next ten years? Have those expectations ever been spoken aloud? Does any written document reflect them? Every blank is a conversation your family needs to have — this year, while everyone is healthy and still talking. Key takeaways Family firms enjoy unique strengths — patience, loyalty, speed — and carry unique risks: roles by birth, mixed money and delayed succession. The three-circle model of family, ownership and business explains most family conflicts. Apply the Four Separations: ownership from management, owners’ returns from employees’ pay, family money from company money, and family conversations from business decisions. Begin succession five to ten years ahead, with a dignified future role for the founder. Put governance in place: a family constitution, a family council, independent board voices, wills and agreements that match, and professional managers. Families that last for generations don’t agree on everything. They agree, in advance, on how they will disagree.

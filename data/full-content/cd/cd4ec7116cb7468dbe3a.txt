Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); Zydus Healthcare Limited Vs ACIT (Sikkim High Court) Summary: The Sikkim High Court allowed three writ petitions filed by Zydus Healthcare Limited challenging reassessment proceedings for Assessment Years 2015-16, 2017-18 and 2018-19. The central question was whether audit objections, without new books of account, documents or evidence revealing escaped income, could justify reopening completed assessments beyond three years under the reassessment provisions applicable to the impugned notices. The Court distinguished an audit objection constituting “information” under Section 148 from the separate jurisdictional requirement under Section 149(1)(b). It held that an audit opinion based on material already available during assessment did not, merely because it was recorded in writing, become new documentary evidence authorising reassessment. For AY 2015-16, the original assessment dated 26 December 2018 had accepted the returned income as nil. Reopening followed an audit objection concerning the treatment of an excise-duty refund of ₹22,99,92,702 as a capital receipt and its alleged effect on taxable income and book profits. Notices under Sections 148A(b) and 148 and an order under Section 148A(d) were issued, followed by a reassessment order dated 26 March 2024. The assessee challenged the initiation of proceedings as lacking jurisdiction, barred by limitation and based on a change of opinion. The Revenue argued, among other things, that the refund deposited in the bank account represented an asset, that the original scrutiny had been limited, and that the subsequent assessment and statutory appeal rendered the writ challenge inappropriate. The Court rejected the preliminary objections. Following Red Chilli International Sales v. ITO, it held that an alternative remedy did not bar examination of a jurisdictional challenge. Completion of reassessment during the pendency of the writ petition did not make that challenge infructuous: if the initiating notice was without jurisdiction, the subsequent assessment could not survive. The issues could be decided on admitted material and did not require resolution of disputed facts. Referring to Union of India v. Rajiv Bansal, the Court applied the law existing when the notice was issued. On the merits of reopening, the Court held that the Assessing Officer had to establish possession of the material required by Section 149(1)(b), disclose it in the Section 148A(d) order, and demonstrate the statutory basis for invoking the extended period. An audit objection could prompt independent examination and acquisition of relevant evidence; it did not itself compel reopening or confer jurisdiction. The Department had produced no new material beyond the audit objection. Re-examination of the same returns, financial disclosures and bank records was a change of opinion. The Court discussed CIT v. Kelvinator of India Limited, the Gujarat High Court’s Songwon Speciality Chemicals decision and the Supreme Court’s dismissal of the Revenue’s challenge, and Sapphire Foods India Ltd. v. ACIT, which distinguished reassessment from review of a concluded assessment. The Court also examined the excise-duty refund issue. The assessee had disclosed the refund and claimed its exclusion as a capital receipt arising under the industrial policy for establishing an undertaking in Sikkim. Its treatment rested on judicial authority, including Shree Balaji Alloys. The Court noted that the Supreme Court had dismissed the Revenue’s appeals in that litigation by reference to Ponni Sugars and, alternatively, Meghalaya Steels. It rejected the Revenue’s reliance on GKN Driveshafts, P.V.S. Beedies and Molbio Diagnostics as distinguishable. It further held that an original assessment being conducted under CASS limited scrutiny did not dispense with the statutory requirements governing reassessment. For AY 2017-18, an audit-based notice raised six issues, including depreciation on goodwill created on amalgamation, deduction of other comprehensive income, CSR expenditure, a provision for sales returns and carry-forward of MAT credit. The notice quantified alleged escapement at ₹1,037.56 crore, while the Section 148A(d) order concluded that ₹1,033.16 crore had escaped assessment. For AY 2018-19, an original scrutiny assessment had already been followed by a first reassessment. A further reopening in August 2024 relied on audit observations concerning other comprehensive income and goodwill depreciation, with alleged escapement ultimately quantified at ₹769.29 crore. The Court found that neither the audit objections nor the impugned notices and orders identified new material satisfying the jurisdictional requirement. On goodwill, the assessee relied on amalgamations approved by the High Court and depreciation previously allowed in scrutiny assessments. The Court discussed CIT v. Smifs Securities Ltd., under which goodwill qualified as an asset for depreciation under the provisions relevant to the years in dispute. It held that a contrary audit opinion, without new material, could not justify reopening. The Court also found that the AY 2017-18 assessment order expressly recorded “complete scrutiny”, contrary to the Revenue’s assertion of limited scrutiny. In any event, the nature of the original scrutiny could not relax the reassessment conditions. Accordingly, the Court held the challenged reassessment proceedings to be without jurisdiction and barred by limitation. It quashed the Section 148 notices and Section 148A(d) orders dated 27 April 2022, 27 March 2024 and 27 August 2024, together with the subsequent proceedings and orders arising from them, and disposed of all three writ petitions and pending interlocutory applications. Reason for the Decision: The Sikkim High Court held that although an audit objection may constitute information for initiating examination under Section 148, it does not automatically satisfy the separate requirements of Section 149(1)(b) for reassessment beyond three years. The Revenue had failed to identify new books of account, documents or evidence establishing the statutory basis for extended-period reopening. The proceedings merely reconsidered information already available during the original assessments, amounting to a change of opinion. Consequently, the reassessment proceedings for AYs 2015-16, 2017-18 and 2018-19 were held to be without jurisdiction and barred by limitation. Cases Discussed Sapphire Foods India Ltd. v. The judgment of the Supreme Court in Red Chilli International Sales (supra), reassures this Court on the correctness of its ruling dated 5.11.2025. Writ petition was not infructuous 5.(ii) The respondent’s objection that the writ petition has become infructuous as final order has been passed, is next to be considered. The learned Counsel for the petitioner counters this argument by submitting that as the writ petition has challenged the very jurisdiction of the respondent for issuance of notice under section 148 and if this Court comes to the conclusion that the respondent did not have the jurisdiction to issue the impugned notice, the subsequent proceedings would in any case not survive. The learned counsel also submitted that the appeal filed by the petitioner against the assessment order has been filed without prejudice to their rights and contentions before this Court in the pending writ petition. 5.(iii) I am of the view that the impugned notices under section 148 were challenged by the petitioner before this Court on the ground that the same were issued without jurisdiction. Adjudication of the proceedings during the pendency of the writ petition cannot render the writ petition infructuous if the petitioner establishes that the impugned notices were issued without jurisdiction. If it is so then the question of further proceedings resulting in final assessment by the respondent would not arise. If the notice under section 148 is held to be without jurisdiction then consequently the subsequent assessment order must also be held to be without jurisdiction. Thus, I am of the considered view that the present writ petition challenging the jurisdiction of issuance of notices under section 148 must be examined. The writ petition does not involve disputed questions of facts 5.(iv) The present writ petition does not involve disputed questions of facts as asserted by the learned Deputy Solicitor General. The issue raised in the writ petition can be decided on the basis of admitted materials furnished by the parties. The writ petition raises jurisdictional issues and therefore, I am of the view that this Court is competent to examine it in writ jurisdiction. 5.(v) As the petitioner contends that the reassessment proceedings was without jurisdiction; it was barred by limitation; it could not have been initiated unless the requirement of section 149(1)(b) was fulfilled; the audit party objection could not be considered “books of account or other documents or evidence” although it may be “information”, it would be important for me to examine the mandate of the relevant provisions. Notice has to be judged according to law as it existed on the date of notice 5.(vi) In Union of India vs. Rajiv Bansal10, the Supreme Court held that notices have to be judged according to the law existing on the date the notice is issued. Since, notice under section 148 in the present case was issued on 27.04.2022, section 149(1)(b) and section 148 quoted above as substituted by the Finance Act, 2022, w.e.f., 1.04.2022, would apply. Mandate of the provisions of law under consideration Section 147 5.(vii) On a perusal of section 147, it is clear that the Assessing Officer gets the jurisdiction to reassess the assessment already done only when income has “escaped” assessment which has come to his notice subsequently in the course of the proceedings under section 147. The legislature had advisedly used the word “escaped” to signify what is required to be seen if reassessment is contemplated. It is only when the Assessing Officer comes to a conclusion that income has “escaped” assessment, can further steps be taken. Section 148 5.(viii) However, under section 148 before making the assessment, reassessment or recomputation under section 147 subject to the provisions of section 148A, the Assessing Officer is required to serve on the assessee a notice, along with a copy of the order passed, if required, under clause (d) of section 148A requiring him to furnish within a specified period a return of his income. It is a trite law that the Revenue can reopen assessments based on audit objections to the effect that the assessment in the case of the assessee for the relevant assessment year has not been made in accordance with the provisions of the Act. In fact, clause (ii) of Explanation 1 to section 148 of the Act, which was incorporated into the Act by virtue of the Finance Act, 2022 ((2022) 442 ITR (St.) 91) empowers the Assessing Officer to issue notice reopening the assessment when audit objections suggests that income has escaped assessment. However, the first proviso to section 148 states that no notice shall be issued under the provision, unless the Assessing Officer has information with him which suggests that income chargeable to tax has escaped assessment in the case of the assessee for the relevant assessment year. The question that arises now is whether notice can be issued under section 148, notwithstanding the fact that the issue flagged by the audit party was subject matter of examination in the assessment proceedings and a final decision in terms of an assessment order. We are of the view that the mere fact that objections were raised by the audit party cannot change or expand the nature of the power vested in the Assessing Officer to assess/reassess the income of the assessee to a power to review an already concluded assessment. 57. It is clear from the above that in terms of clause (ii) of Explanation 1 to section 148 of the Act, an audit objection to the effect that the assessment in the case of an assessee was not made in accordance with the provisions of the Act, is information for the purpose of section 148 as well as section 148A of the Act. 26. The contention advanced on behalf of the Revenue is founded on interpreting Explanation 1 as a mandatory command to issue a notice under section 148 of the Act notwithstanding the issue flagged under the audit objection has been subject matter of the examination in assessment proceedings and a final decision in terms of an assessment order. This requires us to examine section 148 of the Act in the light of the import of word ‘information’ as used in the main provision. 27. The term ‘information’ is used in the first proviso to section 148 of the Act. The said proviso proscribes issuance of notice under section 148 of the Act unless there is ‘information’ with the Assessing Officer, which suggests that an assessee’s income chargeable to tax has escaped assessment for the relevant assessment year. Thus, if the Assessing Officer is in receipt of an audit objection, the same is required to be construed as information that suggests that the income of the assessee has escaped assessment. The proviso to section 148 of the Act is couched in the negative. Whilst, the Assessing Officer is proscribed from issuance of the notice under section 148 of the Act, unless it has the ’information’ that suggests that the assessee’s income has escaped assessment, it is not mandatory for the Assessing Officer to issue such a notice, or to review the assessment order merely because issues were flagged in an audit objection. The Assessing Officer is required to apply its mind to the audit objection and form an independent, informed view. 28. It is apparent from the above, the term ‘information’ is used in clause (a), clause (b) of section 148A of the Act and proviso (c) of the said section. Clause (a) of section 148A of the Act requires the Assessing Officer to conduct the enquiry, if necessary, with respect to the information which suggests the income chargeable to tax has escaped assessment. Accordingly, when an audit objection is raised, the Assessing Officer would have such information in respect of which an enquiry may be conducted under clause (a) of section 148A of the Act, if required. However, this does not mandate that the Assessing Officer proceeds to issue a notice under section 148A of the Act merely on the basis of the audit objection. Clause (b) of section 148A of the Act requires the Assessing Officer to provide the assessee with the basis of the information, which suggests that the income chargeable to tax has escaped assessment, in order to enable the assessee to respond to the said notice. 30. The case of Shree Balaji Alloys 5.(xxiii) In Shree Balaji Alloys (supra), the issue that arose for determination, inter alia, was: “(1) Whether the amount of Excise refund and interest subsidy received by the appellants – assesses, in pursuance of the incentives announced and sanctioned vide Government of India, Ministry of Commerce and Industry (Department of Industrial Policy and Promotion)’s Office Memorandum No. 1(13)2000-NER dated June 14, 2002 and Central Excise Notification Nos. 56 and 57, dated November 14, 2002 and other Notifications issued on the subject, pertaining to the industrial policy introduced in the state of Jammu and Kashmir is a capital receipt and, thus not liable to tax under the provisions of the Act, or revenue receipt, as opined by the authorities under the Act.” 5.(xxiv) The Jammu & Kashmir High Court examined the issue in detail and concluded: “35. For all what has been said above the finding of the tribunal on the first issue that the Excise duty refund, interest subsidy and insurance subsidy were production incentives, hence revenue receipt, cannot be sustained, being against the law laid down by the Hon’ble Supreme Court of India in Sahney Steel Case (1997) 228 ITR 253 and Ponni Sugars Case (2008) 306 ITR 392. 36. the finding of the tribunal that the incentives were revenue receipt is, accordingly set aside holding the incentives to be capital receipt in the hands of the assesses.” 5.(xxv) The Supreme Court dismissed the appeals filed by the Revenue on the ground that the issue raised in the appeals were covered against the Revenue by the decision of the Court in CIT vs. Ponni Sugars & Chemicals Ltd.16 or in the alternative, in CIT vs. Meghalaya Steels Ltd.17 The judgments relied upon by the respondent 5.(xxvi) The judgments relied upon by the respondent are distinguishable in the facts of the present case. In GKN Driveshafts (supra), the Supreme Court was examining assessment proceedings for assessment years 1992-93 to 1994-95, 1997-98 and 1998-99. The Supreme Court clarified that when a notice under section 148 of the Income Tax Act was issued, the proper course of action for the noticee is to file return and if he so desires, to seek reasons for issuing notices as the Assessing Officer was bound to furnish reasons within a reasonable time. This was the mandate of law as it existed then which is different now as the provisions have been amended. 5.(xxvii) In P.V.S. Beedies (supra), the Supreme Court was once again examining reopening of assessment for the accounting years 1974-1975 and 1975-1976. The Supreme Court opined that information given by internal audit party could be treated as information within the meaning of section 147(b) as it existed then. The petitioner does not contest that audit party objection is information. Therefore, the judgment also does not come to the aid of the respondent. 5.(xxviii) In Molbio Diagnostics Limited (supra), the High Court of Bombay at Goa in the facts of that case opined that the jurisdictional preconditions contemplated under section 148A(b) for reopening of assessment after three years have been met and the impugned order cannot be said to be without jurisdiction. In the present case, I am of the view that the reassessment proceedings suffers from a jurisdictional error. Therefore, the judgment of the High Court of Bombay at Goa would not help the respondent. 5.(xxix) Therefore, I am of the considered view that even on the merits of the case regarding treating Excise Duty refund as capital receipt by the petitioner is concerned, the same has been examined by the Supreme Court and rendered its opinion against the Revenue. The issue regarding initial assessment under CASS raised by the respondent. 5.(xxx) The only issue which is left to be considered as raised by the respondent, is regarding the limited scrutiny under CASS which did not include the Excise Duty refund during the initial assessment. The initial scrutiny is done under section 143(3). Section 143 relates to assessment after returns have been filed or in response to a notice under sub-section (1) of section 142. In the returns filed by the petitioner, Excise duty was declared as income credited to profit and loss account, which is exempt. The petitioner also furnished statement of total income to the Assessing Officer before the initial assessment. This fact was not contested by the respondent. In the statement of income so furnished, the petitioner had claimed exemption in respect of Central Excise Refund in the form of re-credit received by it under the industrial policy of the Central Government for establishing a new industrial undertaking in Sikkim, the same being in the nature of capital receipts not chargeable to income tax. Even otherwise, the mandate of reassessment proceedings cannot change because the initial scrutiny was only a limited scrutiny under CASS. The Assessing Officer must establish and place on record any “books of account or other documents or evidence” which was not available with the Assessing Officer at the time of initial assessment, which would reflect that income chargeable had escaped assessment. The provisions relating to reassessment proceedings, as discussed above, does not permit the reassessment proceedings to be initiated because the initial scrutiny was only a limited scrutiny. I am, therefore, of the opinion that this ground raised by the respondent is liable to be rejected. 5.(xxxi) The respondent also contends that the present case falls under section 149(1)(b) and not under section 149(1)(a). On a perusal of section 149, I am of the opinion that section 149(1)(a) and section 149(1)(b) must be read together and not in isolation. That would be clear from the words “unless the case falls under clause (b)” appearing in section 149(1)(a). 5.(xxxii) The respondent also contends that Excise duty refund deposited in the Bank Account of the petitioner were “assets” in view of Explanation to section 149(1) and therefore, this reflected in the Bank Account would be evidence in possession of the Assessing Officer. This submission although innovative discounts an important consideration, i.e., when was the evidence available. If the Excise duty refund was reflected in the returns at the time of initial assessment then it would be clear that the Bank Accounts statement would be available with the Assessing Officer even at the time of initial assessment. Re-examining the same evidence would only be a change of opinion based on the same material. Conclusion 6. Issue of Goodwill 11.(i) Besides the five contentions raised by the petitioner, both the writ petitions also involve the question of Goodwill. The petitioner’s contend that Goodwill on which depreciation has been claimed was created as a consequence of the amalgamation duly approved by the High Court in accordance with law during the preceeding financial years 2015-16 [W.P.(C) No. 13 of 2024] and 2015-16 and 2016-17 [W.P.(C) No. 50 of 2025] and the claim of depreciation on such Goodwill was duly allowed in the scrutiny assessment for assessment years, following the ratio of the judgment in Commissioner of Income-tax, Kolkata vs. Smifs Securities Ltd.20 11.(ii) In both the cases pursuant to audit objections raised on the claim for depreciation on goodwill by the petitioner, the respondents initiated reassessment proceedings. The petitioner had relied upon the judgment of the Supreme Court in Smifs Securities Ltd. (supra), in which it was held that Goodwill is an asset under explanation 3(b) to section 32(1) of the Income Tax Act. The Supreme Court examined a case in which in the relevant assessment year, one ‘Y’ limited amalgamated with assessee company. In accordance with a scheme of amalgamation of ‘Y’ limited with assessee, assets and liabilities of ‘Y’ limited were transferred to and vested with the assessing company. According to the assessee, excess consideration paid by it over the value of net assets acquired of ‘Y’ limited should be considered as Goodwill arising on amalgamation. The assessee’s claim for depreciation on Goodwill was rejected by the Assessing Officer on ground that Goodwill was not an asset falling under Explanation 3 to section 32(1). On appeal, the Supreme Court held that Explanation 3 to section 32(1) states that expression ‘asset’ shall mean an intangible asset, being know how patents, copyrights, trademarks, licences, franchises or other business or commercial rights of similar nature, a reading of the words ‘any other business or commercial rights of similar nature’ in clause (b) of Explanation 3 indicates that Goodwill would fall under the expression ‘any other business or commercial rights of a similar nature’. The principle of ejusdem generis would strictly apply while interpreting the said expression which finds place in Explanation 3(b). In view of the above, the Supreme Court opined that Goodwill is an “asset” under Explanation 3(b) to section 32(1). It was also held that depreciation on Goodwill is allowable under section 32. 11.(iii) The claim for depreciation on Goodwill by the petitioner was not merely their contention but based on the law laid down by the Supreme Court in Smifs Securities Ltd. (supra). Therefore, if the respondent did not have any new material at the time of proposed reassessment, it could not have issued the notice under section 148 merely because the audit objection had a different opinion. Accordingly, it is held that the respondent could not have initiated the reassessment proceedings on this ground merely because of their change of opinion based on audit objection. 11.(iv) I will now endeavour to examine the submissions made by the learned Deputy Solicitor General on behalf of the respondents. The submissions in both the writ petitions are identical. Respondent’s submission 12. The learned Deputy Solicitor General of India submitted that: (i) The petitioner had an alternative remedy to file an appeal and therefore, the writ petition should not be entertained. (ii) Although assessment proceedings under section 143(3) were completed, the issues raised in the reopening were not addressed and therefore, cannot be a ‘change of opinion’. (iii) Order u/s 148A(d) is a speaking order for issuance of notice and not a conclusion. (iv) Upon being satisfied, the Statutory Notice u/s 148 was issued, requiring to furnish return of income in respect of income escaping assessment. No decision has been taken by the Revenue so far. It is absolutely upon the assessee to prove its case before the Revenue Authority. (v) Statutory requirement u/s 149(1)(b) is satisfied. Under section 149 of the Income Tax Act, reopening beyond three years (up to ten years) is permissible where, the Assessing Officer has in his possession books of account or other documents or evidence revealing escapement of income. (vi) the expression “other documents or evidence” is of wide amplitude and is not restrictively defined. Therefore, any official, credible and documentary material in possession of the Assessing Officer which reveals escapement, qualifies under this provision. (vii) Audit paragraph is a formal written record issued by a constitutional authority based on examination of accounts and records. Hence, it squarely qualifies as a document. (x) “other documents or evidence” must be interpreted broadly. The legislature has deliberately used the expression:”books of account or other documents or evidence”. The use of “or” and broad terminology indicates inclusive and expansive scope not confined to assessee’s own records. (xi) Legislative Intent behind section 149(1)(b): the purpose of extended limitation is to enable reopening where credible documentary evidence emerges later. Audit conducted by CAG is a post-assessment statutory scrutiny precisely intended to detect such escapement. Hence, it fits squarely within the legislative intent. Consideration 13. I am of the view that it is precisely a change of opinion and nothing more. The mandate of the law is to come to an opinion that taxable income has escaped assessment based on “books of account or other documents or evidence”. In both these reassessment proceedings, the respondents have not been able to show any such new material which would allow the Assessing Officer to reassess the income already assessed. 13.(iii) The contention that the order under section 148A(d) is unassailable as it is a speaking order; and the Assessing Officer had verified bank transactions and examined financial disclosures, which reflects his application of mind, cannot be accepted. When a law requires you to do a certain thing in a certain way, it must be done in that way and in no other. The mandate of section 149(1)(b) is that no notice can be issued under section 148 unless the Assessing Officer has in his possession “books of account or other documents or evidence” which reveal that income chargeable to tax, represented in the form of asset, which has escaped assessment amounts to or is likely to amount to fifty lakhs rupees or more for that year. Merely because the Assessing Officer revisited the assessment already made and came to a different conclusion may reflect application of mind, however, the application of mind was not as per law and the mandate of section 148 and 149. 13.(iv) The respondent’s contention that the statutory notice under section 148 was issued directing the petitioners to furnish his return in respect to income assessing escapement only requires that much and no more and since no decision has been taken by the respondent so far, it is upon the petitioner to prove his case discounts the mandate of section 149(1). The language of the provision is clear – “No notice under section 148 shall be issued”. This implies a mandatory precondition for issuance of notice under section 148 sans which the reassessment proceeding would be without jurisdiction. 13.(v) As submitted by the respondent, it is correct that the statutory requirement of section 149(1)(b) would be satisfied if the Assessing Officer has in his possession “books of account or other documents or evidence”. However, the subsequent notices and orders issued for reassessment does not reflect that the Assessing Officer had such “books of account or other documents or evidence” in his possession. 13.(vi) The expression ‘other documents or evidence’, as contended by the learned Deputy Solicitor General is of wide amplitude and any official, credible and documentary material in possession of the Assessing Officer which would reveal escapement qualifies as such. However, I am of the view that audit paragraph cannot qualify into this category as it is ordinarily a communication or an opinion pointing out an alleged error in the assessment. I am of the view that the audit objection although raised by a Constitutional authority based on examination of accounts and records is still an opinion. This opinion would be of great relevance for the respondents. Based on the opinion, if the respondents applied their mind and procured “books of account or other documents or evidence”, then the respondents could have the necessary jurisdiction to issue the notice under section 148. Merely because the Explanation to section 148 recognises audit objection as “information”, it does not automatically qualify as “books of account or other documents or evidence”. 13.(vii) As rightly contended by the learned Deputy Solicitor General, the legislative intent behind section 149(1)(b) is to enable reopening where “credible documentary evidence emerges later”. Admittedly, however, besides the audit objection, the respondent did not have any credible documentary evidence whatsoever. Therefore, reassessments were without jurisdiction in both the proceedings and also barred by limitation. 13.(viii) Besides the above submissions, the respondent has also raised the issue of limited scrutiny under CASS in W.P.(C) No. 13 of 2024. It is contended that the original assessment made and order passed under section 143(3) was under CASS limited scrutiny as the Assessing Officer had no power to delve into the reasons. However, the facts of the present case is on a completely different issue which was not highlighted in the scrutiny assessment proceedings under section 143(3). As such, it is a fresh aspect which was not covered under the original assessment. The petitioner contests this assertion and states that it is an incorrect statement. It is stated that the assessment order dated 7.04.2021 clearly mentions that the case was selected for “complete scrutiny assessment” and not under CASS as suggested. I have perused the order dated 7.04.2021 and I find that the petitioner’s contention is correct. The initial scrutiny is done under section 143(3). Section 143 relates to assessment after returns have been filed or in response to a notice under sub-section (1) of section 142. Even otherwise, the mandate of reassessment proceedings cannot change because the initial scrutiny was only a limited scrutiny under CASS as pleaded by the respondent even if the respondents contention is to be believed. The Assessing Officer must establish and place on record any “books of account or other documents or evidence” which was not available with the Assessing Officer at the time of initial assessment that would reflect that income chargeable had escaped assessment. The provisions relating to reassessment proceedings, as discussed above, does not permit the reassessment proceedings to be initiated because the initial scrutiny was only a limited scrutiny without anything more. The opinion of the Assessing Officer at the time of reassessment has to be that income chargeable to tax had escaped assessment which must be based on “books of account or other documents or evidence”. I am, therefore, of the opinion that this ground raised by the respondent is also liable to be rejected. Conclusion 14. Consequently: (i) W.P.(C) No. 13 of 2024 is allowed and the impugned Notice dated 27.03.2024 under section 148 of the Income Tax Act, 1961, the impugned Order dated 27.03.2024 under section 148A(d) of the Income Tax Act, 1961 and the subsequent proceedings and orders, are quashed. (ii) W.P.(C) No. 50 of 2025 is allowed and the impugned Notice dated 27.08.2024 under section 148 of the Income Tax Act, 1961, the impugned Order dated 27.08.2024 under section 148A(d) of the Income Tax Act, 1961 and the subsequent proceedings and orders, are quashed. 15. Accordingly, the writ petitions stand disposed of along with all other pending interlocutory applications.

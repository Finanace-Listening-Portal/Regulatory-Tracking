(NCLAT Delhi) Summary: The National Company Law Appellate Tribunal, Principal Bench, New Delhi, dismissed Mehul Vyas’s appeal against the NCLT Mumbai order dated 25 June 2026 restructuring the shareholding and management of Dreams the Mall Company Limited. The company, incorporated to administer and maintain the mall at Bhandup, had more than 1,000 shop-owner shareholders. A group of shareholders had instituted proceedings alleging oppression and mismanagement. The final NCLT order provided for share entitlement proportionate to carpet area, cancellation of excess allotments, constitution of a new Board, determination and recovery of dues, and a general body meeting. Vyas, who owned two shops, challenged these directions individually. The appellant alleged that his intervention application remained undecided, that subsequent developments had been disregarded, and that the NCLT had failed to examine maintainability, alleged fraud and the suitability of the proposed Board. He also questioned the delegation of shareholding and liability determinations to that Board and the protection extended to its members. Referring to fires, revoked approvals, municipal recovery proceedings and property tax dues exceeding ₹60.15 crore, he contended that redevelopment was the only viable course. He relied on a structural repair estimate of ₹41.20 crore for the fire-affected area, disputed further maintenance demands and claimed advance credits towards common area maintenance charges. The NCLAT found that the assertion concerning the pending intervention application was contrary to the record. The NCLT’s order dated 16 January 2020 had heard the applicants, effectively disposed of their application and permitted shop owners to pursue grievances collectively in a representative capacity after complying with maintenance-payment directions. The appellant had not used that remedy and instead continued individual proceedings. His failure to annex that material order, coupled with the express assertion that the application remained pending, could not be treated as a mere procedural omission. The Tribunal also noted that the earlier appeals against interim orders had been disposed of on 18 September 2026 following the final NCLT order. Regarding I.A. No. 168/2026, the NCLAT upheld the NCLT’s refusal to receive additional material once the petition had been extensively heard, written submissions received and judgment reserved. Additional documents could not reopen the concluded hearing as a matter of right, particularly when the opposing parties would require an opportunity to rebut them. The NCLT had also recorded that substantial directions could not be sought by the appellant in a petition filed by others and that grievances could be placed before the new management. The appellate Tribunal treated the restructuring directions as a mechanism benefiting shareholders and shop owners collectively, rather than an order confined to the appellant. It noted that no other shop owner, or the company, its directors, promoters or developer, had appealed against the final judgment. Interim measures under Section 242(2)(k) stood subsumed in the final order. The NCLAT additionally rejected the contention that the entire mall had been gutted. The June 2022 structural audit recorded that 31,913.72 sq. mt. out of 1,44,736.59 sq. mt. had been affected by fire, while 1,12,822.87 sq. mt. remained unaffected, and described the structure as repairable. Finding the appeal not maintainable in the manner filed and no sustainable ground for interference, the Tribunal dismissed it. For non-disclosure of the earlier order, the incorrect assertion of continued pendency and repeated individual proceedings that multiplied litigation and consumed judicial time, it imposed exemplary costs of ₹5 lakh, payable to the Prime Minister’s National Relief Fund within four weeks. All pending applications were disposed of. Reason for the Decision: The NCLAT found that the appellant had failed to disclose the NCLT’s order dated 16 January 2020, which had effectively disposed of his intervention application and provided a representative remedy for shop owners. Instead of pursuing that remedy, he continued individual proceedings and incorrectly claimed that his application remained undecided. The Tribunal also upheld the refusal to admit additional documents after the hearing had concluded and judgment was reserved. It found no sustainable ground to interfere with the collective restructuring arrangements and imposed ₹5 lakh exemplary costs for the appellant’s litigation conduct. We have heard the rival contentions and perused the material on record. The first and most important issue which arises for consideration is whether the Appellant has the locus to maintain the present appeal against the final order dated 25.06.2026. 12. The Appellant has claimed that its Intervention Application No. 859/2019 remained pending throughout the proceedings and even when the Impugned Order was passed. This submission, however, is not borne out from the record. The order dated 16.01.2020 passed by the Ld. NCLT specifically dealt with MA No. 859/2019. The applicant(s) in MA No. 859/2019, of whom the Appellant was one, were present and were heard. The application was accordingly disposed of with directions which also provided a course of action to the shop owners in a representative capacity. 13. In the present appeal, at page 15 of the appeal paper book itself, the Appellant states that “The said intervention application remained pending throughout the proceedings of the Company Petition and even at the time of passing of the Impugned Order.” The record, however, shows otherwise. 14. It is brought to our notice by the respondent that in the intervention application (MA 859/2019) along with other MAs in the main CP 265/2017 an order dated 16.01.2020 was passed by the learned NCLT as follows: “Heard the administrator. The case of the administrator who has been appointed by this court to oversee the operations of the mall is that an amount of ₹15 per sq. ft. on the carpet area has been fixed by this court as common area maintenance charges. A very few shops are complying with the orders and most of the other shop owners are not complying with the same. Apart from that the applicant in MA 859, who is present in the court, submits that in the normal course only ₹3 per sq. ft. were charged, whereas the increase in the amount as maintenance charges of ₹15 per sq. ft. is a burden, something which they cannot afford. It has been submitted by the administrator that several of the shop owners are not complying with the orders of this court. As a result the administrator is not in a position to make both ends meet and the maintenance of the mall is suffered. This is a very peculiar issue. If only a few shop owners make the payment and others do not pay, there will be a serious deficit. Unless strenuous action is taken against the non-payers, it would be very difficult to maintain the mall. In view of the same we hereby direct all the shop owners in the mall to forthwith comply with the directions of the court and make the payment of ₹15 per sq. ft. as maintenance charges, as ordered by this court earlier. Subsequently if at all there is any difficulty on their part after making the payment, they may approach this bench in a representative capacity and agitate their grievance. Without making the payment and by putting the administrator into difficulty, no purpose can be served. The asset value will be depleted because of non-maintenance. We once again reiterate that all the shop owners, whosoever is conducting business or whosoever is holding the premises, shall make the payment without fail. They may approach this bench after making the payment for any clarification in a representative capacity. That means as a group together but not individually. The administrator is directed to submit the list of defaulters on 30/1/2020. List the matter for further consideration on 30/1/2020.” 15. Perusal of above noted order dated 16.01.2020 shows that the intervention application in MA No. 859/2019 was taken up in the presence of applicant(s) of which Appellant was one. The Appellant was heard and the application was effectively disposed of. The order specifically permitted the shop owners to approach the Ld. NCLT in a representative capacity and to agitate their grievance as a group together and not individually. 16. We also find that the Appellant did not enclose the order dated 16.01.2020 with the present appeal. The Appellant later stated in the written submissions that the order had inadvertently not been filed. The fact remains that the order was material to the question of the Appellant’s locus and the manner in which its grievance could be pursued. Its non-disclosure, coupled with the specific assertion that the intervention application was still pending, cannot be treated as a mere procedural omission. The Appellant’s pleading was contrary to the record and the material order was not brought to the notice of this Tribunal at the stage of filing of the appeal. 17. The order dated 16.01.2020 also assumes significance for another reason. The Appellant had been given an opportunity to pursue the grievances of the shop owners in a representative capacity. The Appellant did not avail of that opportunity. Instead, it continued to pursue individual proceedings and appeals against interim orders. The record refers to Company Appeal (AT) No. 58 of 2021 and Company Appeal (AT) No. 250 of 2024, filed by the Appellant, which were disposed of vide Order dated 18.09.2026 in view of passing of the final order. 18. In these circumstances, the Appellant cannot now claim an independent and continuing right to intervene in the representative company petition and challenge the final order in its individual capacity. The right granted by the Ld. NCLT was to approach it in a representative capacity. Having failed to avail that remedy, the Appellant cannot repeatedly invoke individual proceedings arising from the same Company Petition and thereby enlarge the scope of the litigation. 19. The Company Petition along with the connected applications was heard extensively on 15.05.2026. Written submissions were taken on record and the matter was closed for orders. In such circumstances, the subsequent filing of I.A. No. 168/2026 could not confer upon the Appellant a right to reopen the concluded hearing or to introduce additional material as a matter of right. 22. The Ld. NCLT gave reasons for declining to take the additional documents on record. It recorded that accepting new material facts after the matter had been closed for orders would deprive the opposing parties of an opportunity to rebut such material and would affect the principles of natural justice. It also recorded that the Appellant could not seek substantial directions in a petition filed by others. These reasons cannot be said to be irrelevant or without jurisdiction. 23. The Ld. NCLT had earlier, vide order dated 23.09.2025, directed constitution of the Board as an interim measure. The final Judgment thereafter provides a step-by-step mechanism for redetermination of shareholding, determination of dues, recovery of dues and fixation of future maintenance. The Appellant’s individual objections cannot be examined in isolation from this larger framework. 24. We further note that the Impugned Order is not confined to the Appellant. The directions issued by the Ld. NCLT concern the restructuring of the shareholding and management of Dreams the Mall Company Limited and operate for the benefit and protection of the shareholders and shop owners as a class. The Impugned Order provides a mechanism for redetermination of shareholding, determination of dues, recovery of dues from shareholders according to their entitlement, constitution of the Board and convening of the General Body Meeting. The order therefore cannot be viewed as an order made only against or only for the Appellant. 25. The record further shows that the Company Petition was pending for several years and involved a large number of shareholders and other parties. The Appellant owns two shops in the Mall. No other shop owner has challenged the final Judgment before us. The Company, its Directors, Promoters and Developer were parties before the Ld. NCLT, and none of them has preferred an appeal against the final Judgment. This circumstance is relevant while examining the Appellant’s claim that it is entitled to continue the litigation individually in respect of issues concerning the larger body of shareholders. 26. The conduct of the Appellant also has to be considered in the background of the long pendency of the Company Petition and the several interventions and appeals arising from it. The repeated individual proceedings, despite the earlier liberty to pursue the grievance in a representative capacity, have the effect of multiplying litigation arising from the same Company Petition. The appellate process cannot be permitted to become a means for continuing such individual proceedings when the final order itself provides a mechanism applicable to the shareholders as a whole. 27. We are also conscious that the Company Petition was a long-pending company appeal before the Ld. NCLT. The matter had already been argued at length; written submissions had been taken on record and judgment had been reserved. At that stage, permitting an individual intervenor to place a fresh set of documents and seek substantial directions would reopen the proceedings and prolong the adjudication. The Appellant had already had opportunities to place its grievances before the Ld. NCLT. Therefore, the rejection of I.A. No. 168/2026 at that stage does not furnish a ground to interfere with the final order. 28. Beyond maintainability we have also considered the averments concerning the fire incidents. The Appellant contends that the entire Mall was gutted in the fire. However, the Structural Audit Report dated June 2022, referred to in the record, records that out of the total area of 1,44,736.59 sq. mt., 31,913.72 sq. mt. was affected by fire and 1,12,822.87 sq. mt. remained unaffected. The Report also records that the structure was in repairable condition. Therefore, the submission that the entire Mall was gutted cannot be accepted on the material placed before us. 29. On an overall consideration of the record, we find that the present appeal is not maintainable at the instance of the Appellant in the manner in which it has been filed. The Appellant had already been heard in the intervention application and had been given an opportunity to pursue the grievance in a representative capacity. It did not avail that opportunity. The Appellant also did not disclose the order dated 16.01.2020 in the appeal, despite relying upon the continued pendency of the very same intervention application. So far as the interim measures under Section 242(2) (k) are concerned, the said measures were interim in nature and get subsumed once the final order has been passed and hence do not require our consideration at the appellate stage. The final order is intended to operate for the benefit of all the shareholders and shop owners and is not confined to the Appellant. 30. The subsequent intervention, filed after the Company Petition had been finally heard and reserved for orders, also cannot be used to reopen a long-pending proceeding. The Ld. NCLT was justified in declining to take the additional material on record at that stage, particularly when the other parties would have been entitled to an opportunity to respond to such material. The course adopted by the Appellant has resulted in further litigation arising out of the same Company Petition, despite the earlier opportunity to pursue the grievances in a representative capacity. 31. We, therefore, find no ground to interfere with the Judgment and Final Order dated 25.06.2026. The appeal does not disclose any legally sustainable ground for setting aside or modifying the Impugned Order. The Appellant has failed to establish a right to maintain the present appeal in the manner in which it has been filed, and the grounds raised by the Appellant do not warrant appellate interference. Order 32. In view of the above discussion and findings, Company Appeal (AT) No. 342 of 2026 is dismissed. 33. We further find that the non-disclosure of the order dated 16.01.2020, the assertion that the intervention application continued to remain pending, and the repeated individual proceedings despite the liberty granted to approach the Ld. NCLT in a representative capacity are matters which cannot be ignored. Such conduct has contributed to multiplication of litigation and has consumed judicial time. 34.

Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); Renu Arun Talwar Vs Circle (ITAT Mumbai) Two Adjoining Flats Qualify under Section 54 for AY 2012–13; Accepted Building Cost Cannot Be Omitted from Capital Gains Background of the Case The Mumbai Tribunal accepted the assessee’s claim for Section 54 deduction on investment in two adjoining residential flats, applying the statutory language relevant to Assessment Year 2012–13. It also held that where the transferred property comprised land and a building, and the building’s acquisition cost had been accepted during assessment, that cost could not be omitted while computing indexed acquisition cost without a stated basis. The assessee, an individual deriving capital gains and income from other sources, originally filed her return on 31 July 2012, declaring income of ₹3,42,54,515. Reassessment proceedings were subsequently initiated following information concerning the transfer of a plot with a structure in Sindh Cooperative Housing Society, Aundh, Pune, and the valuation adopted for computing its historical acquisition cost. Delay Condoned Considering Advanced Age The appeal before the Tribunal was delayed. The assessee’s condonation application stated a delay of 140 days, explaining that she was approximately 80 years old and lived with her husband, aged about 85. Because of her age and limited understanding of electronic communications, she could not effectively monitor emails and portal communications. Her representative discovered the appellate order on accessing the portal, following which steps were taken to file the appeal. The Tribunal accepted the explanation as bona fide. Finding no material suggesting a contrary intention, it condoned the delay, relying on Collector, Land Acquisition v. Mst. Katiji (1987) 167 ITR 471 (SC). Additional grounds challenging the validity of the Section 148 notice were dismissed as not pressed. Building Cost Accepted but Excluded from Computation The assessee relied on a valuation report separately identifying the value of the land and the structure as on 1 April 1981. Her grievance was that the Assessing Officer considered the indexed cost attributable to the land but omitted the building component, although the property transferred included both. The Tribunal found that the building’s existence was not disputed. The assessment proceedings also indicated acceptance of the historical cost attributable to the land and building. It held that excluding an accepted component of acquisition cost produced an internally inconsistent capital gains computation. Separate valuation of land and structure did not justify disregarding the building when the consideration related to transfer of the entire property. Recalculation of Indexed Building Cost Directed The Tribunal directed the Assessing Officer to include the building’s cost as on 1 April 1981, to the extent accepted and allowable under the Act, and grant the applicable indexation. The direction remained subject to verification of the arithmetic and ensuring that the cost was neither duplicated nor allowed beyond what the law permitted. The assessee was to receive an opportunity of hearing during this limited exercise. Section 54 Deduction Restricted to One Flat The assessee had invested ₹3,35,36,982 in two adjoining residential flats. In her original return, she claimed deduction only for one flat, amounting to ₹1,76,21,954. In the return filed pursuant to Section 148, she claimed deduction for the investment in both flats. She explained that the flats were adjoining, purchased on the same date and intended to provide one residential accommodation for her joint family. The Assessing Officer restricted the deduction to one flat. The CIT(A) upheld the restriction, referring to the absence of evidence such as a combined sanctioned plan, common utility connection, municipal assessment or architect’s certification establishing a single unit. Pre-Amendment Law Governs the Claim The Tribunal observed that Section 54, as applicable to Assessment Year 2012–13, did not contain the express requirement of investment in “one residential house in India”. The amendment introducing that language took effect from 1 April 2015 and did not govern the assessment year before it. Relying on CIT v. D. Ananda Basappa (2009) 309 ITR 329 (Karn.) and CIT v. Geeta Duggal (2013) 357 ITR 153 (Delhi), the Tribunal recognised that “a residential house” was not necessarily confined to a single independent physical unit. The nature of the accommodation and the surrounding facts required consideration. Both Adjoining Flats Accepted for Deduction The Tribunal noted that the assessee previously occupied a bungalow and required accommodation for a joint family. The two flats were admittedly adjoining and had been acquired together with the intention of using them as one residential accommodation. It held that the claim could not be rejected merely because the investment involved two adjoining flats. The Assessing Officer was directed to allow Section 54 deduction with reference to the ₹3,35,36,982 investment in both flats, subject to verification of actual investment, satisfaction of the remaining statutory conditions and computation of eligible capital gains. The appeal was allowed, with consequential recomputation directed. Author’s Comments The ruling emphasises two practical principles. Capital gains computation must account for every established and allowable component of acquisition cost, and a Section 54 claim must be tested against the statutory language applicable to the relevant assessment year. The adjoining-flat relief is expressly founded on the pre-amendment law governing AY 2012–13 and the particular residential arrangement. It should not be presented as an unrestricted proposition covering every investment in multiple flats. Equally, the Tribunal’s direction remains subject to verification of investment and the other statutory requirements; it does not dispense with those conditions. List of Cases Discussed 1. Collector, Land Acquisition v. Mst. Katiji & Others — (1987) 167 ITR 471 (Supreme Court): Relied upon for the liberal interpretation of sufficient cause in applications for condonation of delay. The Tribunal applied this principle to the assessee’s explanation involving advanced age and difficulties with electronic communication. 2. CIT v. D. Ananda Basappa — (2009) 309 ITR 329 (Karnataka High Court): Relied upon for interpretation of the expression “a residential house” under the pre-amendment Section 54, recognising that qualifying residential accommodation need not invariably be confined to one independent physical unit. 3. CIT v. Geeta Duggal — (2013) 357 ITR 153 (Delhi High Court): Relied upon for the principle that the nature and use of residential accommodation must be examined when determining eligibility for exemption under the statutory language applicable before the 2014 amendment. Issue 2 arising for consideration is whether the assessee is entitled to deduction under section 54 of the Act in respect of the entire investment of ₹3,35,36,982/- made in the purchase of two adjoining residential flats, as against the deduction of ₹1,76,21,954/- allowed by the Ld.AO with reference to one flat. 7.1. The Ld.AR submitted that the assessee was residing in a bungalow having a built-up area of approximately 300 sq. metres, situated on a plot of land measuring 877.93 sq. metres. It was submitted that the assessee belonged to a joint family and, having regard to the residential requirements of the family, required a larger residential accommodation. Upon the transfer of the aforesaid land and building, the assessee purchased two adjoining residential flats on the same date, having a combined area of approximately 530 sq. metres, with the intention of using them as a single residential unit. 7.2. The Ld.AR further contended that, under the provisions of section 54 of the Act applicable to the assessment year under consideration, there was no statutory restriction limiting the exemption to investment in only one residential unit. It was submitted that the restriction concerning investment in more than one residential house was introduced by the Finance (No. 2) Act, 2014, with effect from 01.04.2015, and was therefore not applicable to the year under consideration. Accordingly, the Ld. AR submitted that the deduction could not be restricted merely because the investment was made in two adjoining flats, particularly when both were intended to constitute a single residential accommodation. 7.3. The Ld.DR, on the other hand, relied upon the orders of the authorities below and supported the restriction of the deduction to the investment made in one residential flat. We have perused the submissions advanced by both the sides in the light of records placed before us. 8. On consideration of the rival submissions and perusal of the material available on record, we find that the provisions of section 54 of the Act, as applicable to the assessment year under consideration, did not contain the express restriction that the investment must be made in “one residential house in India”. The expression “a residential house” was interpreted by various High Courts, having regard to the facts of the respective cases, to permit exemption in respect of more than one residential unit where the units constituted a single residential house in substance or were interconnected and used as one residential unit. 8.1. In this regard, we draw support from the decisions of the Hon’ble Karnataka High Court in case of CIT v. D. Ananda Basappa reported in (2009) 309 ITR 329 and decision of Hon’ble Delhi High Court in CIT v. Geeta Duggal reported in (2013) 357 ITR 153. These decisions recognise that the expression “a residential house” is not necessarily confined to a single independent physical unit and that the nature and use of the residential accommodation must be considered in the facts and circumstances of each case. 8.2. We further note that the restriction relating to investment in more than one residential house was introduced by the Finance (No. 2) Act, 2014, with effect from 01.04.2015. The said amendment is not applicable to the assessment year 2012-13 under consideration. The assessee’s claim must, therefore, be examined with reference to the statutory provisions applicable to the relevant assessment year and the judicial interpretation prevailing thereunder. 8.3. In the present case, the assessee was earlier residing in a bungalow having a built-up area of approximately 300 square metres, situated on a plot measuring 877.93 square metres. The Ld.AR has explained that the assessee was residing in a joint family and required adequate residential accommodation for its members. Upon transfer of the said property, the assessee purchased two adjoining residential flats on the same date, having a combined area of approximately 530 square metres, with the intention of using them as a single residential accommodation. The Revenue has not disputed that the flats are adjoining. These circumstances lend support to the assessee’s contention that the investment was made to acquire residential accommodation commensurate with the requirements of the family. 8.4. In our considered view, the claim cannot be rejected merely on the ground that the investment was made in two adjoining flats, without examining the nature of the residential accommodation acquired and the assessee’s intention to use the same as one residential house. Having regard to the statutory provisions applicable to the assessment year under consideration, the judicial precedents referred to above, and the facts placed on record, we find merit in the assessee’s claim. 8.5. We, accordingly, direct the Ld.AO to allow the deduction under section 54 of the Act in respect of the investment of ₹3,35,36,982/- made in the purchase of both residential flats, subject to verification of the actual investment, satisfaction of the other statutory conditions and the computation of the eligible capital gains in accordance with law. The Ld.AOshall give effect to this finding while recomputing the capital gains for the year under consideration. Accordingly, the assessee’s ground challenging the restriction of deduction under section 54 is allowed, subject to the aforesaid verification. Grounds relating to computation of capital gains 9. The assessee has also raised grounds challenging the computation of capital gains. We direct the Ld.AO to recompute the capital gains in accordance with law, having regard to the findings recorded hereinabove, including the allowable Cost Of Acquisition” target=”_blank” rel=”noopener”>indexed cost of acquisition of the land and building and the deduction admissible under section 54 of the Act. Accordingly, the grounds relating to computation of capital gains are allowed for statistical purposes, subject to the directions recorded above. In the result, appeal filed by the assessee stands allowed. Order pronounced in the open court on 16.10.2026.

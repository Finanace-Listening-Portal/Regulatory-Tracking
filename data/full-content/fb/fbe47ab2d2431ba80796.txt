Summary: Indian taxpayers with investments in Dubai and other UAE jurisdictions must distinguish foreign-asset disclosure from taxation of foreign income. For individuals who qualify as Resident and Ordinarily Resident in India, Schedule FA may require reporting of UAE company shares, bank and custodial accounts, signing authority, property, financial interests and other specified foreign assets even when no dividend or income is received. Residential status must be determined under Indian income-tax law rather than inferred from citizenship, a UAE residence visa or FEMA status. The applicable foreign-asset reporting period, acquisition cost, balances and currency conversion rules also require careful attention. The article explains separate reporting of personally owned assets and company assets, the possible relevance of Schedules FSI and TR for foreign income and tax relief, appropriate ITR form selection, the implications of non-disclosure, and practical recordkeeping for Dubai founders. It also addresses dormant UAE companies, corporate subsidiaries, overseas direct investment documentation, international financial information exchange and the need to maintain consistent shareholding, banking and tax records. Correct disclosure from the first year of ownership is preferable to reconstructing records or seeking remedial relief after omissions are discovered. A resident Indian may hold shares in a Dubai mainland company, own an entity in DMCC, Meydan, IFZA, Dubai South or another free zone, maintain a UAE corporate or personal bank account, invest in UAE securities, or own property connected with the business. From a UAE perspective, these investments may be completely legitimate and properly documented. The Indian income-tax return creates a separate compliance question. For taxpayers who are resident and ordinarily resident in India, foreign assets and foreign income can carry detailed disclosure obligations. In many cases, the requirement exists even where the UAE investment produced little or no income during the year. This creates an important distinction: Paying the correct tax is not necessarily the same as making the correct disclosure. A taxpayer may have invested entirely from disclosed Indian funds, complied with FEMA, obtained a valid Dubai trade licence and paid all applicable UAE taxes—and still create an Indian compliance issue simply by failing to report the foreign asset correctly in the income-tax return. For Indian founders with UAE investments, Schedule FA therefore deserves much more attention than it usually receives. Why UAE Investments Have Become a Major Reporting Issue Dubai has become a natural business base for many Indians. The typical structure is no longer limited to an NRI buying a residential property in the UAE. Indian residents increasingly hold: shares in UAE operating companies; interests in free-zone companies; UAE corporate bank accounts; personal UAE bank accounts; investment accounts; UAE real estate; directorship or signing authority in UAE companies; and interests in regional holding structures. At the same time, international exchange of financial information has become far more sophisticated. A taxpayer should therefore not assume that an overseas asset is outside the Indian reporting system merely because it is located in Dubai. If the Indian return requires disclosure, the safer approach is to disclose it accurately and consistently. First Question: What Is the Taxpayer’s Residential Status? Before completing foreign-asset disclosures, the taxpayer’s residential status under Indian income-tax law must be established. This is fundamental. Broadly, taxpayers may fall into one of three categories: Resident and Ordinarily Resident; Resident but Not Ordinarily Resident; Non-Resident. Schedule FA is principally relevant to taxpayers who are Resident and Ordinarily Resident in India. A non-resident or a person who qualifies as Resident but Not Ordinarily Resident is generally not required to complete Schedule FA merely because they hold a foreign asset. That distinction is particularly important for people moving between India and Dubai. Consider two Indian citizens who each own 100% of a Dubai company. One has permanently relocated to the UAE and qualifies as non-resident in India. The other continues to live and conduct most of his affairs from India and is Resident and Ordinarily Resident. Their shareholding may be identical. Their Indian disclosure obligations may not be. Citizenship alone does not decide the answer. Residential status must be examined for the relevant year. FEMA Residence and Income-Tax Residence Are Not the Same Test This is one of the most common areas of confusion in India–UAE structures. A person’s residential status under FEMA and residential status under income-tax law are determined under different legislation and for different purposes. FEMA primarily looks at foreign-exchange regulation. Income-tax residence determines the scope of taxation and various disclosure obligations. Therefore, someone should not conclude: “My Dubai investment complied with FEMA, so there is nothing else to report in my ITR.” Nor should the taxpayer assume: “I have a UAE residence visa, therefore I am automatically non-resident for Indian income-tax purposes.” A UAE residence visa can be commercially and practically important, but it does not by itself determine Indian tax residence. The individual’s actual facts for the relevant year must be reviewed. Schedule FA Is the Main Foreign-Asset Disclosure Schedule For taxpayers to whom it applies, Schedule FA is the central part of the Indian income-tax return dealing with foreign assets. It seeks information regarding various categories of assets and interests located outside India. For a Dubai-linked taxpayer, these can include: foreign depository accounts; foreign custodial accounts; foreign equity or debt interests; financial interests in foreign entities; immovable property; other foreign capital assets; accounts in which the taxpayer has signing authority; foreign trusts in relevant circumstances; and income arising from foreign assets. A Dubai company shareholding can therefore create more than one reporting question. For example, the taxpayer may own shares in the UAE company and also have signing authority over its bank account. These are not necessarily the same disclosure item. Shares in a Dubai Company Can Require Schedule FA Reporting Suppose an Indian resident and ordinarily resident individual incorporates a Dubai consultancy company. The founder owns 100% of its shares. The UAE company has a valid trade licence, office address and corporate bank account. Even if the company retains all profits in Dubai and pays no dividend to the shareholder during the year, the underlying foreign equity interest can still be relevant for Schedule FA. This surprises some founders. They assume that because no money came back to India, nothing needs to appear in the Indian return. But foreign-asset disclosure is not limited to income remitted to India. The existence of the asset itself can create a reporting obligation. That distinction should be understood from the first year of ownership. Free-Zone Shares Are Still Foreign Assets Another misconception sometimes appears with Dubai free-zone companies. A shareholder may think that because a company is incorporated in a free zone, or because its shares are not publicly traded, it somehow falls outside normal foreign-asset reporting. That is not the correct approach. Whether a company is established in: DMCC; DIFC; JAFZA; Meydan; IFZA; Dubai South; Dubai Internet City; or another UAE jurisdiction, the fundamental question for the Indian return is the nature of the taxpayer’s foreign ownership or financial interest. The specific free zone can matter for UAE tax and regulatory purposes. It does not make the underlying foreign interest invisible for Indian reporting. Schedule FA Uses a Calendar-Year Reference for Foreign Assets One of the practical difficulties in completing Schedule FA is that the reporting period for foreign assets does not always align naturally with the Indian financial year. The Schedule requires specified foreign-asset information with reference to the relevant calendar year ending on 31 December. This can create confusion for Indian taxpayers accustomed to preparing accounts from 1 April to 31 March. For example, a shareholder may acquire shares in a Dubai company in September. The Indian tax return preparer should not simply look at the March year-end balance sheet and assume that is sufficient. The foreign-asset reporting period needs to be understood separately. This is why maintaining a dedicated record of overseas investments is useful. The Acquisition Cost Should Be Traceable When reporting UAE equity, taxpayers should retain documentation showing how the investment was acquired. For a Dubai company, this might include: incorporation documents; share certificate or share register; memorandum or constitutional documents; subscription agreement; UAE licence; Indian bank remittance documentation; ODI records where applicable; valuation documents where relevant; and evidence of subsequent capital contributions. The exact information required in the return depends on the relevant Schedule fields. But the broader principle is simple: The investment shown in the Indian return should be capable of being reconciled with the UAE corporate records and the Indian banking trail. A discrepancy is much harder to explain several years later than at the time of filing. Dubai Bank Accounts Can Create Separate Disclosure Questions Consider a founder who owns a UAE company and is also an authorised signatory on the company’s Dubai bank account. There can be two different facts: ownership of shares in the foreign company; and signing authority over a foreign bank account. The return should be reviewed for both. Similarly, an Indian resident may maintain a personal UAE bank account originally opened while working in Dubai. If the person later returns to India and becomes Resident and Ordinarily Resident, the fact that the account was opened years earlier does not automatically remove the current disclosure question. The taxpayer’s status and the existence of the foreign account for the relevant reporting period need to be considered. A Corporate Bank Account Is Not Necessarily the Shareholder’s Personal Asset Care is also required not to over-report inaccurately. A company is a separate legal person. If an Indian resident owns shares in a Dubai company, the balance in the UAE company’s bank account is not automatically the shareholder’s personal bank balance. The relevant disclosures should reflect the actual legal relationship. For example: equity ownership may be disclosed as foreign equity or financial interest; signing authority may need separate reporting where applicable; but the company’s cash should not simply be treated as though it personally belongs to the shareholder. Accurate reporting is better than either under-reporting or indiscriminately reporting the same economic item several times. UAE Property Requires Separate Consideration Dubai real estate is another common foreign asset. An Indian resident may own: a residential apartment; an office unit; a warehouse; commercial premises; or another UAE property. Where Schedule FA applies, foreign immovable property can require disclosure independently of shares held in a UAE company. The legal owner matters. If the individual owns the Dubai property personally, that is different from a Dubai company owning the property while the individual owns shares in the company. The return should follow the legal structure. This distinction can become particularly important in family holding arrangements and property investment structures. Income From the UAE Is a Different Question From Asset Disclosure Foreign asset reporting and foreign income reporting should not be treated as the same exercise. A taxpayer may need to report: the asset itself; income generated from that asset; and foreign tax paid or relief claimed. For example, assume an Indian Resident and Ordinarily Resident taxpayer owns shares in a Dubai company and receives a dividend. The shareholder may need to consider: disclosure of the UAE shareholding; reporting of the dividend income; whether the income is taxable in India; whether any UAE tax has been suffered; and whether foreign tax credit is available. Likewise, rental income from a Dubai apartment may involve both asset disclosure and income reporting. The absence of tax in the UAE does not necessarily mean that the income is not taxable in India. India’s taxation of a Resident and Ordinarily Resident taxpayer generally extends to global income, subject to applicable law and treaty relief. Schedule FSI and Schedule TR May Also Become Relevant Where foreign income has arisen, Schedule FA may not be the only schedule requiring attention. Schedule FSI is used for reporting income from sources outside India. Schedule TR relates to foreign tax relief. This can matter for an entrepreneur with a UAE business who receives: dividends; salary; interest; capital gains; professional income; or other UAE-source income. The correct reporting depends on the nature of income and the taxpayer’s circumstances. A useful filing principle is: Schedule FA tells the tax department about the foreign asset. Schedule FSI and related schedules address foreign income and relief where applicable. One should not be used as a substitute for the other. ITR-1 and ITR-4 Can Be the Wrong Forms for Taxpayers With Foreign Assets The selection of the return form itself is important. ITR-1 and ITR-4 do not contain the full foreign-asset reporting framework required for taxpayers who need to complete Schedule FA. Therefore, a taxpayer with a reportable Dubai investment should not select the simplest return form merely because their income otherwise appears straightforward. For example, an employee in India may have: only salary income in India; modest interest income; and shares in a Dubai company. Looking only at the Indian income could suggest a simple return. The foreign asset changes the filing analysis. The correct ITR form should therefore be selected after considering both income and asset disclosures. Disclosure Can Be Required Even if Income Is Below the Normal Taxable Limit This is a particularly important point. A Resident and Ordinarily Resident taxpayer holding specified foreign assets may have a return-filing obligation even where income would otherwise fall below the normal threshold requiring a return. This demonstrates how seriously foreign assets are treated in the Indian compliance framework. The question is not simply: “Do I owe tax?” It may also be: “Do I hold a reportable foreign asset?” For UAE-linked individuals, this can arise where they own a dormant Dubai company or retain an overseas account but have relatively little current Indian taxable income. A Dormant Dubai Company Should Not Automatically Be Ignored Suppose an Indian resident incorporated a Dubai company three years ago. The business did not succeed. The company has no revenue and almost no bank balance, but the licence has not yet been formally cancelled and the individual continues to hold the shares. The founder may think: “There is no business anymore, so I do not need to mention it.” That can be a mistake. If the foreign equity interest still legally exists during the relevant reporting period and Schedule FA applies, the absence of business activity does not necessarily remove the disclosure obligation. Legal ownership should be distinguished from commercial activity. If the Dubai company has been liquidated, the investor should retain the liquidation and share-cancellation documents to establish when the foreign interest actually ceased. A UAE Residence Visa Does Not Remove Schedule FA by Itself Dubai company owners frequently obtain UAE residence visas. Some then assume that the visa automatically changes their Indian income-tax position. It does not. Indian tax residence depends upon the statutory residence rules and the person’s facts for the relevant year. A taxpayer may have: UAE Emirates ID; Dubai residence visa; UAE company; UAE bank account; and still be Resident and Ordinarily Resident in India depending upon their circumstances. Conversely, another individual with the same UAE documents may qualify as non-resident. The return should follow the actual Indian residential-status analysis rather than the immigration document alone. Example: Indian Resident Owning a Dubai Consultancy Company Consider an individual living predominantly in India who owns 100% of a Dubai mainland consultancy company. During the relevant period: original investment in shares: AED 50,000; UAE corporate bank balance: AED 300,000; no dividend was declared; the shareholder was an authorised signatory; the company earned profits in Dubai. Assuming the shareholder qualifies as Resident and Ordinarily Resident in India, several separate questions arise. The foreign shareholding may require disclosure. Signing authority over the UAE account may need consideration. The retained profit of the company should not automatically be treated as though it were personally received by the shareholder merely because they control the company. However, any salary, dividend, director remuneration or other amount actually accruing or received by the individual needs separate tax analysis. This illustrates why the UAE company’s accounting and the shareholder’s personal Indian return should not be mixed together. Example: Founder Relocates From India to Dubai Now consider a different founder. The individual leaves India to establish and run a Dubai technology company and spends most of the year in the UAE. The person holds: UAE residence visa; 100% ownership of the Dubai company; UAE personal bank account; UAE corporate bank account; and a rented Dubai residence. The correct Indian return cannot be determined simply by listing the assets. The first step is to determine the individual’s residential status under Indian income-tax law for that particular year. If the taxpayer is non-resident or Resident but Not Ordinarily Resident, Schedule FA treatment can differ materially from that of a Resident and Ordinarily Resident individual. This is why residence should always be determined before foreign-asset schedules are completed. Example: Indian Company Owns a Dubai Subsidiary Now take an Indian private limited company that owns 100% of a UAE subsidiary. The position is different from an individual shareholder. The Indian company’s financial statements, overseas investment reporting, related-party transactions, tax treatment of distributions and applicable income-tax-return schedules all need to be considered at entity level. If the UAE subsidiary pays dividends, charges management fees or undertakes transactions with the Indian parent, additional tax and transfer-pricing questions can arise. The acquisition or establishment of the subsidiary may also have separate FEMA reporting requirements. The broader lesson is that ODI reporting and income-tax reporting are connected, but they are not the same compliance exercise. Completing FEMA filings does not automatically complete income-tax disclosure. The Value Reported Should Be Based on the Relevant ITR Requirement Foreign-asset schedules may request information such as: date of acquisition; initial investment or cost; peak value or balance; closing value; income derived; and other prescribed details, depending on the category of asset. The taxpayer should therefore avoid simply converting the current market value of every UAE investment and entering it everywhere. Different fields have different meanings. Currency conversion should also follow the methodology applicable to the relevant ITR requirement. A working paper showing: the original AED amount; the applicable conversion basis; the INR value reported; and the source documents can be extremely useful if the return is reviewed later. Foreign Asset Disclosure Is Increasingly Data-Driven Taxpayers sometimes believe that foreign disclosure is relevant only when the tax department already knows about the overseas asset. That is not a sensible basis for compliance. India participates in international information-exchange frameworks, and foreign financial information can reach Indian tax authorities through multiple channels. Dubai and the wider UAE have become major international financial and business centres, which means Indian taxpayers should expect cross-border financial arrangements to be capable of regulatory scrutiny. The proper approach is therefore not: “Will the department find this asset?” It is: “Does the return require this asset to be disclosed?” That is a much safer compliance standard. Consequences of Non-Disclosure Can Be Disproportionate to the Tax Involved A foreign asset may generate no taxable income and still create reporting risk if disclosure requirements are ignored. India’s Black Money legislation contains separate provisions dealing with undisclosed foreign income and assets. The law has also been amended over time to provide relief in certain cases involving relatively smaller foreign assets, but taxpayers should not interpret such relief as permission to omit disclosure. Foreign-asset non-compliance can involve consequences beyond merely paying the tax that would otherwise have been due. This is why inadvertent omissions should be corrected promptly where the law permits rather than allowed to continue from year to year. The 2026 Changes Make Accurate Disclosure Even More Relevant The Finance Act, 2026 introduced further changes in the area of foreign-asset compliance, including a framework aimed at certain small taxpayers with previously undisclosed foreign assets or foreign-source income and amendments to aspects of the penalty and prosecution regime. These developments reflect an important policy direction. The government is distinguishing more carefully between serious undisclosed offshore wealth and smaller or inadvertent foreign-asset reporting failures. But this should not be misunderstood. The underlying expectation remains that taxpayers disclose foreign assets correctly when the return requires it. For a new Dubai investment, the best strategy remains correct reporting from the first year rather than relying later on a regularisation mechanism. Dubai Founders Should Maintain a Separate “India Tax File” In practice, one of the simplest ways to avoid reporting problems is to maintain a dedicated record for the UAE investment. For an Indian founder, that file could contain: Dubai company incorporation certificate; current trade licence; memorandum and articles; share certificate or share register; corporate structure; UAE bank details; personal UAE bank details where relevant; capital contribution records; Indian outward-remittance documentation; ODI filings; annual UAE financial statements; dividend resolutions; UAE Corporate Tax returns; sale or transfer documents; and liquidation records if the company is closed. This sounds administrative. In reality, it can save considerable time during Indian return preparation. The accountant preparing the Indian return should not have to reconstruct the entire Dubai investment from WhatsApp messages and old bank statements every year. Corporate Tax in the UAE Does Not Replace Indian Reporting The introduction of UAE Corporate Tax has created another misconception. A founder may say: “My Dubai company is already paying Corporate Tax in the UAE, so India should not need the details.” These are different obligations. UAE Corporate Tax deals with taxation of the UAE entity. Schedule FA deals with disclosure of foreign assets by the relevant Indian taxpayer. If the shareholder is Resident and Ordinarily Resident in India, the fact that the UAE company is fully compliant with UAE Corporate Tax does not automatically remove the Indian shareholder’s foreign-asset reporting obligations. Similarly, UAE tax registration documents can actually help support the Indian filing because they establish the identity and financial records of the foreign entity. The two systems should therefore be coordinated rather than treated as alternatives. Particular Care Is Needed When a Dubai Business Becomes Valuable Foreign-asset reporting tends to be neglected when a company is new. This is precisely when compliance should be strongest. Imagine an Indian founder invests AED 50,000 in a Dubai technology company. For the first two years, the business has little value. Five years later, an investor values the company at AED 20 million. At that point, any inconsistency in historic ownership records, ODI documentation or Indian disclosures becomes much more significant. The asset itself has not suddenly appeared. Its value has simply increased. A clean reporting history becomes an advantage when the company is: sold; restructured; subjected to due diligence; used for investor onboarding; or included in succession planning. Compliance is easiest when established from the beginning. Don’t Confuse Company Revenue With Personal Foreign Income This deserves particular emphasis for owner-managed Dubai companies. Suppose a UAE consultancy earns AED 2 million. Its Indian resident shareholder owns 100% of the company. That does not automatically mean the shareholder personally earned AED 2 million. The company and shareholder are legally distinct. The shareholder may personally receive: salary; director remuneration; dividend; loan; reimbursement; or other payments. Each has its own tax treatment. The foreign company itself may also need separate examination for issues such as Place of Effective Management depending on where real management decisions are taken. But merely owning the company does not mean its entire turnover should be entered as the shareholder’s personal income. Foreign-asset disclosure must be accurate without disregarding basic corporate law. What Should Be Reviewed Before Filing the ITR? For a taxpayer connected with Dubai, the annual review should begin with a simple inventory. Residential status Is the taxpayer Resident and Ordinarily Resident, RNOR or non-resident? Dubai company ownership Did the taxpayer hold shares or another financial interest at any time during the relevant reporting period? UAE bank accounts Were any personal or custodial foreign accounts held? Signing authority Did the taxpayer have authority over a UAE company account or another foreign account? UAE property Was any Dubai or other UAE immovable property held directly? Foreign income Was any salary, dividend, interest, rent, capital gain or other UAE-source income earned? Foreign tax Was any UAE tax actually paid that may qualify for relief? Changes during the year Were shares purchased, sold, transferred, diluted or cancelled? Company closure Was a UAE company actually liquidated, or was the licence merely allowed to expire? Supporting records Can every significant figure reported in the ITR be reconciled with documentation? This annual exercise is far easier than reconstructing several years of information after a notice is received. A Practitioner View: The Biggest Mistake Is Often Assuming “No Income Means No Disclosure” Among UAE-linked taxpayers, this is perhaps the most important misconception to correct. Consider a founder who invested in a Dubai startup. The company made a loss. No dividend was paid. The shareholder received nothing. The immediate reaction is often: “There is no income, therefore there is nothing to report.” That conclusion confuses taxation with disclosure. Schedule FA can require information about the foreign asset itself. The better question is: “Did I hold a reportable foreign asset during the relevant period?” Only after answering that should the taxpayer move to the separate question of whether taxable foreign income arose. This small change in thinking prevents a surprisingly large number of foreign-asset reporting problems. A Practical Reporting Approach for Dubai Investors An Indian taxpayer with UAE investments can make the annual process considerably cleaner by following a consistent sequence. First, determine Indian residential status. Do not start with Schedule FA until this has been established. Second, prepare a list of all UAE assets and interests. Include company shares, accounts, properties and relevant signing authorities. Third, reconcile those assets with the previous year’s return. Identify acquisitions, disposals and changes. Fourth, collect UAE financial information. Obtain relevant balances, statements and corporate records. Fifth, identify foreign income separately. Do not confuse company income with shareholder income. Sixth, determine whether Schedule FSI or foreign tax relief schedules apply. Seventh, check the appropriate ITR form. Do not use an incompatible return simply because it is easier. Eighth, retain the supporting calculation and documents. A clean working paper today can prevent considerable uncertainty later. Conclusion Dubai has made international business ownership accessible to Indian entrepreneurs on a scale that would have been unusual a decade ago. An Indian resident can now own a UAE consulting company, technology startup, trading business, investment vehicle or property interest with relative ease. But ease of acquisition does not eliminate Indian reporting obligations. For a Resident and Ordinarily Resident taxpayer, shares in a Dubai company, UAE bank accounts, signing authority, property and other foreign financial interests can require detailed disclosure in the Indian income-tax return. The most important compliance distinction is straightforward: Foreign income and foreign assets are not the same thing. A UAE investment may require disclosure even when it has produced no dividend, no capital gain and no cash remittance to India. Similarly, FEMA reporting of the investment does not replace income-tax reporting, and a UAE residence visa does not automatically determine Indian tax residence. For Indian founders operating in Dubai, the best approach is to treat Indian return reporting as part of the investment lifecycle from the beginning. The licence, bank account, ODI records, shareholding documents, UAE financial statements and Indian tax return should all tell a consistent story. When that documentation is maintained properly, a successful UAE business remains an international asset rather than becoming an Indian compliance problem.

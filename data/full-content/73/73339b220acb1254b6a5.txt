(Supreme Court of India) Summary: The Supreme Court of India, in Dhanraj Aswani Vs Amar S. Mulchandani & Anr., delivered an important judgment on 9 September 2024, resolving a significant legal controversy concerning the maintainability of an application for anticipatory bail under Section 438 of the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973, where the applicant is already in judicial custody in connection with a different criminal case. The three-Judge Bench comprising Chief Justice Dr. Dhananjaya Y. Chandrachud, Justice J.B. Pardiwala and Justice Manoj Misra examined whether the physical confinement of an accused in one proceeding extinguishes his statutory right to seek protection against arrest in another proceeding involving a separate non-bailable offence. The controversy had arisen because different High Courts had adopted conflicting interpretations of Section 438 CrPC, particularly regarding the statutory requirement that an applicant must have reason to believe that he may be arrested. The Court held that custody in one criminal case does not, by itself, eliminate the possibility or apprehension of arrest in a separate case. Consequently, an accused who is already in custody remains entitled to approach the competent High Court or Court of Session seeking anticipatory bail in relation to another offence. The Supreme Court emphasised that the maintainability of such an application must be distinguished from the actual grant of anticipatory bail, which remains dependent upon the facts and circumstances of the individual case. The dispute originated from criminal proceedings against respondent No. 1, Amar S. Mulchandani, who had been arrested in connection with ECIR No. 10 of 2021. While remaining in custody in that proceeding, he apprehended arrest in another criminal case registered as CR No. 806 of 2019 at Pimpri Police Station, involving allegations under Sections 406, 409, 420, 465, 467, 468 and 471 read with Section 34 of the Indian Penal Code, 1860. The accused accordingly approached the Bombay High Court by filing Anticipatory Bail Application No. 2801 of 2023. The original complainant, Dhanraj Aswani, intervened and objected to the maintainability of the application, contending that a person already arrested and confined in judicial custody cannot seek anticipatory bail because the remedy is intended to protect persons who have not yet been arrested. By its order dated 31 October 2023, the Bombay High Court rejected this preliminary objection and held that existing custody in one criminal proceeding does not prohibit an application seeking protection from arrest in a separate proceeding. Aggrieved by the rejection of his objection, the complainant approached the Supreme Court. The proceedings before the Supreme Court were therefore concerned principally with the legal maintainability of the anticipatory bail application and not with a final determination of whether the accused deserved bail on the merits of the criminal allegations. Appearing for the appellant-complainant, Senior Advocate Sidharth Luthra argued that the very concept of anticipatory bail presupposes that the applicant is not already under arrest. According to the appellant, arrest involves placing the body of an individual under the physical control of lawful authorities, and once a person is already in custody, the statutory requirement of apprehending arrest cannot be satisfied. He submitted that Section 438 CrPC was enacted primarily to protect innocent persons from humiliation, harassment and unjustified detention arising from false or motivated criminal accusations, whereas an individual who has already been arrested cannot claim the same protection in that form. The appellant further contended that granting anticipatory bail to a person already in judicial custody would create practical difficulties in complying with conditions under Section 438(2), particularly the obligation to remain available for police interrogation. It was also submitted that an accused apprehending arrest in another proceeding is not without a legal remedy, since he may surrender before the competent court and seek regular bail under Sections 437 or 439 CrPC. Reliance was placed on several judicial decisions, including Gurbaksh Singh Sibbia v. State of Punjab and Sushila Aggarwal v. State (NCT of Delhi), as well as judgments of the Rajasthan, Allahabad and Delhi High Courts, to contend that the remedy of anticipatory bail could not be invoked by a person already in custody. Opposing the appeal, Senior Advocate Siddharth Dave, representing the original accused, submitted that the right to approach a competent court for protection of personal liberty cannot be extinguished merely because the applicant happens to be detained in a different criminal proceeding. He relied upon the constitutional guarantees under Articles 14 and 21 and the principle that where a legal right exists, an effective remedy must ordinarily be available. Referring to Anita Kushwaha v. Pushap Sudan, counsel emphasised the importance of access to justice as an essential constitutional protection. He argued that Section 438 CrPC requires only a reasonable apprehension of arrest on an accusation involving a non-bailable offence and contains no general prohibition against entertaining an application from a person who is already in custody in another case. The respondent also highlighted that Parliament had expressly imposed restrictions upon anticipatory bail in specified categories of offences through Section 438(4) CrPC and that special enactments, including Sections 18 and 18A of the Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes (Prevention of Atrocities) Act, 1989, contain their own statutory exclusions. The absence of a corresponding restriction concerning custody in a separate case was therefore material. Referring to the Constitution Bench judgment in Sushila Aggarwal, the respondent contended that courts should not introduce additional blanket restrictions into Section 438 through judicial interpretation when the legislature had not enacted them. The Supreme Court undertook an extensive examination of the conflicting judicial approaches adopted by different High Courts. In Sunil Kallani v. State of Rajasthan, the Rajasthan High Court had held that an anticipatory bail application was not maintainable where the accused was already in police or judicial custody in another case. That interpretation was subsequently followed in Rajesh Kumar Sharma v. CBI by the Allahabad High Court and in Bashir Hasan Siddiqui v. State (GNCTD) by the Delhi High Court. These decisions proceeded substantially on the reasoning that a person who already stands arrested cannot reasonably apprehend arrest and that the grant of anticipatory bail in another matter may interfere with investigation. In contrast, the Bombay High Court in Alnesh Akil Somji v. State of Maharashtra held that an accused already arrested in one or more cases could independently seek anticipatory bail in another case and that each application must be considered according to its own merits. The Orissa High Court adopted a comparable approach in Sanjay Kumar Sarangi v. State of Odisha, explaining that an order granting anticipatory bail in a separate proceeding could become operative upon the accused’s subsequent release and exposure to arrest in that proceeding. The Supreme Court examined these divergent interpretations and concluded that the mere existence of physical custody cannot be treated as a universal statutory disqualification from seeking anticipatory bail concerning a separate criminal accusation. In analysing the legislative purpose and historical development of anticipatory bail, the Supreme Court referred to the absence of an equivalent statutory remedy under the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1898, and the recommendations of the 41st Law Commission Report of 1969 and the 48th Law Commission Report of 1972. The Court explained that Section 438 CrPC was introduced to prevent misuse of criminal proceedings through false accusations and unnecessary deprivation of liberty, particularly where individuals could otherwise be arrested before obtaining an opportunity to approach the courts. It examined the principles established in the Constitution Bench judgments in Gurbaksh Singh Sibbia and Sushila Aggarwal, including the distinction between anticipatory bail and regular bail, the necessity of a reasonable apprehension of arrest, judicial discretion, and the prohibition against introducing inflexible restrictions unsupported by statutory language. The Court clarified that an accused cannot seek anticipatory bail in the very case in which he has already been arrested; in such circumstances, the appropriate remedy is regular bail. However, the same restriction cannot automatically be extended to a different offence merely because the applicant is detained elsewhere. The Court also examined the legal distinctions between arrest, judicial custody, police custody, production warrants and remand, recognising that involvement in another criminal proceeding may give rise to an independent legal basis for arrest or custody. The Supreme Court further explained that the rights of the accused and the investigative powers of the police must be considered separately in relation to each criminal proceeding. Where an accused is already in custody in one case but reasonably apprehends arrest in another, the subsequent case remains an independent proceeding for the purposes of statutory rights and investigative obligations. The investigating agency may, where legally justified, seek remand of the accused in the subsequent case while he remains in custody in the earlier case, provided that an anticipatory bail order has not already been obtained in relation to the subsequent offence. If such remand is authorised before anticipatory bail is granted, the accused can no longer invoke the pre-arrest remedy in that subsequent case and must pursue regular bail. Conversely, where anticipatory bail has already been granted in the subsequent case, the investigating agency cannot thereafter seek remand of the accused in that case in a manner contrary to the protection granted by the court. The Court emphasised that a grant of anticipatory bail does not confer immunity from investigation, nor does it prevent the imposition of appropriate conditions requiring cooperation. It also considered the practical injustice that could result if a detained individual were prohibited from approaching the court until release, since investigating authorities might immediately arrest him in another case before he could exercise his statutory remedy. Such an interpretation would undermine the protection of personal liberty guaranteed by Article 21 and could give rise to arbitrary consequences inconsistent with Article 14 of the Constitution. Delivering its final conclusions, the Supreme Court held that there is no express or implied prohibition under Section 438 CrPC preventing an accused already in custody in one criminal case from applying for anticipatory bail in connection with another separate non-bailable offence. The Court reaffirmed that the essential statutory requirement is a reasonable apprehension of arrest in the particular case for which anticipatory bail is sought, and detention in a different matter does not extinguish that apprehension. It rejected the interpretation that would automatically exclude all persons in custody from exercising the statutory remedy, observing that such an approach could produce unreasonable consequences and expose individuals to successive arrests without a meaningful opportunity to seek judicial protection. The Court also distinguished Narinderjit Singh Sahni v. Union of India, explaining that the earlier judgment concerned a request for broad protective relief under Article 32 in the context of multiple criminal proceedings and did not decide the maintainability question presented in the present appeal. Referring to the constitutional principles recognised in A.R. Antulay v. R.S. Nayak and State of West Bengal v. Anwar Ali Sarkar, the Supreme Court reiterated that procedural safeguards constitute valuable legal rights deserving judicial protection. Accordingly, Criminal Appeal No. 2501 of 2024 was dismissed, and the Bombay High Court was directed to proceed with the anticipatory bail application of the respondent accused on its own merits. The judgment establishes that existing custody in an unrelated criminal proceeding is not, by itself, a ground for rejecting an anticipatory bail application as non-maintainable, while preserving the competent court’s discretion to grant or refuse relief after considering the circumstances of the particular offence. Cases Discussed 1. Amir Chand v. The Crown (Punjab High Court); 1949 SCC OnLine Punj 20 — The Supreme Court examined this Full Bench decision to explain the position under the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1898, when no specific statutory provision for anticipatory bail existed. The earlier law generally contemplated bail following arrest, detention or surrender. 2. State of West Bengal v. Anwar Ali Sarkar (Supreme Court); (1952) 1 SCC 1 — The Constitution Bench decision was relied upon for the principle that procedural safeguards confer valuable rights and that their protection is an essential requirement of equality before the law under Article 14 of the Constitution. 3. Gurbaksh Singh Sibbia v. State of Punjab (Supreme Court); (1980) 2 SCC 565 — A foundational Constitution Bench authority extensively examined regarding the scope, purpose and limitations of anticipatory bail under Section 438 CrPC. The Supreme Court reiterated that anticipatory bail cannot be sought in the same offence after arrest, but that this principle does not prohibit an application relating to a different offence. Read related judgment and principles. 4. S. Harsimran Singh v. State of Punjab (Punjab and Haryana High Court); 1984 Cri LJ 253 — Discussed regarding the possibility of formally arresting a person in another criminal case notwithstanding existing judicial custody and the authority of the competent Magistrate to order custody for investigation in the separate case. 5. A.R. Antulay v. R.S. Nayak (Supreme Court); (1988) 2 SCC 602 — The Constitution Bench decision was relied upon to emphasise that procedural rights cannot be denied through an unauthorised procedure and that an accused must receive the protection of law guaranteed by Articles 14 and 21 of the Constitution. 6. Central Bureau of Investigation, Special Investigation Cell-I, New Delhi v. Anupam J. Kulkarni (Supreme Court); (1992) 3 SCC 141 — Extensively examined concerning police custody, judicial custody and the possibility of formally arresting a person in a different criminal case while the person remains in judicial custody in an earlier proceeding. 7. Kartar Singh v. State of Punjab (Supreme Court); (1994) 3 SCC 569 — Cited by the appellant during submissions concerning the legal nature of arrest, custody and the operation of statutory safeguards in criminal proceedings. 8. Ranjeet Singh v. State of Uttar Pradesh (Allahabad High Court); 1995 Cri LJ 3505 — Referred to in the discussion concerning production warrants, the movement of persons already in custody and the procedure applicable when their presence is required in another criminal proceeding. 9. State of Maharashtra v. Yadav Kohachade; 2000 Cri LJ 959 — Considered in the discussion of production warrants and the distinction between producing an accused before another court and effecting arrest or obtaining remand in a separate criminal investigation. 10. Narinderjit Singh Sahni v. Union of India (Supreme Court); (2002) 2 SCC 210 — Distinguished by the Supreme Court. The earlier decision concerned requests for broad protective relief under Article 32 in multiple criminal proceedings and did not determine whether a person already in judicial custody could maintain an anticipatory bail application in another case. 11. Siddharam Satlingappa Mhetre v. State of Maharashtra and Others (Supreme Court); (2011) 1 SCC 694 — Discussed regarding the importance of personal liberty, the presumption of innocence, the purpose of anticipatory bail and the judicial considerations relevant to granting protection against arrest. Read related judicial discussion. 12. State v. K.N. Nehru (Madras High Court); 2011 SCC OnLine Mad 1984 — Referred to in connection with the statutory procedure for production of a person already in custody and the distinction between production under Section 267 CrPC and a lawful order authorising custody in another proceeding. 13. Anita Kushwaha v. Pushap Sudan (Supreme Court); (2016) 8 SCC 509 — Invoked by the respondent accused to support the constitutional importance of access to justice and the principle that legally recognised rights must be accompanied by access to effective judicial remedies. 14. Sushila Aggarwal v. State (NCT of Delhi) (Supreme Court); (2020) 5 SCC 1 — The Constitution Bench judgment was extensively considered for the scope of Section 438 CrPC, the absence of automatic time limitations on anticipatory bail, the need for case-specific conditions and the principle that courts should not introduce blanket statutory restrictions through judicial interpretation. Read TaxGuru judgment. 15. Prathvi Raj Chauhan v. Union of India (Supreme Court); (2020) 4 SCC 727 — Cited by the respondent in relation to the statutory exclusion of anticipatory bail under the Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes (Prevention of Atrocities) Act, 1989, and the requirement that the statutory conditions for invoking the exclusion must be satisfied. 16. Sunil Kallani v. State of Rajasthan (Rajasthan High Court); 2021 SCC OnLine Raj 1654 — A principal contrary authority examined by the Supreme Court. The Rajasthan High Court had held that an accused already in police or judicial custody could not maintain an anticipatory bail application in another case. The Supreme Court rejected the reasoning insofar as it imposed a blanket prohibition on such applications. 17. Alnesh Akil Somji v. State of Maharashtra (Bombay High Court); 2021 SCC OnLine Bom 5276 — The Bombay High Court had recognised that an accused already in custody could seek anticipatory bail in another criminal case. The Supreme Court examined this interpretation while resolving the conflicting decisions of different High Courts. 18. Rajesh Kumar Sharma v. CBI (Allahabad High Court); 2022 SCC OnLine All 832 — Discussed as an authority adopting the restrictive approach of the Rajasthan High Court and holding that an anticipatory bail application was not maintainable where the applicant was already in custody in another case. 19. Tejesh Suman v. State of Rajasthan (Supreme Court); 2023 SCC OnLine SC 76 — Cited by the appellant among the authorities relied upon in support of the arguments concerning the nature of arrest, custody and the availability of anticipatory bail. 20. Bashir Hasan Siddiqui v. State (GNCTD) (Delhi High Court); 2023 SCC OnLine Del 7544 — The Delhi High Court had followed the Rajasthan and Allahabad High Court decisions in holding that an anticipatory bail application would not be maintainable when the applicant was already in custody in a different criminal case. This restrictive approach was examined and rejected by the Supreme Court. 21. Sanjay Kumar Sarangi v. State of Odisha (Orissa High Court); 2024 SCC OnLine Ori 1334 — Extensively examined for the proposition that there is no statutory bar against applying for anticipatory bail in a separate case while already in custody. The decision also explained the relationship between anticipatory bail, future arrest, police remand and regular bail. 22. Tusharbhai Rajnikantbhai Shah v. State of Gujarat (Supreme Court); 2024 SCC OnLine SC 1897 — Referred to in connection with Section 267 CrPC, production warrants and the limits on obtaining custody in another criminal case after an anticipatory bail order has been granted in relation to that case. Legal Principle Established: A person already arrested and detained in connection with one criminal case may maintain an application for anticipatory bail under Section 438 CrPC in another criminal case, provided the person has not already been arrested in the latter case. The competent court must decide the application independently on its merits, subject to applicable statutory restrictions.

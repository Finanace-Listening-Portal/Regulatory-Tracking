(Supreme Court of India) The Supreme Court of India dismissed an appeal filed by German company M/s Iveco Magirus Brandschutztechnik GmbH, challenging criminal defamation proceedings initiated by Nirmal Kishore Bhartiya. The dispute concerned allegations made in correspondence addressed to public authorities following a tender floated by the Airports Authority of India for supplying 40 Airfield Crash Fire Tenders. The Court examined whether a Magistrate must consider the exceptions to Section 499 of the Indian Penal Code before summoning an accused, whether a High Court can quash defamation proceedings under Section 482 of the Code of Criminal Procedure by applying those exceptions, and whether a company can face prosecution for allegedly defamatory statements made by its authorised representative. The controversy originated when the Airports Authority of India rejected the appellant company’s tender in July 2008 and awarded the contract to Rosenbauer International AG. The appellant’s local representative, M.C. Aggarwal, subsequently sent letters to various government authorities alleging favouritism and irregularities in the tender process. The complainant, whose company was associated with Rosenbauer, alleged that these communications contained defamatory imputations. Following a criminal complaint, the Additional Chief Metropolitan Magistrate, New Delhi, issued summons against Aggarwal, the appellant company and its District Manager (Asia) for offences under Sections 500, 107 and 34 IPC. The Delhi High Court dismissed the appellant’s petition challenging the summons on 10 December 2010. The appellant contended before the Supreme Court that it had neither written nor authorised the allegedly defamatory letters and that merely executing a power of attorney in favour of Aggarwal could not establish criminal liability. It argued that an agent’s authority under Section 188 of the Indian Contract Act, 1872, extended only to lawful acts. The company further submitted that documents filed in judicial proceedings enjoyed protection and that the Fourth Exception to Section 499 IPC applied. It relied on decisions including Rajendra Kumar Sitaram Pande v. Uttam and Aroon Purie v. State of NCT of Delhi to contend that the benefit of statutory exceptions could be considered before trial. The complainant opposed the appeal, asserting that Aggarwal had acted on behalf of the company and that the allegedly defamatory letters were subsequently incorporated into litigation concerning the tender. He argued that the company’s claimed lack of knowledge or authorisation required examination of disputed evidence. The complainant also maintained that the power of attorney and other defence materials had not been admitted or proved and therefore could not justify quashing the proceedings at the preliminary stage. The Supreme Court consequently examined an extensive line of judicial precedents concerning the issuance of summons, the burden of establishing exceptions to criminal defamation and the limits of inherent jurisdiction. The Supreme Court explained that there is no universal rule requiring a defamation accused to undergo trial before an exception to Section 499 IPC can be considered. A Magistrate must judicially assess the complaint, supporting evidence and permissible inquiry materials to determine whether sufficient grounds exist for proceeding. Where reliable material available at that stage clearly establishes an applicable exception, the Magistrate is not necessarily prohibited from considering it. Equally, the High Court may exercise its inherent powers in an appropriate case, but it cannot ordinarily resolve disputed factual defences, evaluate unproved documents or conduct a premature trial. The Court reconciled the relevant precedents by emphasising that the outcome depends upon the materials lawfully available and the particular circumstances of each case. Applying these principles, the Supreme Court held that the appellant had not established grounds for interference with the summoning order. The power of attorney relied upon by the company remained unproved, while the questions of authorisation, knowledge, consent and responsibility for Aggarwal’s communications required evidentiary examination. The Court observed that corporate liability for defamatory statements made by an authorised agent depends on the facts established at trial. It also declined to extend the benefit of the Fourth Exception to Section 499 IPC at this stage, leaving the appellant free to establish that exception or any other available defence before the Trial Court. The Supreme Court accordingly dismissed Criminal Appeal No. 1959 of 2012, vacated the interim order and made no order as to costs. It left all questions not expressly decided open for consideration by the Trial Court and encouraged expeditious disposal because the criminal proceedings had already been delayed considerably. The judgment reinforces the distinction between preliminary judicial scrutiny and the adjudication of contested factual defences, while recognising that statutory exceptions to defamation may, in suitable cases supported by reliable material, be considered before the commencement of trial.

Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); ACIT Vs Jagdish Kumar Dhingra (ITAT Delhi) Brokerage Accrues When the Right to Receive Vests and Taxable Income Is Limited to Commission That Has Become Due The Dispute Over ₹1.11 Crore of Brokerage The Delhi ITAT held that where brokerage becomes payable with reference to amounts realised by the promoter from buyers, the taxable income is the commission on which the broker’s right to receive has vested. A seized spreadsheet calculated brokerage of ₹1,11,85,948 against the assessee’s name. The Tribunal read that spreadsheet alongside other project records, worked out the assessee’s share of commission that had actually become due, and directed verification of the brokerage already offered to tax. Third-Party Search Led to Assessment Under Section 153C A search under section 132 of the Income-tax Act, 1961 was conducted in the Ravinder Singh Group on 18 September 2018. The material examined by the Department included emails, WhatsApp exchanges and accounting records concerning RST Horizon, a commercial project at Karkardooma developed by Summit Promoter Pvt. Ltd. The Assessing Officer issued notice to the assessee under section 153C. An Excel attachment titled BSP Detail of Summit Master recorded unit-wise bookings, buyer details, sale values and brokerage computations. The Assessing Officer also relied on the statement of Sandip Sahni, a director of Solutrean Buildings Technologies Pvt. Ltd., recorded under section 131(1A). Sahni stated that Dhingra acted as a broker for the project and was entitled to commission on sales facilitated by him. Reading the material together, the Assessing Officer concluded that Dhingra had received ₹1,11,85,948 in cash and added it under section 69A read with section 115BBE. CIT(A) Restricted the Addition to ₹50,000 The CIT(A) found that the spreadsheet contained a computation of brokerage and that the evidence did not establish actual receipt of the entire amount shown against the assessee. The CIT(A) also took into account the difficulties faced by the project and the cancellation of bookings. For the year under appeal, the CIT(A) found that the assessee had received ₹5.50 lakh through banking channels and had disclosed ₹5 lakh. Accordingly, the addition was restricted to ₹50,000, and the Revenue challenged the deletion of the balance. Seized Records Established the Brokerage Arrangement The Tribunal found that the spreadsheet was a systematic record of project transactions. It contained details of individual bookings and separately identified sales facilitated by Dhingra and Solutrean. The Tribunal also noted that Summit Promoter had deducted tax at source on brokerage paid to the assessee. These circumstances supported the finding that Dhingra had acted as a broker and was entitled to commission at 9%, subject to the applicable payment terms. The Tribunal observed that the assessee had failed to avail himself of two opportunities for cross-examination and had responded to the seized material with general denials. On these facts, it held that the assessee had failed to rebut the presumptions it drew with reference to the search provisions, including section 292C. The records nevertheless had to be read together to determine the amount that had become payable. Buyer Realisations Determined When Brokerage Accrued The Tribunal distinguished between brokerage calculated on bookings and brokerage that had become payable on collections. The combined sales attributed to the assessee and Solutrean were approximately ₹17.45 crore, on which the spreadsheet computed commission of approximately ₹1.57 crore. A separate reconciliation showed that ₹61,94,824 had become due to both brokers by reference to the promoter’s actual realisations from buyers. The Tribunal treated this lower amount as the real income that had accrued to the two brokers. It also noted that some bookings had been cancelled. Where the arrangement links commission to buyer payments, the promoter’s realisation of the sale proceeds determines when the broker acquires an enforceable right to receive the corresponding commission. Tribunal Quantified the Assessee’s Share at ₹44.13 Lakh The Tribunal apportioned ₹61,94,824 in the ratio of the brokerage figures attributed in the seized spreadsheet to Dhingra and Solutrean, namely ₹1,11,85,948 to ₹45,15,708. The assessee’s share worked out to ₹44,13,151. After provisionally deducting ₹10 lakh as brokerage already declared, the Tribunal sustained ₹34,13,151, subject to the Assessing Officer verifying how much of the ₹10 lakh had been offered in the relevant year or subsequent years and granting credit accordingly. The Revenue’s appeal was partly allowed. Author’s Comments The promoter’s collections and the broker’s receipts answer different questions. Buyer collections determine when commission becomes payable under a collection-linked arrangement. The broker’s bank entries and returns help establish how much has already been received or offered to tax. Here, reading the booking spreadsheet with the collection reconciliation allowed the Tribunal to recognise brokerage that had accrued while excluding the portion for which the right to receive had yet to vest. The verification direction also matters because the CIT(A) and the Tribunal referred to different figures for the brokerage already declared.

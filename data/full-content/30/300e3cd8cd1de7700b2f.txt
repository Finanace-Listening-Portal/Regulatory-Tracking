Summary: On 13 October 2026, reports quoted Finance Minister Nirmala Sitharaman as saying that she was open to hearing stakeholder views regarding removal of long-term capital gains (LTCG) tax. The statement is significant for investors, financial advisers and market participants because it acknowledges an active policy debate. It is not, by itself, a Cabinet decision, a Finance Bill proposal, a statutory amendment or an effective exemption. Investors must continue to apply the capital gains law currently in force until a valid legislative change is enacted and brought into effect. It influences decisions about portfolio rebalancing, asset allocation, liquidity and the timing of disposals. Supporters of relief argue that reducing taxation may improve long-term investment incentives and market participation. Others point to revenue implications, distributional effects and the need for a consistent framework across asset classes. The policy choice cannot be assessed solely by the stock market’s immediate response to a headline. Existing Treatment of Listed Equity For qualifying transfers of listed equity shares, equity-oriented mutual fund units and business trust units covered by section 112A of the Income-tax Act, 1961, long-term capital gains above the applicable annual threshold are subject to the statutory special rate, subject to conditions. Following the 2024 changes, the generally applicable section 112A rate is 12.5% on qualifying gains exceeding Rs. 1.25 lakh, with applicable surcharge and cess. The applicable treatment of transactions in FY 2026-27 should also be checked against the Income-tax Act, 2025 and its corresponding provisions, as the new Act has come into force from 1 April 2026. The correct statute and transition rules must be applied for the particular tax year. Different Assets, Different Tax Consequences LTCG is not a single uniform tax imposed identically on every investment. Listed shares, equity mutual funds, immovable property, unlisted shares, debt-oriented fund holdings and other assets may be governed by different holding-period tests, charging provisions, rates, grandfathering rules and exceptions. Certain funds or securities can be subject to special deemed short-term treatment. A broad public debate about abolishing LTCG should therefore not be interpreted as a proposal with settled coverage for every asset. The scope of any eventual amendment will matter as much as the headline rate. How a Tax Removal Could Affect Investors If Parliament eventually removed tax on specified long-term gains, the post-tax return on those eligible disposals could improve. However, the effect would depend on whether relief applied prospectively, to all assets, only to listed equity, or subject to conditions and anti-avoidance rules. There could also be behavioural effects, including portfolio reshuffling and a change in the timing of sales. Any calculation of potential savings today remains hypothetical. Investors should not recognise a tax benefit in financial projections as though an announced exemption already exists. Revenue, Fairness and Policy Trade-Offs Capital gains taxes generate revenue and form part of the overall structure for taxing investment income. Policymakers must balance capital formation and investment incentives with revenue needs, equity between different classes of taxpayers and opportunities for tax arbitrage. A selective exemption might encourage migration between asset classes. A universal exemption would have broader revenue implications. Alternatives to complete removal could include rate rationalisation, higher thresholds, inflation adjustments or improved loss set-off rules, each with different winners and administrative consequences. Market Reaction Is Not a Legal Change Reports linked the Finance Minister’s openness to the discussion with a positive market reaction. Market prices may respond quickly to anticipated policy shifts, but that reaction reflects expectations rather than the legal status of an exemption. Equity prices also move because of earnings, global markets, interest rates, liquidity, currency developments and many other factors. Investors should distinguish a report about stakeholder consultations from an officially introduced amendment. A decision based exclusively on a short-lived headline can expose the investor to both market risk and tax risk. Planning for FY 2026-27 and AY 2027-28 Taxpayers planning transactions during FY 2026-27, corresponding to tax year 2026-27 under the Income-tax Act, 2025, should determine the applicable holding period, cost basis, transaction charges, exemptions and loss-adjustment rules under the law governing that year. Historic purchase dates and grandfathering provisions may be particularly important for long-held investments. Before a major sale, the investor should prepare a transaction-wise capital gains statement and consider advance-tax implications where relevant. A future announcement may not operate retrospectively and cannot safely be assumed in a present-day computation. Implications for Businesses and Investment Funds Corporate treasury teams, family offices, investment funds and high-net-worth individuals may need scenario analyses rather than immediate changes to tax positions. Investment managers should explain that an exemption remains speculative and document the assumptions used in forecasts. For financial reporting, deferred tax and current tax assessments must reflect enacted or substantively enacted requirements as applicable under the relevant accounting framework, rather than an informal expression of willingness to receive suggestions. Fund documents and investor communications should avoid promising tax-free exits. What to Watch Next The next meaningful developments would be a formal consultation paper, a Finance Ministry statement identifying the assets and transactions under consideration, a proposed legislative text, or a Finance Bill. Any proposal should be examined for commencement date, transitional treatment, holding-period rules, the treatment of existing losses and the interaction with securities transaction tax. Until such documents emerge, the appropriate description is that the Government is open to views, not that LTCG tax has been abolished or that abolition is assured. Conclusion The Finance Minister’s reported remarks have reopened an important debate over the taxation of long-term investment gains. They create an opportunity for stakeholders to make evidence-based submissions, but no immediate tax exemption follows from the statement. Investors should maintain existing compliance, avoid speculative tax planning and reassess their positions only when a concrete legal proposal is available.

Articles 4 min read Full AI autonomy in NBFC lending remains distant as human oversight and risk management take priority: Industry leaders NBFCs could automate a larger share of lending operations through generative and agentic AI, but decisions involving credit risk, customer rights and regulatory compliance will continue to require human oversight, Industry leaders said at the ETBFSI NBFC Leaders’ Retreat 2026. ETBFSI Staff ETBFSI Published On Oct 12, 2026 at 05:08 AM IST var news_msid = 134879810; console.log(news_msid); Select ETBFSI as Preferred Choice on Google Copy Link Share on WhatsApp Share on Linkedin Share on X Share on Telegram Share on Facebook NBFCs could automate 50–80% of business processes over the next few years using artificial intelligence (AI), but fully autonomous lending remains a distant prospect as high-risk decisions require human judgment and accountability, NBFC leaders said at the ETBFSI NBFC Leaders’ Retreat 2026.Dharmvir Singh, CTO, Sammaan Capital, estimated that NBFCs could achieve 50–60% automation across business functions over the next two to three years. Premraj Avasthi, CIO, GIC Housing Finance, offered a more ambitious estimate of 75–80% autonomy, with humans retaining accountability for consequential decisions. Advt Sudesh Puthran, CTO, CreditAccess Grameen, said decisions such as loan rejections, restructuring, write-offs and legal proceedings should remain subject to human judgment, while document extraction, identity verification and data entry could be automated more extensively.Himanshu Gupta, Co-Founder and Chief AI Officer, WeRize, said the degree of automation should depend on the risk involved. “The automation will be inversely proportional to risk,” he said.AI is improving lending productivitySingh said Sammaan Capital was using AI to process lengthy legal and technical reports, automate document classification and reduce manual workloads. Documents running into around 100 pages could be processed to extract and summarise relevant information, helping evaluators handle larger volumes without a proportionate increase in manpower.AI was also being used for infrastructure monitoring, information security and real-time language translation in customer service, he said.Binod Roy, CTO, Wonder Home Finance, highlighted the use of AI in underwriting and collections. AI tools can identify missing documents, flag incomplete loan applications and prompt field executives to obtain additional information. Roy said the company's collections through digital channels had increased by around 30%, while technology-enabled monitoring had improved contact-centre efficiency.Singh also pointed to AI-assisted software development as a way to reduce technology development time and costs. Sammaan Capital took around five to six months to develop a loan origination journey on Salesforce, while a comparable self-service journey using an open-source technology stack took around three months, he said. Advt Data integration remains a key challengeAvasthi said legacy technology and fragmented data remained major obstacles to AI adoption. At GIC Housing Finance, the technology transformation began with a structured data architecture to support management information systems, regulatory reporting and business intelligence.The company adopted a three-layer data lakehouse architecture comprising bronze, silver and gold layers to organise information across existing applications.Roy said NBFCs should modernise legacy platforms in phases rather than replace them abruptly, ensuring that new systems can reliably support business operations before older platforms are retired. Advt For lenders, building reliable data foundations will be critical to deploying AI effectively across underwriting, customer servicing and risk management.Rural lending needs human validationPuthran said CreditAccess Grameen processes around 1.8 lakh loan applications daily, making consistent and efficient credit assessment essential. However, many rural borrowers lack structured financial records that accurately reflect their household income or business activity.The company has been testing AI tools to assess income from dairy activities using images captured by field officers. The models identify livestock characteristics and estimate potential milk production, with the resulting assessment presented to field officers for validation. Advt Puthran said the initiative had completed a proof of concept and was moving towards implementation. AI-assisted conversations could also help monitor whether field officers had asked required questions, summarise borrower responses and flag missing information.Such applications could improve consistency in lending decisions without eliminating the contextual understanding of field staff, he said.Data privacy and bias require stronger safeguardsPuthran said consent management, purpose-specific data usage and retention requirements under India's Digital Personal Data Protection framework would need to be incorporated into technology architecture from the outset.Gupta said AI agents should be treated like digital employees, with appropriate access controls, accountability and data-handling standards. The use of external AI models and cloud infrastructure also raises questions about the handling and location of sensitive customer information.He cautioned that AI models trained on historical lending data could reproduce existing biases and continue excluding borrower groups that had previously received limited access to credit. Lenders must assess model behaviour and begin with smaller financial exposures before expanding automation, he said.Gupta also highlighted the potential of AI to improve financial inclusion by helping rejected applicants understand their credit profiles, identify factors affecting eligibility and learn how to improve their chances of accessing credit.AI adoption must deliver measurable returnsAvasthi flagged the cost of large language models, particularly token-based pricing, as a potential constraint on large-scale deployment. He called for more predictable commercial models and stronger safeguards from AI providers.Singh also cautioned against deploying increasingly autonomous systems without adequate controls, while Puthran said employees would need to adapt and develop capabilities suited to roles requiring greater judgment and specialised expertise.The discussion highlighted that the transition from generative AI to agentic AI will depend on more than deploying new applications. While routine workflows, document processing and operational monitoring are likely to see greater automation, decisions involving borrower distress, creditworthiness and legal consequences will require stronger human oversight.For NBFCs, the challenge will be to combine AI-led productivity with reliable data, cybersecurity, regulatory compliance and clear accountability, while ensuring that technology investments deliver sustainable business outcomes.

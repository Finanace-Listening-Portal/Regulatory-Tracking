Articles 5 min read RBI's FERR rule puts banks' own FX hedges under pressure; lenders seek alternative to 20% cash reserve Banks are taking a conservative approach to forex hedging after RBI’s latest measures, with oil demand shifting out of the spot market, forward activity turning cautious and lenders flagging possible double counting of the 20 per cent reserve. FEDAI is seeking clarity, which bankers expect within days. Shrishti Sharma ETBFSI Published On Oct 14, 2026 at 01:13 PM IST var news_msid = 134936119; console.log(news_msid); Select ETBFSI as Preferred Choice on Google Copy Link Share on WhatsApp Share on Linkedin Share on X Share on Telegram Share on Facebook Banks have turned conservative on quoting and booking FX derivatives as they await clarity on the 20 per cent FERR requirement.RBI’s special dollar window for oil companies has taken a significant source of demand away from the spot market, helping the rupee strengthen.Banks are concerned that the reserve could effectively be counted twice on client and interbank legs of the same transaction.FEDAI is in discussions with RBI and bankers expect operational clarity through FAQs within the next few days.The Reserve Bank of India’s decision to extend the 20 per cent Foreign Exchange Risk Reserve (FERR) to banks’ own rupee-linked FX hedges has prompted lenders to take a conservative approach to forex activity, with banks seeking clarity on the operational framework and cost implications, according to seven treasury and banking officials ETBFSI spoke with.The measure means banks will also have to maintain FERR when hedging currency risk on their own assets and liabilities, adding to costs for lenders with significant foreign-currency exposures.“It is an enhanced burden. Had it been an incremental CRR or something, there was nothing to take up with the Reserve Bank. It is a directive and you would have complied with. But this will put pressure, especially on banks which have a big presence overseas or assets denominated in foreign currencies. Naturally, for all that hedging, enhanced costs will have to be incurred,” said the Treasury Head of a large public sector bank. Advt The banker also pointed to tighter system liquidity.“Liquidity in the system has reduced considerably in the last one month. So, whether putting this much into the requirements can be looked into or revisited by the Reserve Bank,” the banker said.A Treasury Head at a foreign bank said the RBI’s special dollar window for oil companies had already reduced spot-market demand. “We already see that the spot has moved lower because, importantly, oil companies have been taken out of the system. They will purchase through a special window. The covering from importers is expected to reduce, so the dollar purchase on account of that is expected to reduce,” the banker said.The banker said banks were taking a conservative view until clarity emerges, with FERR costs being factored into client pricing. Most activity, the banker said, was concentrated in short-tenor forwards.“Due to the FERR reserves that we have to maintain, the cost of hedging goes up. If there is a necessity for hedging, the cost would be looked at by the client and accordingly they would take those decisions,” the banker said.The banker expects operational clarity through an FAQ, though he said the interpretation could take about a week.Double counting concernsOne of the concerns among banks is that the same economic transaction could effectively attract the 20 per cent reserve twice — once on the client hedge and again on the bank’s interbank hedge. Advt “It will impact the bank's cost of funding directly. It's kind of a double-counting thing. Suppose a client is there with USD 20 million and I am using it in the interbank market. I am providing 20 per cent on the client hedge and I am providing 20 per cent on my hedge. However, the trade is the same. This is like one leg with the client and one leg with interbank. So there are so many things like that which need discussion,” said the Treasury Head of another private sector bank.The banker expects a conclusive FAQ or framework within two-three days, most likely through FEDAI and that RBI had seen hedging against anticipated exposures rise to two-three times normal levels. Advt “There is a certain extent of speculation which has built into the market, and probably RBI is trying to remove that so that dollar-rupee can return to actual fundamental levels,” the banker said.FCNR coupon hedgesA Treasury Head at a large private sector bank said the measure could also affect banks hedging their own FCNR coupon payments.“The principals are anyway hedged with the RBI, so there is no tension there. But the interest payments, the coupon payments, were open, which banks had to hedge on their own. They were waiting for an opportunity to hedge this when the spot went down. And the spot did go down,” the banker said. Advt “I think RBI sensed that and has put this out. Which means now if banks want to hedge their own interest payment coupons, the FERR will also be applicable. That is the understanding as of now,” the banker said.The banker said banks would remain cautious wherever the framework was unclear. “If it's a grey area, it's not black and white, they will prefer to just take a very conservative view,” the banker said.The banker also flagged uncertainty over how the threshold would be assessed where a customer hedges the same underlying exposure across multiple transactions.For instance, a customer with a USD 10 million underlying exposure could hedge USD 1.5 million on one day and another USD 1.5 million the next day, raising questions over whether the threshold is assessed per transaction or cumulatively.“Some banks are saying that I might not charge him today. But tomorrow, if he comes and then cumulatively for the same underlying it is more than USD 2 million, we will charge retrospectively. But retrospectively charging means an operational hassle,” the banker said.Banks seek alternatives to cashBanks are also seeking alternatives to the non-interest-bearing reserve, particularly given the impact on funding costs and balance sheets.“Can you take a bank guarantee? Can you take something else in place of that? That's effectively blocking a lot of capital with RBI at no cost. You are not getting anything in return. Especially banks with smaller balance sheets are more affected,” the banker said.Banks are already incorporating the cost into forward pricing, with the higher reserve requirement expected to increase the cost of hedging for customers.“Banks have built that 20 per cent capital cost into their forward premium. If the forward premium for, say, three months is Rs 1, instead of Rs 1, it will be Rs 1.50,” the large private sector bank Treasury Head said.A Treasury Head of a mid-sized private sector bank said the higher cost would ultimately be passed on to customers.

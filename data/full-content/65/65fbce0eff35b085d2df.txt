Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); Rama Narang Vs Ramesh Narang and Others (Supreme Court of India) The Supreme Court dismissed a contempt petition filed by Rama Narang against Ramesh Narang and others arising from a prolonged family dispute concerning the management of Narang International Hotels Limited (NIHL). The central question was whether proceedings initiated before the Company Law Board (CLB), and interim orders passed to address a management deadlock, constituted contempt of earlier consent orders and undertakings recorded by the Supreme Court. The Court held that the contempt petition lacked merit, particularly because the CLB had exercised its statutory jurisdiction to protect the company and its employees, and its orders could not be treated as contempt merely because they affected the working arrangements established by the earlier family settlement. Background and family settlement: The dispute arose between Rama Narang and his sons Ramesh and Rajesh Narang over the management and control of NIHL and its subsidiaries. Following several rounds of litigation, the parties entered into a family settlement incorporated into the Supreme Court’s orders dated 12 December 2001 and 8 January 2002. The settlement provided that Rama, Ramesh and Rajesh would remain directors of NIHL, with Rama and Ramesh sharing management and control and Rajesh serving as the permanent whole-time director responsible for day-to-day operations. Major company decisions required mutual consent, and financial transactions exceeding Rs. 10 lakh were subject to joint-signature requirements. The settlement was intended to preserve joint participation in the family business and resolve the outstanding litigation. Disputes nevertheless continued. Rama Narang alleged that Ramesh and Rajesh had excluded him from management, taken unilateral decisions, divided high-value transactions into smaller payments to circumvent joint-signature requirements and acted contrary to the consent terms. Earlier contempt proceedings culminated in the Supreme Court’s judgment dated 15 March 2007, in which Ramesh and Rajesh were found guilty of wilful disobedience and sentenced to two months’ simple imprisonment with a fine of Rs. 2,000 each. However, considering the possible consequences for the company’s operations and employees, the Court kept the imprisonment sentence in abeyance and directed strict compliance with the undertakings. Proceedings before the Company Law Board: The management dispute subsequently created serious operational difficulties. Ramesh Narang instituted Company Petition No. 47 of 2008 before the CLB under Sections 397 and 398 of the Companies Act, 1956, alleging that the company’s affairs had reached a standstill because of continuing disagreements among the directors. Salaries and wages remained unpaid, suppliers were affected and employees had resorted to protests. The CLB observed that the interests of the company and more than 3,000 workers required immediate protection. By an order dated 10 April 2008, it appointed former Chief Justice Arvind V. Savant as a facilitator to help resolve urgent operational disputes and, where consensus was impossible, take binding decisions concerning essential business operations. Rama Narang challenged the CLB’s intervention through the present contempt petition, contending that the appointment of a facilitator and the conferment of decision-making authority upon him undermined the Supreme Court’s earlier consent orders. Subsequent CLB proceedings resulted in further directions empowering the facilitator to deal with disagreements among directors, including matters involving cheques, minutes and statutory records. Different facilitators and advisers were appointed over time to address the company’s continuing difficulties. Meanwhile, the Supreme Court itself passed several orders concerning the preparation and audit of accounts, compliance with the Companies Act and Income-tax Act, and appointment of independent professionals to protect the interests of the company, employees and public revenue. Question of contempt: The Supreme Court examined whether the respondents’ invocation of the CLB’s statutory jurisdiction and their participation in proceedings before that forum amounted to wilful disobedience of the earlier Supreme Court orders. The Court distinguished between deliberate violation of a binding judicial direction and resort to a legally available remedy for resolving subsequent corporate disputes. It considered the purpose of the earlier consent orders, the continuing management deadlock and the circumstances in which the CLB had intervened. The Court also examined the scope of the CLB’s authority under the Companies Act to make interim arrangements designed to prevent disruption of the company’s affairs. Jurisdiction and binding nature of interim orders: The Court considered the principle recognised in Tayabbhai M. Bagasarwalla v. Hind Rubber Industries (P) Ltd. that a judicial or statutory forum may issue interim orders while examining objections to its jurisdiction and that such orders remain binding unless set aside in accordance with law. Rama Narang had raised objections before the CLB concerning maintainability, but he had not challenged the CLB’s order dated 10 April 2008 before an appropriate appellate forum. The Supreme Court held that he could not treat the CLB proceedings and resulting interim orders as contempt merely by asserting that the proceedings were not maintainable. At the same time, the Court expressly clarified that it was not finally deciding the maintainability of the underlying company proceedings and that the parties remained free to raise their available objections before the competent forum. Company interests and statutory remedies: The Court noted that the CLB’s intervention was prompted by serious operational difficulties affecting salary payments, suppliers, statutory compliance and the company’s ability to continue functioning. Its interim arrangements were directed toward protecting the company and its workforce rather than nullifying the Supreme Court’s earlier orders. The Supreme Court also declined to issue fresh directions under Article 142 compelling compliance with a facilitator’s decision dated 30 April 2019. Since the relevant company proceedings had subsequently moved to the National Company Law Tribunal, the Court considered it appropriate for the parties to pursue available statutory remedies before that tribunal instead of seeking additional relief through contempt proceedings. Final decision: The Supreme Court dismissed Contempt Petition (Civil) No. 92 of 2008 as being without merit. It also declined to entertain the substantive relief sought through Interlocutory Application No. 87565 of 2019 and disposed of that application, leaving the parties free to seek appropriate relief in accordance with law. All other pending interlocutory applications were disposed of. The judgment clarifies that contempt jurisdiction cannot be used as a substitute for statutory appellate or company-law remedies and that interim orders passed by a competent forum for protecting a company’s operations cannot be disregarded merely because jurisdictional objections have been raised.

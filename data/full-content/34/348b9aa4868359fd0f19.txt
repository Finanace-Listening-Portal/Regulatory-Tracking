Summary: Selling a Dubai or UAE company and bringing the proceeds to India involves more than a share-transfer agreement and bank remittance. For a seller resident in India under FEMA, the transaction may constitute Overseas Direct Investment (ODI) disinvestment, requiring verification of the original investment, compliance with the one-year holding condition, arm’s-length valuation and completion of outstanding reporting. Sale proceeds must generally be repatriated within 90 days, while disinvestment reporting through Form FC is ordinarily due within 30 days of receipt. The designated authorised dealer bank should review the transaction before closing, especially where payment is deferred, held in escrow or involves related parties. UAE mainland or free-zone share-transfer procedures must be completed, and any shareholder loans, guarantees and outstanding investment dues should be addressed. Indian capital-gains taxation depends on the seller’s income-tax residential status, which is distinct from FEMA residence. Foreign tax credit, UAE tax treatment and Schedule FA disclosures may also require examination. The article provides practical guidance on pricing, banking documentation, full and partial exits, liquidation, tax computations and reporting, with a worked example and a detailed pre-closing checklist to reduce regulatory and banking delays. This is not decided solely by: Indian citizenship; A UAE residence visa; Emirates ID; Indian passport; NRI status used casually in conversation; or Location of the bank account receiving the money. A person living and working genuinely in Dubai may be resident outside India for FEMA purposes. Another individual who holds a UAE visa but continues to live and manage business predominantly from India may remain a person resident in India. The residential status on the sale date affects: Whether the transaction is governed as ODI disinvestment; Whether the proceeds must be repatriated; Which bank must handle the reporting; Whether Form FC is required; How foreign accounts may be used; and The Indian tax and disclosure analysis. The seller should not rely on the residential status that existed when the UAE company was incorporated. Status may have changed by the time the shares are sold. Determine whether the shares are ODI or OPI Shares in most privately held Dubai mainland and free-zone companies are unlisted equity. Their acquisition by an Indian resident will generally have been classified as Overseas Direct Investment. This is different from Overseas Portfolio Investment, which primarily covers eligible non-ODI foreign securities. The classification matters because ODI disinvestment is subject to specific conditions, including: Compliance of the original investment; Minimum holding-period requirements; Arm’s-length pricing; Repatriation of proceeds; Clearance of outstanding investment dues in a full exit; Form FC reporting; and Reporting through the designated AD bank. The seller should retrieve the original ODI file before negotiating the sale. It should contain the UIN, Form FC filings, bank remittance records, share certificate, valuation and annual compliance documents. An initially non-compliant investment cannot be cured by selling it A person resident in India is not permitted to hold or transfer an overseas investment if the original acquisition itself was not permitted under FEMA. A sale does not automatically regularise: Unreported share subscriptions; Shares purchased through an unauthorised payment route; Ownership recorded in a partner’s or relative’s name without proper disclosure; Missed UIN requirements; Unreported capitalisation; Excess investment beyond the permitted limit; or Acquisition of shares in a prohibited structure. If the original investment file is incomplete, the seller should approach the designated bank before entering into a binding sale agreement. Some reporting delays may be regularised through the applicable late-submission mechanism. More serious contraventions may require compounding or another regulatory process. Attempting to sell first and explain the investment later can delay both the UAE transfer and receipt of the proceeds in India. Pending ODI reporting can block the exit A person with delayed overseas-investment reporting may be restricted from making further financial commitment or transferring the investment until the delay has been regularised. The authorised dealer bank may review whether the seller has completed: Initial Form FC reporting; Evidence-of-investment submission; Annual Performance Reports; Foreign Liabilities and Assets returns; Reporting of loans and guarantees; Reporting of step-down subsidiaries; Reporting of restructuring; and Previous changes in shareholding. A company may have renewed its Dubai license every year while its Indian ODI filings remained incomplete. UAE corporate compliance does not replace Indian reporting. The ODI compliance review should therefore be completed before the buyer’s closing date is fixed. The one-year holding condition Where disinvestment relates to ODI, the resident transferor must generally have remained invested for at least one year from the date of making the ODI. This prevents an ODI investment from being made and immediately exited without satisfying the prescribed holding condition. The one-year period should be tested using reliable documents, including: Date of the remittance; Date of share allotment; Date appearing on the share certificate; Effective date in the UAE company register; Form FC reporting date; and Any later allotment or acquisition date. Where shares were acquired in different tranches, each tranche may require separate examination. Specific exceptions may apply to qualifying mergers, demergers or amalgamations in which the aggregate ownership of the Indian entity is not changed or diluted. An ordinary private sale should not assume that an exception is available. Full and partial disinvestment are treated differently A seller may dispose of all the UAE shares or retain a minority interest. In a full disinvestment, other than through liquidation, the resident transferor must not have outstanding dues that they are entitled to receive from the UAE company as an investor in equity or debt. Such dues may include: Declared but unpaid dividends; Shareholder loans; Interest on shareholder debt; Redeemable amounts; Proceeds from an earlier capital reduction; or Other amounts arising from the equity or debt investment. These amounts should be identified and settled before the full exit. The RBI has clarified that the requirement does not ordinarily extend to unrelated commercial receivables, such as genuine export receivables, merely because the same UAE company is the customer. Those balances remain subject to the rules applicable to the underlying trade transaction. In a partial sale, the seller continues to hold an interest in the UAE company. The remaining ownership, control, debt exposure and guarantees must therefore be reviewed after the transaction. Sale price must be determined at arm’s length The transfer of UAE company shares by a resident Indian seller is subject to arm’s-length pricing. The authorised dealer bank must satisfy itself that the consideration is commercially supportable. It may use a valuation prepared under an internationally accepted methodology in accordance with its internal policy. For an operating Dubai company, the valuation may consider: Audited and current management accounts; Revenue and profitability; Assets and liabilities; Customer contracts; Intellectual property; Licenses and regulatory approvals; Working capital; Related-party balances; Contingent liabilities; Future cash flows; and Market conditions. The nominal share capital shown on the UAE license is not necessarily the market value of the business. A Dubai company with AED 50,000 of stated capital may have developed valuable customers, contracts and goodwill. Selling those shares at nominal value to a related buyer without commercial support could attract questions. The same applies where the price appears artificially high and the buyer may effectively be transferring funds to the Indian seller for another purpose. Related-party sales require stronger documentation A sale to an unrelated commercial buyer is usually easier to explain than a transfer to: A relative; Another company controlled by the seller; A family holding company; A trust or foundation; An existing business partner; An Indian group company; or Another offshore entity within the same group. A related-party transfer is not automatically prohibited, but the seller should document: Commercial reason for the restructuring; Independent valuation; Source of the buyer’s funds; Rights transferred; Liabilities assumed; Treatment of shareholder loans; Continuing management arrangements; and Any connection between the sale price and other contracts. A low-value transfer described as family restructuring may still constitute a taxable or reportable transaction. UAE share-transfer formalities must be completed properly The ownership change must comply with the rules of the UAE jurisdiction where the company is registered. For a Dubai mainland company, the process may include: Share-transfer agreement; Partners’ resolution; Amendment of the memorandum; Notarisation; Regulatory or activity-specific approval; Updating the commercial register; Issuance of an amended trade license; and Updating beneficial-ownership information. For a Dubai free-zone company, the authority may require: Share-transfer or sale application; Board and shareholder resolutions; Buyer’s KYC documents; Source-of-funds information; Existing and revised constitutional documents; No-objection or clearance documents; Settlement of authority charges; Revised register of members; New share certificate; and Amended license or company extract. The UAE authority’s effective transfer date should be consistent with the sale agreement, payment documents and Indian disinvestment reporting. Do not confuse share-sale proceeds with company money The seller must identify what has actually been sold. A share sale means the shareholder transfers ownership of the company. The consideration belongs to the shareholder. An asset sale means the UAE company sells its business, inventory, contracts or other assets. The proceeds belong to the UAE company, not directly to its shareholder. The owner cannot transfer an asset-sale amount to India and label it personal share-sale proceeds. The UAE company must first account for the sale, settle liabilities, consider UAE Corporate Tax and then lawfully distribute the remaining funds through dividend, capital reduction, liquidation or another permitted method. This distinction is frequently overlooked where one individual owns 100% of the Dubai company. The owner and the company remain separate persons even in a wholly owned structure. Route the proceeds through the designated AD bank ODI transactions relating to a particular UAE entity are ordinarily routed through the authorised dealer bank designated for that UIN. The seller should provide the bank with the proposed transaction documents before closing. The bank may request: Share-purchase agreement; Valuation report; Original ODI and UIN records; Form FC history; Proof of the seller’s residential status; Buyer’s KYC documents; UAE company license; Constitutional documents; Shareholder register; Board or partner resolutions; Audited financial statements; Details of loans and guarantees; Evidence of UAE authority approval; Proof of payment; Bank advice identifying the remitter; Tax declarations; and Explanation of the source of funds. The buyer’s payment instructions should identify the transaction clearly. A vague remittance description such as “personal transfer” or “consultancy payment” may cause unnecessary compliance questions. The 90-day repatriation requirement A person resident in India holding ODI must generally realise and repatriate the consideration received on transfer or disinvestment to India within 90 days from the date of transfer or disinvestment. The proceeds should ordinarily be received in a freely convertible currency through an authorised banking channel. The seller should align four dates before signing: The agreement date; The UAE legal transfer date; The date on which consideration becomes payable; and The expected remittance date. If the buyer needs extended time to pay, the payment structure must be examined before the shares are transferred. A commercial agreement allowing payment over several years should not be assumed to override the FEMA repatriation requirement. Deferred and instalment payments require advance planning A buyer may propose: An upfront deposit; Payment on transfer; Deferred instalments; Retention for warranties; Escrow; Earn-out consideration; or Adjustment after completion accounts. Deferred consideration can be accommodated in specified overseas-investment transactions where the period and terms are defined in the underlying agreement. However, the structure must comply with the regulations, repatriation requirements and authorised dealer bank’s documentation expectations. Each receipt of disinvestment proceeds in an approved tranche must be reported appropriately. The agreement should clearly state: Total consideration; Fixed and contingent components; Instalment dates; Escrow arrangements; Conditions for release; Currency; Adjustment formula; Maximum earn-out; Treatment of default; and Responsibility for bank charges and taxes. An undefined payment such as “the balance will be paid when the buyer has sufficient funds” is difficult to support. Escrow does not automatically amount to repatriation In a UAE business sale, part of the purchase price may be held in escrow to cover warranty claims or final adjustments. The seller should not assume that money deposited into a UAE escrow account has been repatriated to India. The funds may remain outside India and may not yet be under the seller’s unrestricted control. The escrow period, release conditions and maximum retention should be discussed with the designated AD bank. If a portion of the consideration is retained beyond the permitted timeline, the seller may need regulatory clarification or approval rather than relying solely on the commercial agreement. Form FC reporting after disinvestment The disinvestment must generally be reported through Form FC within 30 days of receiving the sale proceeds. Where approved proceeds are received in tranches, each receipt should be reported. The reporting should show: Percentage and number of shares sold; Buyer’s identity and country of residence; Sale consideration; Valuation; Date of transfer; Amount repatriated; Gain or loss; Remaining ownership, if any; Outstanding financial commitment; and Status of the UAE entity and its subsidiaries. In a complete exit, the bank may also examine whether the UIN can eventually be treated as closed after all obligations and reporting requirements have been completed. Loans and guarantees do not disappear when shares are sold A shareholder may sell the UAE shares while loans, guarantees or security arrangements remain outstanding. The parties must determine whether those commitments will be: Repaid before closing; Assigned to the buyer; Converted into equity; Released by the lender; Continued after the sale; or Settled from the purchase price. A personal or corporate guarantee issued for the UAE company does not automatically end when the shareholder ceases to own the business. The seller should obtain a written release from the UAE bank or creditor. Otherwise, the former owner may remain liable for a company they no longer control. Any assignment, repayment, invocation or release may create separate FEMA reporting requirements. UAE bank account versus direct remittance to India The cleanest route will often be for the buyer to remit the consideration directly to the seller’s designated Indian bank account with complete transaction details. In some cases, the proceeds may first be received in an existing UAE account. Whether this is acceptable depends on: Seller’s FEMA residential status; Nature and permissibility of the foreign account; Terms of the transaction; Repatriation timeline; Banking evidence; and Designated AD bank’s requirements. A resident Indian seller should not leave the proceeds indefinitely in a Dubai personal or corporate account merely because the account was lawfully opened earlier. Receipt outside India and repatriation to India are separate steps. Both must be supportable. Indian tax depends on residence when the gain arises Sending the money to India does not, by itself, create the capital gain. The taxable event generally arises from the transfer of the shares. The Indian tax treatment depends significantly on the seller’s residential status for the relevant tax year. For a resident and ordinarily resident individual, gains from the sale of foreign shares are generally included within the scope of Indian taxation. For a resident but not ordinarily resident individual, the position may differ depending on the source of income and whether it is connected with a business controlled in or profession established in India. A non-resident’s foreign share sale may fall outside Indian taxation unless the income is received, deemed received, accrues or is deemed to accrue in India under the applicable provisions. FEMA residence and income-tax residence are separate tests. A seller may be non-resident under one framework but resident under the other. Calculating the Indian capital gain The seller should preserve the complete cost history of the UAE shares. Relevant documents include: Original incorporation or subscription agreement; Outward-remittance records; Share certificates; UAE capital-increase documents; Acquisition agreements; Gift or inheritance records; Valuation reports; Restructuring documents; Sale agreement; Professional and transaction expenses; and Foreign exchange conversion workings. The computation may require consideration of: Cost of acquisition; Previous owner’s cost in a qualifying gift or inheritance; Period of holding; Applicable capital-gains classification; Currency-conversion rules; Expenses incurred wholly in connection with transfer; UAE tax paid, if any; and Foreign tax credit. The amount credited to the Indian bank account is not automatically the taxable gain. It includes return of capital as well as profit. UAE tax treatment should be reviewed separately The UAE consequences depend on who owns and sells the shares. Where an individual sells UAE company shares as a personal investment, the treatment may differ from a sale conducted as part of a licensed business activity. Where a UAE company sells shares in another entity, the gain may enter the UAE Corporate Tax computation, subject to any applicable exemption or participation relief. The transaction may also affect: Tax-group membership; Qualifying Free Zone Person status; Related-party disclosures; Carried-forward losses; Accounting treatment; Beneficial ownership; and Corporate Tax deregistration if the company is later closed. The assumption that every UAE share sale is tax-free should be avoided. Foreign tax credit may be available If eligible tax is paid in the UAE and the same gain is taxable in India, the seller should examine relief under Indian foreign-tax-credit rules and the India–UAE tax treaty. The taxpayer should preserve: UAE tax return; Tax assessment or statement; Proof of payment; Computation of the UAE gain; Indian tax computation; Currency conversion; Residency documents; and Prescribed Indian foreign-tax-credit filings. A foreign tax credit is not automatic merely because money was withheld or paid abroad. The tax must be eligible, properly documented and claimed within the prescribed procedure. Foreign-asset reporting must continue until the disposal date A resident and ordinarily resident individual may be required to disclose the UAE company shares in the foreign-asset schedule of the Indian income-tax return. The disclosure for the year of sale should be consistent with: Date of transfer; Ownership percentage; Sale consideration; Capital-gains schedule; UAE bank accounts; Income derived from the company; and ODI disinvestment records. If the individual continues to hold another class of shares, a shareholder loan, signing authority or a UAE bank account, the reporting obligation may not end merely because the main equity stake was sold. Sale at a loss attracts additional scrutiny A genuine business can decline in value, and FEMA does not require every overseas investment to be profitable. However, a significant loss may lead the bank to request stronger evidence concerning: Historical financial performance; Reason for the decline; Independent valuation; Accumulated losses; Buyer’s relationship with the seller; Efforts made to find other buyers; Outstanding receivables; Write-offs; and Commercial necessity of the exit. A sale at a nominal value to a related party is particularly sensitive. The authorised dealer bank must be satisfied about the transaction’s bona fides. The sale should not be used to shift value to an overseas relative or related entity. Liquidation is not the same as a share sale If no buyer is available, the shareholder may decide to liquidate the UAE company. In a liquidation: Assets are realised by the company; Liabilities are settled; Employees and visas are cancelled; Tax and licensing matters are closed; The liquidator distributes the net realisable value; and The shareholder repatriates the amount received. The 90-day repatriation period for liquidation proceeds generally runs from the actual distribution of assets by the official liquidator. The seller should maintain the liquidation report, authority cancellation, tax deregistration, bank-closure evidence and distribution statement. Practical example Assume an Indian resident incorporated a Dubai free-zone consulting company three years ago through a properly reported ODI investment. The individual now agrees to sell the entire company to an unrelated UAE buyer for AED 1.5 million. Before signing, the seller asks the designated Indian bank to review the proposed exit. The bank confirms the UIN and checks whether APR and other reporting are up to date. An independent valuation supports the negotiated price. The seller also identifies an outstanding shareholder loan and unpaid dividend. These amounts are settled before the full disinvestment. The free-zone authority approves the transfer, updates the register and issues revised company documents. The buyer remits the purchase price through banking channels, clearly identifying it as consideration for the share acquisition. The seller repatriates the proceeds within the prescribed period and files Form FC through the designated bank within 30 days of receipt. The individual separately calculates the Indian capital gain and completes the applicable foreign-asset and income disclosures. The transaction succeeds because the FEMA, UAE corporate, banking and tax workstreams were coordinated before closing. Common mistakes that delay repatriation The most common problems include: Checking FEMA residence only after signing; Selling shares before completing one year of ODI ownership; Ignoring overdue APR or Form FC filings; Using nominal share capital as the sale value; Completing a related-party sale without independent valuation; Leaving shareholder loans outstanding during a full exit; Receiving money with an incorrect remittance purpose; Holding sale proceeds in Dubai beyond the applicable period; Assuming escrow automatically counts as repatriation; Failing to report each deferred instalment; Forgetting to obtain releases from personal guarantees; Treating an asset sale as a shareholder’s share sale; Reporting the gain inconsistently in the Indian tax return; Omitting the disposed shares from the foreign-asset schedule; and Closing the UAE company before preserving its financial and corporate records. Exit checklist Before completing the sale, the seller should confirm: FEMA residential status on the transfer date; Income-tax residential status for the relevant year; ODI or OPI classification; Legality of the original acquisition; Completion of the one-year holding period; UIN and designated AD bank details; Status of Form FC, APR and FLA filings; Independent arm’s-length valuation; UAE authority transfer requirements; Settlement of equity and debt dues; Treatment of guarantees and security; Buyer’s identity and source of funds; Payment currency and remittance description; Escrow, retention and deferred-payment conditions; Repatriation within the prescribed period; Form FC reporting within 30 days of receipt; Indian capital-gains computation; UAE tax consequences; Foreign tax credit documentation; and Foreign-asset disclosure for the year of disposal. Conclusion Bringing UAE company sale proceeds back to India is not simply a bank-transfer exercise. It is the final stage of an overseas investment and must be supported by a compliant acquisition history, arm’s-length valuation, proper UAE share transfer, timely repatriation and complete disinvestment reporting. For a resident Indian seller, the central FEMA timelines are particularly important: the consideration must generally be repatriated within 90 days, and the disinvestment must be reported through Form FC within 30 days of receiving the proceeds. The seller should involve the designated authorised dealer bank before the agreement becomes unconditional. A well-planned exit protects the purchase price from banking delays and ensures that the commercial success of the Dubai business is not followed by avoidable FEMA or tax complications in India.

Articles 4 min read FCNR(B) windfall to squeeze bank NIMs for next few quarters as funds await deployment: S&P Global's Geeta Chugh Lenders are likely to keep credit growth calibrated despite the large deposit inflow, while smaller and mid-sized banks could face greater refinancing challenges as FCNR(B) maturities bunch up from 2029, according to S&P Global MD Geeta Chugh. Anushka Sengupta ETBFSI Published On Oct 1, 2026 at 07:00 AM IST var news_msid = 134603071; console.log(news_msid); Select ETBFSI as Preferred Choice on Google Copy Link Share on WhatsApp Share on Linkedin Share on X Share on Telegram Share on Facebook The massive FCNR(B) deposit mobilisation by Indian banks has eased their immediate funding and liquidity constraints, but could put pressure on net interest margins (NIMs) over the next few quarters as lenders take time to deploy the funds into higher-yielding assets, according to Geeta Chugh, Managing Director and Sector Lead, Financial Institutions Ratings, South and Southeast Asia, S&P Global Ratings.“The deposit growth in the last few years has consistently lagged behind credit growth. That has changed very significantly this year with very large levels of FCNR(B) deposits that Indian banks raised. In one stroke India got close to $130 billion of FCNR(B) deposits, and that has solved the deposit problem for now,” Chugh told ETBFSI in an interview. Advt However, she said the sizeable inflow could weigh on margins because a large portion of the deposits is currently parked in government securities and money-market instruments and will take time to be redeployed into higher-yielding assets.FCNR inflows to weigh on NIMsAccording to Chugh, the impact on NIMs will come through two channels. First, banks will need time to deploy the large quantum of FCNR(B) deposits into loans and other higher-yielding assets.“A large part of these FCNR(B) deposits is currently parked in government securities and money-market securities and they would take time to be redeployed into higher-yielding assets over a period of time. So we think that itself will depress margins,” she said.The second pressure comes from the fact that a portion of the FCNR(B) deposits has been funded through lending provided by banks themselves through their overseas banking books. Such lending, she said, has taken place at around 25–50 basis points, which will also weigh on overall NIMs.Despite the margin compression, the balance-sheet expansion can remain return-on-equity accretive as loans against FCNR deposits do not carry a capital cost, Chugh said.“So over the next few quarters we expect banking-sector NIMs to be under pressure. Of course, it will still be ROE-accretive because your balance sheets are increasing and you are able to do larger lending, while loans against FCNR have no capital cost,” she said. Advt Banks unlikely to chase aggressive growthDespite the additional funding available to banks, Chugh does not expect the FCNR(B) inflows alone to materially alter the trajectory of credit growth.Chugh said lenders are continuing to focus on calibrated growth and underwriting discipline rather than using the additional liquidity to sharply accelerate balance-sheet expansion.“In our conversations with the banks, most banks are still looking to grow in a calibrated fashion. I wouldn’t say that banks are looking to increase their balance sheets sharply. They are looking to grow in a calibrated fashion and continue to focus on underwriting,” she said. Advt She expects Indian banks to maintain strong credit growth, projecting 15% growth in the current year and 14% in each of the following two financial years.Chugh said the prolonged downturn in the banking sector and the deleveraging of Indian corporates should also prevent a sharp resurgence in leverage. While S&P expects corporate capex to remain strong over the next five years, it expects much of the investment to be funded through internal accruals rather than a major increase in corporate leverage.Smaller lenders face refinancing riskThe impact of the FCNR(B) inflows, however, could be more pronounced for individual banks with a high concentration of such deposits. Advt At the system level, FCNR(B) deposits account for around 4.5% of aggregate banking-sector deposits as of March 31, 2026, Chugh said. But the proportion is significantly higher for some individual lenders, close to 9% of deposits for ICICI Bank and around 23% for RBL Bank, she said.The maturity profile is another concern, particularly for smaller and mid-sized banks.“A lot of these maturities are bunched up between 2029 and 2031. As per the RBI Governor himself, 50% of them will mature in five years. The rest will start to mature from 2029 onwards. Larger banks should be able to easily manage to refinance these liabilities,” Chugh said.“Smaller banks, mid-sized banks, some of them have raised deposits which are quite sizeable for their balance-sheet size. I think that is where we are going to see some challenges for banks,” she added.Chugh, however, continues to see asset quality in the banking sector as strong, with its assessment of weak loans at less than 3%.Credit growth to remain strongChugh expects the broader BFSI sector to remain an important driver of India's economic activity, with credit growth continuing to remain strong and asset quality holding up.“We expect credit growth to continue to remain strong. We are projecting 15% growth this year and 14% growth over the next two financial years. So we are expecting credit-growth momentum to remain strong,” she said.At the same time, she said the quality of bank assets remains significantly stronger than in previous cycles, although rural stress and the greater exposure of MFIs remain key risks to watch.

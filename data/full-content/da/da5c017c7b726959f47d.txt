Summary: India’s capital markets are undergoing a structural transformation that extends beyond rising investor participation, record fundraising and expanding assets under management. A growing proportion of household savings is entering equities, mutual funds and other regulated investment products, while companies have access to increasingly diverse capital-raising routes. IPOs, qualified institutions placements, preferential issues, rights issues and corporate bonds now operate alongside investment structures such as Real Estate Investment Trusts (REITs), Infrastructure Investment Trusts (InvITs), Small and Medium REITs (SM REITs) and Alternative Investment Funds (AIFs). These developments convert assets and investment opportunities that were previously illiquid or institutionally concentrated into securities governed by disclosure, valuation, investor-protection and compliance requirements. Domestic institutional investment has also strengthened the market’s capacity to absorb foreign capital outflows, although it does not eliminate volatility or liquidity risks. Regulatory developments, including the GARUDA mechanism for AIFs, reforms to share buy-backs, settlement-cycle changes and the expanding IFSCA framework in GIFT City, illustrate how market innovation is creating new regulatory responsibilities. For Company Secretaries, this expansion presents opportunities extending beyond traditional corporate filings into transaction structuring, pricing and dilution analysis, secretarial certification, fund compliance, institutional-investor engagement, stewardship, investor servicing and governance advisory. The article also identifies vulnerabilities involving SME IPO pricing, corporate bond liquidity and the challenges of supervising newer investment structures. Its central argument is that the widening of India’s capital-market architecture must be accompanied by comparable improvements in disclosure quality, intermediary accountability, professional competence and governance standards. A portfolio of early-stage venture bets. A half-built office tower in Bengaluru. Five years ago, none of these looked like something a retail investor could hold in a demat account. In 2026, all three do. That shift, not the headline IPO or fundraising totals, is what this article is about: the steady widening of what counts as an investable, regulated instrument in India, and what gets pulled inside the perimeter as a result. That widening carries a direct professional stake for Company Secretaries, one that goes deeper than the closing paragraph most articles on this theme reserve for it. Every new instrument and every new route to capital creates more than paperwork. It creates new categories of regulated entity that need a compliance officer, new transaction structures that need a governance professional who understands the economics behind them, and new points in a company’s life where a board needs advice on which instrument to use, not just certification that the one it already chose was filed correctly. What the Widening Investor Base Actually Signals It is easy to treat the growth in demat accounts, 25.7 crore as of June 2026, up a further 1.1 per cent in a single quarter (Source: SEBI, Monthly Bulletin, July 2026, sebi.gov.in), as a simple participation statistic. It is better read as a statement about where Indian household capital formation now happens. The Economic Survey 2025-26 puts a number on this shift: the combined share of equity and mutual funds in annual household financial savings has risen from around 2 per cent in FY12 to over 15.2 per cent in FY25, while individual investors’ direct and indirect share of aggregate market equity ownership has climbed to 18.8 per cent by September 2025, from roughly 11 per cent in FY14 (Source: Economic Survey 2025-26, Ministry of Finance/PIB, January 2026). This is not merely more people buying shares; it is a rewiring of the channel through which household surplus reaches the corporate sector, away from the bank-deposit-to-bank-loan route that dominated Indian finance for decades, and toward direct and indirect capital-market exposure. That rewiring cuts both ways. On one hand, it is precisely the deepening that Indian capital markets have needed: a domestic savings base large and sticky enough to fund the country’s own growth without disproportionate dependence on volatile foreign flows. On the other, it means the consequences of poor disclosure, mis-selling or governance failure no longer fall narrowly on sophisticated institutional investors who can absorb losses and litigate; they fall increasingly on first-time retail savers with SIP mandates of a few thousand rupees a month. The mutual fund industry’s growth to ₹88.31 lakh crore in average assets, with contributing SIP accounts crossing 10 crore for the first time in August 2026 (Source: AMFI, Monthly data, August 2026), should be read less as evidence that Indian retail investors have become sophisticated and more as evidence that a very large number of savers now have direct, recurring exposure to market outcomes through products distributed through financial intermediaries and digital platforms. For Company Secretaries, this is precisely where the profession’s relevance has quietly grown. A retail base of this size and this composition raises the value of every function a CS is trained to sit closest to: the accuracy of an offer document, the plain-language quality of risk factors, the completeness of continuing disclosures, the integrity of the shareholder grievance and investor-servicing mechanism a listed company is required to maintain. As the number of first-time, unsophisticated participants grows, so does the practical importance of the compliance officer who signs off on that disclosure, and of the Practicing Company Secretary retained to certify that a company’s investor-facing processes are not merely present on paper but actually functioning. Scale, in other words, does not by itself imply investor understanding; it raises the professional and reputational stakes attached to the governance work that stands in for that understanding. Domestic Capital has grown up, but that is not the same as Market Stability The willingness of domestic institutions to absorb sustained foreign selling through much of 2026, DIIs bought close to ₹4.3 trillion of equities in the first half of the calendar year against FPI selling of roughly ₹2.8 trillion, extending a buying streak that reached 36 consecutive months by July (Source: NSDL data, cited in exchange disclosures, 2026), is frequently described in market commentary as a sign of Indian markets having “matured.” That framing deserves scrutiny rather than repetition. What the data actually show is that a structurally growing pool of domestic savings, channelled through mutual funds and insurers, was large enough to absorb a specific episode of foreign outflows without a disorderly correction. That is a genuine structural strength, and it is a direct dividend of the financialisation trend discussed above. But it is not the same claim as “the Indian market no longer depends on foreign capital,” and the more defensible conclusion is narrower: India’s capital market now has two large, largely independent sources of demand instead of one. For listed companies and their boards, this shift has a quieter but arguably more durable implication, and one that falls squarely within a Company Secretary’s remit. As domestic mutual funds and insurers accumulate larger, stickier blocks in listed companies, their engagement policies, proxy-voting patterns and stewardship codes are becoming as material to governance outcomes, board composition, related-party scrutiny, executive compensation, as anything in the statute book. A company that has spent years managing its relationship with foreign institutional shareholders now needs an equally serious domestic institutional-investor relations function, and the Company Secretary who understands what a domestic institutional proxy adviser will actually flag in a resolution, rather than simply what the Companies Act requires the resolution to contain, is positioned to advise the board before a vote is lost, not merely to record the outcome after it. That is advisory work, not filing work, and it is opening up precisely because the shareholder base a CS now has to think about has changed in composition, not only in size. The Real Argument behind “More Routes to Capital” The conventional narrative around India’s primary market centres on the IPO, and FY26 gave that narrative plenty of material: 366 IPOs raising approximately ₹1.9 trillion, a record, anchored by 109 mainboard listings worth ₹1.7 trillion (Source: SEBI, Monthly Bulletin, April 2026, sebi.gov.in). But treating the IPO as the primary lens through which to view capital formation actually understates what is happening. In June 2026 alone, QIPs and preferential issues together mobilised close to ₹30,000 crore, more than ten times the ₹2,844 crore mobilised that month through IPOs and rights issues combined (Source: NSE, Market Pulse Report, July 2026). For a board or a CFO actually deciding how to raise the next tranche of capital, the real menu, IPO, QIP, preferential issue, rights issue, private placement of debt, is now genuinely a menu, each with different pricing formulas, different disclosure burdens, different investor bases and different timelines. That expanding menu is, in practical terms, an expanding advisory opportunity for exactly the professionals who understand the mechanics of each route well enough to recommend between them. An IPO, a QIP, a preferential issue and a rights issue are not interchangeable transactions that happen to end with a company raising money; they involve different valuation and pricing formulas under the ICDR Regulations, different timelines, different disclosure obligations and, critically, different points at which a Company Secretary’s certification is the thing standing between a transaction proceeding and a transaction being challenged. A CS who can walk a promoter or a board through why a preferential issue’s pricing formula under Regulation 164 produces a different outcome from the pricing framework applicable to a QIP, and why that choice has consequences for dilution, control and disclosure, is offering something closer to transaction strategy than to compliance execution, and that is exactly the kind of work this widening menu of instruments is creating more of, not less. It is also worth noting plainly that routes attracting less public and media attention, preferential issues in particular, are not routes that attract less need for scrutiny. Pricing formulas, related-party involvement and the genuine commercial rationale for a preferential issue matter exactly as much as they would in a headline IPO; they simply matter to a narrower audience, which is precisely why the certification a Practicing Company Secretary provides on such transactions carries real weight even when the transaction itself rarely makes the financial press. The debt market illustrates a related but distinct point. Corporate bonds outstanding stood at ₹53.6 trillion as of March 2025, built on record issuance of ₹9.9 trillion in FY25 (Source: RBI, Financial Stability Report, June 2025), yet the RBI’s own report flags average monthly secondary market turnover at under 4 per cent of outstanding value, meaning disclosure and covenant quality at the point of issuance are doing far more of the investor-protection work than they would in a genuinely liquid market. That, again, is where the debenture trustee compliance certificates, the secretarial audit reports and the continuous disclosure obligations a CS oversees are not paperwork attached to the transaction; in a thinly traded market, they are effectively the market’s primary safeguard. Converting Illiquid Assets into Securities: What REITs, InvITs and SM REITs actually change REITs and InvITs together now hold assets of roughly ₹8.76 lakh crore, ₹2,38,406 crore and ₹6,37,290 crore respectively, across six REITs and 28 InvITs (Source: SEBI Annual Report 2025-26). The more useful observation than the number itself is what kind of transformation it represents. A commercial office tower or a road concession has historically been an asset class accessible only to large developers or institutional investors with the balance sheet to hold an illiquid, lumpy asset for years. The REIT and InvIT structure converts that illiquid, indivisible asset into a fractional, exchange-traded, continuously priced security, subject to a mandatory distribution of at least 90 per cent of Net Distributable Cash Flow (NDCF), which changes its fundamental character from a piece of property to a security, with everything that implies for the disclosure and governance obligations attached to it. Every one of those obligations, unit-holder meetings, related-party transaction approvals involving the sponsor, valuation disclosures, distribution compliance, sits inside a governance architecture that did not meaningfully exist in Indian capital markets a decade ago, and that architecture needs company secretarial and compliance capability to run it. SM REITs push this further down the size spectrum and, in doing so, create an entirely new tier of demand for the profession: real estate assets valued between ₹50 crore and ₹500 crore, previously transacted through fractional-ownership platforms operating largely outside SEBI’s perimeter, can now be securitised through a regulated structure, with six SM REITs currently registered, Property Share Investment Trust, ImpactR SM REIT, EMBERSTONE SM REIT, Amsa Small and Medium Real Estate Investment Trust, hBits Investment Trust and Yield Foundry SM REIT (Source: SEBI, Registered SM REITs, sebi.gov.in), against an addressable opportunity that rating agencies have estimated in the range of ₹67,000-71,000 crore across roughly 53 million square feet of eligible office space. Each SM REIT investment manager requires the same kind of compliance officer function, board governance structure and continuing disclosure discipline that a listed company requires, scaled to a smaller asset base but not to a lower standard, and that is a genuinely new line of work for Practicing Company Secretaries willing to build the sector expertise early, well before the segment reaches the scale that will eventually make this obvious to everyone. A framework’s credibility is set by its weakest registered entity, not its best one, and it is the compliance officers and secretarial auditors working inside these structures, more than the headline regulation, who will actually determine whether that credibility holds. Alternative Investment Funds and the Limits of “More Access” The growth of India’s AIF industry, commitments of ₹16.94 lakh crore by March 2026, a 25 per cent year-on-year increase, with actual drawn-down capital crossing ₹7 lakh crore for the first time (Source: SEBI data, March 2026), is often presented as evidence that private equity, venture capital and private credit have become more “accessible.” That framing needs qualification: AIFs remain a high-minimum-ticket, long-lock-in structure aimed at investors sophisticated enough, in regulatory theory, to evaluate illiquid strategies without the continuous disclosure protections attached to listed securities. Their growth represents the institutionalisation of India’s private capital market rather than its democratisation, more of it now sits inside a regulated wrapper with a SEBI-registered manager, a custodian and reporting obligations than sat there before, and that institutionalisation is itself the source of new compliance demand. Every AIF, across all three categories, needs a compliance officer function, and SEBI’s own regulatory response, the GARUDA mechanism, first proposed in a May 2026 consultation paper and formally operationalised through a circular dated July 30, 2026 that allows a compliant scheme to launch within ten working days of filing its placement memorandum, and the June 2026 Master Circular mandating dematerialisation of AIF units and structured performance benchmarking (Source: SEBI, circular dated July 30, 2026, and Master Circular dated June 3, 2026), raises the compliance stakes attached to that function rather than lowering them. A faster launch timeline means less regulatory friction at the front end and, correspondingly, a heavier reliance on the fund’s own compliance officer and secretarial support to ensure the self-certified filing is actually accurate. For Company Secretaries, this is a specific and growing niche, AIF registration support, ongoing compliance officer roles, unit dematerialisation compliance, performance benchmarking documentation, that sits adjacent to traditional listed-company secretarial work but requires its own body of regulatory familiarity, and demand for that familiarity is growing roughly in step with the 25 per cent annual growth in the industry’s committed capital. Passive Products and the GIFT City Opening The growth of India’s passive fund industry, assets of ₹14.12 lakh crore in March 2026, up 23 per cent year-on-year, with gold ETF assets growing 191 per cent and silver ETFs 420 per cent over the same period (Source: AMFI, Monthly Note, March 2026), is a genuine improvement in the efficiency of Indian household balance sheets, each rupee moving from a physical gold holding to a gold ETF becomes visible to the regulated financial system in a way it never was before. This segment creates comparatively less direct secretarial work than the structures discussed above, since passive funds are typically managed within existing asset management company compliance frameworks, but it is worth noting alongside a related development that does carry direct professional relevance: the continued growth of the IFSCA-regulated GIFT City ecosystem, now home to more than 1,100 registered entities spanning fund management entities, banking units and capital market intermediaries operating largely outside India’s exchange control regime (Source: EY, citing IFSCA data, April 2026). GIFT City entities are subject to their own company secretarial and compliance requirements under the IFSCA framework, distinct from, though overlapping with, the Companies Act and SEBI framework a CS is trained on, and the ecosystem’s continued growth represents a genuinely new geography of work for professionals who take the trouble to understand that framework early. For a profession historically organised almost entirely around domestic company law and SEBI regulations, an under-explored but expanding avenue, one that will reward the Company Secretaries who build IFSCA familiarity now rather than after the ecosystem has matured. Has Regulatory Innovation kept pace with Market Innovation? SEBI has not been a passive observer of this expansion. Over 2026 alone, the regulator approved an automated lock-in mechanism for pledged pre-issue shares, a simplified abridged prospectus, and the reintroduction of open-market share buy-backs through stock exchanges effective August 1, 2026 (Source: SEBI (Buy-Back of Securities) (Amendment) Regulations, 2026). This builds on the broader settlement-cycle reform already under way, the optional T+0 cycle for the top 500 listed companies by market capitalisation, whose phased rollout began in January 2025 and is still being extended as broker systems are upgraded (Source: SEBI, circular dated December 10, 2024). Each of these is a genuine improvement in market infrastructure, and each also changes, in a small but real way, what a compliance calendar looks like for the professionals managing it: a buy-back conducted through the open market rather than a tender offer carries a different disclosure and reporting rhythm, and a company moving to T+0 settlement changes the operational timeline within which a CS’s secretarial team must coordinate with registrars and depositories. The honest answer to whether regulatory pace has kept up with market innovation is “unevenly.” Settlement-cycle and buy-back reforms are structural improvements that apply uniformly and are relatively straightforward to supervise. Newer instruments, SM REITs, GARUDA-fast-tracked AIF schemes, are harder to supervise precisely because the innovation that makes them attractive, speed, lower entry thresholds, reduced friction, is in tension with the depth of scrutiny new categories of assets or managers need most in their early years. That tension is not a reason for governance professionals to be cautious about engaging with these structures; it is, if anything, the reason their engagement matters more. A regulator that removes friction at the front end is implicitly relying on the compliance officers, secretarial auditors and Practicing Company Secretaries working inside these newer structures to supply the scrutiny that faster approval timelines no longer provide upfront, and that reliance is itself a form of professional mandate, whether or not it has been stated in those terms. Where the Expansion is Genuinely Fragile The data also point, with some consistency, to where this broader architecture remains thin rather than deep. SEBI’s own bulletin documents that SME IPOs subscribed over 150 times delivered listing-day gains of roughly 40 to 90 per cent (Source: SEBI, Monthly Bulletin, August 2026), a pattern I read as far more consistent with allotment scarcity in a thinly traded segment than with any credible reassessment of business fundamentals within a single trading session. The corporate bond market’s persistent illiquidity has already been discussed, and the scale of FPI selling through much of 2026, even though absorbed by domestic buying, is a reminder that resilience demonstrated during one episode is not the same as structural immunity to the next one. None of this is an argument against the expansion described through this article. It is an argument for a specific discipline, that the pace of adding new instruments, new investor categories and new capital-raising routes should be matched by an equivalent investment in the supervisory, disclosure and governance capacity needed to make each of them trustworthy at scale. For SME IPOs in particular, that capacity runs directly through the merchant bankers and Practicing Company Secretaries certifying due diligence, and a segment showing this pattern of listing-day behaviour is, if anything, a segment where the certifying professional’s diligence matters more than the current allotment-driven price action might suggest. What this means for Company Secretaries The practical consequence of everything described above is a genuine, and arguably underappreciated, redefinition of what capital-markets work means for a Company Secretary, and it is worth setting out plainly rather than as a closing gesture. A decade ago, that work meant IPO compliance, secretarial audits and routine filings, organised around a single, well-understood instrument and a fairly predictable compliance calendar. Today, the same professional is realistically expected to move between SM REIT investment manager eligibility, AIF compliance under the current Master Circular, related-party and pricing scrutiny in a preferential issue attracting a fraction of an IPO’s public attention, GIFT City entity compliance under the IFSCA framework, and the trustee-manager governance architecture of a REIT distributing cash every six months. These are not variations on a single compliance checklist; they are genuinely different regulatory logics, and each is opening a distinct line of advisory or practice work for the professional willing to build expertise in it before it becomes mainstream. Four specific opportunities are worth naming directly. First, the sheer expansion of capital-raising routes, IPO, QIP, preferential issue, rights issue, means promoters and boards increasingly need advice on which instrument to use before they need certification that the chosen one was executed correctly, and a Company Secretary who can offer that upstream advice is doing transaction strategy work rather than compliance work. Second, the newer regulated categories, SM REITs, GARUDA-track AIFs, GIFT City entities, are all sufficiently early in their growth that the professionals who build sector fluency now, rather than after volumes make it obvious, are positioned to become the specialists these segments turn to as they scale. Third, the domestic institutionalisation of shareholding means proxy advisory and institutional-investor engagement is becoming a distinct governance advisory skill in its own right, separate from routine compliance. Fourth, the growing retail base and its documented gaps in sophistication raise the practical value, and the fee-worthiness, of investor-servicing and disclosure-quality advisory that goes beyond the statutory minimum a listing agreement requires. Taken together, this is the direction in which the expansion of instruments described in this article is pushing the profession, whether or not individual practitioners have consciously chosen to move that way yet: from compliance execution toward genuine capital-markets and governance advisory. The professionals who will benefit most from India’s broadening capital-market architecture are not necessarily those who specialise most narrowly in any single instrument, but those who can credibly move across IPOs, QIPs, preferential issues, REITs, InvITs, SM REITs and AIFs while holding governance standards constant regardless of which door a client happens to be using, and who treat each of the newer, less mature structures discussed in this article as an area worth understanding now, while the field of specialists is still comparatively small. Conclusion The data reviewed in this article, record demat accounts, a mutual fund industry approaching ₹88 lakh crore, a corporate bond market outstanding above ₹53 lakh crore, AIF commitments near ₹17 lakh crore, a REIT-InvIT ecosystem holding close to ₹8.76 lakh crore, is not, at bottom, a story about size. It is evidence of a genuine structural transformation: the conversion of previously inaccessible assets and previously narrow capital-raising options into regulated, investible instruments available to a rapidly widening base of Indian savers and companies. Whether this transformation ultimately strengthens Indian capital formation will be decided by whether disclosure quality, intermediary accountability and professional judgment expand at the same pace as the architecture itself. For Company Secretaries, that is not an abstract concern about market quality; it is the actual shape of the opportunity in front of the profession. Every new instrument discussed in this article needs a compliance officer, a certifying professional or a governance adviser standing behind it, and the base of opportunities widening across India’s capital markets is, for those prepared to build the relevant expertise early, also a widening base of professional opportunity for the Company Secretary.

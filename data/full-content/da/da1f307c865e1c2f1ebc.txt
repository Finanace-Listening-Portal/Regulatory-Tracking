Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); Radiant Digitek Network Limited Vs Bhoomika Media Initiative Private Limited (NCLT Jaipur) Summary: The NCLT Jaipur Bench allowed an application seeking liquidation of Bhoomika Media Initiative Private Limited under Section 33(2) of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016, following approval by the Committee of Creditors with 100% voting share. However, it declined the request of Ms. Aparna Bhardwaj, the Deemed Resolution Professional, to be appointed as liquidator. Applying amended Section 34(4), effective from 26.05.2026 as recorded in the order, the Tribunal held that an insolvency professional who had acted as resolution professional during the corporate debtor’s CIRP could not be appointed as its liquidator. Although the CoC’s liquidation vote concluded on 31.12.2025, no liquidation order or liquidator appointment had preceded the amendment. The Tribunal therefore appointed Ms. Divya Somani, subject to verification of eligibility and written consent. The insolvency proceedings originated from unpaid channel placement charges owed to Radiant Digitek Network Limited. The corporate debtor operated in media and broadcasting and had availed services to make its television channel, “AJTV”, available through DTH and cable networks. According to the applicant, outstanding operational debt reached ₹2,90,56,000 as on 31.03.2023. A demand notice under Section 8 did not result in payment or a pre-existing dispute being raised within the statutory period. The Section 9 petition was admitted on 28.05.2025, and Ms. Aparna Bhardwaj was appointed IRP. Punjab National Bank initially constituted the CoC with 100% voting share. Following admission of Dar Credit & Capital Limited’s financial claim, the CoC was reconstituted with PNB holding 60.50% and Dar Credit holding 39.50%. As no resolution confirming or replacing the applicant secured the requisite majority, she continued as deemed RP. The applicant described persistent non-cooperation by the suspended directors, including failure to provide complete accounts, Tally data, statutory records, digital credentials and custody of assets. The registered office was reportedly vacated, while some broadcasting equipment, servers and studio infrastructure were found intermingled with another entity’s assets. Partial accounting data allegedly disclosed discrepancies with signed financial statements. An application under Section 19(2) resulted in cooperation directions dated 10.11.2025, but the applicant maintained that meaningful compliance did not follow. A proposed forensic audit failed to obtain the necessary voting support. Two registered valuers assessed the available assets, but incomplete handover restricted valuation and the preparation of a comprehensive Information Memorandum. The FY 2024-2025 auditor issued a disclaimer of opinion and recorded material uncertainty regarding going concern, cessation of operations, continuing losses and erosion of net worth. Expressions of interest were invited, but the applicant stated that the responses failed to meet eligibility and procedural requirements, including documentary and earnest money deposit requirements. PNB recommended liquidation at the fifth CoC meeting on 18.10.2025, while Dar Credit sought additional time to identify a prospective resolution applicant. Dar Credit again sought time at the sixth meeting on 17.11.2025 and subsequently conveyed its inability to identify an applicant. The initial 180-day CIRP period expired on 24.11.2025; an order dated 05.12.2025 extended it by 90 days up to 23.02.2026. At the seventh meeting on 16.12.2025, liquidation was formally proposed, and voting concluded on 31.12.2025 with both creditors supporting liquidation. No viable resolution plan had been received. The Tribunal held that Section 33(2) recognises the CoC’s authority to decide on liquidation before confirmation of a resolution plan, including before preparation of the Information Memorandum. The relevant conditions were a decision by a duly constituted CoC with the prescribed voting majority, communication through the RP, and a decision before confirmation of a plan. Here, the decision was taken during the extended CIRP period, and unanimous approval exceeded the statutory 66% threshold. The CoC had considered revival over successive meetings and afforded additional opportunities before deciding on liquidation. An incomplete Information Memorandum or incomplete financial information did not invalidate an otherwise lawful liquidation decision. Referring to K. Sashidhar v. Indian Overseas Bank & Ors., the Tribunal recognised the relevance of the CoC’s commercial wisdom and limited judicial interference, while noting that the statutory requirements for this application were specifically governed by Section 33(2). On appointment of the liquidator, the Tribunal considered that the applicant had continued to perform the RP’s functions, had furnished consent and an eligibility declaration, and had not been proposed for replacement by the CoC. Nevertheless, the prohibition introduced by the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (Amendment) Act, 2026 applied because no liquidation order or appointment had been made before the amended provision commenced. Her description as a deemed RP did not permit her appointment as liquidator of the same corporate debtor. Ms. Divya Somani was appointed instead. The Tribunal clarified that liquidation did not terminate the need to identify and recover assets, obtain complete records, or examine transactions. The liquidator must exercise independent statutory judgment, and the CoC’s earlier failure to approve a forensic audit did not prevent further examination. Transactions potentially falling under Sections 43, 45, 50 and 66 were to be examined and appropriate action taken where warranted. No finding of fraud, preferential transaction, wrongful trading or other misconduct was recorded. Pending applications, including IA (IBC) No. 397/JPR/2025 concerning alleged transactions and conduct during CIRP, would be adjudicated independently without prejudice to the parties’ rights. Regarding fees, the applicant had initially stated that her approved monthly fee was ₹1,00,000, that ₹3,33,000 had been received and ₹4,40,333 remained unpaid. The order subsequently records that her fees had since been paid and IA No. 552/JPR/2025 disposed of on 30.06.2026. Additional CIRP expenses circulated on 06.01.2026 had received Dar Credit’s approval, while PNB sought consideration at a CoC meeting. The Tribunal did not finally determine disputed or outstanding expenses in this liquidation application. It ordered liquidation, cessation of the Section 14 moratorium, application of Section 33(5) restrictions, vesting of management powers in the liquidator, cooperation by relevant personnel, asset and transaction examination, statutory announcements, and communication to the Registrar of Companies and IBBI. IA (Liq.) No. 1/JPR/2026 was allowed and disposed of. Cases Discussed K. Sashidhar v. Indian Overseas Bank & Ors., (2019) 12 SCC 150 (Supreme Court) — Cited for the primacy of the CoC’s commercial wisdom and the limited jurisdiction of the Adjudicating Authority to interfere. The Tribunal acknowledged that the decision concerned resolution plans, while finding its principle relevant to the CoC’s liquidation decision, subject to Section 33(2). We have heard the submissions advanced on behalf of the Applicant and perused the material placed on record. The present Application has been filed under Section 33(2) of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016, seeking liquidation of Bhoomika Media Initiative Private Limited, pursuant to the decision of the Committee of Creditors taken in its seventh meeting held on 16.12.2025, the voting in respect of which concluded on 31.12.2025. The Applicant has also sought her appointment as Liquidator under Section 34 of the Code. 34. The principal question arising for our consideration is whether the requirements prescribed under Section 33(2) of the Code stand satisfied and whether the Corporate Debtor is liable to be placed into liquidation in accordance with the decision of the CoC. 35. Section 33(2) of the Code provides that where the Resolution Professional, at any time during the Corporate Insolvency Resolution Process but before confirmation of a resolution plan, intimates the Adjudicating Authority of the decision of the Committee of Creditors, approved by not less than 66% of its voting share, to liquidate the Corporate Debtor, the Adjudicating Authority shall pass a liquidation order as referred to in Section 33(1) of the Code. The Explanation appended to Section 33(2) further clarifies that the Committee of Creditors may take a decision to liquidate the Corporate Debtor at any time after its constitution and before confirmation of a resolution plan, including at any time before preparation of the Information Memorandum. 36. Thus, the statutory scheme expressly recognizes the authority of the CoC to decide upon liquidation even before the expiry of the prescribed CIRP period or completion of all steps contemplated in the resolution process. The relevant requirements are that the decision must be taken by a duly constituted CoC with the prescribed voting majority, communicated to the Adjudicating Authority through the Resolution Professional, and taken before confirmation of a resolution plan. 37. In the present case, the CIRP of the Corporate Debtor commenced on 28.05.2025 pursuant to the order passed by this Adjudicating Authority in CP (IB) No. 23/9/JPR/2023. The initial period of 180 days expired on 24.11.2025. Subsequently, by order dated 05.12.2025, an extension of 90 days was granted, extending the CIRP period up to 23.02.2026. The decision to liquidate the Corporate Debtor was taken during the extended CIRP period and before confirmation of any resolution plan. 38. The material placed on record shows that Punjab National Bank initially constituted the CoC with 100% voting share. Subsequently, upon admission of the claim of Dar Credit & Capital Limited as a Financial Creditor, the CoC was reconstituted, with Punjab National Bank holding 60.50% voting share and Dar Credit & Capital Limited holding 39.50% voting share. The Applicant has placed on record the particulars of the CoC meetings and the decision taken regarding liquidation. 39. It is observed that the CoC considered the prospects of resolution over successive meetings. In the fifth meeting held on 18.10.2025, Punjab National Bank expressed its view that the EOI’s received did not satisfy the prescribed eligibility requirements and recommended liquidation. Dar Credit & Capital Limited, however, sought further time to explore the possibility of identifying a prospective resolution applicant. The matter was again considered during the sixth meeting held on 17.11.2025, when Dar Credit sought additional time. Subsequently, Dar Credit also conveyed its inability to identify a prospective resolution applicant and expressed its willingness to support liquidation. 40. The aforesaid sequence demonstrates that the decision to liquidate was preceded by consideration of the possibility of resolution. The CoC did not proceed immediately to liquidation upon receipt of the initial EOI’s, but afforded further opportunity to explore the possibility of revival. Ultimately, no resolution plan was received for consideration and approval. 41. In the seventh CoC meeting held on 16.12.2025, the proposal for liquidation was placed under Agenda Item No. 9. The resolution was thereafter put to voting, which concluded on 31.12.2025. As recorded in the application, both Punjab National Bank, holding 60.50% voting share, and Dar Credit & Capital Limited, holding 39.50% voting share, voted in favour of liquidation. The resolution was thus approved with 100% voting share, substantially exceeding the minimum requirement of 66% prescribed under Section 33(2) of the Code. 42. We further note that the Applicant has referred to persistent non-cooperation by the suspended directors in providing complete books of account, Tally data, statutory records, details of assets and other relevant information. It is also stated that discrepancies were noticed in the available financial records and that certain assets were found intermingled with assets of another entity. The Applicant was consequently constrained to approach this Adjudicating Authority under Section 19(2) of the Code, whereupon directions for cooperation were issued by order dated 10.11.2025. 43. The Applicant has also stated that the incomplete records and limited availability of assets affected the preparation of a comprehensive Information Memorandum and the valuation exercise. The valuation reports were stated to have been prepared on the basis of the assets and information available at the relevant time. The Independent Auditor’s Report for the financial year 2024-2025 is also stated to contain a Disclaimer of Opinion arising from the non-availability of complete books and supporting records, among other matters. 44. In this context, it is necessary to clarify that the inability to prepare a complete Information Memorandum or obtain comprehensive financial information does not, by itself, constitute a legal impediment to an order of liquidation under Section 33(2). The Explanation to Section 33(2) specifically permits the CoC to decide upon liquidation even before preparation of the Information Memorandum. Therefore, the incomplete preparation of the Information Memorandum in the present case cannot invalidate an otherwise lawful decision of the CoC to liquidate the Corporate Debtor. 45. At the same time, the difficulties encountered during CIRP regarding identification of assets, incomplete accounting records and alleged non-cooperation of the suspended directors cannot be treated as having come to an end merely because liquidation is ordered. These matters will require appropriate examination and action during liquidation. The Liquidator shall be required to take all permissible steps under the Code and the Liquidation Regulations for obtaining possession and control of the assets and records of the Corporate Debtor, identifying and verifying its assets and liabilities, and examining such transactions as may warrant further action under the applicable provisions of law. 46. It is also relevant to observe that the CoC’s commercial decision to liquidate the Corporate Debtor is entitled to due weight within the statutory framework. In K. Sashidhar v. Indian Overseas Bank & Ors., (2019) 12 SCC 150, the Hon’ble Supreme Court recognized the primacy of the commercial wisdom of the CoC and the limited jurisdiction of the Adjudicating Authority to interfere with such commercial decisions. Although the said judgment arose in the context of consideration of resolution plans, the principle of limited judicial interference in matters entrusted to the commercial judgment of the CoC is relevant. In the present case, the statutory requirements for liquidation are specifically governed by Section 33(2) of the Code. 47. Having regard to the decision of the CoC, the voting share with which the resolution was approved, the absence of any resolution plan and the fact that the decision was taken before confirmation of a resolution plan, we are satisfied that the requirements of Section 33(2) stand fulfilled. No circumstance has been brought to our notice which would justify declining to give effect to the said decision in accordance with the statutory mandate. 48. We now consider the prayer for appointment of the Applicant as Liquidator. The Applicant was initially appointed as Interim Resolution Professional pursuant to the admission order dated 28.05.2025 and has continued to discharge the functions of the Resolution Professional during CIRP. It is stated that the CoC did not pass any resolution either confirming her appointment or replacing her, and that she has continued to function as Deemed Resolution Professional. The CoC has not proposed her replacement for the purposes of liquidation. The Applicant has also furnished her written consent and declaration of eligibility to act as Liquidator. 49. We note that the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (Amendment) Act, 2026 has substituted sub-section (4) of Section 34, with effect from 26.05.2026, whereby an Insolvency Professional who has acted as Resolution Professional during the CIRP of a Corporate Debtor is prohibited from being appointed as Liquidator of the same Corporate Debtor. In the present case, although the CoC resolved to liquidate the Corporate Debtor on 31.12.2025, no liquidation order or appointment of Liquidator had been made prior to the commencement of the amended provision. Accordingly, the Applicant, who has been functioning as the Resolution Professional during CIRP, cannot be appointed as Liquidator. Ms. Divya Somani, having Registration No. IBBI/IPA-001/IP-P-02407/2021-2022/13670 & Email: [email protected], Mobile No. 9772211292, is appointed as Liquidator subject to compliance. 50. The Applicant has also referred to certain pending interlocutory applications, including IA (IBC) No. 397/JPR/2025 concerning alleged transactions and conduct during CIRP. The pendency of these applications does not constitute an impediment to liquidation under Section 33(2). However, the issues raised therein shall remain subject to independent adjudication in accordance with law. The present order shall not be construed as expressing any opinion on their merits. 51. As regards the professional fees and CIRP expenses, the Applicant has stated that her monthly professional fee was approved by the CoC and that a certain amount remains unpaid. It is further stated that additional CIRP expenses were circulated for approval or ratification on 06.01.2026, whereupon Dar Credit conveyed its approval while Punjab National Bank requested consideration in a CoC meeting. We are of the view that the present liquidation application does not require determination of the disputed or outstanding CIRP expenses. Such claims shall be considered in accordance with the provisions of the Code and applicable Regulations, without prejudice to the rights of the parties in the pending proceedings. No amount shall be treated as finally determined or approved merely by reason of the present order. 52. We also clarify that the failure of the CoC to approve the proposal for a forensic audit during CIRP does not, by itself, prevent the Liquidator from examining the transactions of the Corporate Debtor or taking such steps as are permissible under the Code and the Liquidation Regulations. The Liquidator shall exercise independent statutory judgment regarding the necessity of further examination of transactions and shall proceed in accordance with law. Nothing in this order shall be construed as recording a finding of fraud, preferential transaction, wrongful trading or other misconduct against any person. 53. In view of the foregoing discussion, we are satisfied that the decision of the CoC to liquidate the Corporate Debtor, approved with 100% voting share, satisfies the requirements of Section 33(2) of the Code. The application therefore deserves to be allowed, and the Corporate Debtor, Bhoomika Media Initiative Private Limited, is liable to be placed into liquidation. Conclusion 54. Accordingly, IA (Liq.)

Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); Neptune Sales Corporation Vs A N Yadav (GSTAT Lucknow) Summary: The GST Appellate Tribunal (GSTAT), State Bench Lucknow, in M/s Neptune Sales Corporation Vs A N Yadav (Appeal No. APL/11/LCK/2026, dated 12.11.2026), upheld a penalty of ₹2,52,216 imposed under Section 129(3) of the Uttar Pradesh Goods and Services Tax Act, 2017, for transportation of goods without a valid e-way bill and prescribed e-invoice at the time of interception. The dispute arose when the Mobile Squad intercepted a vehicle on 10.09.2024 at approximately 4:03 AM near Sarojini Nagar, Kanpur Road, Lucknow, carrying 2,520 kg of paper aluminium foil lamination rolls valued at ₹8,26,660, inclusive of tax. The goods were being transported by M/s Neptune Sales Corporation to M/s Kay Flavours Pvt. Ltd., Lucknow. Although the driver produced a manually prepared tax invoice, no e-way bill or e-invoice acknowledgement was available during verification. Since the consignment value exceeded ₹50,000 and the supplier’s aggregate turnover exceeded ₹5 crore, the authorities considered compliance with the mandatory e-way bill and e-invoicing provisions essential. The proper officer initiated proceedings under Section 129 and imposed a penalty of ₹1,26,108 each under CGST and SGST, aggregating ₹2,52,216. The First Appellate Authority upheld the penalty, prompting the taxpayer to approach the GSTAT. The appellant contended that the transaction was genuine, the goods were properly accounted for, and there was no discrepancy in their description, quantity, weight or value. Both the supplier and recipient were registered GST taxpayers, and the goods were accompanied by a tax invoice containing the relevant particulars. According to the appellant, technical difficulties on the GST portal prevented timely generation of the e-way bill, while the prescribed electronic documents were subsequently generated and furnished to the authorities before completion of the adjudication proceedings. The e-invoice acknowledgement was generated at 6:15 PM and the e-way bill at 10:42 PM on 10.09.2024. The appellant argued that the subsequent production of these documents, together with the absence of any physical discrepancy, demonstrated the bona fide nature of the transaction and absence of intention to evade tax. It further submitted that the adjudicating authority had failed to properly consider the explanations and supporting documents furnished in response to the show cause notice, rendering the penalty order arbitrary and contrary to the requirements of Section 75(6) of the CGST Act, 2017. In support of its contentions, the appellant relied upon several judicial precedents, including Falguni Steels v. State of U.P. and Others, Shyam Sel and Power Limited v. State of U.P. and Others, J.K. Cement Ltd. v. State of U.P. and Others, Modern Traders v. State of U.P. and Assistant Commissioner (ST) v. Satyam Shivam Papers Pvt. Ltd. It argued that penalty proceedings under Section 129 should consider the surrounding circumstances, genuineness of the transaction, availability of supporting commercial documents and absence of tax evasion. Particular reliance was placed on the Supreme Court’s dismissal of the special leave petition in Satyam Shivam Papers Pvt. Ltd., where the issue of intention to evade tax had been considered in the context of transportation-related contraventions. The appellant also submitted that the goods were specially printed and branded for the recipient, thereby establishing their commercial identity and supporting the genuineness of the consignment. It maintained that subsequent generation of the documents before issuance of the penalty order should have been considered sufficient to avoid the harsh consequences of Section 129. The Revenue opposed the appeal, contending that compliance with the statutory requirements must exist at the commencement of transportation and cannot be established retrospectively through documents generated after interception. It emphasised that the vehicle was intercepted at approximately 4:03 AM, whereas the e-invoice acknowledgement was generated more than fourteen hours later and the e-way bill nearly nineteen hours later. The supplier was covered by the mandatory e-invoicing provisions under Rule 48(4) of the CGST Rules, 2017, read with Notification No. 10/2023-Central Tax dated 10.05.2023. Consequently, the manually prepared invoice could not be treated as a compliant tax invoice in the absence of the prescribed Invoice Reference Number and acknowledgement particulars. The Revenue further submitted that Rule 138 required the e-way bill to be generated before commencement of movement of goods. The explanation regarding technical difficulties was unsupported by any contemporaneous portal error report, system-generated failure acknowledgement, grievance reference or other reliable evidence. It argued that the absence of physical discrepancies did not eliminate independent statutory obligations relating to electronic invoicing and transportation documentation. After examining the facts, statutory provisions and judicial precedents, the Tribunal held that the appellant had failed to comply with two independent mandatory requirements, namely generation of a valid e-invoice under Rule 48(4) and Rule 48(5), and generation of an e-way bill under Rule 138 before commencement of transportation. The Tribunal observed that Section 31 of the CGST Act required issuance of a tax invoice in the prescribed manner and within the prescribed time. Since the appellant was covered by mandatory e-invoicing, the manually issued invoice without the prescribed Invoice Reference Number could not satisfy the statutory requirements. The subsequent generation of the e-invoice and e-way bill did not retrospectively validate the transportation already undertaken without the prescribed documents. The Tribunal distinguished the decisions in Falguni Steels, Shyam Sel and Power Limited and Satyam Shivam Papers Pvt. Ltd. on their factual circumstances, particularly the nature of the documentation deficiencies and evidence explaining the delays. It also rejected the contention that the absence of discrepancies in the goods, registered status of the parties or special branding of the consignment could substitute for mandatory electronic documentation. The Tribunal found that the proper officer had examined the relevant facts and explanations and that the penalty order could not be characterised as non-speaking merely because the appellant’s explanation was rejected. The Tribunal further relied upon the Allahabad High Court decisions in Shanti Enterprises v. State of U.P. and Others and Akhilesh Traders v. State of U.P. and 3 Others, which recognised that transportation without prescribed documents at the time of interception could not automatically be excused merely because the documents were subsequently produced. It observed that the statutory defaults in the present case were substantive and could not be treated as minor clerical or technical mistakes. The appellant had failed to establish that the non-generation of the e-way bill and e-invoice before dispatch resulted from any substantiated technical impediment. The Tribunal also noted that the responsibility for ensuring compliance with the prescribed electronic documentation requirements rested upon the registered supplier before permitting movement of the goods. Accordingly, it concluded that the proper officer had established contravention of the applicable provisions of the UPGST/CGST Act, 2017, and the corresponding Rules, justifying proceedings under Section 129(3). By its order dated 12.11.2026, the GSTAT dismissed the appeal and sustained both the original penalty order dated 11.09.2024 and the First Appellate Authority’s order dated 29.03.2025. The decision highlights the importance of generating mandatory GST transportation documents before dispatch, particularly for taxpayers covered by the e-invoicing regime, and establishes that subsequent production of documents without a satisfactory explanation does not, by itself, cure an earlier statutory contravention.

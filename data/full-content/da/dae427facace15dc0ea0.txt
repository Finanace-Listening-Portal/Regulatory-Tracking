Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); Vikram Kumar Kabra Vs Principal Commissioner of Customs (CESTAT Hyderabad) Summary: The Customs, Excise and Service Tax Appellate Tribunal (CESTAT), Hyderabad, considered an appeal filed by Vikram Kumar Kabra against an order upholding the absolute confiscation of 310.07 grams of gold and a penalty of ₹3,15,000 under Section 112(a) and (b) of the Customs Act, 1962. Customs officers had received intelligence regarding the domestic transportation of allegedly foreign-origin gold through flights from Hyderabad to Mumbai and Jaipur on 2 October 2020. Acting on this information, officers examined consignments at the domestic Air Cargo Complex and recovered gold bars, cut pieces, jewellery and precious stones belonging to different persons, including the appellant. The adjudicating authority ordered confiscation under Sections 111(d), 111(l) and 111(j), which was subsequently upheld by the Commissioner (Appeals). The appellant challenged the order before the Telangana High Court, which permitted him to pursue the statutory appellate remedy. The Tribunal examined whether Customs had sufficient grounds to invoke the reverse burden of proof under Section 123 of the Customs Act. The assayer’s report confirmed that the seized material was 24-carat gold but expressly stated that its Indian or foreign origin could not be determined. The report covered gold belonging to several persons without separately identifying the appellant’s goods, and neither the show-cause notice nor the seizure records established specific foreign markings on the gold. The Tribunal held that confidential intelligence could justify initiating an investigation but could not, by itself, establish a reasonable belief that the goods were smuggled. Since the technical report also failed to establish foreign origin, the statutory condition for shifting the burden of proof to the appellant was absent. Consequently, the responsibility to establish smuggling remained with the Revenue. The appellant had consistently denied that the gold was smuggled and explained that it had been received from his nephew, Srikanth Darak, for conversion into jewellery for family marriages. He relied on an invoice dated 14 March 2015, bearing No. 3686, issued by M/s M. Rajendra Kumar Jewellers & Pearls Dealers for the purchase of 300 grams of pure gold valued at ₹7,95,000 by Hari Narayan Ram Prasad, along with a supporting letter from his nephew dated 22 August 2022. Although the lower authorities rejected this explanation and the invoice, the Tribunal found that the departmental case fundamentally rested on a seizure unsupported by reasonable belief and lacked independent evidence of smuggling. It therefore held that Section 123 had been wrongly invoked, set aside the confiscation of the appellant’s gold and the associated penalty, and allowed the appeal with consequential relief. By the impugned order, the Commissioner (Appeals) upheld, qua the appellant, absolute confiscation of 310.07 grams of gold under section 5 111(d), 111(l) & 111(j) of the Customs Act, 1962 and also upheld the imposition of penalty of Rs.3,15,000/- under section 112(a) & 112(b) of the Customs Act. 8. I have heard the learned Counsel for the appellant and learned Authorized Representative for the Revenue and perused the records. The fundamental question which is to be considered before deciding the confiscation of gold and imposition of penalty on the appellant is on whom does the burden of proof lie. As per section 123 of the Customs Act, the burden of proof is shifted under certain circumstances. It reads as follows: “123. Burden of proof in certain cases. (1) Where any goods to which this section applies are seized under this Act in the reasonable belief that they are smuggled goods, the burden of proving that they are not smuggled goods shall be- (a) in a case where such seizure is made from the possession of any person,- (i) on the person from whose possession the goods were seized; and (ii) if any person, other than the person from whose possession the goods were seized, claims to be the owner thereof, also on such other person; (b) in any other case, on the person, if any, who claims to be the owner of the goods so seized. (2) This section shall apply to gold, [and manufactures thereof, Substituted by Act 40 of 1989, Section 2, for “diamonds, manufacturer of gold or diamonds” watches, and any other class of goods which the Central Government may by notification in the Official Gazette specify.” 9. In cases not covered by section 123, the burden of proving that any goods are smuggled rests on the Revenue because it is Revenue that asserts that they are smuggled. In this case, I find that the belief under which the officers have seized the gold is the information which they had received and the test report. There is nothing on record to show that there is anything else based on which they formed the opinion that the gold and other goods were of smuggled origin. The information which was received by the officers was confidential information and it is not part of the evidence. It helped the Customs Act officers start the investigation and examine the goods. That, by itself, cannot give an officer reasonable belief that any goods were of smuggled origin. The second document which they had was the test report, which categorically states that it cannot be determined if the gold is of foreign origin or of Indian origin. Thus, I find that the officers had, in the first place, no reasonable belief that the goods were smuggled or that they were liable to confiscation at all and should not have seized them at all. Therefore, section 123 was wrongly applied in this case for seizing the goods under Panchanama, for issuing SCN and for confiscating the goods by the Additional Commissioner. The Commissioner (Appeals) has erred in upholding such confiscation and consequential imposition of penalty. 10. Both the lower authorities have rejected the submissions made by the appellant during personal hearing and also at the time of the investigation. They also rejected the invoice submitted by the appellant which matches with the statement made by the appellant. 13. In view of the above, I find that the entire case was built on the basis of seizure without reasonable belief that the goods in question were smuggled goods. Once such belief does not exist, section 123 would not apply and it is for the department to establish that they were smuggled goods. There is no evidence, whatsoever, in the entire case that the seized goods were smuggled goods. 14. In view of the above, the appeal is allowed and the impugned order upholding the OIO, insofar as it pertains to the confiscation of the gold of the appellant and imposition of penalty on the appellant is set aside. The appellant will be entitled to consequential relief.

Vs ACIT/ACIT/DCIT (ITAT Mumbai) Summary: The Income Tax Appellate Tribunal, Mumbai, “E” Bench, comprising Judicial Member Shri Anikesh Banerjee and Accountant Member Shri Girish Agrawal, delivered a consolidated order dated 28 January 2025 disposing of 42 appeals filed by HDFC Bank Ltd., as successor to Housing Development Finance Corporation Ltd. (HDFC Ltd.), and the Income Tax Department. The proceedings covered nineteen assessment years, from AY 2002-03 to AY 2020-21, and involved numerous disputes concerning deductions, disallowances, capital gains, accounting adjustments, transfer pricing and penalties under the Income-tax Act, 1961. HDFC Ltd., originally established in 1977 as a housing finance institution, amalgamated with HDFC Bank Ltd. pursuant to an order of the National Company Law Tribunal dated 17 March 2023, effective from 1 July 2023. The Tribunal accordingly recognised HDFC Bank as the successor assessee. Considering the recurring nature of the disputes, the Bench consolidated the grounds into twenty-five substantive issues and adopted common findings across assessment years wherever the relevant facts and legal provisions remained comparable. It also condoned delays in seven Revenue appeals after accepting the explanations furnished for their late filing. A principal controversy concerned the deduction under Section 14A and the computation of taxable income, alongside the independently disputed deduction under Section 36(1)(viii) for profits derived from the business of providing long-term finance. The assessee claimed that its eligible housing finance business included profits attributable to housing loans having terms shorter than five years, interest on funds temporarily deployed through treasury operations and certain other financing activities. Relying on coordinate Bench decisions in its own cases for AYs 1998-99 and 1999-2000, the Tribunal accepted that shorter-duration residential housing loans could form part of the same integrated housing finance business and that income from temporary deployment of funds raised for that business could qualify for the deduction. Conversely, income from financing for non-residential purposes was held ineligible. The Tribunal also directed appropriate reallocation of interest, administrative expenses and related costs between eligible and ineligible income instead of mechanically accepting arbitrary allocations. Its conclusions were based on the character of the income, the business undertaken and the applicable statutory requirements rather than merely on the duration of individual loan transactions. The Tribunal further examined eligibility under Section 36(1)(viii) for interest arising from funds advanced to NTPC Ltd. for infrastructure development and income arising from securitisation of housing finance receivables, including income associated with Pass-Through Certificates of securitisation trusts. Regarding the NTPC loan, the assessee maintained that it qualified as a specified financial entity and that its financing of infrastructure development fell within the scope of the provision. The Tribunal considered the treatment of this income in earlier assessment and appellate proceedings, including the Revenue’s acceptance of favourable findings for certain years. Regarding securitisation, it observed that converting housing loan receivables into securitised instruments did not necessarily sever the underlying connection with the housing finance business. In particular, the Tribunal considered the continuing exposure of the assessee to defaults and the nature of the underlying housing loans. Following the reasoning in DCIT Vs AIG Home Finance India Ltd., it allowed the deduction claim on qualifying securitisation income and income from specified Pass-Through Certificates. The various Section 36(1)(viii) issues were consequently disposed of with relief or recomputation directions according to their individual factual and statutory contexts. Another substantial group of disputes related to disallowances under Section 14A read with Rule 8D and consequential adjustments in computing book profits under Section 115JB. The assessee challenged the allocation of interest expenditure to investments producing exempt income and the methodology adopted for determining administrative expenses attributable to such investments. The Tribunal considered the availability of the assessee’s own funds, the Assessing Officer’s statutory obligation to record dissatisfaction with the assessee’s computation, and the requirement to consider investments actually generating exempt income during the relevant year. It applied the principles governing Section 14A and Rule 8D in accordance with the facts of the respective assessment years. Separately, the Tribunal upheld the position that disallowance calculated under Section 14A read with Rule 8D for normal income-tax computation cannot automatically be imported into the computation of book profit under Section 115JB. Following the Special Bench ruling in ACIT Vs Vireet Investment Pvt. Ltd. and other judicial precedents, the Tribunal rejected the relevant Revenue grounds challenging the appellate relief on this issue. The order also addressed deductions associated with employee stock option schemes, dividend income, lease equalisation and special reserves. HDFC contested disallowances relating to the discount on employee stock options and expenditure incurred under Employee Stock Option Schemes, contending that the amounts represented employee compensation incurred for business purposes. The Tribunal examined the treatment of these expenses in the light of established principles governing ESOP deductions, including those discussed in Biocon Ltd. Vs DCIT. It separately considered the computation of deduction under Section 80M, including the attribution of interest and administrative expenditure to dividend income and the relevance of the assessee’s own funds. Other disputed items included amounts credited to the lease equalisation account and withdrawals from the special reserve created under Section 36(1)(viii). These matters involved the distinction between accounting recognition and statutory taxation, the source of the relevant income, and whether deductions or additions had been computed in accordance with the provisions applicable to the respective assessment years. The Tribunal gave issue-specific findings rather than treating every accounting adjustment as automatically allowable or taxable. Several additional grounds concerned ordinary business expenditure and capital transactions. The Tribunal examined the disallowance of club entrance fees and subscriptions, exemption under Section 54EC in respect of capital gains arising from depreciable assets, expenditure associated with Foreign Currency Convertible Bond issues, the set-off of short-term capital losses, income from India Value Fund and capital gains on the sale of property. It also considered additions based on amounts appearing in Income Tax Statement details that allegedly did not correspond with receipts recorded in the assessee’s books. Further disputes arose from claims made during appellate proceedings, including an additional deduction sought after an inadvertent suo-motu disallowance, and the assessee’s request for refund of excess dividend distribution tax. Each of these issues required examination of the relevant statutory conditions, accounting records, documentary material and previous judicial decisions. The Tribunal assessed the claims in their respective factual settings, with certain matters resulting in relief and others requiring verification, recomputation or application of earlier binding or coordinate Bench rulings. The later assessment years raised further questions concerning transfer pricing adjustments in respect of specified domestic transactions covered by Section 40A(2)(b), disallowance of year-end provisions and corresponding additions to book profit under Section 115JB. The Tribunal also examined deductions for provisions for bad and doubtful debts under Section 36(1)(viia), particularly in the context of the assessee’s status and the statutory provisions applicable to non-banking financial companies, and claims for actual bad debts written off under Section 36(1)(vii). It considered the interaction between the provision for bad and doubtful debts, the write-off of irrecoverable debts and the requirement to prevent impermissible duplication of deductions. Other grounds concerned the taxation of interest received on income-tax refunds under Section 244A, including situations in which the original refund interest had subsequently been recovered or adjusted by the Department. Where rectification proceedings under Section 154 had already granted relief, the Tribunal treated the corresponding grounds as infructuous. Questions relating to penalty proceedings under Sections 270A and 271(1)(c) were adjudicated separately from the underlying quantum additions. In its final operative order, the Mumbai Tribunal disposed of all 42 appeals through a detailed assessment-year-wise table. Most appeals filed by the assessee were partly allowed, reflecting the different conclusions reached on individual deductions and additions. Several Revenue appeals were dismissed, while others were partly allowed, and the Revenue’s appeal in ITA No. 4217/Mum/2017 for AY 2013-14 was allowed. Importantly, the four appeals filed by the assessee against penalties under Section 271(1)(c), namely ITA Nos. 4313 and 4314/Mum/2010 for AYs 2002-03 and 2003-04 and ITA Nos. 2866 and 2867/Mum/2012 for AYs 2005-06 and 2006-07, were allowed. The result was therefore a mixed outcome rather than an unqualified victory for either HDFC or the Revenue. The common order is significant for housing finance institutions, banks and other financial entities because it addresses the treatment of financing income, securitisation receipts, exempt-income expenditure, employee stock compensation, special reserves, bad debts and related taxation issues across multiple assessment years while requiring compliance with the particular conditions of each statutory provision.

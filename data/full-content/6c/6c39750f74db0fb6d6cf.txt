Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); Kamal Gupta Vs Nisha Gupta (Supreme Court of India) Business Turnover, Bank Deposits and Investments Support ₹1.50 Lakh Monthly Maintenance: Supreme Court Introduction The financial capacity of a spouse may require examination of several indicators, including business activities, bank transactions, investments and property dealings. In Kamal Gupta v. Nisha Gupta, 2026 INSC 1215, decided on 16 November 2026, the Supreme Court upheld the enhancement of interim maintenance payable to the wife from ₹75,000 to ₹1,50,000 per month. The Court examined the parties’ financial affidavits and supporting material and found that the High Court’s reasoning was neither perverse nor unreasonable. The order illustrates the importance of a comprehensive financial picture when assessing the reasonableness of interim maintenance. Background of the Dispute The parties married on 3 December 1997 and had two sons. The elder son was an adult and employed, while the younger son was stated to be pursuing a Bachelor of Technology course. By an order dated 10 January 2025, the Family Court granted the wife interim maintenance of ₹75,000 per month. Both parties challenged that determination. The husband sought interference with the maintenance award, while the wife filed a cross-appeal seeking enhancement. The High Court dismissed the husband’s appeal and allowed the wife’s cross-appeal, increasing interim maintenance to ₹1,50,000 per month. The husband approached the Supreme Court against those orders. Financial Circumstances Examined by the Court The Supreme Court noted that the husband had a partnership firm engaged in manufacturing auto cables, operating from a factory in Delhi. The firm’s annual turnover was ₹6.6 crore. The High Court had also considered several other financial circumstances. The husband maintained seven bank accounts and held various credit and debit cards. Deposits aggregating ₹91 lakh had been made into his bank account over the preceding two years. Further, he had invested ₹16 lakh in mutual funds through a single transaction. The material also disclosed that he had gifted a flat to his brother through a gift deed dated 21 May 2018. These circumstances formed part of the financial record considered when deciding whether the enhanced maintenance was justified. Financial Affidavits Under Rajnesh v. Neha The Supreme Court expressly referred to the financial affidavits mandated by its earlier decision in Rajnesh v. Neha and Another, (2021) 2 SCC 324. It examined those affidavits along with the written submissions filed by both parties. The conclusion therefore followed scrutiny of the financial material placed before the Court. This aspect is significant because maintenance proceedings frequently involve conflicting claims about income, expenditure, assets and liabilities. Complete and accurate financial disclosure enables the court to assess those claims against identifiable financial circumstances. In the present case, the business operations, banking transactions and investments supported the High Court’s assessment of the husband’s financial capacity. Enhancement Was Neither Perverse Nor Unreasonable After examining the record, the Supreme Court found no justification for interfering with the enhancement. Keeping in view the husband’s business and investments, the Court held that the High Court’s reasoning in increasing interim maintenance from ₹75,000 to ₹1,50,000 per month was neither perverse nor unreasonable. The husband’s civil appeals were accordingly dismissed. The order upholds the amount on the particular financial material before the Court. It does not prescribe a fixed percentage of business turnover, bank deposits or investments for calculating maintenance. Non-Payment of Arrears and Contempt Proceedings The Supreme Court also dealt with contempt petitions filed by the wife alleging wilful disobedience of its earlier payment directions. Through orders dated 18 December 2025 and 15 January 2026, the husband had been directed to clear the entire arrears of interim maintenance at the rate allowed by the High Court, within four months from 18 December 2025. However, the arrears remained unpaid when the main proceedings were heard and reserved for orders. While dismissing the civil appeals, the Supreme Court granted the husband a further three months from 16 November 2026 to pay the entire arrears. It expressly permitted the wife to file fresh contempt petitions if he failed to comply. The existing contempt petitions were disposed of on those terms. Author’s Comments The order demonstrates the practical importance of examining financial capacity through multiple sources of evidence. Business activity, substantial deposits, investments and property transactions can together assist a court in evaluating a spouse’s means. However, business turnover is distinct from personal income. Similarly, bank deposits do not automatically represent disposable earnings, and investments may have identifiable sources. Parties should therefore explain these figures through reliable records, including accounts, bank statements and evidence of liabilities. The reference to the flat gifted to the husband’s brother should also be understood carefully. The Supreme Court recorded that transaction as part of the financial circumstances; it did not declare the gift fraudulent or set it aside. For practitioners, the central lesson is that maintenance disputes require coherent financial disclosure supported by documents. The decision also reinforces the need to comply with payment directions: the husband’s unsuccessful challenge left the enhanced maintenance intact, together with the obligation to clear arrears within the further period granted. Cases Discussed Rajnesh Vs Neha & Anr. (Supreme Court of India); (2021) 2 SCC 324; AY: Not Applicable. The Supreme Court expressly relied upon the requirement of financial affidavits laid down in this decision and examined the affidavits filed by the parties while assessing the enhancement of interim maintenance. Under the impugned order, the High Court has dismissed the appeal of the appellant- husband and, at the same time, it allowed the cross appeal filed by the respondent-wife, which were preferred against the order dated 10.01.2025 passed by the Family Court granting interim maintenance to the respondent-wife at the rate of Rs.75,000/- per month. The High Court has enhanced the said amount of interim maintenance to the tune of Rs.1,50,000/- per month. 3. The parties were married on 3.12.1997 and have two sons in the wedlock. The elder son – Mr. Aditya Gupta is major and employed, whereas the younger son – Mr. Nikunj Gupta is stated to be pursuing Bachelor of Technology. 4. Having heard the learned senior counsels for the parties and having examined the material placed on record, it turns out that the appellant has a partnership firm engaged in manufacturing of auto cables in a factory located at Delhi. The annual turnover of the firm is Rs.6.6 crores. The High Court also noted that the appellant has seven bank accounts and various credit and debit cards; he has deposited Rs.91 lakhs in his bank account over the last two years; he has invested Rs.16 lakhs by a single transaction as an investment in mutual funds; and he has also gifted a flat to his brother vide Gift Deed dated 21.05.2018. 5. We have perused the written submissions filed by both the parties and the financial affidavits as mandated by this Court in Rajnesh vs. Neha and Another1 and, on close scrutiny of the material, we are of the considered view that the reasoning adopted by the High Court while enhancing the interim maintenance from Rs.75,000/- per month to Rs.1,50,000/- per month, is neither perverse nor unreasonable, keeping in view the business carried out by the appellant, his investments, etc. 6. In view of the above, the Civil Appeals deserve to be dismissed and the same are hereby dismissed. CONTEMPT PETITION (CIVIL) NO(S).214-217 OF 2026 IN S.L.P. (CIVIL) NO(S).36536-36537 OF 2025 1.

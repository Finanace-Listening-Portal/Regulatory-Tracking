Summary: The Supreme Court decisions in G.R. Infra Projects Limited and Tata Steel Limited reinforce the statutory distinction between ordinary GST disputes and cases involving fraud, wilful misstatement or suppression of facts to evade tax. Section 74 of the CGST Act, applicable to periods up to FY 2023-24, cannot be mechanically invoked merely because of return mismatches, input tax credit discrepancies, audit objections or the expiry of limitation under Section 73. The proper officer must independently examine the available material and disclose the foundational facts supporting allegations of deliberate tax evasion in the show cause notice itself. Subsequent affidavits or adjudication findings cannot substitute for allegations absent from the original notice. The article examines the evidentiary requirements for establishing fraudulent conduct, the relationship between the alleged wrongdoing and the resulting tax liability, and the importance of natural justice in GST adjudication. It also discusses the circumstances in which High Courts may exercise jurisdiction under Article 226 to quash proceedings lacking essential statutory ingredients. While genuine cases of tax evasion remain subject to lawful enforcement, administrative delays and protective proceedings cannot justify the mechanical invocation of aggravated demand provisions. The author advocates greater accountability among GST officers, reasoned adjudication and effective constitutional supervision to safeguard taxpayers against jurisdictional abuse while preserving the Revenue’s legitimate enforcement powers. One fine morning, he receives a show cause notice accusing him of fraud, wilful misstatement and suppression of facts with intent to evade tax. Yet, on reading the notice, he discovers that the entire allegation rests upon a routine mismatch in returns, an audit objection or a difference in the interpretation of law. There is no allegation of fabricated documents, no evidence of concealed transactions and not even a whisper of any deliberate act intended to evade payment of tax. Nevertheless, he is branded as a tax evader and threatened with an extended period of limitation and severe penal consequences. Can a mere suspicion be elevated to fraud by the mechanical reproduction of statutory expressions? Can an enquiry by the Revenue become proof of deliberate tax evasion? And, more importantly, should constitutional courts remain silent spectators when such proceedings are initiated without the foundational facts necessary to sustain them? These questions strike at the very heart of fairness in GST administration. Statutory Distinction Between Sections 73 and 74 of the GST Act The Goods and Services Tax was introduced with the promise of a transparent, predictable and fair system founded upon self-assessment and voluntary compliance. Yet a disturbing practice persists. The routine invocation of Section 74 of the GST Acts in matters which disclose neither fraud nor wilful misstatement nor suppression of facts with intent to evade tax. An exceptional provision meant for deliberate evasion is frequently employed to address ordinary return mismatches, accounting differences and disputes of interpretation. This not only exposes taxpayers to serious penal consequences but also undermines the statutory distinction between genuine tax disputes and fraudulent conduct. For financial years up to 2023-24, Section 73 governed tax unpaid or short paid, erroneous refunds and wrongly availed or utilised input tax credit for reasons other than fraud, wilful misstatement or suppression of facts to evade tax. Section 74 dealt with the same categories of tax liability where those aggravated ingredients existed. The former generally prescribed a three-year period for an order. The latter allowed five years and exposed the taxpayer to a penalty equivalent to the tax determined, subject to statutory concessions. The distinction is substantive. It cannot be erased because limitation under Section 73 is approaching expiry or because an audit has identified a potential demand. Return Mismatches and Mechanical Allegations of Fraud In practice, many notices merely recite that the taxpayer ‘wilfully suppressed facts with intent to evade tax’ without identifying the concealed fact, the allegedly false statement, the supporting material or the circumstances evidencing intention. Differences between GSTR-1 and GSTR-3B, inconsistencies in input tax credit reconciliation, delayed reversals, or divergent views on exemption and classification may justify enquiry and, if warranted, determination of tax. They do not automatically establish fraud. A mismatch is a starting point for verification, not proof of a dishonest design. The burden of providing a factual foundation for the fraud allegation rests on the Revenue. Understanding Fraud, Wilful Misstatement and Suppression Under GST Fraud, in this setting, connotes deliberate deception directed towards an unlawful tax advantage. Wilful misstatement involves a consciously false declaration of a material fact, made with knowledge of its falsity and with the object of evading tax. Illustrations of possible GST fraud include obtaining input tax credit through invoices for goods or services never supplied, fabricating transport documents or purchase records to support fictitious transactions, creating or controlling non-existent firms to circulate invoices without underlying supplies, or deliberately concealing taxable outward supplies while maintaining parallel accounts. Examples of wilful misstatement may include knowingly describing taxable supplies as exempt in returns despite contrary records, intentionally understating the value or quantity of supplies through false invoices, falsely declaring that goods have been exported to claim an unwarranted refund, or knowingly claiming input tax credit on invoices identified internally as fictitious. These are illustrations, not automatic conclusions. Even serious discrepancies must be proved, and the evidence must connect the particular taxpayer with the alleged conduct and the resulting evasion. Quality of Evidence and the Taxpayer’s Knowledge The legal character of the evidence is as important as its volume. A supplier’s default in filing returns, for instance, does not by itself establish that the recipient knowingly participated in an invoice racket. Similarly, the absence of a document at the first stage of an enquiry may justify seeking further records, but cannot automatically prove that a document was fabricated or that a statement was intentionally false. Where the taxpayer has disclosed the transaction, recorded consideration through banking channels, produced contemporaneous commercial records and given an explanation capable of verification, the proper officer must examine those circumstances fairly. Conversely, evidence of circular movement of funds, fabricated delivery acknowledgements, identical patterns of fictitious invoicing, contradictory internal instructions or the knowing use of non-existent counterparties may, when corroborated, support an inference of deliberate evasion. The inference must rest on the totality of the evidence and must distinguish conscious wrongdoing from negligence, poor record-keeping and a debatable legal interpretation. Demonstrable Connection Between Fraud and Tax Evasion Fraud must also bear a demonstrable connection to the proposed tax consequence. A false statement unrelated to the non-payment of tax cannot, merely because it is false, justify the aggravated tax demand under Section 74. The officer should identify the relevant taxable supply, input tax credit or refund, explain the legal rule said to have been breached. He should quantify the resulting tax effect and show how the alleged dishonest act caused or facilitated that effect. This discipline is essential because a taxpayer may owe tax without being fraudulent, just as an enquiry may reveal irregularity without demonstrating intent to evade. An allegation that the assessee ‘failed to explain’ a mismatch is not the equivalent of proof that the assessee devised a fraudulent transaction. The statutory notice must disclose a coherent chain of reasoning, supported by ascertainable material, rather than leave the taxpayer to guess the case or require the adjudicating authority to construct the allegation after the event. Statutory Safeguards Before Invoking Section 74 Before invoking Section 74, the enforcement authority should therefore identify the precise statutory declaration or document said to be false or withheld. He should establish what the taxpayer actually knew; examine invoices, e-way bills, transport and bank records, stock movements, electronic communications and reconciliations and then consider whether the apparent discrepancy has a credible explanation in timing, accounting, classification or legal interpretation. A statement recorded in enquiry, a third-party allegation or an unexplained mismatch may provide a lead, but should be tested against independent, relevant material rather than treated as conclusive proof. The officer must articulate how the alleged act or omission caused the non-payment, short payment, erroneous refund or wrongful input tax credit, and what objective circumstances support the inference of a conscious intention to evade tax. The notice should state these foundational facts with sufficient evidence to permit an effective reply. Subsequent observation in adjudication order cannot be used to invent a charge never disclosed in the notice. These safeguards distinguish genuine enforcement from speculative accusations. Audit Objections Cannot Replace Independent Statutory Satisfaction The misuse is particularly acute when audit objections or computer-generated discrepancies are reproduced in show cause notices without independent examination. An audit report may bring a possible liability to the notice of the proper officer, but it cannot replace the officer’s statutory satisfaction. Nor can an administrative delay justify the extended period. Limitation is a legislative safeguard, not an inconvenience to be avoided by adding the language of Section 74 to a notice otherwise fit for Section 73. A lawful demand and a lawful allegation of fraud are two distinct questions, both requiring application of mind. Supreme Court Judgments Against Mechanical Invocation of Section 74 The Supreme Court’s decisions in M/s G.R. Infra Projects Limited Ratlam v. State of Madhya Pradesh and Others, decided on 19 August 2026, and M/s Tata Steel Limited v. Union of India and Others, decided on 25 August 2026, provide timely guidance. These decisions expose the infirmity in mechanically invoking Section 74 and underscore that the factual ingredients supporting the extended period must be disclosed in the show cause notice itself. They should now inform the conduct of every officer entrusted with GST investigations and adjudication. G.R. Infra Projects Limited: Foundational Facts Must Exist in the Show Cause Notice In G.R. Infra Projects, the proceedings concerned a notice dated 13 June 2025 for financial year 2018-19. The Revenue sought to sustain an action under Section 74 although the ordinary limitation under Section 73 had expired. The notice contained general allegations of fraud and concealment, without identifying the conduct or circumstances from which those conclusions could reasonably follow. The Supreme Court rejected the attempt to repair the notice by later assertions in a counter-affidavit. The legality of the notice had to be tested on what the taxpayer was actually told in that notice, not on an improved case subsequently advanced before a court. The notice was accordingly quashed. The significance of G.R. Infra Projects is fundamental. A person accused of deliberate evasion must know precisely what he is called upon to answer. Bare reproduction of the statutory expressions cannot give the Revenue jurisdiction to employ an exceptional penal provision. If the essential factual allegations are missing when the notice is issued, they cannot be supplied retrospectively through departmental pleadings. This principle protects both the taxpayer’s right to an effective defence and the integrity of the adjudication process. Tata Steel Limited: Protective GST Assessments Are Not Statutorily Permitted In Tata Steel, the dispute arose from audit objections concerning input tax credit and alleged short payment of tax for financial years 2018-19 to 2020-21. The Department had kept the matter in its call book and contested the audit objections, yet sought to initiate or revive Section 74 proceedings, including on a protective basis because the matter was regarded as time-bound. The Supreme Court emphasised that GST law does not authorise a protective assessment in this manner. The proper officer must independently form the satisfaction required by the statute; audit observations cannot be treated as conclusive findings of fraudulent conduct. The Court found that the notice did not disclose the foundational circumstances necessary to sustain an allegation of suppression with intent to evade tax. A broad assertion relating to input tax credit and the use of statutory terminology were not substitutes for material particulars. The show cause notice and consequential adjudication order were set aside. Importantly, the Court left the Department at liberty to initiate appropriate proceedings under Section 74 within the applicable period of limitation if genuine foundational facts existed and were duly disclosed. The judgment therefore does not immunise wrongdoing. It requires the Revenue to proceed lawfully when alleging it. Combined Effect of the Two Supreme Court Judgments Taken together, Tata Steel and G.R. Infra Projects establish that a tax discrepancy is not synonymous with fraud, that the independent satisfaction of the proper officer is indispensable, and that the notice must itself disclose the facts constituting the alleged fraud, wilful misstatement or suppression. The Revenue cannot use an audit objection, an impending limitation deadline or a subsequent affidavit as a substitute for those requirements. These principles are especially important where a taxpayer has disclosed transactions in statutory returns and the controversy is one of reconciliation or interpretation rather than concealment. Show Cause Notice, Natural Justice and Reasoned Adjudication A show cause notice is the very foundation of adjudication. Natural justice requires it to set out an intelligible accusation and the material on which the authority relies. Where it merely copies the words of Section 74, the taxpayer cannot meaningfully answer why an accounting or interpretational dispute is said to constitute deliberate evasion. A personal hearing cannot necessarily cure the absence of the original charge. The eventual adjudication order must independently explain both why tax is payable and why the penal ingredients of Section 74 are established. Proof of one is not proof of the other. Role of Constitutional Courts in Preventing Jurisdictional Abuse The constitutional courts have a special duty in such cases. Although interference with a show cause notice is ordinarily exceptional, judicial restraint must not become a licence for jurisdictional abuse. When the Revenue has merely commenced enquiries, raised reconciliation queries or identified differences in returns, but labels the matter as fraud without disclosing any act of deception, wilful misstatement or conscious suppression to evade tax, the foundation for invoking Section 74 is absent. Where that absence is apparent on the face of the notice and does not require a trial of disputed facts, the High Courts exercising jurisdiction under Article 226 should intervene at the threshold and quash the proceedings rather than compel the taxpayer to endure an inherently defective adjudication and successive appeals. Constitutional supervision is most necessary when the extraordinary allegation has been made without the extraordinary facts which the statute demands. This approach does not invite High Courts to undertake a premature assessment of the merits in every tax dispute. If the notice identifies specific acts and materials capable of supporting an allegation of deliberate evasion, disputed factual questions may properly be examined by the statutory authority. But where the notice contains no legally relevant foundation whatsoever, insisting upon exhaustion of ordinary remedies may perpetuate the very injustice that judicial review exists to prevent. The Allahabad High Court’s intervention in M/s Varanasi Sangam Expressway Private Limited v. Commissioner of State Tax, setting aside a Section 74 notice lacking the necessary ingredients while preserving lawful departmental remedies, illustrates that distinction. Invocation of Section 74 After Proceedings Under Section 73 Particular caution is warranted when proceedings under Section 73 have previously been examined or dropped, and Section 74 is thereafter invoked on substantially the same disclosed facts. A change of departmental opinion, without new material indicating fraudulent conduct, cannot by itself convert an ordinary controversy into a fraud case. The officer must identify what was deliberately concealed and explain why that inference is supported by the record. Likewise, a speaking order must deal with the taxpayer’s explanation and evidence, not merely reproduce the show cause notice and confirm the demand. Practical Remedies for Taxpayers and Accountability of GST Officers A taxpayer receiving a vague notice should therefore challenge the absence of the statutory ingredients at the outset, while answering the proposed tax demand on merits without conceding the applicability of the fraud provision. Where the notice is facially deficient and the challenge raises a clear jurisdictional issue, recourse to Article 226 may be justified. Equally, supervisory scrutiny must ensure that notices invoking serious allegations are not issued mechanically on the basis of audit templates or pressure to preserve revenue. In appropriate cases of manifestly reckless or repeated disregard of settled law, courts may consider costs and administrative accountability, subject always to fairness and the circumstances of the case. Conclusion The effectiveness of GST cannot be measured solely by demands raised or revenue recovered. It depends equally upon lawful process and public confidence. Section 74 was designed for deliberate evasion, not as a device to prolong limitation or penalise bona fide differences. Tata Steel and G.R. Infra Projects should mark a turning point from mechanical allegations to responsible adjudication. The governing principle is simple. If the State alleges fraud, it must disclose facts capable of constituting fraud. Where it does not, constitutional courts should not hesitate to protect the taxpayer at the threshold. Such judicial intervention is not an obstacle to tax enforcement. It is an indispensable safeguard of the rule of law.

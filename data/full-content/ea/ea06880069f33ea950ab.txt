Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); Sigamony Anitasally Vs ITO (ITAT Chennai) Cash Deposits Established as Business Receipts Cannot Be Taxed in Full under Section 69A: Chennai ITAT Summary: The Chennai Income Tax Appellate Tribunal has held that cash deposits supported by evidence of business activity cannot be treated entirely as unexplained money merely because regular books and complete transaction records were not maintained. In Sigamony Anitasally v. ITO, the Tribunal deleted an addition of ₹1,17,70,000 under Section 69A and substituted it with estimated additional business income of ₹9,41,600, calculated at 8% of the disputed cash deposits. Crucially, this addition was over and above ₹10,21,600 already declared under Section 44AD. The Tribunal expressly clarified that the further estimation was confined to the peculiar facts of the case and did not establish a general rule requiring enhancement of presumptive income by another 8%. This article is based on the supplied order: C:UsersUserDesktoptaxguru officeviewOrder.pdf Facts of the Case The assessee, an individual employed with the Life Insurance Corporation of India, also carried on activities involving coconuts in Kanyakumari District, Tamil Nadu. She explained that she took coconut-yielding gardens on lease, harvested coconuts through labourers, purchased coconuts from other agriculturists, and sold them after semi-dehusking. During Financial Year 2019–20, the assessee deposited ₹1,17,70,000 in cash into her bank accounts. Initially, she understood the activities to generate exempt agricultural income. Subsequently, she filed an updated return under Section 139(8A) on 16 March 2023, disclosing: Business turnover: ₹1,27,70,000. Presumptive business income at 8%: ₹10,21,600. Total income, including the business income: ₹24,96,430. The assessee maintained that the disputed cash deposits formed part of the business turnover already disclosed. Why the Assessing Officer Made the Addition The Assessing Officer rejected the explanation because the assessee had not produced regular books, purchase bills, sales invoices, stock registers, written lease documents or agricultural revenue records. The officer also relied on the fact that the deposits were made in round figures and considered the connection between the alleged coconut sales and bank deposits unsubstantiated. Consequently, the entire ₹1,17,70,000 was assessed as unexplained money under Section 69A, attracting treatment under Section 115BBE. The CIT(A) upheld the addition. Issue Before the Tribunal The central question was whether the cash deposits represented unexplained money or receipts from the coconut business included in the disclosed turnover. The Tribunal examined the evidence cumulatively rather than treating the absence of regular books as conclusive. 1. Bank Transactions Supported the Business Explanation The assessee maintained an “Overdraft General” account with South Indian Bank, Nagercoil Branch. The Tribunal observed that an overdraft facility, although not conclusive proof of business by itself, was a relevant circumstance. The account reflected recurring deposits, withdrawals, payments and movement of funds throughout the year. The Assessing Officer had concentrated principally on deposits without adequately examining the corresponding withdrawals and payments. The Tribunal clarified that withdrawals do not automatically prove redeposit of the same money. Nevertheless, the overall banking pattern was relevant to determining whether the account was operated for business. 2. Round-Figure Deposits Were Not Sufficient to Invoke Section 69A The Tribunal rejected the inference that round-figure cash deposits necessarily represented unexplained money. In cash-based trading, particularly in agricultural commodities, collections may be accumulated and deposited in convenient amounts. This could justify further verification, but could not, by itself, support taxation of the entire deposits as unexplained money. 3. Purchaser Confirmations Could Not Be Ignored The assessee had furnished confirmations from certain coconut purchasers before the CIT(A). The Tribunal found that their evidentiary value had not been adequately examined. If the authorities doubted the transactions, they could have undertaken verification. However, no specific adverse material demonstrating that the confirmations were false or the transactions fictitious was brought to the Tribunal’s notice. The confirmations did not establish the source of every individual deposit, but constituted relevant corroborative evidence of the business activity. 4. Declared Business Income Had Been Retained in the Assessment The Assessing Officer adopted the returned income of ₹24,96,430, which included the ₹10,21,600 presumptive business income, as the starting point for assessment. However, while retaining that income, the officer separately treated the ₹1,17,70,000 deposits as unexplained money without demonstrating that they were outside the disclosed turnover of ₹1,27,70,000. The Tribunal found this approach inconsistent. It clarified that adopting returned income does not necessarily establish acceptance of every underlying transaction. Nevertheless, where business turnover is disclosed and supporting evidence exists, the officer must examine whether the deposits are already included in that turnover before treating them independently as unexplained. 5. Section 44AD Does Not Automatically Protect Unexplained Deposits The Tribunal expressly cautioned that an assessee cannot avoid Section 69A merely by describing deposits as business receipts or declaring presumptive income under Section 44AD. The nature and source of the money must still be supported by evidence. Here, the combination of bank statements, purchaser confirmations, consistent explanations and disclosure in the updated return supported the conclusion that the deposits arose from the coconut business. 6. Gross Business Receipts Are Different from Taxable Profits Having accepted the business connection, the Tribunal observed that gross trading receipts ordinarily include purchase costs and business expenditure. Accordingly, the entire receipts could not be equated with taxable income merely because complete books were unavailable. The relevant amount was the profit element, subject to the applicable provisions. The Tribunal therefore held that the Section 69A addition could not survive in its existing form. Why an Additional 8% Was Estimated The Tribunal did not accept the returned business income without adjustment. It noted the substantial cash transactions and deficiencies in purchase, sales, stock and expenditure records, which prevented independent verification of the actual profit margin. Considering these circumstances, it estimated additional profit at 8% of ₹1,17,70,000, amounting to ₹9,41,600. This was a case-specific additional estimate, not a statutory rule that an eligible Section 44AD declaration must routinely be increased. Section 44AD itself provides a presumptive framework for eligible assessees and businesses, subject to its prescribed conditions. Final Relief Granted The Tribunal directed the following treatment: Particulars Amount Original addition under Section 69A ₹1,17,70,000 Substituted addition as estimated business income ₹9,41,600 Balance addition deleted ₹1,08,28,400 Business income already declared, remaining taxable ₹10,21,600 The substituted addition was directed to be assessed under “Profits and gains of business or profession”, alongside the business income already declared. The appeal was partly allowed. Practical Takeaway The decision highlights the importance of corroborating the business source of cash deposits, even where income is offered presumptively. Bank activity, purchaser confirmations and reconciliation with disclosed turnover can materially support that explanation. It also requires careful reading of the relief: the Tribunal removed the Section 69A treatment but sustained an additional business-income estimate. Its express qualification prevents the further 8% estimate from being presented as a general rule applicable to all Section 44AD cases. Cases Discussed: Smt. Aruna Vs ITO (ITAT Bangalore); ITA No. 537/Bang/2024; 22/04/2024. Distinguished on facts: the taxpayer in that matter had not filed a return or adequately substantiated the alleged tractor business or third-party loans. FULL TEXT OF THE ORDER OF ITAT CHENNAI The present appeal preferred by the assessee is directed against the order dated 02.04.2026 passed by the learned Commissioner of Income Tax (Appeals), National Faceless Appeal Centre, Delhi (hereinafter referred to as “the ld.CIT(A)”), arising out of the assessment order dated 13.03.2024 passed u/s.143(3) of the Income-tax Act, 1961 (hereinafter referred to as “the Act”) by the Assessing Officer, Assessment Unit, National Faceless Assessment Centre, Delhi (hereinafter referred to as “the ld. AO”), for the Assessment Year 2020-21. 2. The brief facts of the case, as emanating from the orders of the authorities below and the material available on record, are that the assessee is an individual, deriving income from salary and also claiming to have undertaken activities relating to the procurement, harvesting and sale of coconuts in Kanyakumari District, Tamil Nadu. The assessee was employed with the Life Insurance Corporation of India (LIC) and had declared a total income of Rs.24,96,430/- for the assessment year under consideration. The assessee had also claimed to be engaged in activities involving the taking of coconut-yielding gardens on lease from agriculturists, harvesting coconuts therefrom by engaging labourers, purchasing coconuts directly from other agriculturists, and selling the same in the market after carrying out semi-dehusking operations. 3. The case of the assessee was selected for scrutiny principally on account of information available with the Revenue indicating substantial cash deposits in the bank accounts maintained by the assessee during the relevant previous year, notwithstanding the fact that no business income had originally been disclosed in relation to such transactions. As per the information available with the AO, the assessee had deposited an aggregate sum of Rs.1,17,70,000/- in cash in her bank accounts during the financial year 2019-20. The said deposits constituted the principal subject matter of examination in the scrutiny assessment proceedings. 4. Pursuant to the selection of the case for scrutiny, notice u/s.143(2) of the Act was issued on 01.06.2023. Subsequently, notice u/s.142(1) of the Act was issued on 07.08.2023, calling upon the assessee to furnish, inter alia, the computation of income, particulars of income-earning activities, copies of bank statements, financial statements and supporting documentary evidence explaining the nature and source of the substantial cash deposits noticed in her bank accounts. 5. In response to the notice issued u/s.142(1) of the Act, the assessee furnished a reply dated 03.09.2023, explaining the nature of her activities and the source of the cash deposits. The assessee submitted that she was engaged in taking coconut-yielding gardens on lease from various agriculturists in Kanyakumari District and harvesting coconuts from such gardens by engaging labourers. It was further submitted that, apart from the coconuts harvested from the leased gardens, the assessee also purchased fresh coconuts directly from agriculturists in the surrounding villages and, after carrying out semi-dehusking operations, sold the coconuts in the market. 6. The assessee explained that the cash deposits aggregating to Rs.1,17,70,000/- represented the receipts generated from the aforesaid activities involving coconuts. According to the assessee, the activities were predominantly agricultural in character and the income arising therefrom was understood by her to be agricultural income exempt from taxation u/s.10(1) of the Act. It was on account of such understanding that the assessee had not originally disclosed the receipts from the said activities as business income in her return of income. 7. The assessee further submitted that, since the income arising from the aforesaid activities had been considered agricultural income and, subsequently, business income was offered on a presumptive basis u/s.44AD of the Act, regular books of account and financial statements had not been prepared. The assessee nevertheless furnished copies of her bank account statements and explained that the cash deposits represented receipts from the sale of coconuts harvested from leased gardens as well as coconuts purchased from other agriculturists. 8. It is also noticed from the assessment order that, in the course of the proceedings, the AO had referred to cash withdrawals aggregating to Rs.16,81,300/- and the purchase of a motor car for a consideration of Rs.11,13,861/-. In the reply furnished by the assessee, the said financial transactions were explained as being connected with the activities undertaken by her, and the purchase of the motor car was stated to be for travelling in connection with such activities. However, the ultimate addition made in the assessment was confined to the entire cash deposits of Rs.1,17,70,000/-. 9. The AO examined the explanation furnished by the assessee in relation to the cash deposits and was not satisfied with the nature and source thereof. According to the AO, although the assessee had claimed that the deposits represented receipts arising from agricultural activities involving coconut- yielding gardens and the sale of coconuts, no satisfactory documentary evidence had been furnished to substantiate the actual carrying on of such activities. 10. The AO observed that the assessee had not produced books of account, purchase bills, sales invoices, bills relating to agricultural inputs, documents evidencing ownership or leasehold rights over agricultural lands, or other primary records to establish that the assessee had actually undertaken the agricultural activities claimed by her. The AO further noted that no adangal extracts or other agricultural revenue records had been produced indicating the particulars of crops raised, the names of the owners or lessees of the lands, the extent and nature of the agricultural lands, the sources of irrigation, and other relevant details pertaining to the alleged agricultural operations. 11. In the absence of such supporting documentary evidence, the AO held that the assessee had failed to establish that she was carrying on agricultural operations as claimed. The explanation that the cash deposits represented receipts arising from agricultural activities was, therefore, considered unacceptable. 12. The AO further examined the claim of the assessee that the income arising from the sale of coconuts was agricultural income exempt u/s.10(1) of the Act. In this regard, the AO observed that, although coconuts were admittedly agricultural produce, the activity of purchasing or collecting coconuts from agriculturists and selling the same in the market could not, by itself, be regarded as an agricultural activity. According to the AO, such transactions were in the nature of commercial or business activities rather than agricultural operations. 13. The AO, therefore, distinguished between the cultivation of agricultural produce and the subsequent purchase and sale of such produce in the market. The AO was of the view that the mere fact that the commodity dealt with by the assessee was an agricultural product would not render the income arising from its trading as agricultural income. Consequently, the claim of the assessee that the entire receipts from the sale of coconuts constituted agricultural income exempt u/s.10(1) of the Act was rejected. 14. Apart from rejecting the claim of agricultural income, the AO also examined the bank statements furnished by the assessee. On such examination, the AO noticed that the cash deposits aggregating to Rs.1,17,70,000/- had been made in round figures. The AO considered this feature of the transactions to be inconsistent with the normal pattern of receipts arising from regular business activities. 15. The AO further observed that the assessee had not maintained or produced monthly sales summaries, stock registers or other contemporaneous records evidencing the quantity of coconuts purchased, harvested and sold, the sale proceeds realised, and the corresponding deposits made in the bank accounts. According to the AO, in the absence of such records, it was not possible to verify whether the cash deposits had actually arisen from the sale of coconuts as claimed by the assessee. 16. The AO was, therefore, of the opinion that the explanation furnished by the assessee remained unsubstantiated by independent documentary evidence. The mere assertion that the deposits represented receipts from the sale of coconuts was not considered sufficient to discharge the onus cast upon the assessee to explain the nature and source of the money deposited in her bank accounts. 17. Accordingly, a show-cause notice dated 13.02.2024 was issued to the assessee, proposing to treat the cash deposits aggregating to Rs.1,17,70,000/- as unexplained money and to bring the same to tax u/s.69A r.w.s 1158BBE of the Act. The assessee was required to furnish her objections, duly supported by documentary evidence, on or before 20.02.2024. The assessee was also informed that, in the event of failure to furnish a satisfactory explanation within the stipulated time, the assessment would be completed on the basis of the material available on record. 18. The AO recorded that the assessee had not furnished any response to the aforesaid show-cause notice. In the circumstances, the AO proceeded to complete the assessment on the basis of the material available on record and the explanations furnished by the assessee during the earlier stages of the proceedings. 19. Upon consideration of the material on record, the AO reiterated that the assessee had failed to establish the agricultural or business activities claimed by her through supporting documentary evidence. The AO also reiterated that the cash deposits were in round figures and that no corresponding sales records, stock registers or other books of account had been produced to establish a nexus between the alleged sale of coconuts and the cash deposited in the bank accounts. 20. The AO consequently concluded that the assessee had failed to satisfactorily explain the nature and source of the cash deposits aggregating to Rs.1,17,70,000/- made during the financial year 2019-20. The AO, therefore, treated the entire amount of such deposits as unexplained money within the meaning of section 69A of the Act and brought the same to tax u/s.115BBE of the Act. 21. Accordingly, the AO, vide assessment order dated 13.03.2024 passed u/s.143(3) of the Act, determined the total income of the assessee at Rs.1,42,66,430/-, as against the returned income of Rs.24,96,430/-, by making an addition of Rs.1,17,70,000/- u/s.69A of the Act. 22. Aggrieved by the assessment order dated 13.03.2024, particularly the addition of Rs.1,17,70,000/- made u/s.69A r.w.s 115BBE of the Act, the assessee preferred an appeal before the ld.CIT(A). 23. Before the ld.CIT(A), the assessee challenged the action of the AO in treating the entire cash deposits as unexplained money, notwithstanding the explanation that the deposits represented receipts arising from the activities of harvesting, purchasing and selling coconuts. The principal contention of the assessee was that the AO had failed to appreciate the actual nature of the transactions and had proceeded to tax the gross cash deposits without considering the commercial activities from which such receipts had arisen. 24. The assessee submitted that she was engaged in taking coconut-yielding trees and gardens on lease from agriculturists on payment of a lump-sum consideration. Under such arrangements, the assessee was entitled to harvest coconuts from the leased trees periodically, generally once in about forty days. The harvested coconuts were thereafter sold in the market. In addition to the coconuts obtained from leased gardens, the assessee also purchased coconuts from other agriculturists and resold them in the market. 25. It was contended that the aforesaid activities involved both agricultural and commercial elements. The assessee explained that, under a bona fide but mistaken understanding that the activities were agricultural in nature, the receipts had not originally been offered to tax as business income. However, upon subsequently recognising the business character of the transactions, the assessee had filed an updated return of income and offered income arising from the said activities to tax under the presumptive taxation provisions contained in section 44AD of the Act. 26. The assessee specifically submitted that, in the updated return filed on 16.03.2023, the total turnover from the aforesaid activities had been declared at Rs.1,27,70,000/- and income at the presumptive rate of 8% thereof, amounting to Rs.10,21,600/-, had been offered to tax u/s.44AD of the Act. According to the assessee, the said income had been included in the returned income and the corresponding tax liability had been discharged. 27. The assessee further contended that the business was conducted in the nature of a small-scale and informal trade in agricultural produce and that regular books of account had not been maintained. It was submitted that the very object of section 44AD of the Act was to provide a presumptive method of computing business income in the case of eligible assessees, without requiring the determination of actual profits through detailed books of account, subject to the conditions prescribed therein. 28. In this background, the assessee submitted that the AO was not justified in rejecting the explanation regarding the business receipts merely because regular books of account, purchase invoices, sales bills and stock registers had not been maintained. It was the contention of the assessee that, having disclosed the turnover and offered income therefrom on a presumptive basis u/s.44AD of the Act, the entire amount of cash deposits could not once again be treated as unexplained money u/s.69A of the Act. 29. With regard to the absence of written lease agreements, the assessee explained that the arrangements for harvesting coconuts from coconut-yielding gardens were generally entered into orally with the owners of the agricultural lands. It was submitted that the landowners were ordinarily reluctant to execute written lease agreements on account of an apprehension that such documents might subsequently be relied upon by the lessees to assert rights over the agricultural lands through civil proceedings. 30. According to the assessee, the absence of written lease deeds was, therefore, attributable to the prevailing practice governing such transactions and could not, by itself, justify the conclusion that the activities were fictitious or that the receipts arising therefrom were unexplained. The assessee accordingly contended that the AO had placed undue emphasis on the non-production of formal lease agreements without properly appreciating the nature of the business carried on by her. 31. The assessee also submitted that the AO had proceeded to treat the gross cash deposits of Rs.1,17,70,000/- as unexplained money without taking into account the corresponding cash withdrawals from the bank accounts. It was contended that the deposits and withdrawals had to be considered together in order to ascertain the actual nature of the transactions and the income, if any, arising therefrom. 32. In this regard, the assessee pointed out that the difference between the opening and closing balances of the bank accounts worked out to Rs.9,68,349.28, which was less than the additional income of Rs.10,21,600/- already declared u/s.44AD of the Act. Relying upon the aforesaid figures, the assessee contended that the AO had erred in considering only the deposits while disregarding the withdrawals and other corresponding transactions reflected in the bank accounts. 33. The assessee thus sought to contend that the cash deposits could not be viewed in isolation from the underlying business activities and the movement of funds through the bank accounts. It was submitted that the action of the AO in bringing the entire gross deposits to tax u/s.69A of the Act, without considering the explanation regarding the source of the receipts and the income already offered u/s.44AD of the Act, was contrary to the scheme of presumptive taxation. 34. The assessee accordingly prayed that the addition of Rs.1,17,70,000/- made u/s.69A of the Act be deleted, as the said amount represented receipts from the coconut-related activities and not unexplained money belonging to the assessee. 35. The ld.CIT(A), upon considering the assessment order, the grounds of appeal and the material available on record, proceeded to examine the sustainability of the addition of Rs.1,17,70,000/- made u/s.69A of the Act. The ld.CIT(A) took note of the fact that the AO had made the impugned addition on account of the failure of the assessee to satisfactorily explain the nature and source of the substantial cash deposits. The ld.CIT(A) reproduced the relevant observations of the AO and noted that the assessee had failed to furnish supporting documentary evidence either during the assessment proceedings or in the course of the appellate proceedings to establish the activities claimed to have been undertaken by her. 36. The ld.CIT(A) observed that the assessee had failed to respond to the show-cause notice issued during the assessment proceedings. It was further observed that, even during the appellate proceedings, the assessee had not furnished credible documentary evidence substantiating the explanation that the deposits represented receipts from the sale of coconuts. According to the ld.CIT(A), the explanation offered by the assessee remained unsupported by primary evidence and was, therefore, incapable of acceptance merely on the basis of the assertions made in the grounds of appeal. 37. The ld.CIT(A) thereafter examined the claim that the deposits arose from agricultural activities. In this regard, the ld.CIT(A) observed that the assessee had not furnished documents evidencing the ownership or lease of agricultural lands, particulars of agricultural operations, details of crops harvested, agricultural revenue records, or any other contemporaneous evidence establishing that the assessee had undertaken agricultural activities during the relevant previous year. 38. The ld.CIT(A) held that the mere assertion of having taken coconut- yielding gardens on lease and having harvested coconuts therefrom, without any supporting documentary evidence, was insufficient to establish the claim of agricultural income. In the absence of evidence regarding the agricultural operations allegedly undertaken by the assessee, the ld.CIT(A) concurred with the AO that the claim of exempt agricultural income u/s.10(1) of the Act had not been established. 39. The ld.CIT(A) also considered the alternative contention of the assessee that the receipts represented turnover from business activities and that the income therefrom had already been offered to tax on a presumptive basis u/s.44AD of the Act. The ld.CIT(A), however, was not persuaded to accept the said contention. 40. According to the ld.CIT(A), the applicability of section 44AD of the Act presupposed the existence of an identifiable business and ascertainable turnover arising therefrom. The ld.CIT(A) observed that the assessee had failed to establish, by producing supporting documentary evidence, even the existence of the business activities from which the alleged turnover was stated to have arisen. 41. The ld.CIT(A) was of the view that the presumptive taxation provisions contained in section 44AD of the Act could not be invoked merely to explain or regularise cash deposits whose nature and source had not otherwise been satisfactorily established. The ld.CIT(A), therefore, held that the assessee’s reliance upon section 44AD of the Act was misplaced and that the declaration of presumptive income did not, by itself, establish that the entire cash deposits represented genuine business receipts. 42. The ld.CIT(A) further considered the observations of the AO regarding the pattern of cash deposits in the bank accounts. The ld.CIT(A) noted that the deposits were made in round figures and that the assessee had failed to produce corresponding records establishing any correlation between such deposits and the alleged purchase and sale of coconuts. 43. The ld.CIT(A) observed that no purchase bills, sales invoices, stock registers, cash flow statements or other relevant business records had been furnished to establish the movement of goods or the generation of sale proceeds corresponding to the amounts deposited in the bank accounts. In the absence of such evidence, the ld.CIT(A) held that the cash deposits could not be regarded as having arisen from regular business transactions merely on the basis of the explanation furnished by the assessee. 44. The ld.CIT(A) also examined the contention of the assessee that the AO had erred in considering the gross deposits without giving credit for the corresponding withdrawals from the bank accounts. In this regard, the ld.CIT(A) observed that the assessee had not furnished any cash flow statement or other documentary evidence establishing a nexus between the cash withdrawn from the bank accounts and the amounts subsequently deposited therein. 45. According to the ld.CIT(A), the mere existence of withdrawals in the bank accounts did not automatically establish that the amounts so withdrawn had been redeposited or that the deposits represented rotation of the same funds. In the absence of a demonstrable nexus between the withdrawals and subsequent deposits, the ld.CIT(A) held that the contention seeking adjustment of withdrawals against deposits was not acceptable. 46. The ld.CIT(A) further observed that, in the absence of books of account and supporting cash flow statements, the claim of the assessee regarding the recycling or redeposit of cash could not be verified. The ld.CIT(A), therefore, declined to accept the contention that the gross cash deposits were required to be reduced by the withdrawals reflected in the bank accounts. 47. Having rejected the explanation regarding agricultural income, the alternative claim of business receipts assessable u/s.44AD of the Act, and the contention regarding adjustment of cash withdrawals, the ld.CIT(A) proceeded to examine the applicability of section 69A of the Act to the facts of the case. 48. The ld.CIT(A) observed that section 69A of the Act contemplates the treatment of money as deemed income where the assessee is found to be the owner thereof and fails to furnish a satisfactory explanation regarding its nature and source. In the present case, the ld.CIT(A) noted that the ownership of the money deposited in the bank accounts was not in dispute, inasmuch as the assessee herself had acknowledged the deposits. 49. The ld.CIT(A) further observed that the explanation furnished by the assessee regarding the source of the cash deposits had remained unsubstantiated by credible evidence. The assessee had neither established the agricultural activities claimed by her nor produced satisfactory evidence regarding the alleged business transactions from which the cash deposits were stated to have arisen. 50. In the aforesaid circumstances, the ld.CIT(A) held that the essential conditions for invoking section 69A of the Act stood satisfied, since the assessee was admittedly the owner of the money deposited in the bank accounts and had failed to satisfactorily explain the nature and source thereof. 51. The ld.CIT(A), therefore, concurred with the findings recorded by the AO and held that the cash deposits aggregating to Rs.1,17,70,000/- were liable to be treated as unexplained money u/s.69A of the Act. Consequently, the ld.CIT(A) confirmed the addition of Rs.1,17,70,000/- made by the AO. Accordingly, the ld.CIT(A), vide the impugned appellate order dated 02.04.2026, upheld the assessment order insofar as it related to the addition of Rs.1,17,70,000/- u/s.69A of the Act and dismissed the appeal preferred by the assessee. 52. Aggrieved by the aforesaid order of the ld.CIT(A), the assessee is in further appeal before this Tribunal, challenging the confirmation of the addition of Rs.1,17,70,000/- made u/s.69A of the Act, notwithstanding the claim that the deposits represented receipts from the coconut-related activities and that income therefrom had already been offered to tax under the presumptive taxation provisions of section 44AD of the Act. 53. The ld.AR appearing for the assessee submitted that the authorities below had erred in treating the cash deposits of Rs.1,17,70,000/- as unexplained money u/s.69A of the Act, without properly appreciating the nature of the business carried on by the assessee and the evidence furnished in support thereof. He submitted that the assessee was engaged in the business of purchasing and selling coconuts. The assessee used to take coconut-yielding gardens on lease from agriculturists, harvest coconuts by engaging labourers and sell the same in the market. Apart from this, the assessee also purchased coconuts from other agriculturists and sold them after semi-dehusking. The cash deposits in the bank account represented the sale proceeds realised from the aforesaid business activities. 54. The ld. AR invited our attention to the bank statement placed at pages 15 to 29 of the paper book and submitted that the assessee was maintaining an overdraft account with South Indian Bank Ltd., Nagercoil Branch. He submitted that current accounts and overdraft facilities were ordinarily maintained for carrying on business and commercial transactions. Therefore, the very nature of the bank account supported the explanation of the assessee that she was carrying on business activities. He further submitted that the cash deposits of Rs.1,17,70,000/- were not made on a single day but were deposited on various dates throughout the relevant financial year. The bank statement also reflected frequent withdrawals, payments and other transactions. According to the ld. AR, the regular deposits and withdrawals clearly indicated the movement of funds in the course of business. The AO had considered only the cash deposits without properly appreciating the corresponding withdrawals and other transactions appearing in the bank account. 55. The ld.AR further invited our attention to the confirmations furnished by various purchasers of coconuts, placed at pages 39 to 68 of the paper book. He submitted that these confirmations were duly furnished before the ld.CIT(A) during the appellate proceedings. The said confirmations supported the claim of the assessee that she was engaged in the business of selling coconuts. However, the ld.CIT(A), without properly examining the confirmations, had concluded that the assessee failed to establish the existence of business activities. The ld.AR submitted that neither the AO nor the ld.CIT(A) had brought any material on record to disprove the transactions confirmed by the purchasers. Therefore, considering the nature of the bank account, the regular deposits and withdrawals and the confirmations furnished by the purchasers, it was sufficiently established that the assessee had carried on the business of purchasing and selling coconuts and that the cash deposits were attributable to the said business. 56. The ld.AR further submitted that the assessee had filed an updated return of income u/s.139(8A) of the Act on 16.03.2023, declaring a total business turnover of Rs.1,27,70,000/- and offering income of Rs.10,21,600/-, being 8% of the turnover, under the presumptive taxation provisions of section 44AD of the Act. The said income was included in the total income of Rs.24,96,430/- declared in the updated return. The ld.AR submitted that the cash deposits of Rs.1,17,70,000/- formed part of the aforesaid business turnover already disclosed by the assessee. He further submitted that the AO had not disturbed the turnover declared by the assessee or the presumptive business income offered u/s.44AD of the Act. On the contrary, the AO had adopted the returned income of Rs.24,96,430/-, which included the business income of Rs.10,21,600/-, and thereafter made a separate addition of Rs.1,17,70,000/- u/s.69A of the Act. 57. The ld.AR contended that, having accepted the returned income which included the profits computed on the disclosed business turnover, the AO was not justified in treating the cash deposits forming part of the very same turnover as unexplained money u/s.69A of the Act. He submitted that the AO had not brought any material on record to establish that the cash deposits represented money received from any source other than the business carried on by the assessee or that the deposits were over and above the turnover already disclosed. Therefore, the addition of Rs.1,17,70,000/- u/s.69A of the Act, while retaining the presumptive business income offered on the same receipts, resulted in a double addition in respect of the very same business transactions. 58. The ld.AR further submitted that the provisions of section 44AD of the Act provided for computation of business income on a presumptive basis without requiring the assessee to maintain regular books of account, subject to the conditions prescribed therein. The assessee had declared income at 8% of the gross turnover in accordance with the said provision. Therefore, the authorities below were not justified in rejecting the business explanation merely because the assessee had not maintained regular books of account, purchase bills, sales invoices or stock registers. He submitted that, once the cash deposits were attributable to the disclosed business activities, the entire deposits could not be treated as income, since only the profits arising from the business were liable to be brought to tax. 59. The ld.AR also submitted that the ld.CIT(A) had failed to appreciate the distinction between business receipts and unexplained money. He contended that the assessee had explained the nature and source of the deposits by furnishing the bank statements, particulars of the business activities, confirmations from purchasers and the updated return disclosing the corresponding turnover. The authorities below had not established that the deposits represented any independent or undisclosed source of income. In these circumstances, the invocation of section 69A of the Act was wholly unjustified. 60. The ld.AR, therefore, submitted that the cash deposits of Rs.1,17,70,000/- represented the business receipts of the assessee, which had already been included in the turnover of Rs.1,27,70,000/- declared in the updated return, and the presumptive income therefrom had already been offered to tax u/s.44AD of the Act. Accordingly, he contended that the addition of the entire cash deposits u/s.69A of the Act amounted to taxing the same receipts twice and was unsustainable in law. He, therefore, prayed that the order of the ld.CIT(A) be set aside and the addition of Rs.1,17,70,000/- made by the AO u/s.69A of the Act be deleted. 61. Per contra, the ld.DR strongly supported the orders of the authorities below and submitted that the assessee had failed to satisfactorily explain the nature and source of the cash deposits aggregating to Rs.1,17,70,000/-. The ld.DR contended that the assessee had not maintained books of account or produced any reliable documentary evidence to substantiate the alleged business activities and the corresponding cash receipts. The ld.DR further submitted that the mere disclosure of turnover and offering of presumptive income u/s.44AD of the Act would not automatically establish the genuineness of the cash deposits or absolve the assessee from the obligation to explain their nature and source. The ld.DR further contended that the confirmations furnished by the assessee were insufficient to establish that the entire cash deposits represented business receipts. Further, the ld.DR drew our attention to the bank statement in respect of payments made by the assessee towards purchase of goods vehicle, to establish that the presumptive taxation of 8% of the receipts does not comply with the corresponding investments made during the year. In support of her contentions, the ld.DR placed reliance on the decision of the Bangalore Bench of the Tribunal in ITA No.537/Bang/2024 and submitted that the provisions of section 44AD of the Act could not be invoked to explain cash deposits whose source had not been satisfactorily established. The ld.DR, therefore, submitted that the AO was justified in treating the impugned cash deposits as unexplained money u/s.69A of the Act and that the ld.CIT(A) had rightly confirmed the addition. Accordingly, the ld.DR prayed that the order of the ld.CIT(A) be upheld and the appeal filed by the assessee be dismissed. 62. We have heard the rival submissions, perused the orders of the authorities below and carefully considered the material available on record, including the paper book filed by the assessee. We have also gone through the judicial precedent relied upon by the ld.DR. The solitary issue that arises for our consideration in the present appeal is whether the authorities below were justified in treating the cash deposits aggregating to Rs.1,17,70,000/- made in the bank account of the assessee during the relevant previous year as unexplained money u/s.69A of the Act, notwithstanding the claim of the assessee that the said deposits represented receipts from the business of purchasing and selling coconuts and formed part of the turnover already disclosed in the updated return of income filed u/s.139(8A) of the Act. 63. The undisputed facts are that the assessee had filed an updated return of income on 16.03.2023, declaring a total income of Rs.24,96,430/-, which included business income of Rs.10,21,600/- computed at 8% of the gross turnover of Rs.1,27,70,000/- u/s.44AD of the Act. The assessee had explained that she was engaged in the business of purchasing and selling coconuts and that the cash deposits aggregating to Rs.1,17,70,000/- represented sale proceeds realised from the said business activities. The AO, however, rejected the explanation primarily on the ground that the assessee had not maintained regular books of account, purchase bills, sales invoices, stock registers and other supporting documents. Accordingly, the entire cash deposits were treated as unexplained money u/s.69A of the Act and added to the returned income. The ld.CIT(A) sustained the addition for substantially similar reasons. 64. At the outset, we find that the principal basis for making the impugned addition is the conclusion of the AO that the assessee had failed to establish the existence of the business activities claimed by her. It is, therefore, necessary to examine whether the material available on record supports the explanation that the assessee was engaged in the business of purchasing and selling coconuts and whether the cash deposits could reasonably be attributed to such activities. 65. In this regard, we find that the assessee was maintaining an overdraft account bearing No.0053081000002117 with South Indian Bank Ltd., Nagercoil Branch. A copy of the bank statement for the relevant previous year has been placed at pages 15 to 29 of the paper book. The bank statement specifically describes the account as an “Overdraft General” account. It is relevant to observe that current accounts and overdraft facilities are ordinarily associated with business or commercial transactions, involving regular receipts, payments and movement of funds. Though the mere maintenance of an overdraft account cannot, by itself, conclusively establish the existence of a business, it is certainly a relevant circumstance which requires consideration along with the other material available on record. 66. On perusal of the bank statement, we further notice that the cash deposits were not made on a single day or as one isolated transaction. The deposits were made on different dates during the relevant previous year. The account also reflects various cash withdrawals, payments through banking channels, transfers and other debit and credit transactions. Thus, the bank statement reveals a recurring movement of funds rather than a solitary deposit of an unexplained amount. In our considered view, such a pattern of transactions is consistent with the explanation of the assessee that the account was being utilised for carrying on business activities involving the purchase and sale of coconuts. 67. We further find that the AO had principally concentrated on the cash deposits appearing on the credit side of the bank account without adequately examining the corresponding withdrawals and other transactions appearing on the debit side. It is a settled principle that, while examining the nature and source of deposits in a bank account, the transactions must be appreciated in their proper factual perspective. The deposits cannot invariably be considered in isolation from the withdrawals and payments reflected in the same account. Although the existence of withdrawals does not automatically establish that every subsequent deposit represents redeposit of the same funds, the regular movement of money is nevertheless a relevant circumstance in determining whether the account was being operated in connection with business activities. 68. We also notice that the AO had drawn an adverse inference from the fact that certain cash deposits were made in round figures. In our considered opinion, the mere fact that deposits were made in round figures cannot, by itself, lead to the conclusion that the deposits were unexplained. In cash-based trading activities, particularly those involving agricultural commodities, it is not unusual for cash collections to be accumulated and deposited in convenient denominations. Such a circumstance may justify further examination, but cannot constitute a sufficient basis for treating the entire deposits as unexplained money, particularly when the assessee has furnished an explanation regarding the nature of the underlying transactions. 69. We further find that the assessee had explained the nature of her business activities before the AO as well as the ld.CIT(A). The assessee had stated that she was engaged in taking coconut-yielding gardens on lease from agriculturists, harvesting coconuts by engaging labourers, purchasing coconuts from other agriculturists and selling the same in the local market. The explanation regarding the nature of the business had remained substantially consistent throughout the proceedings. 70. It is also noticed that the assessee had furnished confirmations from certain purchasers of coconuts during the appellate proceedings before the ld.CIT(A). Copies of these confirmations have been placed at pages 39 to 68 of the paper book. The said confirmations constitute relevant documentary material supporting the claim of the assessee that she was engaged in the business of selling coconuts. We find that the ld.CIT(A), while sustaining the addition, had not adequately dealt with the evidentiary value of these confirmations and had proceeded to conclude that the assessee failed to establish the existence of the business. 71. In our considered opinion, the confirmations furnished by the purchasers could not have been disregarded without examining their contents and the transactions referred to therein. If the authorities entertained any doubt regarding the genuineness of the transactions, it was open to them to undertake appropriate verification. However, no specific adverse material has been brought to our notice demonstrating that the confirmations were false or that the transactions referred to therein were fictitious. Though such confirmations may not, by themselves, establish the source of every individual deposit, they constitute relevant corroborative evidence regarding the existence of the business activities claimed by the assessee. 72. Thus, when the nature of the overdraft account, the recurring deposits and withdrawals, the explanation regarding the coconut business, the confirmations furnished by the purchasers and the disclosure of business turnover in the updated return are considered cumulatively, we are of the view that there is sufficient material to support the existence of the business activities carried on by the assessee. The authorities below were, therefore, not justified in rejecting the business explanation in its entirety merely because the assessee had not maintained regular books of account or produced certain primary documents. 73. There is yet another important aspect of the matter which, in our considered opinion, goes to the root of the controversy. We find from the updated return of income placed at pages 1 to 12 of the paper book and the computation of total income placed at pages 13 and 14 thereof that the assessee had disclosed gross turnover of Rs.1,27,70,000/- from the business of dealing in coconuts and had offered income of Rs.10,21,600/-, being 8% thereof, u/s.44AD of the Act. The said income formed part of the total income of Rs.24,96,430/- declared in the updated return. 74. Significantly, while completing the assessment, the AO adopted the returned income of Rs.24,96,430/- as the starting point for computing the assessed income. The said returned income admittedly included the presumptive business income of Rs.10,21,600/- offered by the assessee. The AO had neither disturbed the turnover of Rs.1,27,70,000/- declared by the assessee nor separately recomputed the profits arising from the said business. Instead, the AO proceeded to make a further addition of Rs.1,17,70,000/- u/s.69A of the Act, treating the cash deposits as unexplained money. 75. We find considerable force in the contention of the ld.AR hat the approach adopted by the AO is inconsistent. On the one hand, the AO has retained the business income declared by the assessee on the basis of the turnover of Rs.1,27,70,000/-, while, on the other hand, he has treated the cash deposits of Rs.1,17,70,000/-, which according to the assessee formed part of the very same turnover, as unexplained money. The assessment order does not contain any specific finding that the cash deposits were over and above the turnover already disclosed or that they represented receipts from any independent source. 76. It is true that the mere adoption of the returned income does not necessarily amount to an express acceptance of the genuineness of every transaction constituting the turnover. Nevertheless, where the assessee has disclosed business turnover, offered presumptive profits thereon and furnished material supporting the existence of business activities, the AO cannot simply disregard the explanation and assess the entire corresponding cash deposits as unexplained money without examining whether such deposits are already included in the disclosed turnover. 77. In the present case, the cash deposits of Rs.1,17,70,000/- are less than the gross turnover of Rs.1,27,70,000/- disclosed by the assessee. The assessee has consistently maintained that the said deposits form part of the business receipts already offered as turnover. The AO has not demonstrated that the deposits represent receipts outside the disclosed turnover. In these circumstances, and having regard to the material supporting the existence of the coconut business, we are inclined to accept the explanation that the impugned deposits are attributable to the business activities of the assessee. 78. We are conscious of the fact that an assessee cannot escape the application of section 69A merely by describing unexplained deposits as business receipts or by declaring presumptive income u/s.44AD of the Act. The nature and source of the money must be examined on the basis of the facts and evidence available on record. However, in the present case, the explanation is not based merely on an unsupported assertion. The assessee has furnished the bank statements, disclosed the corresponding business turnover in the updated return and produced confirmations from purchasers. These circumstances, considered together, sufficiently support the conclusion that the deposits arose in the course of the business activities of the assessee. 79. Section 69A of the Act provides for treating money, bullion, jewellery or other valuable articles as deemed income where the assessee is found to be the owner thereof and the explanation offered regarding their nature and source is either absent or found unsatisfactory. Thus, the applicability of the said provision necessarily depends upon the facts and circumstances of each case and the explanation furnished by the assessee regarding the source of the money. 80. In the case before us, the assessee has explained that the cash deposits represent receipts from the business of purchasing and selling coconuts. As discussed hereinabove, the said explanation is supported by the nature and operation of the overdraft account, the recurring bank transactions, the confirmations furnished by purchasers and the disclosure of turnover in the updated return. The AO has not brought any positive material on record to establish that the deposits arose from an undisclosed or independent source unconnected with the business activities of the assessee. 81. We further observe that the gross receipts of a trading business cannot ordinarily be equated with the income or profits derived therefrom. In a business involving the purchase and sale of goods, the sale proceeds necessarily include amounts representing the cost of purchases and other business expenditure. Consequently, where the receipts are found to be attributable to business activities, the entire gross receipts cannot be treated as taxable income merely because the assessee has not maintained regular books of account. What is liable to be brought to tax is the profit element embedded in such receipts, subject to the applicable provisions of the Act. 82. We are, therefore, of the considered view that, having regard to the peculiar facts and circumstances of the present case, the authorities below were not justified in treating the entire cash deposits of Rs.1,17,70,000/- as unexplained money u/s.69A of the Act. The impugned deposits are more appropriately regarded as receipts attributable to the business activities carried on by the assessee. Accordingly, the addition made u/s.69A of the Act cannot be sustained in its present form. 83. The ld.DR has placed reliance on the decision of the Bangalore Bench of the Tribunal in the case of Smt.Aruna v. ITO, ITA No.537/Bang/2024, order dated 22.04.2024, wherein the Tribunal upheld the addition made in respect of deposits in the bank account of the assessee. We have carefully gone through the said decision. In our considered opinion, the facts obtaining in the said case are materially distinguishable from the facts of the present appeal. 84. In the case of Smt. Aruna (supra), the assessee had not filed a return of income for the assessment year under consideration and had also failed to comply with the notices issued by the AO. Consequently, the assessment was completed ex parte u/s.144 of the Act by treating the bank deposits aggregating to Rs.18,30,591/- as unexplained. It was only during the appellate proceedings that the assessee claimed that the deposits represented receipts from the business of plying two tractors and sought computation of income under the presumptive taxation provisions of section 44AE of the Act. 85. The Bangalore Bench specifically noticed that the assessee in that case had failed to furnish any evidence establishing the ownership of the two tractors allegedly used in the business. The assessee had not produced the registration certificates of the tractors, even when specifically called upon by the Tribunal. Further, no supporting evidence such as business licences or other relevant documents had been furnished to establish that the assessee was actually carrying on the alleged transport business during the relevant previous year. 86. The Tribunal also noticed that the bank deposits in that case included certain amounts claimed to have been received as loans from third parties. However, the assessee had failed to furnish confirmations or other evidence establishing the identity and creditworthiness of the alleged creditors and the genuineness of the transactions. In the absence of such evidence, the Tribunal held that the assessee had failed to satisfactorily explain the nature and source of the deposits. 87. More importantly, the Bangalore Bench observed that the assessee had neither filed a return of income nor opted for presumptive taxation u/s.44AE of the Act for the assessment year under consideration. It was in these circumstances, coupled with the complete absence of evidence establishing the alleged business activities, that the Tribunal declined to accept the contention that the bank deposits should be treated as business receipts and the income estimated on a presumptive basis. 88. The facts of the present case stand on a materially different footing. Firstly, the assessee herein had filed an updated return of income u/s.139(8A) of the Act on 16.03.2023, declaring gross business turnover of Rs.1,27,70,000/- and offering income of Rs.10,21,600/- u/s.44AD of the Act. Secondly, the said business income formed part of the returned income adopted by the AO in the assessment order. Thirdly, the assessee had furnished bank statements evidencing recurring deposits, withdrawals and other transactions in an overdraft account. Fourthly, the assessee had produced confirmations from certain purchasers of coconuts before the ld.CIT(A), thereby furnishing corroborative evidence regarding the existence of business activities. 89. Thus, unlike the case of Smt. Aruna (supra), where the very existence of the alleged business remained wholly unsubstantiated and no return of income had been filed, the present assessee has disclosed the business turnover in her updated return and furnished supporting material regarding the business activities. Further, in the present case, the AO has retained the presumptive business income declared by the assessee without separately disturbing the disclosed turnover, whereas no such circumstance existed in the case before the Bangalore Bench. 90. We also find that the Bangalore Bench itself emphasised that the estimation of business income on the basis of bank deposits would be permissible only where the assessee established that the deposits represented business receipts. Thus, the principle emerging from the said decision is that the claim of business receipts must be supported by evidence and cannot be accepted merely on the basis of an unsupported assertion. In the present case, as discussed hereinabove, the assessee has furnished material supporting the existence of the business activities and the nexus of the deposits with the disclosed turnover. Therefore, the aforesaid decision, properly appreciated, does not militate against the claim of the present assessee. 91. In view of the material factual distinctions discussed above, we are of the considered opinion that the decision of the Bangalore Bench in Smt. Aruna v. ITO (supra), relied upon by the ld.DR, is distinguishable on facts and does not govern the issue arising for consideration in the present appeal. 92. Having held that the cash deposits aggregating to Rs.1,17,70,000/- are attributable to the business activities of the assessee and that the addition of the entire amount u/s.69A of the Act is unsustainable, the next question that arises for our consideration is whether the income declared by the assessee u/s.44AD of the Act should be accepted as such or whether, having regard to the peculiar facts and circumstances of the case, a reasonable further estimation of business income is warranted. 93. In this regard, we find that, although the assessee has furnished material supporting the existence of business activities, it is equally true that the assessee has not maintained regular books of account, purchase bills, sales invoices, stock registers or other contemporaneous records from which the actual profits arising from the business could be independently verified. The confirmations furnished by certain purchasers establish the existence of business dealings, but do not necessarily establish the complete particulars of the purchases, sales and expenditure incurred during the relevant previous year. 94. We further notice that the assessee had originally treated the activities as agricultural in nature and had subsequently offered the receipts as business turnover in the updated return. Though this circumstance, by itself, does not justify treating the receipts as unexplained money, it is nevertheless relevant while examining the reliability of the profit declared by the assessee. In the absence of complete supporting records, the correctness of the actual profit margin from the business cannot be verified with precision. 95. It is well settled that the estimation of income must be fair and reasonable and should be based on the facts and circumstances of the case. Such estimation cannot be arbitrary or punitive. At the same time, where the assessee has failed to maintain sufficient records to enable proper verification of the actual profits, a reasonable estimation may be warranted to ensure that the income chargeable to tax is appropriately brought to assessment. 96. In the present case, the assessee has already offered income at 8% of the disclosed turnover of Rs.1,27,70,000/- u/s.44AD of the Act. Having regard to the substantial volume of cash transactions, the absence of regular books of account and supporting records, and the inability to independently verify the actual profit margin, we are of the considered view that the ends of justice would be met by estimating an additional profit of 8% on the impugned cash deposits of Rs.1,17,70,000/-, over and above the business income already declared by the assessee. 97. We make it clear that the aforesaid estimation of additional profit is made having regard to the peculiar facts and circumstances of the present case and is not to be understood as laying down any general proposition that income declared u/s.44AD of the Act must invariably be enhanced by a further 8%. The additional estimation is considered appropriate in the present case in view of the deficiencies in the supporting records and the attendant circumstances discussed hereinabove. 98. Accordingly, we direct the AO to make an additional income of 8% of the cash deposits of Rs.1,17,70,000/-, which works out to Rs.9,41,600/-. The said amount shall be assessed under the head “Profits and gains of business or profession” along with the business income of Rs.10,21,600/- already declared by the assessee u/s.44AD of the Act. Thus, the addition of Rs.1,17,70,000/- made by the AO u/s.69A of the Act and sustained by the ld.CIT(A) is deleted as such and substituted by an addition of Rs.9,41,600/- towards estimated business income. The balance addition of Rs.1,08,28,400/- stands deleted. 99. In view of the foregoing discussion, we set aside the order of the ld.CIT(A) insofar as it confirms the addition of Rs.1,17,70,000/- u/s.69A of the Act and direct the AO to restrict the addition to Rs.9,41,600/- under the head “Profits and gains of business or profession”. The grounds raised by the assessee are accordingly partly allowed. 100. In the result, the appeal filed by the assessee is partly allowed.

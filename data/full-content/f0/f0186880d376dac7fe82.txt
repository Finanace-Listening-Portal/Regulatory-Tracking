Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); Shree Vijayalakshmi Charitable Trust Vs Commissioner of GST and Central Excise (CESTAT Chennai) Summary: The Customs, Excise and Service Tax Appellate Tribunal (CESTAT), Chennai, comprising Technical Member M. Ajit Kumar and Judicial Member Ajayan T.V., dismissed two appeals filed by Shree Vijayalakshmi Charitable Trust, Coimbatore, and upheld a service tax demand of ₹50,75,26,810, together with applicable interest and penalties, on amounts received from leasing vacant lands for mining and quarrying activities. The principal controversy was whether the Trust’s arrangements with a quarry operator constituted taxable renting of immovable property under Section 65B(41), Section 65B(44) and Section 66E(a) of the Finance Act, 1994, or represented a genuine joint venture involving revenue sharing from the extraction and sale of minerals. The dispute arose from two show cause notices covering the period from 1 January 2013 to 31 March 2014. The adjudicating authority confirmed service tax of ₹50.75 crore, imposed penalties of ₹11,98,32,990 under Section 78(1) and ₹13,39,30,415 under Section 76 of the Finance Act, 1994, besides penalties under Section 77(2). The Trust contended that the amounts represented its share of mining proceeds and could not be characterised as consideration for a taxable service. The Department maintained that the Trust had merely leased its land for an annual rent and additional rent calculated with reference to mineral sale receipts. After examining the lease deeds, notarised agreements, financial records, statutory provisions, judicial precedents and allegations of suppression, the Tribunal concluded that the arrangements were genuine leases rather than joint ventures and that the confirmed demand, interest and penalties were legally sustainable. Background of the mining agreements and departmental investigation: Shree Vijayalakshmi Charitable Trust, established in 1992 for charitable activities including educational scholarships, medical assistance and donations to Government programmes, owned vacant lands at Palathurai Village, Madukkarai, Coimbatore. It entered into two registered lease arrangements with Shri M. Palanisamy covering approximately 22.68 acres and 14.86 acres for five-year periods commencing on 29 January 2010 and 17 May 2010, respectively. The annual lease rentals were fixed at ₹22,680 and ₹14,860, calculated at ₹1,000 per acre. Alongside these lease deeds, the parties executed separate notarised agreements requiring the lessee to pay the Trust an additional amount equal to 75% of the receipts earned from the sale of minerals extracted from the leased lands. The lessee undertook quarrying operations involving blasting, crushing and processing rough stones into blue metal, manufactured sand and other construction materials. A further agreement involving the District Collector, Coimbatore, recognised the Trust as the registered landholder and authorised the lessee to quarry and remove minor minerals belonging to the Government. During the investigation, the Department examined the registered and notarised agreements, statements of the lessee, the Trust’s financial statements, ledger accounts and income-tax documents. The records showed that substantial amounts were paid and recorded as lease rent or additional lease rent. The Department also relied on an income-tax certificate issued on 24 July 2012 permitting payment of rent up to ₹150 crore without tax deduction at source. Although the Trust was already registered under service tax for renting commercial property and was paying tax on receipts from SIV Towers, it had not discharged service tax on the substantial additional amounts received from the quarry operator. Trust’s defence based on revenue sharing, joint venture and sale of minerals: The Trust argued that the agreements, when read in their entirety, established a commercial arrangement for sharing revenue from mining activities rather than a simple letting of immovable property. It submitted that the lessee undertook extraction, processing and sale of blue metal and mineral sand, while the Trust contributed the land and accepted the economic risks associated with its use for quarrying. According to the Trust, the additional amount equivalent to 75% of mineral sale receipts represented its agreed share of the business proceeds and not rent for providing a taxable service. It relied on New Horizons Ltd. Vs Union of India and Faqir Chand Gulati Vs Uppal Agencies Pvt. Ltd. concerning the essential characteristics of a joint venture, including common commercial objectives, mutual responsibilities and participation in profits and risks. The Trust also invoked the decision in Mormugao Port Trust Vs Commissioner of Customs, Central Excise and Service Tax and B.G. Exploration and Production India Ltd. Vs Commissioner of CGST to contend that transactions undertaken by co-venturers on a principal-to-principal basis do not automatically constitute services rendered by one party to another. Further reliance was placed on Inox Leisure Ltd. Vs Commissioner of Service Tax and the departmental Circular No. 109/3/2009-ST dated 23 February 2009 concerning revenue-sharing arrangements. The Trust additionally submitted that the minerals had already suffered VAT when sold by the lessee and that service tax could not be imposed on the same underlying commercial proceeds merely because its books described them as additional lease amounts. It argued that contractual nomenclature and accounting treatment should not override the real substance of the transaction. Tribunal’s findings that the transaction was a lease rather than a joint venture: The Tribunal rejected the Trust’s interpretation after examining the registered lease deeds, corresponding notarised agreements and the joint agreement involving the District Collector. It relied on S. Chattanatha Karayalar Vs Central Bank of India, reported in (1965) 3 SCR 318, for the principle that several documents forming part of the same commercial transaction must be read together to ascertain their true legal effect. Applying that principle, the Tribunal found that the Trust had leased its vacant lands to Shri M. Palanisamy in exchange for a specified annual lease payment and an additional lease payment measured as 75% of the mineral sale receipts. The method used to quantify additional rent did not alter its character as consideration for the use of land. The quarrying permission had been obtained by the lessee from the District Collector, and the lessee independently undertook the mining operations, bore the operational risks, paid the applicable seigniorage fees and discharged responsibilities under the Tamil Nadu Minor Mineral Concession Rules. The agreements did not provide for joint management, shared losses, mutual operational control or the establishment of a common business undertaking. The Tribunal therefore distinguished the principles discussed in Faqir Chand Gulati, Mormugao Port Trust and B.G. Exploration and Production India Ltd., observing that those decisions involved arrangements with materially different contractual features. It held that the Trust’s subsequent characterisation of additional rent as profit sharing was inconsistent with the contemporaneous documents. Since the annual rental and additional rental were both payments received for allowing the lessee to use the land, the Tribunal held that the entire consideration was attributable to the activity of leasing immovable property. Taxability under the Finance Act and rejection of charitable exemption: The Tribunal examined the statutory definition of service under Section 65B(44), the meaning of renting under Section 65B(41), and the declaration of renting of immovable property as a taxable service under Section 66E(a) of the Finance Act, 1994. It observed that renting includes permitting another person to access, occupy or use immovable property, whether or not possession or control is transferred, and encompasses leasing, licensing and similar arrangements. The Trust’s grant of vacant land to the quarry operator in return for annual and additional rental payments satisfied these requirements. The taxable transaction was the leasing of land by the Trust, not the extraction, manufacture or sale of minerals by the lessee. Consequently, the Trust’s contention that VAT had been paid on the sale of minerals did not affect the independent service tax liability arising from the leasing arrangement. The Tribunal further rejected the claim that the activity was excluded by the negative list under Section 66D, because the relevant exclusion relating to renting of land for agricultural purposes did not cover leasing land for quarrying and mining. It also examined Notification No. 25/2012-Service Tax dated 20 June 2012, particularly Entry 4 read with the definition of charitable activities under paragraph 2(k). The Tribunal held that registration as a charitable trust under Section 12AA of the Income-tax Act, 1961 did not automatically exempt every activity undertaken by the Trust from service tax. Renting vacant land for commercial mining did not fall within the specified charitable activities. The Tribunal also noted that the place of provision of services relating to immovable property under Rule 5 of the Place of Provision of Services Rules, 2012 was the location of the property, which was in India. Therefore, the leasing arrangement attracted service tax under Section 66B of the Finance Act, 1994. Extended limitation, suppression of facts and penalties: The Trust challenged invocation of the extended limitation period and argued that the Department had obtained the relevant agreements, financial statements and accounting records during investigation, thereby negating any allegation of deliberate suppression. It relied on decisions including Anand Nishikawa Co. Ltd. Vs Commissioner of Central Excise, Continental Foundation Joint Venture Vs Commissioner of Central Excise, Padmini Products Ltd. Vs Commissioner of Central Excise and Pushpam Pharmaceuticals Company Vs Collector of Central Excise, contending that a bona fide interpretational dispute could not justify extended limitation or penal consequences. The Tribunal rejected this defence, emphasising that the Trust was already registered for renting of immovable property services, regularly paid service tax on rent received from its commercial building and filed ST-3 returns. Nevertheless, it had not disclosed the agreements for leasing mining lands or the substantial additional lease receipts to the service tax authorities. The relevant transactions came to departmental knowledge only through investigation, particularly through information obtained from the lessee. The Tribunal found that the Trust’s failure to disclose rental receipts running into crores of rupees, while discharging tax on other renting activities, constituted wilful suppression of material facts with intent to evade payment of service tax. Accordingly, invocation of the extended period under Section 73(1) of the Finance Act, 1994 was upheld. The Tribunal also rejected the request for relief under Section 80, finding that the Trust had not demonstrated a reasonable or bona fide belief supporting non-payment. It upheld the penalties imposed under Sections 76, 77(2) and 78, noting that they related to separate show cause notices and different periods and were within the applicable statutory limits. In particular, the Tribunal found no impermissible simultaneous imposition of Sections 76 and 78 penalties on the same default. Final ruling, consistency of tax positions and observations on excessive citations: The Tribunal concluded that the Trust had consistently treated the transactions as leases in its registered agreements, notarised documents, financial statements, income-tax records and representations before State authorities. It therefore rejected the attempt to characterise the same arrangements as joint ventures or profit-sharing transactions solely to resist service tax liability. In this context, it relied upon R.N. Gosain Vs Yashpal Dhir, reported in (1992) 4 SCC 683, concerning the doctrine of approbation and reprobation, under which a party cannot accept a transaction for obtaining one advantage and subsequently reject its legal character to obtain another inconsistent advantage. The Tribunal observed that the Trust could not claim income-tax benefits on the footing that the amounts were rent and simultaneously deny that characterisation for service tax purposes, particularly when the contractual documents themselves supported the renting arrangement. It held that the Department had discharged its burden of establishing taxability and that the adjudicating authority had passed a reasoned and legally sustainable order. Accordingly, the Tribunal upheld Order-in-Original Nos. 06–07/2018-COMMR dated 25 June 2019 in entirety, sustained the service tax demand of ₹50,75,26,810 with applicable interest and penalties, and dismissed both appeals. Before concluding, the Tribunal also expressed concern over unnecessarily lengthy written submissions containing numerous annexures, circulars and judicial precedents. Referring to Rashmi Metaliks Vs Kolkata Metropolitan Development Authority, reported in (2013) 17 SCR 345, it observed that excessive and indiscriminate citation of authorities can obscure the relevant issues and impede effective adjudication. The decision demonstrates that revenue-linked consideration may retain the character of rent when the underlying agreement is a genuine lease, charitable status does not create blanket immunity from service tax, and inconsistent tax positions unsupported by the contractual record cannot defeat a substantiated demand. Cases Discussed / Relied Upon S. Chattanatha Karayalar Vs Central Bank of India & Others (Supreme Court); (1965) 3 SCR 318 — Relied upon by the Tribunal for the principle that multiple documents forming part of a single transaction must be read and interpreted together. Applying this principle, the Tribunal considered the registered lease deeds, notarised agreements and the agreement involving the District Collector collectively to determine the true character of the transactions. R.N. Gosain Vs Yashpal Dhir (Supreme Court); (1992) 4 SCC 683 — Expressly relied upon by the Tribunal while applying the doctrine of approbation and reprobation. A party cannot accept the legal character of a transaction to secure an advantage and subsequently reject that same character to obtain another advantage. The principle was applied to the Trust’s inconsistent treatment of rental receipts under different tax laws. New Horizons Ltd. Vs Union of India (Supreme Court); (1995) 1 SCC 478 — Cited by the Trust on the concept of joint ventures and commercial arrangements involving multiple participants. The Tribunal distinguished the authority because the agreements under consideration did not establish joint management, common business responsibilities or shared operational risks. Pushpam Pharmaceuticals Company Vs Collector of Central Excise, Bombay (Supreme Court); 1995 (78) E.L.T. 401 (SC) — Cited by the Trust concerning the requirements for invoking extended limitation on the ground of suppression. The Tribunal distinguished the authority because it found positive evidence of non-disclosure of substantial lease rental receipts despite the Trust’s existing service tax registration. Bharat Sanchar Nigam Ltd. Vs Union of India (Supreme Court); 2006 (2) S.T.R. 161 (SC); (2006) 3 SCC 1 — Relied upon by the Trust in arguing that sales liable to VAT could not also be subjected to service tax. The Tribunal distinguished the principle because the impugned tax was levied on the separate activity of leasing immovable property, not on the lessee’s sale of quarried minerals. Continental Foundation Joint Venture Vs Commissioner of Central Excise, Chandigarh-I (Supreme Court); 2007 (216) E.L.T. 177 (SC) — Cited on the interpretation of suppression and the requirements for extended limitation. The Tribunal found the precedent distinguishable because the Trust had withheld information concerning additional rental receipts from the service tax authorities. Faqir Chand Gulati Vs Uppal Agencies Pvt. Ltd. (Supreme Court); 2008 (10) S.T.R. 401 (SC); (2008) 10 SCC 345 — Discussed concerning the essential characteristics of a genuine joint venture, including common objectives, shared responsibilities, management and participation in profits and losses. The Tribunal found that these characteristics were absent from the Trust’s mining-land lease arrangements. Imagic Creative Pvt. Ltd. Vs Commissioner of Commercial Taxes (Supreme Court); 2008 (9) S.T.R. 337 (SC); (2008) 2 SCC 614 — Cited concerning the distinction between VAT and service tax. The Tribunal held that the principle did not exclude service tax on the land-leasing transaction merely because the lessee separately paid VAT on mineral sales. Commissioner of Customs, Central Excise and Service Tax Vs Mormugao Port Trust (Supreme Court); 2018 (19) G.S.T.L. J118 (SC), affirming Mormugao Port Trust Vs Commissioner, 2017 (48) S.T.R. 69 (CESTAT Mumbai) — The Trust relied on the genuine joint venture and revenue-sharing principles discussed in the Tribunal’s decision, which had been affirmed by the Supreme Court. CESTAT Chennai distinguished the arrangement because the Trust did not participate jointly in mining operations or share the lessee’s operational responsibilities. Inox Leisure Ltd. Vs Commissioner of Service Tax (CESTAT Hyderabad); 2022 (60) G.S.T.L. 326 — Cited by the Trust to support the proposition that a genuine revenue-sharing arrangement does not automatically establish a taxable service. The Tribunal distinguished the decision because the present contracts evidenced actual leasing for consideration. B.G. Exploration and Production India Ltd. Vs Commissioner of CGST (CESTAT Mumbai); 2022 (64) G.S.T.L. 578 — Cited in support of the contention that contributions and obligations under a genuine joint operating agreement do not necessarily represent consideration for services. The Tribunal distinguished the case because the Trust’s agreements did not establish the mutual obligations and commercial characteristics of a joint venture. Meghraj Cinema Vs Commissioner of Service Tax, Mumbai-VII (CESTAT Mumbai); 2024 (8) TMI 1053 — Cited by the Trust in its compilation of revenue-sharing authorities. The Tribunal did not find the cited line of authorities applicable to the documented lease arrangements before it. Hardy Exploration and Production (India) Inc. Vs Commissioner of GST and Central Excise, Chennai (CESTAT Chennai); 2024 (1) TMI 456 — Cited by the Trust concerning commercial arrangements and taxability of receipts. The Tribunal distinguished the authorities relied upon because the present dispute involved an identifiable rental service under the actual agreements. Rashmi Metaliks Ltd. Vs Kolkata Metropolitan Development Authority (Supreme Court); 2013 INSC 606; (2013) 17 SCR 345; 11/09/2013 — Expressly referred to by the Tribunal in its concluding observations criticising excessive written submissions and indiscriminate citation of judicial authorities. The Tribunal emphasised that lengthy compilations of irrelevant precedents can hinder rather than assist judicial adjudication.

Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); Gautam Navlakha Vs National Investigation Agency (Supreme Court of India) Case Summary: The Supreme Court of India, in Gautam Navlakha Vs National Investigation Agency, Criminal Appeal No. 510 of 2021, decided on 12 May 2021, delivered an extensive judgment concerning the legal character of house arrest, police custody, judicial custody and the right to default bail under Section 167 of the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973. The central controversy was whether the 34 days spent by the appellant under house arrest between 28 August 2018 and 1 October 2018 could be counted towards the statutory period for filing a charge-sheet, thereby entitling him to default bail. The Court examined the relationship between judicially ordered restrictions on personal liberty and detention authorised under Section 167, particularly where an earlier transit remand order had subsequently been declared illegal. While recognising that house arrest can constitute a legally permissible form of custody under Section 167 in appropriate cases, the Supreme Court concluded that the particular house arrest imposed on the appellant had not been ordered, or purported to have been ordered, under that provision. Consequently, the period of house arrest could not be included in calculating the statutory detention period for his default-bail application. The appeal was dismissed, but the judgment established important principles concerning house arrest as an alternative form of judicial custody, the constitutional protection of personal liberty, and the circumstances in which courts may employ such custody in future cases. The criminal proceedings originated from FIR No. 4 of 2018 registered on 8 January 2018 at Vishrambagh Police Station, Pune, Maharashtra, concerning alleged offences under provisions of the Indian Penal Code, including Sections 153A, 505(1B), 34 and subsequently Section 120B. Various provisions of the Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act, 1967, including Sections 13, 16, 17, 18, 18B, 20, 38 and 40, were later invoked. The appellant’s name was added to the investigation on 22 August 2018, and he was arrested from his residence in Delhi on 28 August 2018. A Chief Metropolitan Magistrate at Saket granted transit remand for his production before the competent court in Pune. The appellant immediately approached the Delhi High Court through a writ petition seeking habeas corpus relief. The High Court initially restrained his removal from Delhi and directed that he remain at his residence under police supervision, without permission to leave the premises or meet persons other than his lawyers and ordinary residents of the house. Meanwhile, proceedings were instituted before the Supreme Court in Romila Thapar and Others Vs Union of India and Others, (2018) 10 SCC 753, concerning the arrest of several activists. Interim orders passed in those proceedings extended the arrangement of house arrest. Ultimately, on 1 October 2018, the Delhi High Court set aside the transit remand order for non-compliance with mandatory requirements of Articles 22(1) and 22(2) of the Constitution and relevant provisions of the CrPC, bringing the appellant’s house arrest to an end while leaving the investigating authorities free to proceed according to law. Thereafter, the appellant pursued proceedings challenging the FIR and seeking protection from arrest. The Bombay High Court dismissed his petition seeking quashing of the FIR on 13 September 2019, although interim protection continued for a limited period. Subsequent applications for anticipatory bail were unsuccessful before the competent courts, including proceedings before the Supreme Court. The appellant ultimately surrendered before the National Investigation Agency on 14 April 2020. He was placed in police custody from 15 April 2020, followed by further remand and subsequent judicial custody. On 11 June 2020, the appellant filed an application seeking default bail under Section 167(2) CrPC. His calculation included the earlier period of 34 days of house arrest in 2018 together with the periods of police and judicial custody undergone after surrender in April 2020. The prosecution disputed the inclusion of the earlier house arrest period, maintaining that it was not custody authorised under Section 167. The NIA Special Court rejected the default-bail application on 12 July 2020. The NIA subsequently filed a charge-sheet against the appellant on 9 October 2020. By judgment dated 8 February 2021, the Bombay High Court dismissed his appeal against the rejection of default bail, holding that the earlier house arrest period could not be treated as authorised statutory custody. This decision was challenged before the Supreme Court, which was required to determine whether the nature and legal foundation of the earlier detention permitted it to be added to the subsequent period of custody. The appellant contended that the restriction imposed upon him between 28 August 2018 and 1 October 2018 amounted to actual deprivation of personal liberty and could not be disregarded merely because it took the form of house arrest rather than detention in a police station or prison. It was argued that the Supreme Court and Delhi High Court had continued and modified the initial custody arrangement and that the absence of interrogation by the investigating agency was not decisive because even ordinary judicial custody does not necessarily involve police interrogation. The appellant maintained that Section 167 CrPC permits the Magistrate to authorise such custody as considered appropriate and does not confine the meaning of custody exclusively to police or prison detention. It was further submitted that the Delhi High Court’s judgment setting aside the transit remand had not declared the entire period of house arrest nonexistent or void. According to the appellant, a judicial declaration that the initial remand was illegal could not retrospectively erase the actual period during which his movements and personal freedom were restricted under court orders. He also argued that the statutory period could comprise separate periods of custody and need not necessarily consist of one uninterrupted period of confinement. On this interpretation, the earlier house arrest and subsequent detention following surrender were capable of being aggregated for computing the period relevant to default bail. The appellant relied upon the constitutional significance of personal liberty and argued that a contrary interpretation would effectively deny recognition to a substantial period of actual detention. The National Investigation Agency opposed the appeal, contending that the house arrest arrangement had been ordered as an interim protective measure in proceedings challenging the appellant’s arrest and not as police or judicial custody under Section 167 CrPC. The respondent emphasised that the transit remand order was stayed and subsequently set aside by the Delhi High Court, which rendered it incapable of providing a valid statutory foundation for counting the subsequent house arrest period as authorised detention. It was argued that the appellant had remained at his residence, with restricted access to others, but the investigating agency could not interrogate him in the manner ordinarily associated with police custody. The prosecution also relied upon the appellant’s subsequent conduct, including his applications for anticipatory bail and his eventual surrender in April 2020, to contend that the earlier house arrest arrangement had not been treated as continuing statutory custody. Another important submission concerned the timing of police custody under Section 167 and the special provisions of Section 43D of the UAPA. The NIA argued that accepting the appellant’s computation would create an inconsistency because subsequent police remand in April 2020 would then have occurred long after the initial statutory period in which such custody could ordinarily be authorised. The respondent further relied upon judicial decisions distinguishing a person’s actual deprivation of liberty from detention authorised by a competent Magistrate for purposes of statutory remand and default bail. The Supreme Court undertook an extensive examination of the constitutional and statutory framework governing arrest, detention, remand and investigation. It discussed Articles 21 and 22 of the Constitution of India, which safeguard personal liberty, require communication of grounds of arrest and mandate production before a Magistrate within the constitutionally prescribed period. It analysed Sections 41, 48, 56, 57, 76, 77, 156, 157 and 167 of the CrPC, together with provisions governing further judicial proceedings and remand. The Court explained that a transit remand is traceable to Section 167 and that a Magistrate who does not possess jurisdiction to try the case may nevertheless exercise statutory authority in relation to the detention and production of an arrested person, subject to the conditions prescribed by law. The judgment also considered the relationship between habeas corpus proceedings and judicial orders of remand. A critical distinction was drawn between lawful custody pursuant to judicial authorisation and detention resulting from an order that is absolutely illegal, without jurisdiction or passed mechanically. The Court recognised that an otherwise valid remand order cannot ordinarily be challenged through habeas corpus merely because the accused disputes the grounds of detention, but exceptional circumstances may justify intervention where the remand itself suffers from fundamental illegality or jurisdictional defects. Its analysis reinforced that orders affecting physical liberty must have a recognised legal foundation and that the requirements of statutory remand cannot be dispensed with merely because a superior court has exercised interim jurisdiction to protect an individual. On the question of default bail, the Supreme Court acknowledged that the right arising upon expiry of the prescribed statutory period is an important protection of personal liberty and has been recognised as having constitutional significance. However, it emphasised that the right arises only when the statutory conditions are fulfilled. The relevant detention must be custody under Section 167, and the court order must be referable to that statutory power. The Supreme Court examined the circumstances in which the appellant had originally been placed under house arrest and noted that the arrangement arose in the context of proceedings seeking protection against arrest and an independent investigation. The orders of the Delhi High Court and Supreme Court had operated to protect the appellant and other persons from ordinary physical incarceration while restricting their movements. Those courts had not purported to exercise the statutory power of remand under Section 167 when directing the house arrest arrangement. The Supreme Court accepted that the appellant’s liberty had in fact been curtailed for 34 days and that setting aside the initial transit remand could not erase that historical deprivation. Nevertheless, actual deprivation of liberty was not by itself sufficient to establish detention under Section 167 for computing default bail. Since the house arrest orders in the appellant’s case lacked the necessary statutory character, the period could not be counted towards the statutory detention period. The Court therefore rejected the contention that the earlier house arrest necessarily generated an entitlement to default bail when combined with subsequent custody in 2020. An important independent aspect of the judgment was the Court’s recognition that house arrest can legally constitute custody under Section 167 CrPC. The Court noted that custody under Section 167 had traditionally been understood in terms of police custody and judicial custody, with judicial custody ordinarily being associated with detention in prison. However, the statutory language did not compel the conclusion that house arrest must always remain outside the scope of permissible custody. After examining the nature of a house arrest order involving substantial restrictions on movement, communication and freedom, the Court concluded that such custody could fall within Section 167 where it was ordered under the relevant statutory authority. It observed that courts may, in appropriate and deserving cases, order house arrest instead of conventional detention, having regard to factors including the age of the accused, health condition, antecedents, nature of the alleged crime, necessity for other forms of custody and the practical ability to enforce the conditions imposed. It further recognised that house arrest could be considered under Section 309 CrPC in suitable cases involving judicial custody during subsequent stages of criminal proceedings. The Court referred to the problems of overcrowding in prisons, the financial burden of maintaining prisoners and the need to consider suitable alternatives to conventional incarceration. The judgment nevertheless did not establish an unrestricted entitlement of an accused to demand house arrest. Rather, it recognised judicial discretion to employ this form of custody subject to the relevant statutory requirements, case-specific considerations and enforceable safeguards. Ultimately, the Supreme Court dismissed Gautam Navlakha’s appeal and upheld the refusal to include the 34-day house arrest period from 28 August 2018 to 1 October 2018 in calculating the statutory period for default bail. The decisive consideration was not that house arrest could never amount to custody, but that the particular orders directing the appellant’s house arrest had not been passed, or purported to have been passed, under Section 167 CrPC. The Court clarified that a historical deprivation of personal liberty and a period of custody qualifying for statutory default-bail computation are related but legally distinct concepts. It simultaneously acknowledged that properly authorised house arrest may constitute custody within Section 167 and may be employed by courts as an alternative to conventional detention in suitable circumstances. The Supreme Court also indicated that house arrest could be considered under Section 309 in deserving cases and left the possible use of house arrest in post-conviction situations for legislative consideration. The appeal was accordingly dismissed without any order as to costs. The ruling is significant for its treatment of the statutory basis of remand, the constitutional importance of personal liberty, the computation of detention for default bail and the permissible use of house arrest as a judicially supervised form of custody. Its operative distinction is that house arrest may qualify as statutory custody when lawfully ordered under the appropriate provision, but the appellant’s particular period of house arrest did not qualify for default-bail computation. Cases Discussed / Relied Upon 1. Romila Thapar and Others Vs Union of India and Others (Supreme Court); (2018) 10 SCC 753; 28.09.2018. The Supreme Court examined the proceedings concerning the arrests of five activists and the directions granting and extending house arrest. The majority declined to order an independent investigation, while Justice D.Y. Chandrachud dissented. The orders passed in those proceedings formed an important part of the factual and legal background of the present appeal. 2. Chaganti Satyanarayana and Others Vs State of Andhra Pradesh (Supreme Court); (1986) 3 SCC 141. This decision was considered on the commencement of the statutory detention period under Section 167 CrPC. The principle that the statutory period is reckoned with reference to remand rather than merely the earlier physical arrest was relevant to determining whether the appellant’s 2018 house arrest could be included in the computation of default bail. 3. Bikramjit Singh Vs State of Punjab (Supreme Court); Criminal Appeal No. 667 of 2020; 12.10.2020; 2020 SCC OnLine SC 824. The Supreme Court considered the constitutional importance of default bail under Section 167(2), including the operation of the statutory right when the investigating agency fails to complete its investigation within the prescribed period. This precedent was relevant to the right to personal liberty and the conditions governing default bail under special criminal legislation. 4. M. Ravindran Vs Intelligence Officer (Supreme Court); Criminal Appeal No. 699 of 2020; 26.10.2020; (2021) 2 SCC 485. This decision addresses the statutory right to default bail, the consequences of filing an application after expiry of the prescribed period and the limits on defeating an accrued right by subsequent prosecution action. The principles are relevant to the broader discussion of default bail and personal liberty. 5. Manubhai Ratilal Patel Through Ushaben Vs State of Gujarat and Others (Supreme Court); (2013) 1 SCC 314. The Supreme Court examined the judicial character of remand orders and the requirement that a Magistrate exercise independent judicial satisfaction before authorising detention. The judgment was relevant to the distinction between custody authorised by a competent court and detention suffering from fundamental legal defects. 6. Serious Fraud Investigation Office Vs Rahul Modi and Another (Supreme Court); Criminal Appeal Nos. 538–539 of 2019. The Court considered this judgment in connection with judicial remand and the availability of habeas corpus when detention is based on an order passed by a competent Magistrate. The precedent helps distinguish irregularities in remand proceedings from orders that are entirely without jurisdiction or fundamentally illegal. 7. Central Bureau of Investigation, Special Investigation Cell Vs Anupam J. Kulkarni (Supreme Court); (1992) 3 SCC 141. This judgment concerned the interpretation of Section 167 CrPC, particularly the relationship between police custody and judicial custody and the period during which police remand may be authorised. The decision was relevant to the appellant’s submission that different periods of custody could be combined for calculating statutory detention and to the respondent’s argument about the later police remand. 8. Lalita Kumari Vs Government of Uttar Pradesh and Others (Supreme Court); (2014) 2 SCC 1. The Constitution Bench decision was discussed in the context of the duty to register an FIR where information discloses a cognisable offence. It formed part of the Supreme Court’s wider discussion of the statutory framework governing registration of offences, investigation and arrest. 9. State of Punjab Vs Ajaib Singh (Supreme Court); AIR 1953 SC 10. This decision was considered in the discussion concerning arrest, detention and constitutional protections. It was relevant to examining the scope of safeguards against deprivation of liberty and the legal significance of different forms of restraint. 10. Maneka Gandhi Vs Union of India (Supreme Court); (1978) 1 SCC 248. The Supreme Court referred to the constitutional understanding of personal liberty under Article 21. The ruling is relevant to the principle that procedure restricting personal liberty must conform to constitutional standards of fairness, justice and reasonableness. 11. Niranjan Singh and Another Vs Prabhakar Rajaram Kharote and Others (Supreme Court); (1980) 2 SCC 559. The decision was examined in connection with the meaning of custody and the legal consequences of an accused submitting to the authority of the court. It was relevant to distinguishing custody arising from surrender or judicial control from other forms of physical restraint. 12. State of Maharashtra Vs Tasneem Rizwan Siddiquee (Supreme Court); (2018) 9 SCC 745. This precedent was relevant to the availability of habeas corpus where a person is detained pursuant to an operative judicial remand order. The Court examined the distinction between an ordinary challenge to a judicial remand and exceptional situations in which the detention or remand is fundamentally unlawful.

The petitioner challenged the Bank of Baroda Review Committee’s order dated 23 March 2023, which confirmed the Identification Committee’s decision dated 19 August 2022. The principal controversy concerned allegations that Moser Baer India Limited (MBIL), a company with which the petitioner had previously been associated, had invested substantial amounts in subsidiaries and related entities, thereby allegedly diverting or siphoning funds. The Court examined whether the investments could be characterised as diversion of borrowed funds under Clauses 2.1.3(b) and (c) of the RBI Master Circular dated 1 July 2015, whether the petitioner could be held responsible for transactions and write-offs occurring after his resignation, and whether the Bank’s decision was supported by objective evidence. The Court held that diversion or siphoning under the invoked provisions required proof that borrowed funds had been used for purposes other than those for which the finance was sanctioned. The Bank had failed to establish that essential jurisdictional fact. The Court further found that the relevant investments had been disclosed to the consortium lenders during corporate debt restructuring and that the forensic audit relied upon by the Bank did not verify the source of the investment funds. Consequently, the Review Committee’s order declaring the petitioner a wilful defaulter was quashed and set aside. The dispute arose from the financial difficulties of MBIL, a company established by the petitioner’s father, Deepak Puri, in 1983. MBIL initially developed a substantial business in optical storage products, including CDs and DVDs, before diversifying into solar technology through subsidiaries such as Moser Baer Photo Voltaic Limited and Moser Baer Solar Limited. According to the petitioner’s case, developments in technology, changing market conditions, the global financial crisis and competition from overseas solar manufacturers adversely affected the group’s financial performance. MBIL had obtained substantial financial assistance from Bank of Baroda and other consortium lenders. The petitioner maintained that disagreements concerning business strategy had led him to decide to withdraw from the group in 2010. However, because of obligations under an earlier shareholders’ agreement involving Warburg Pincus Group, his formal exit occurred later. He resigned as Executive Director on 30 April 2012, ceased to be a shareholder and resigned from the company’s Board on 16 November 2012. These events were supported by filings made with the Registrar of Companies. The petitioner argued that he could not be retrospectively held responsible for subsequent transactions or defaults merely because he had previously served as a director. The High Court examined this defence in the context of the RBI framework for identifying wilful default, which requires consideration of the nature of the default, relevant evidence and the conduct attributable to the individual concerned. As MBIL’s financial position deteriorated, its lenders considered restructuring the outstanding debt under the Corporate Debt Restructuring (CDR) mechanism. A Joint Lenders’ Meeting was held on 3 February 2012, during which MBIL disclosed its financial difficulties and proposed restructuring. The company submitted a Flash Report identifying, among other factors, its inability to realise investments made in subsidiaries. The lenders obtained a Techno-Economic Viability Report from Ernst & Young and a Stock Audit Report from RRCA & Associates. On 20 July 2012, the CDR-Empowered Group approved a Final Restructuring Scheme after considering these materials. MBIL was classified in Category B under the applicable CDR framework, a classification associated with external business difficulties and management weaknesses rather than the categories specifically addressing diversion of funds. A Master Restructuring Agreement was subsequently executed on 27 December 2012, and the lenders implemented a restructuring arrangement. The petitioner emphasised that the consortium banks, including Bank of Baroda, were aware of MBIL’s investments in subsidiaries when restructuring was approved. He argued that the relevant financial statements and restructuring records disclosed that the investments had been financed from internal accruals rather than borrowed funds. The Court attached substantial significance to this material because wilful default based on diversion or siphoning requires examination of the source, purpose and deployment of borrowed finance. The earlier CDR records therefore formed an important part of the evidentiary assessment of the Bank’s later allegations. MBIL’s restructuring ultimately failed, and the company continued to face repayment difficulties. Other lenders had previously initiated proceedings against the petitioner. Punjab National Bank issued a show-cause notice in October 2015 but subsequently informed the Delhi High Court in November 2017 that it was not pursuing action against him. State Bank of Bikaner & Jaipur issued a separate notice in December 2015 but dropped the proceedings in May 2016 after considering the petitioner’s explanation. Thereafter, corporate insolvency proceedings were initiated against MBIL under the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016, and a forensic audit was conducted by GSA Associates, Chartered Accountants. The report was dated 3 June 2019. Bank of Baroda issued a show-cause notice on 13 March 2020 proposing to declare the petitioner a wilful defaulter on six allegations. The principal surviving allegation concerned investments in subsidiaries and related entities amounting to approximately Rs.1,586.75 crore as on 31 December 2013, together with provisions and write-offs of approximately Rs.287.03 crore during 1 April 2013 to 31 March 2015. The petitioner responded that these allegations did not establish diversion of borrowed funds and that the later write-offs occurred after his resignation. The Identification Committee eventually rejected five allegations but sustained the first, treating the investments as diversion of funds. The Review Committee confirmed that decision on 23 March 2023, resulting in the writ petition before the Delhi High Court. The petitioner contended that the Bank’s decision was legally unsustainable because the RBI Master Circular did not permit a person to be classified as a wilful defaulter merely because a company had made unsuccessful investments in its subsidiaries. The relevant clauses required evidence that loan funds had been diverted from their sanctioned purpose or siphoned off. According to the petitioner, MBIL’s investments were made from internal accruals and cash surpluses, which he claimed exceeded Rs.2,700 crore during the relevant years. He relied upon audited financial statements, restructuring documents and communications with the consortium lenders to demonstrate that the investments had been disclosed and considered before approval of the CDR package. The petitioner also argued that the Bank had failed to address his principal defence and had relied excessively upon his earlier position as a whole-time director. The allegation concerning write-offs during 2013–2015 was particularly disputed because he had ceased to be a director in November 2012. The petitioner further submitted that the forensic audit had been supplied only after issuance of the show-cause notice and had not established any specific diversion attributable to him. He invoked principles of natural justice, reasoned decision-making and judicial review, contending that the Bank could not sustain a finding with serious commercial consequences without establishing the necessary facts and addressing the material placed in his defence. Bank of Baroda defended the declaration, submitting that the Identification Committee and Review Committee had followed the procedure prescribed by the RBI and considered the petitioner’s explanations. The Bank argued that the petitioner had exercised substantial control over MBIL while the investments in subsidiaries were made and that his resignation did not absolve him from responsibility for conduct occurring during his tenure. It maintained that the forensic audit disclosed diversion of funds and that the Court should exercise restraint when reviewing technical and commercial assessments made by financial institutions. The Bank also contended that there was no express limitation period under the Master Circular for initiating wilful-defaulter proceedings and that it had acted after obtaining information through the forensic audit. Reliance was placed upon the regulatory objective of preventing persons responsible for deliberate defaults from accessing further institutional finance. The High Court acknowledged the importance of protecting public funds and maintaining discipline in the banking system. However, it distinguished the public purpose underlying the RBI framework from the requirement that each individual classification must comply with the applicable statutory and regulatory conditions. The Court emphasised that judicial restraint does not exclude scrutiny of jurisdictional errors, arbitrary conclusions or failure to consider material evidence. Accordingly, the Bank’s assertion that the matter involved technical financial assessments did not prevent the Court from examining whether the fundamental condition of diversion or siphoning of borrowed funds had been established. The Delhi High Court examined the RBI Master Circular dated 1 July 2015, particularly Clauses 2.1.3(b) and (c), 2.2.1 and 2.2.2. Clause 2.1.3(b) concerned default accompanied by diversion of finance obtained from a lender, while Clause 2.1.3(c) concerned default accompanied by siphoning of funds. Clause 2.2 explained the circumstances in which diversion and siphoning could arise, including deployment of borrowed funds for purposes inconsistent with the sanction, transfer of borrowed funds to subsidiaries or group companies and use of borrowed funds for unrelated operations. The Court held that the language of these provisions made the nature of the funds fundamental to the inquiry. Investment of a company’s internally generated funds in its subsidiaries could not, without proof of diversion of borrowed finance, satisfy the particular grounds invoked by the Bank. The Court also emphasised that wilful default must be assessed on the borrower’s track record and must be intentional, deliberate and calculated rather than inferred from isolated transactions or subsequent business failure. Referring to State Bank of India Vs Jah Developers Pvt. Ltd., the High Court recognised that declaration as a wilful defaulter has serious consequences for the affected person’s right to conduct business, reputation and access to finance. It therefore held that the regulatory framework must be applied reasonably, with due regard to relevant facts and the requirement of objective satisfaction. On examining the documentary evidence, the High Court found significant deficiencies in the Bank’s conclusion. The petitioner had formally resigned from MBIL in 2012, and the Bank was aware of his exit during the restructuring process. The consortium lenders had also examined MBIL’s financial condition, reviewed disclosures concerning investments in subsidiaries and approved the restructuring package after obtaining independent viability and stock audit reports. The Court considered the Bank’s attempt to rely upon the forensic audit dated 3 June 2019. Importantly, the forensic auditors had expressly stated that the source of funds for investments made in subsidiaries, associates and joint ventures had not been verified because those investments preceded the period covered by their review. The Court held that this limitation was decisive. The report did not establish that the investments had been funded through borrowed money, nor did it identify diversion or siphoning attributable to the petitioner. Consequently, the Bank could not rely upon that report as sufficient evidence of the central allegation. The Court also considered the principle recognised in Mohinder Singh Gill Vs Chief Election Commissioner that an administrative or statutory order must ordinarily be judged by the reasons stated in it and cannot subsequently be justified through additional explanations in affidavits. Although the High Court examined the forensic audit because the respondent was a public-sector bank dealing with public funds, it concluded that the report itself did not support the finding. The Court therefore found that the Bank had failed to establish the necessary factual foundation for invoking the wilful-defaulter provisions. In its final analysis, the Delhi High Court examined the consequences of MBIL’s admission to the CDR mechanism. It noted that the restructuring framework contained safeguards intended to prevent borrowers involved in fraud, malfeasance or diversion of funds from improperly obtaining restructuring benefits. The participating lenders were empowered to commission forensic audits and to classify borrowers according to the nature and seriousness of their financial difficulties. MBIL had been placed in Category B after the lenders reviewed its financial disclosures and the reports obtained during restructuring. The lenders were aware of the investments in subsidiaries and had treated those investments as strategic business investments, rather than proven diversion of borrowed funds. The Court observed that lenders administering public money must make appropriate inquiries to distinguish genuine financial distress from fraud or deliberate misuse of finance. However, where the relevant documents failed to establish diversion or siphoning of borrowed funds, a retrospective declaration of wilful default could not be sustained merely because the business subsequently failed. The Court accordingly held that the reasons recorded by the Review Committee in its order dated 23 March 2023 were unsustainable. It quashed and set aside the Review Committee’s order confirming Ratul Puri as a wilful defaulter and allowed the writ petition. The ruling emphasises that serious regulatory consequences require an objective evidentiary foundation, that investment losses do not automatically establish wilful default, and that findings concerning diversion or siphoning must be linked to borrowed funds and attributable conduct. The decision was rendered under the RBI Master Circular of 2015 applicable to the proceedings and must be distinguished from the requirements of subsequently issued regulatory directions.

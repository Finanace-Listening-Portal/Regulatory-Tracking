Summary: The Allahabad High Court’s decision in M/S Ast Pipes Limited v. Union of India examines the distinction between the power to rectify an error apparent on the face of the record and the limitation applicable to rectification under Section 161 of the CGST Act, 2017. The petitioner challenged the rejection of an application seeking correction of a discrepancy between an ex parte Order in Original and the consequential DRC-07, which reflected a higher tax demand. The petitioner sought a reduction of Rs. 3,17,414. The adjudicating authority rejected the application on the ground that the discrepancy was not a patent clerical or arithmetical mistake arising from an accidental slip or omission. The High Court distinguished the substantive power to rectify errors apparent from the record from the limitation exception contained in the second proviso to Section 161. It held that the discrepancy between the original order and the DRC-07 constituted an apparent error and that the application was covered by the limitation exception. The judgment highlights the importance of examining the original adjudication order, consequential demand and payment adjustments together. However, the Court did not decide the challenge to the underlying assessment and demand themselves. The decision therefore provides guidance on the scope and timing of rectification without treating Section 161 as a mechanism for reopening disputed questions concerning substantive tax liability. The DRC-07 showed a larger tax demand than the Order in Original’s operative portion. The petitioner sought correction of an arithmetical mistake by a reduction of Rs. 3,17,414, and filed the application on 21 May 2026. The adjudicating authority rejected the application by an order dated 10 September 2026, with a summary issued on 11 September 2026. It reasoned that the petitioner had not pointed to any patent, obvious and self-evident error, and that the discrepancy was not a clerical or arithmetical slip from an accidental omission. The authority therefore treated the application as falling outside the second proviso. Two Questions, Not One Section 161 contains two distinct elements. The first is the power itself: an adjudicating authority may rectify “any error which is apparent on the face of record.” The second is timing: rectification ordinarily must be made within six months, but the second proviso removes that limit where the rectification is purely a clerical or arithmetical error arising from an accidental slip or omission. The authority in Ast Pipes collapsed these into one inquiry. It required the error to be both apparent on the record and a clerical slip before it would act. The High Court disagreed. On limitation, the Court held that the claim fell within the second proviso, so the application was in time. On the power to rectify, the Court held that once the authority itself accepted that the Order in Original and the DRC-07 disagreed, that discrepancy was an error apparent on the face of the record. The statutory phrase does not require a typographical mistake. The Court also noted that the Order in Original recorded adjustment of certain sums paid under section 74 read with section 121, and observed that those amounts may never have been reflected in the DRC-07. The point is significant. The proviso decides when a correction may be sought. It does not decide whether the error is correctable at all. Why the Distinction Matters Treating the proviso as the test for correctability has practical consequences. An authority that asks whether a mistake is clerical before considering whether it is apparent from the record may refuse correction of a genuine mismatch that is not a typing error. Conversely, a taxpayer who thinks the six-month limit has lapsed may abandon a claim that the proviso would save. The better approach is to ask three questions in sequence: Is the error apparent on the face of the record, meaning it can be identified by comparing documents already on file without a contested inquiry into the merits? Does the rectification alter the substance of the decision, or only bring the consequential document into line with the order? If the claim is out of time, is the error purely clerical or arithmetical, arising from an accidental slip or omission, so that the second proviso applies? Ast Pipes answers the first and third questions in the taxpayer’s favour on its facts, because the Order in Original itself recorded the tax figure that the DRC-07 departed from. The Limits of the Decision The judgment should be read with care. It dealt with the Section 161 orders only. The petition also sought to quash the ex parte Order in Original and the DRC-07 themselves, and the Court did not decide that relief. A practitioner cannot rely on the case as having set aside the underlying demand. The Court did not discuss whether a statutory appeal lay against the original order, or whether that availability should have shaped the choice of remedy. A revenue party may raise this objection in later proceedings, and the decision does not answer it. The holding is also fact dependent. The strength of the argument came from the adjudicating authority’s own acknowledgement that the operative portion and the consequential demand were out of step. Where the mismatch is less visible, or where correcting it would change the liability and not merely align the documents, the revenue will argue that rectification has been used to reopen the merits. That argument has real force, and the taxpayer should expect it. Practical Points For taxpayers and practitioners facing a mismatch between an order and its consequential demand, four steps follow from the decision: Compare the operative portion of the order line by line with the DRC-07, and identify the exact figures that differ. Reconcile the payment ledger against both documents. Amounts recorded as paid or adjusted in the order but missing from the DRC-07 are strong evidence of an apparent error. Frame the application around the record, avoiding arguments that require the authority to decide disputed questions of fact or law. Address limitation separately and early. If the application is outside six months, state the second proviso and explain why the error is clerical or arithmetical. Conclusion Ast Pipes is a useful corrective. It confirms that Section 161 is not confined to typing errors, and that a discrepancy between an order and its consequential demand can be an error apparent on the face of the record. It also keeps the limitation proviso in its proper place as a question of timing. The decision does not resolve every objection the revenue may raise, and its reach will depend on how other benches apply it. Practitioners should cite it for what it holds, verify the official text before relying on it, and anticipate the arguments that will test its limits.

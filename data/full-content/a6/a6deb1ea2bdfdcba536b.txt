Summary : Ind AS 2 requires inventory to be measured at the lower of cost and net realisable value, but a defensible year-end test is more than a spreadsheet comparison. Management must identify the correct unit of account, estimate the ordinary-course selling price, include completion and selling costs, distinguish firm-contract quantities from excess stock, assess materials through the expected economics of finished goods, and revisit earlier write-downs. This guide turns those requirements into a repeatable evidence file without confusing entity-specific NRV with market-based fair value. Key Takeaways Show all 18 sections Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); NRV is an entity-specific recovery estimate Net realisable value is the estimated selling price in the ordinary course of business less estimated costs of completion and costs necessary to make the sale. It is not automatically the latest list price, a distressed clearance quote, fair value or replacement cost. The estimate reflects how the entity expects to realise the inventory, supported by observable orders, post-reporting-date sales, price lists, ageing, condition, customer terms and completion plans. The lower-of-cost-and-NRV comparison normally operates item by item. Grouping is appropriate only for similar or related items in the same product line that have similar purposes or end uses, are produced and marketed in the same geographical area, and cannot practicably be evaluated separately. A blanket percentage provision across an operating segment may conceal recoverable and impaired items and does not reproduce the standard’s test. NRV component Preferred evidence Common error Estimated selling price Firm orders, recent ordinary-course sales, approved price list Using gross list price despite routine discounts Costs of completion Bill of materials, labour plan, subcontract estimate Ignoring remaining conversion or testing cost Costs necessary to sell Incremental freight, commission, packaging, levies Deducting broad overheads with no sale nexus Unit of account SKU or justified related group Offsetting losses with unrelated profitable stock Reporting-date condition Ageing, damage reports, later confirming sales Using later events that reflect wholly new conditions Separate contract quantities from uncommitted stock Firm sales contracts anchor the relevant price Inventory held to satisfy a firm sales contract is tested using the contract price for the committed quantity, after considering completion and sale costs. If inventory exceeds the contracted quantity, the excess is tested using general selling prices. This split prevents a favourable contract from sheltering surplus stock or a low contract price from depressing unrelated units. The contract register, cancellation terms and customer credit position should be part of the working paper. Loss-making purchase or sales commitments may involve Ind AS 37 A commitment can create an obligation that is not solved solely by writing inventory down. Firm purchase or sales contracts in excess of inventory quantities may require an onerous-contract assessment under Ind AS 37. The inventory team should therefore flag unavoidable commitment losses to the provisions workstream and avoid double counting the same shortfall in both inventory and a provision. Treat raw materials through finished-goods economics Materials and supplies are not written below cost merely because their current purchase price has fallen when the finished goods in which they will be incorporated are expected to sell at or above cost. If the decline indicates that finished-goods cost will exceed finished-goods NRV, the materials are written down; replacement cost may be the best available measure of their NRV in that circumstance. The analysis therefore links raw-material tests to current recipes, yields, conversion cost and finished-goods margins. Obsolete components require a different emphasis. If a material can no longer be used in an active product, management should evaluate alternative use, sale as spare, scrap value and disposal cost. A generic expectation that the component may be used someday is not evidence. Engineering change notices, last-use history and approved production forecasts can establish whether future consumption is realistic. Use later sales only when they illuminate the reporting-date condition Sales after the reporting date often provide persuasive evidence of the amount inventory could realise, but the date and cause matter. A sale shortly after year-end under ordinary terms may confirm conditions existing at year-end. A price collapse caused by a new event after year-end may instead be a non-adjusting event under Ind AS 10, although disclosure may be required. The NRV file should record both the transaction date and why it confirms, or does not confirm, the reporting-date estimate. When the circumstances causing an earlier write-down no longer exist, or clear evidence shows an increase in NRV because economic circumstances changed, the write-down is reversed. The reversal is limited to the original write-down, so the new carrying amount remains the lower of original cost and revised NRV. It is recognised as a reduction in the amount of inventories recognised as expense in the period of reversal. The reversal working should preserve the original cost, original write-down, written-down carrying amount, revised NRV and permitted reversal. Without that history, entities may accidentally write inventory above cost or reverse a loss relating to units already sold. Controls should track batches or SKUs through disposal and distinguish a true reversal from a change in cost allocation for new production. A compact numerical control illustration Assume an item originally cost ₹1,000. At year one, expected selling price is ₹930, remaining completion cost is ₹40 and sale cost is ₹20, giving NRV of ₹870 and a write-down of ₹130. At year two, the item remains on hand and revised NRV is ₹960. The maximum reversal is ₹90: this raises the carrying amount from ₹870 to ₹960, still below original cost. If revised NRV were ₹1,080, the reversal would be capped at ₹130 and carrying amount at ₹1,000. Exception reports should identify slow-moving items, negative margins, stock without recent sales, damaged quantities, products affected by engineering or regulatory change, and items with large post-year-end price movements. Review thresholds can prioritise work, but immateriality should be assessed in aggregate and with qualitative factors, including covenant, bonus and trend effects. Ind AS 2 disclosures include the amount of write-down recognised as expense, the amount of reversal recognised as a reduction of inventory expense, and the circumstances or events leading to reversal. Material judgements and estimation uncertainty may also trigger Ind AS 1 disclosures. The note should be traceable to the approved NRV register and journal entries, with no unexplained difference between gross provision movements and financial-statement presentation. Frequently Asked Questions 1. Can inventory be tested by broad category? Usually the comparison is item by item. Grouping is limited to similar or related items with the specified product-line, end-use, geography and practical interdependence characteristics; an entire segment is not an acceptable default unit. 2. Is NRV the same as fair value less costs to sell? No. NRV is an entity-specific estimate in the ordinary course of business. Fair value is a market-participant exit price. They may differ even when both deduct sale-related costs. 3. Must raw materials be written down when purchase prices fall? Not automatically. Materials are generally retained at cost if finished goods are expected to sell at or above cost. A write-down is considered when falling material prices indicate finished-goods cost will exceed finished-goods NRV. 4. Can post-year-end invoices be used? Yes, when they provide evidence about conditions existing at the reporting date and are comparable in quantity and terms. New-condition events require separate Ind AS 10 analysis. 5. Can a prior write-down be reversed through other income? Ind AS 2 describes the reversal as a reduction in the amount of inventories recognised as expense in the period. Presentation should be consistent with that requirement and the entity’s financial-statement format. 6. May a reversal lift inventory above original cost? No. The reversal is capped at the original write-down. The revised carrying amount is the lower of original cost and revised NRV. 7. What evidence best supports costs necessary to sell? Use sale-specific and supportable amounts such as contractual commission, incremental freight, packaging or levies. Broad administrative allocations should not be deducted merely because they exist. Key Takeaways Calculate NRV as ordinary-course selling price less completion and necessary selling costs. Test item by item unless a narrow related-item grouping is justified. Link raw-material impairment to finished-goods economics and current production evidence.

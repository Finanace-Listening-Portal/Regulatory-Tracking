Summary: From 1 October 2026, the Foreign Exchange Management (Export and Import of Goods and Services) Regulations, 2026, as amended up to 22 September 2026, provide a revised framework for tracking export receivables through the Export Data Processing and Monitoring System (EDPMS). Regulation 4(2) allows an Authorised Dealer (AD) to close an EDPMS entry for a goods shipping bill or services invoice of up to ₹10 lakh, or foreign-currency equivalent, on the exporter’s declaration that payment has been realised in full or otherwise; the exporter may also furnish quarterly declarations for bulk closure. Regulation 6 separately empowers the AD to permit reduction or non-realisation of export value, with a declaration-based route for exports within the same per-shipping-bill or per-invoice ceiling. These are bank-controlled reporting and realisation processes, not an automatic waiver of every receivable or of GST, income-tax and accounting obligations. The amended Regulation 5 generally prescribes nine months for realisation and repatriation, extended to twelve months where exports are invoiced and/or settled in Indian Rupees; project exports follow contractual payment terms and AD banks may extend time on justified requests. Service exporters must also consider the new Export Declaration Form (EDF) requirements and EDPMS reporting. This article distinguishes closure, reduction, payment receipt, GST refund recovery, foreign-exchange differences, bad-debt assessment and audit evidence so exporters do not mistake a system status for conclusive proof of commercial recovery or tax compliance. The official RBI consolidated notification expressly states that it is amended up to 22 September 2026. It covers goods, services, software, trade advances, third-party payments, set-off, merchanting trade and AD-bank reporting. Its Regulation 4(2) is especially relevant to small-value EDPMS entries that otherwise remain outstanding because of missing remittance mapping, small balances or commercial disputes. A critical date check is necessary. The January text originally mentioned 15 months for ordinary exports and 18 months for rupee-invoiced or rupee-settled exports. RBI Notification No. FEMA 23(R)/(1)/2026-RB dated 22 September 2026 substituted nine months and twelve months before the new regulations commenced. An October 2026 article that still repeats 15 and 18 months as current law is inaccurate. TaxGuru’s October 2026 FEMA export-import analysis describes the corrected framework. Regulation 4(2): The ₹10 Lakh Declaration Route An AD bank must satisfy itself about the genuineness of an export receipt or import payment and simultaneously update or close the relevant EDPMS/IDPMS entry. The first proviso to Regulation 4(2) permits EDPMS closure on an exporter declaration where the shipping bill for goods or invoice for services is up to ₹10 lakh, or its equivalent in foreign currency. The declaration states that payment against the identified shipping bill or invoice has been realised in full or otherwise. The exporter can alternatively provide a quarterly declaration for bulk closure of such entries. The ceiling applies to the shipping bill or services invoice, not automatically to the net unpaid balance of a larger invoice. For example, a ₹14 lakh export invoice with only ₹50,000 still outstanding is not transformed into an invoice “up to ₹10 lakh” merely because the balance is small. Conversely, a ₹9.5 lakh invoice can fall within the declaration route even if the amount received is less than the invoice, provided the bank accepts the declaration and all other applicable rules are met. Closure is not identical to realisation EDPMS is a regulatory monitoring system. A closed entry may be supported by actual inward remittance, permitted set-off, an accepted reduction in export value, or the specified declaration route. Closure should not be described as proof that the exporter collected every rupee, extinguished every civil claim or earned an income-tax deduction. The bank may require evidence under its internal policy and remains responsible for the regulatory reporting it undertakes. Its proviso allows reduction, including full non-realisation, based on an exporter declaration where export value is up to ₹10 lakh per goods shipping bill or services invoice. This is a separate legal power from the EDPMS reporting simplification under Regulation 4(2). A proper working paper should identify which route is being invoked. Reasons may include documented commercial claims, defective goods, price adjustments, discounts, quality disputes or recovery failure. The regulation does not make an unsupported statement of “buyer has not paid” a substitute for all evidence. The bank can examine commercial correspondence, credit notes, contracts, recovery efforts and whether the proposed adjustment is genuine. A civil claim can remain alive even when the bank accepts a reduction for FEMA purposes. Regulation 5: Nine Months, Twelve Months and Extensions For ordinary exports of goods, the amended period is nine months from shipment; for services including software it is nine months from invoice. For goods exported to an overseas warehouse, the nine months generally run from sale of the goods from that warehouse. If the export is invoiced and/or settled in Indian Rupees, the corresponding period is twelve months. Project exports follow the payment terms of the contract. These periods are stated in the official RBI consolidated text amended up to 22 September 2026. The AD may extend the period beyond the specified deadline on the exporter’s request citing reasons for delay, if satisfied. The exporter should make the request with evidence and should not assume that an EDPMS status change by itself retrospectively extends the due date. One consolidated EDF can cover multiple service recipients in a month. A non-software services exporter may instead submit an EDF on or before the receipt date, and the AD can extend filing time for reasonable delay. For goods exported through an EDI port, EDF is generally deemed submitted as part of the shipping bill. Under Regulation 18, the AD enters relevant EDF and import information into the monitoring systems within the specified working-day period, reports remittances and follows up for closure. Exporters should supply consistent invoice numbers, dates, customer names, shipping-bill identifiers, purpose codes and payment references. An accounting ERP may show an invoice as settled while EDPMS remains open because the bank did not receive a correct reference; the reverse can also occur when an entry was closed on declaration. A practical reconciliation method Prepare an invoice-wise register with invoice value, currency, shipment or invoice date, customer, AD bank, EDPMS reference, foreign-exchange receipt, approved reductions, GST invoice, credit note and ageing bucket. For each difference, label it as receipt pending, bank mapping pending, authorised reduction, set-off, disputed amount or write-off under review. This prevents finance teams from reporting all open entries as overdue cash and all closed entries as fully collected revenue. Set-off, Third-party Receipts and Small-value Settlements Regulation 7 permits an AD to allow set-off of export receivables against import payables involving the same overseas buyer or supplier, or their overseas group or associate companies, within the realisation period or approved extension. Regulation 8 permits third-party receipts and payments when the AD is satisfied about the bona fides. These are conditional bank-permitted routes; neither provision creates a general right to net unrelated receivables without documentation. A set-off schedule should show both underlying invoices, parties, legal relationships, currencies, exchange rates, consent and accounting entries. If an exporter receives payment from a different entity, the exporter should preserve the contractual or group basis and the AD’s acceptance. The receipt route may also affect GST evidence of export realisation and the audit trail supporting transfer-pricing or related-party documentation. GST Export Refunds and Recovery Risk GST and FEMA have related but different triggers. Export of services under the IGST Act includes conditions relating to receipt of payment in convertible foreign exchange or in Indian rupees wherever permitted by RBI, among other conditions. Export of goods and services can have different zero-rating and refund documentation requirements. Rule 96B of the CGST Rules contains recovery mechanics for specified refund cases where export proceeds are not realised within the period allowed under FEMA, subject to its terms and exceptions. An EDPMS closure on a declaration does not automatically establish that every GST export condition has been met or that a refund is immune from recovery. For services, examine whether payment was actually received in the permitted manner; for goods, check the relevant refund and export-incentive provisions. Approved reductions, genuine bad debts and RBI-permitted non-realisation may require separate GST legal analysis. TaxGuru’s discussion of FEMA realisation and GST export-benefit consequences provides background, but the current 22 September amendment must control the October 2026 deadline. Income Tax, Bad Debts and Foreign Exchange Accounting A reduction accepted by an AD bank does not, without more, determine taxable business income. A credit note may reduce revenue if recognised in accordance with the underlying contract and accounting framework; an irrecoverable receivable may require a bad-debt or impairment analysis. Income-tax deduction depends on the conditions in the applicable Income-tax Act, 2025 provisions and the accounting/tax history of the receivable. The finance team should not debit “FEMA write-off” as an automatic tax deduction without testing recognition and statutory conditions. AS 11 or Ind AS 21 applies to foreign-currency monetary receivables according to the entity’s reporting framework. A receivable denominated in foreign currency is ordinarily translated at the reporting-date exchange rate, with exchange differences recognised according to the relevant standard and applicable exceptions. A genuine settlement discount, bad debt and foreign-exchange gain or loss are three different items and should not be conflated. Rupee-invoiced exports may present different monetary-currency analysis. Controls for Exporters and Auditors Identify all October 2026 goods and services invoices, including new EDF obligations for services. Apply the ₹10 lakh ceiling to each shipping bill or services invoice, not merely to its unpaid balance. Record actual receipts, bank-accepted reductions and permitted set-offs in separate columns. Track nine-month or twelve-month deadlines and document any AD-approved extension. Reconcile EDPMS status to GST refund documents, receivable ledger and foreign-exchange gain/loss records. Retain the AD’s acceptance, declaration copy, invoice and supporting correspondence for audit. Frequently Asked Questions 1. Does the ₹10 lakh limit refer to the outstanding balance? No. Regulation 4(2) refers to the goods shipping bill or services invoice value up to ₹10 lakh, or foreign-currency equivalent. A larger invoice does not become eligible merely because its residual balance is below that amount. 2. Can an exporter submit one quarterly declaration for multiple invoices? The first proviso to Regulation 4(2) expressly permits a quarterly declaration for bulk EDPMS closure of qualifying entries. Each entry should remain traceable to its own shipping bill or invoice. 3. Does EDPMS closure prove that the buyer paid in full? No. The regulation allows declaration-based closure where the exporter states payment has been realised in full or otherwise. A bank system status must be reconciled with actual cash, approved reduction and other supporting facts. 4. What is the ordinary export realisation deadline in October 2026? Under Regulation 5 as amended on 22 September 2026, the general period is nine months; exports invoiced or settled in INR generally have twelve months. Project exports and authorised extensions have their own treatment. 5. Do service exporters have to file EDF? Regulation 3(2) generally requires an EDF within 30 days after the end of the invoice month, subject to the express alternative for non-software services and extension powers of the AD bank. 6. Can a bank accept a complete non-realisation declaration for an ₹8 lakh invoice? Regulation 6 contains a declaration-based route for reduction including full non-realisation up to ₹10 lakh per qualifying invoice or shipping bill, but the AD’s statutory role and the underlying facts remain relevant. 7. Does an EDPMS write-off automatically protect an IGST refund? No. GST zero-rating and refund recovery provisions have independent conditions. Payment receipt, the applicable FEMA period and any permitted exceptions require a separate legal check. 8. Is an AD-approved export reduction automatically deductible as bad debt? No. Book recognition, commercial evidence and the conditions of the Income-tax Act, 2025 must be considered separately. An EDPMS closure is not an income-tax assessment order. Key Takeaways Regulation 4(2) permits declaration-based EDPMS closure for qualifying export shipping bills or services invoices up to ₹10 lakh. Regulation 6 separately governs AD-bank approval of reduction or non-realisation; do not confuse it with system closure. The September 2026 amendment fixed the operative general realisation period at nine months, or twelve months for INR invoicing/settlement. Services exporters must review EDF obligations and invoice-wise EDPMS mapping.

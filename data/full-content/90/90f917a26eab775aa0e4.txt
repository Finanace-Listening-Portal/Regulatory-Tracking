SEO Description: GST Section 74(1) versus 74(9): When a wrong provision, limitation, hearing defects or lack of adjudication can invalidate a demand order.Summary: The article examines whether a GST adjudication order referring to Section 74(1) instead of Section 74(9) of the CGST Act, 2017 is legally sustainable. It explains that Section 74(1) governs issuance of a show-cause notice, whereas Section 74(9) authorises determination of tax, interest and penalty after considering the taxpayer’s representation. A mere incorrect statutory reference does not automatically invalidate an otherwise lawful order, particularly in view of Section 160 and the Supreme Court’s decision in N. Mani v. Sangeetha Theatre. However, an order may be challenged where the authority fails to consider the taxpayer’s reply, denies a mandatory personal hearing, exceeds the scope of the notice, lacks proper jurisdiction or issues the determination beyond the limitation prescribed under Section 74(10) read with Section 75(10). Referring to Metal N Strips and NKAS Services, the article emphasises the importance of reasoned adjudication and procedural safeguards. It further explains that failure to establish fraud, wilful misstatement or suppression may trigger redetermination under Section 75(2), subject to the limitation requirements of Section 73 and CBIC Circular No. 185/17/2022-GST. The article recommends separately challenging tax, interest, penalty, limitation and procedural defects before the First Appellate Authority under Section 107, rather than relying solely on an incorrect statutory citation.A wrong statutory reference may be curable. Failure to adjudicate, absence of jurisdiction and expiry of limitation are different matters. The notice places the department’s proposed case before the taxpayer. The order decides whether that case survives the taxpayer’s explanation, the evidence and the applicable law. Section 74 recognises this distinction expressly. Sub-section (1) requires the proper officer to serve a notice. Sub-section (9) requires the proper officer, after considering the representation, if any, to determine the amount of tax, interest and penalty due and issue an order. Sub-section (10), in turn, prescribes the time within which that order must be issued. Therefore, describing a final determination simply as an “order under Section 74(1)” is legally inaccurate. The correct statutory source of the adjudicatory determination is Section 74(9), read with the applicable provisions of Section 75. However, legal inaccuracy in the heading and legal invalidity of the entire demand are not necessarily the same thing. What the different provisions actually require Provision Statutory function Practical consequence Section 74(1) Service of a show-cause notice where the stipulated fraud-related conditions are attracted The taxpayer must be informed of the proposed demand and its factual and legal foundation. Section 74(2) Notice must be issued at least six months before the Section 74(10) outer deadline A notice issued too late raises a separate limitation objection. Section 74(9) Consideration of the representation and determination of tax, interest and penalty by an order The proposal must be adjudicated; it cannot simply be treated as an established liability. Section 74(10) Ordinarily, five years from the annual-return due date for the relevant financial year, or from the erroneous-refund date Timely initiation does not dispense with timely completion. Section 75(4) Opportunity of hearing where requested in writing or where an adverse decision is contemplated Hearing is a statutory safeguard, not merely an administrative courtesy. Section 75(6) The order must state the relevant facts and the basis of the decision A conclusion without the supporting reasoning does not satisfy the statutory requirement. Section 75(7) The order cannot exceed the notice or confirm demand on different grounds Adjudication must remain within the case the taxpayer was called upon to answer. Section 75(10) Proceedings are deemed concluded if the order is not issued within the prescribed period The consequence of missing the statutory deadline is expressly provided by the Act. These are separate requirements. Compliance with one does not excuse non-compliance with the others. A period-related qualification is also necessary: Section 74 now applies to determination of tax pertaining to periods up to FY 2023–24. Proceedings for subsequent periods require examination under the Section 74A framework and the applicable corresponding State provisions. The date of the order alone does not determine which demand provision applies. Does mentioning Section 74(1) automatically invalidate the order? Not necessarily. Section 160(1) protects proceedings against invalidity merely because of a mistake, defect or omission, provided the proceedings are, in substance and effect, in conformity with the intent, purpose and requirements of the Act. The protection is conditional; it is not a general exemption from statutory compliance. The Supreme Court’s decision in N. Mani v. Sangeetha Theatre , (2004) 12 SCC 278, decided on 16 March 2004, is important here. At paragraph 9, the Court held that an exercise of power is not invalid merely because the authority did not mention the source of power or mentioned a wrong provision, so long as the power exists and can be traced to a lawful source. This was not a GST case, but the principle directly explains the likely departmental response to a wrong-section challenge. Consider two different situations. Situation A: Wrong reference, but an otherwise lawful adjudication The officer: issued a legally sufficient notice; possessed the necessary authority; afforded the required opportunity; considered the reply and evidence; recorded findings; determined the liability within limitation; and inadvertently referred to Section 74(1) instead of Section 74(9). In that situation, the department has a substantial argument that the defect is only an incorrect statutory description, protected by Section 160 and the principle in N. Mani . The error should be raised, but it may not secure annulment by itself. Situation B: Wrong reference accompanied by failure to adjudicate The document repeats the notice, ignores the taxpayer’s substantive explanation, contains no findings on the disputed issues, and directs payment of the proposed amount. Here, the objection is not confined to the wrong sub-section. It is that the officer has failed to discharge the statutory duty under Section 74(9) and has not stated the basis of the decision as required by Section 75(6). If no hearing was afforded despite an adverse decision being contemplated, Section 75(4) is also attracted. The proper appellate question is therefore: Does the impugned document represent a lawful adjudication, or merely the confirmation of a proposal without the adjudicatory exercise required by the Act? Can an officer avoid limitation by calling the document an order under Section 74(1)? No. The statutory character of a final determination cannot be changed merely by changing its label. If the document finally determines liability under Section 74, the department cannot avoid Section 74(10) by stating that it was issued under Section 74(1). Section 74(10) expressly regulates the order under Section 74(9), and Section 75(10) provides for deemed conclusion when the order is not issued within the prescribed period. The taxpayer should put the department to a clear choice: If this is a final adjudication order, it must satisfy Sections 74(9), 74(10) and 75. If it is merely a notice under Section 74(1), it cannot, merely as such, serve as the final adjudication of the disputed liability. This is materially stronger than saying only that “the wrong section has been printed”. Equally, the taxpayer should not argue that no Section 74(9) order exists merely because the number “74(9)” is absent. If the document is, in substance, a final determination, the appellate authority may treat it accordingly. The challenge must address its actual legal defects. “Time-barred” requires three separate examinations In practice, limitation objections are often weakened because these distinct questions are combined. First: Was the notice itself issued within the permissible time? Section 74(2) requires the notice to be issued at least six months before the outer deadline under Section 74(10). It does not merely require that the notice be issued at some point before the five-year period ends. Second: Was the final order issued within the permissible time? A notice issued within time does not give the officer unlimited time to adjudicate. The final order must independently satisfy Section 74(10), subject to any legally applicable exclusion or extension. Section 75(1), for example, provides for exclusion of the period of a court or Tribunal stay in the circumstances stated there. For a particular file, the calculation must identify: The financial year involved. The legally applicable annual-return due date, including relevant extensions. The date of issue and service of the notice. The date of issue of the final order and the evidence supporting that date. Any statutory exclusion, applicable notification or court order relied upon. Do not calculate the period simply from the end of the financial year, the date of inspection or the date on which the return was actually filed. Section 74(10) identifies the statutory starting point. Third: Was Section 74 lawfully invoked at all? A demand may be within the longer Section 74 period but outside the period available under Section 73. In such a case, the existence of the statutory fraud-related ingredients becomes critical. The department cannot justify the longer period merely by pointing to the amount of the demand. Section 74(1) requires the specified connection with fraud, wilful misstatement or suppression of facts to evade tax. Its Explanation 2 also contains a statutory definition of “suppression”, which must be addressed in the taxpayer’s defence rather than overlooked. Thus, where the taxpayer disputes Section 74 jurisdiction, the reply and appeal should examine the actual allegation: what fact was allegedly suppressed, where it was required to be declared, what written information was sought, and how the statutory ingredients are said to arise. If fraud is not established, does the entire liability automatically disappear? Again, not necessarily. Section 75(2) provides that where the Appellate Authority, Tribunal or court concludes that the Section 74 notice is unsustainable because the specified fraud-related charges have not been established, the proper officer shall determine tax as if the notice had been issued under Section 73(1). Consequently, success against Section 74 may produce different results: Where the notice satisfies the applicable Section 73 limitation requirements, tax, interest and the penalty applicable under Section 73 may still require determination. Where the notice falls outside the permissible Section 73 notice period, limitation may defeat the demand after Section 74 fails. In a notice covering several financial years, the result may differ from year to year. CBIC Circular No. 185/17/2022-GST dated 27 December 2022 expressly addresses this issue. It explains that redetermination must respect Section 73(2), read with Section 73(10), and that proceedings must be dropped where the notice, treated as one under Section 73, was issued beyond the permissible period. The circular also explains the two-year period under Section 75(3) for the consequential redetermination order. These are different concepts: time to implement an appellate direction does not dispense with the original notice-limitation requirement. The circular uses the ordinary statutory calculation of two years and nine months for the Section 73 notice. For an actual financial year, relevant extensions, exclusions and their legal status must be separately examined before applying that calculation. For a Karnataka State-tax appeal, the circular is an important interpretative support. Its binding application to a state officer should, however, be addressed with reference to the corresponding KGST provisions and any State adoption or instruction; it should not simply be assumed because CBIC issued it. Relevant decisions—and their limits N. Mani v. Sangeetha Theatre, Supreme Court, 16 March 2004 This decision must be anticipated by the taxpayer. It supports the proposition that an incorrect statutory reference does not, by itself, invalidate an otherwise authorised exercise of power. It does not hold that an authority may disregard limitation, jurisdictional conditions or mandatory procedure. M/s Metal N Strips v. Joint Commissioner of Commercial Taxes , Karnataka High Court, 17 October 2025 This is particularly relevant to your concern about large penalties in Karnataka. The Court examined Sections 74(1), 74(9), 75(7) and 75(13). It explained the distinct role of the notice and adjudication order, the restriction against confirming penalty beyond the notice, and the prohibition against another penalty for the same act or omission where Section 74 penalty has been imposed. The Court set aside the impugned orders insofar as they imposed and confirmed the penalty of ₹6,05,17,933. The decision supports scrutiny of the statutory basis and limits of adjudication. It should not be cited as a ruling that every order mentioning Section 74(1) instead of Section 74(9) is automatically void. Limited v. State of Jharkhand , W.P.(T) No. 2444 of 2021, decided on 6 October 2021 C. NKAS Services Private The Jharkhand High Court held that proceedings under Section 74 must be preceded by a proper show-cause notice. A summary in Form GST DRC-01 could not substitute for a notice satisfying the statutory requirements. The deficient notice and summary were quashed, with liberty for fresh proceedings in accordance with law. The case is useful where the defect begins at the notice stage. It is persuasive authority in Karnataka, not a Karnataka judgment, and it does not establish automatic extinction of tax liability whenever a notice is defective. What about the large interest and penalty demand? Tax, interest and penalty must be examined separately. A failure to establish Section 74 conditions can undermine the fraud-based penalty and longer limitation period. It does not necessarily establish that the underlying tax was never payable. Likewise, interest depends on its statutory foundation and computation; Section 75(9) expressly states that interest on tax short paid or unpaid is payable whether or not specified in the determination order. The appeal should therefore contain separate objections to: the taxable event, valuation or ITC disallowance underlying the tax demand; the legal provision, period, rate and computation used for interest; the factual and statutory foundation for the penalty; any amount or ground going beyond the notice; and any duplicate penalty for the same act or omission prohibited by Section 75(13). If the impugned demand is annulled on limitation or jurisdiction, relief should expressly extend to the consequential interest, penalty and recovery arising from that demand. But an annulment of one adjudication should not be described as extinguishing every possible independent statutory liability. Why are officers using Section 74(1) in final orders? Possible explanations include an inherited drafting format, carrying forward the initiating provision, without examining the orders and departmental instructions, there is no reliable basis to say why a particular officer does so, or that a majority of Karnataka officers follow the practice. or using the phrase “proceedings under Section 74(1)” loosely. These are possibilities, not established findings. Your observation that officers repeatedly and consciously write Section 74(1) deserves to be recorded. Nevertheless, an appeal need not prove their motive. The more effective course is to identify the statutory obligation, show the precise breach and explain its effect on the taxpayer’s rights. Repeated use of a wrong provision is not good administration. But even a repeated drafting error must be distinguished from a jurisdictional or adjudicatory failure. Conclusion Section 74(1) is the statutory entry point for the notice; Section 74(9) is the provision for adjudication. The distinction should appear correctly in departmental orders. Nevertheless, the strength of a taxpayer’s challenge depends on what lies behind the wrong reference. A mistaken number may be curable. An expired limitation period, an unsupported invocation of Section 74, failure to consider the defence, denial of the statutory hearing or confirmation beyond the notice requires a different and more serious examination. The taxpayer’s case should therefore be presented in these terms: The objection is not merely that the officer has mentioned the wrong sub-section. The objection is that the disputed demand has not been lawfully determined in accordance with the statutory conditions, procedure and limitation governing adjudication. Practical approach before the First Appellate Authority File the appeal against the purported final order Do not refrain from appealing on the theory that a document citing Section 74(1) cannot be an order. If it finally determines liability and is being enforced, challenge it as the impugned final determination while expressly reserving the objection to its statutory character. Section 107 permits an aggrieved person to appeal against a decision or order of the adjudicating authority. The ordinary period is three months from communication, with a further one month capable of condonation on sufficient cause. An argument that the order is a nullity should not be used as a reason to let the appeal period expire. For an ordinary tax-demand appeal, Section 107(6) requires payment of admitted dues and the prescribed 10% of the disputed tax, subject to the applicable statutory ceiling. On compliance, recovery of the balance is deemed stayed under Section 107(7). Suggested grounds of appeal The following wording must be adapted to the documents and dates. In particular, do not plead that no reply was considered if the order has, in fact, dealt with it. Ground I: Failure to undertake the statutory adjudicatory exercise The impugned order is expressly described as having been passed under Section 74(1), whereas that provision governs issuance of the show-cause notice. The adjudicatory determination is governed by Section 74(9). The appellant does not rest this objection merely on an erroneous statutory citation. The impugned order fails to deal with the material submissions and supporting records identified in paragraphs _ to _ of the appellant’s reply dated _. It consequently fails to satisfy Section 74(9), read with Section 75(6). Ground II: Limitation of the final determination The applicable last date for issuance of the adjudication order for FY _ was, as demonstrated in the enclosed limitation statement. The impugned order was issued on. In the absence of a legally applicable extension or exclusion, the determination is barred by Section 74(10), and the proceedings stand deemed concluded under Section 75(10). Description of the determination as an order under Section 74(1) cannot displace those provisions. Ground III: Absence of the conditions necessary to invoke Section 74 The notice and order do not establish the statutory ingredients necessary for invoking Section 74. The alleged discrepancy of _ has been treated as sufficient by itself, without identifying and establishing the alleged fraud, wilful misstatement or suppression of facts to evade tax. The appellant’s disclosures and explanation at Annexures _ have not been addressed. The longer limitation period and fraud-based penalty are therefore specifically disputed. Ground IV: Alternative plea under Section 75(2), with limitation preserved Without prejudice, if the proceedings are considered for treatment under Section 73 by operation of Section 75(2), the appellant requests an express finding on compliance with Section 73(2), read with Section 73(10). The notice dated _ falls beyond the applicable permissible notice period for FY _. Accordingly, the demand for that financial year cannot survive such treatment. Reliance is placed on Circular No. 185/17/2022-GST dated 27 December 2022, subject to its applicable legal status under the corresponding State enactment. The statutory and circular basis for these alternative pleas is important: failure of Section 74 does not always mean complete deletion, but conversion cannot be assumed to eliminate the original notice-limitation requirement. Ground V: Section 160 does not answer the substantive defects Any reliance on Section 160 is misplaced insofar as the defects pleaded concern limitation, absence of the statutory foundation for Section 74, and failure to perform the mandatory adjudicatory exercise. The appellant recognises that an incorrect citation alone may be curable. The present challenge concerns non-compliance in substance and effect with the requirements of the Act, which is the very condition on which Section 160 operates. Suggested prayer The appellant respectfully prays that the Appellate Authority: Annul the impugned order and the consequential demand on the grounds established in this appeal; Decide expressly the objections concerning limitation and the statutory conditions for invoking Section 74; Delete the consequential interest and penalty to the extent dependent on the demand annulled; Without prejudice, apply Section 75(2) only after determining the applicable Section 73 limitation separately for each financial year; and Grant such further relief as is permissible in law. For particular Karnataka matter, the most useful next step is to examine the complete signed order, the notice, the relevant financial year and their issue/service dates. Those documents will establish whether “Section 74(1)” is merely a wrong reference or accompanies a defect capable of defeating the demand.

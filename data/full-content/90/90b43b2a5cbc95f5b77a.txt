Summary: Businesses reviewing GST input tax credit on invoices and debit notes relating to FY 2025-26 must focus on the statutory time restriction in section 16(4) of the CGST Act. The ordinary outer limit is 30 November 2026, or the date of furnishing the relevant annual return if earlier. This is a restriction on entitlement, not a blanket extension of return-filing dates or an automatic right to credit. A workable review requires reconciliation of purchase registers, supplier-uploaded records, GSTR-2B, IMS actions, earlier GSTR-3B claims, credit notes and reversals, alongside the substantive conditions in sections 16 and 17. The article explains how to classify missing and disputed credits, why supplier reporting and recipient return timing matter, how to avoid duplicate claims, and what to document before the cutoff. Key Takeaways Source Links Show all 25 sections Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); Why the October 2026 review matters For a business with hundreds or thousands of purchase invoices, input tax credit (ITC) can be a major working-capital item. The critical task in October 2026 is not merely to ask whether the supplier issued an invoice in FY 2025-26. It is to establish whether that invoice or debit note can support a legally admissible credit and whether the credit has been taken within the time prescribed by section 16(4). Errors discovered in a later month may not be curable simply because the supplier eventually uploads a document. Finance teams should therefore treat October as a closing exercise with an owner, exception register and documentary trail rather than as a routine GSTR-3B preparation exercise. The statutory language refers to 30 November following the end of the financial year to which the invoice or debit note pertains, or furnishing of the relevant annual return, whichever is earlier. Accordingly, 30 November 2026 is the ordinary outer date for FY 2025-26 documents, subject to the annual-return condition and any legally applicable special provisions. A taxpayer who has already furnished the relevant annual return should not assume that a later November return reopens the credit window. Nor does the calendar date override requirements concerning receipt of supplies, possession of tax documents, payment to suppliers and eligibility of the particular inward supply. Statutory framework: a time limit plus substantive conditions Section 16(4) is not the only ITC test Section 16 of the CGST Act provides the entitlement framework. The taxpayer ordinarily needs a valid tax invoice or other prescribed document, receipt of goods or services, satisfaction of applicable supplier-reporting and tax-payment conditions, and furnishing of the relevant return. The restrictions of section 17, including blocked credits, remain separate. An invoice that appears in GSTR-2B can still relate to a blocked personal expense, an exempt-supply allocation, an incorrect place of supply or a supply never received. Conversely, a commercial purchase recorded in the ledger does not become eligible simply because the recipient paid the supplier in full. The amended section 16(4) date should not be confused with special retrospective relief under section 16(5) or 16(6). CBIC Circular 237/31/2024-GST explains the limited historical situations addressed by those provisions, including certain registration-cancellation and revocation circumstances. That circular does not create a general waiver of the FY 2025-26 deadline. Applying an exception without satisfying its precise statutory facts may lead to credit reversal, interest and litigation. Timing of the recipient’s return is crucial The deadline concerns taking credit through the prescribed compliance mechanism, not merely entering an invoice in the purchase register. A monthly filer considering FY 2025-26 invoices in October 2026 should examine the October GSTR-3B timetable and ensure a valid eligible claim is made in time. Credit first attempted in the November 2026 GSTR-3B, ordinarily furnished in December, can fall outside section 16(4). Quarterly return filers must pay particular attention to their different filing cadence; the existence of a 30 November statutory date does not by itself create a separate mid-quarter GSTR-3B claim facility. Where the timing of a QRMP taxpayer’s claim is uncertain, the matter needs a specific legal and portal-level assessment rather than an assumed workaround. A consolidated national purchase ledger can be helpful for control, but it cannot replace registration-wise reconciliation. For each mismatch, record the document number, taxable value, CGST/SGST/IGST, supplier status, reason for difference, person responsible and date of resolution. Materiality may determine review priority, but it does not change the legal eligibility conditions. GSTR-2B, IMS and supplier amendments Treat GSTR-2B as evidence, not as an unconditional sanction GSTN’s official GSTR-2B FAQs explain that the statement displays ITC-available and ITC-not-available categories and draws supplier-filed information into the recipient’s view. The portal specifically cautions that additional legal restrictions may apply even where the system has not marked an invoice ineligible. Therefore, the purchase register must be reconciled to GSTR-2B, while legal eligibility is checked separately. Ineligible credits appearing in GSTR-2B should not be carried into a positive claim merely to match the supplier’s declaration. IMS adds a further action layer. The GSTN revised IMS advisory describes acceptance, rejection and pending treatment and explains that actions can affect GSTR-2B generation or recomputation. A record kept pending is not the same as credit lawfully claimed. Finance teams should preserve the IMS action log, the GSTR-2B snapshot relied on and the corresponding return working. Where a supplier issues a correction through GSTR-1A or a later amendment, the recipient should verify the period in which the corrected information actually becomes available and whether the statutory time restriction still permits a claim. Do not use one invoice twice Duplicate ITC may arise when a vendor issues a corrected invoice with a new number, when an original entry is posted in two branches, or when a debit note is incorrectly treated as a second invoice. GSTN’s reconciliation guidance explicitly warns against availing credit twice. The safest control is a unique document key built from supplier GSTIN, invoice or debit-note number, date, taxable value and tax amount, with exception handling for valid amendments. A reviewer should also compare cumulative claims in earlier GSTR-3B periods, not only the current month’s purchase file. Payment conditions and credit reversals Supplier payment within 180 days The ordinary second-proviso mechanism to section 16(2), read with the rules, addresses non-payment of consideration and tax to the supplier within the prescribed 180-day period, subject to statutory exceptions. The recipient may need to reverse the relevant credit and later re-avail it after payment, as permitted by law. This issue is different from section 16(4)’s original availment deadline. A reversal/re-availment case should therefore be documented separately from an invoice never claimed at all. Taxpayers should not assume that making a delayed payment after 30 November cures every time-barred initial claim. Credit notes, returns and blocked credits Supplier credit notes, post-sale discounts and returns of goods can reduce the correct credit amount. The purchase team should communicate debit/credit adjustments promptly to the GST team, with the original invoice linkage and tax effect. Similarly, section 17 restrictions require independent checks for motor vehicles, food and beverages, construction-related expenditure and other listed categories, depending on statutory exceptions and the nature of business. An eligible-looking amount in an auto-populated table cannot override a blocked-credit provision. For common inputs used for exempt and taxable activities, document the allocation and any annual true-up required by the rules. The vendor-mismatch amount may ultimately become eligible or remain disallowed; it must not be presumed recoverable. A useful reconciliation also tracks interest exposure from erroneous past claims and reversals, which is a separate question from fresh credit entitlement. Practical close-out plan before November Assign responsibility to three teams Accounts payable should verify the invoice master, receipt evidence and payment ageing. The GST team should check supplier filing, GSTR-2B, IMS, blocked-credit classification and return mapping. The tax reviewer should sign off on the statutory cutoff, annual-return status and any exceptional legal position. This segregation reduces the chance that an accounts clerk will mark an invoice ‘eligible’ solely because it has been booked. Escalate material vendor disputes early enough to obtain correction and to confirm whether the corrected record will feed the recipient’s admissible return. A weekly exception meeting should distinguish ‘actioned and verified’, ‘waiting for vendor’, ‘legal eligibility disputed’ and ‘time-barred’. Do not erase rejected or unresolved entries: retain them with reasons, because future notices may ask why book ITC differs from GSTR-2B or why an invoice was not claimed. Export relevant portal reports before and after recomputation, and retain the filing acknowledgement and the final GSTR-3B working. A defensible audit trail is especially important when the company has several GST registrations, shared service centres or complex invoice-management workflows. What the 30 November date does not mean It is not a universal extension of all GST return deadlines; it does not authorise claiming an invoice without receipt of the underlying supply; it does not legalise blocked credits; and it does not automatically permit a supplier to amend records after that supplier’s own statutory limits. It also does not guarantee that a late supplier upload will flow to a GSTR-2B that can support a timely recipient claim. Businesses should avoid public statements that ‘all FY 2025-26 GST ITC can be claimed until 30 November’ without adding the annual-return and eligibility qualifications. The correct management question is narrower: which specific FY 2025-26 invoice or debit-note credits remain legally eligible, unclaimed and capable of being taken through a valid return within the operative time limit? A signed document-level answer to that question is more valuable than a large unreconciled ITC figure on a dashboard. Frequently Asked Questions (FAQs) Is 30 November 2026 the final date for every FY 2025-26 GST credit? No. Section 16(4) uses 30 November following the financial year or furnishing the relevant annual return, whichever is earlier. Other eligibility restrictions and special provisions must also be considered. Can a purchase invoice appearing in GSTR-2B be claimed automatically? No. GSTR-2B is a significant reconciliation source, but possession of documents, receipt of supplies, statutory conditions and section 17 restrictions require independent examination. Can an invoice missing from GSTR-2B be claimed merely because it is in the ledger? A ledger entry alone does not establish entitlement. Check the supplier-reporting requirements, portal data, relevant exceptions and the timing of any correction before deciding. Does marking an invoice pending in IMS amount to claiming ITC? No. A pending IMS record does not by itself represent an ITC claim in a return. Its later treatment must be reconciled to GSTR-2B and GSTR-3B. Can an October 2026 monthly GSTR-3B include eligible FY 2025-26 credit? Potentially, if all legal conditions are met and the credit is actually taken within the section 16(4) limit and before any earlier annual-return cutoff. Verify the applicable return and filing date. What if the same invoice was claimed in August and appears again after an amendment? Do not claim it again. Match the amended record to the original, reconcile cumulative return claims and recognise only the net legally eligible effect. Do retrospective section 16(5) and 16(6) rules waive the FY 2025-26 deadline? No general waiver follows. Those provisions address defined historical or registration-related circumstances and require their own conditions to be satisfied. Key Takeaways For FY 2025-26 invoices and debit notes, test section 16(4): 30 November 2026 or the relevant annual-return filing date, whichever is earlier. Reconcile purchase books, GSTR-2B, IMS and historical GSTR-3B claims at GSTIN and document level. Treat supplier corrections, blocked credits, payment reversals and QRMP timing as separate questions.

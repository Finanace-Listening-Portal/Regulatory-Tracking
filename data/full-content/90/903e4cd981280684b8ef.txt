India now accounts for close to half of the world’s real-time payment transactions, highlighting the scale at which the country’s digital payment infrastructure operates. Advt But as digital payments become embedded in everyday commerce, the RBI is shifting the focus from transaction growth to the quality, resilience and trust underpinning every payment.Scale changes the nature of risk“Scale changes the nature of risk,” Murmu said, stressing that payment systems operating 24x7x365 must be designed to withstand stress rather than simply support normal operations.Redundancy, business continuity, incident response and recovery mechanisms need to be built into payment architecture and governance, while disruptions must be communicated to customers promptly and transparently.The RBI official said cybersecurity can no longer be treated merely as an IT issue. With financial ecosystems increasingly interconnected, vulnerabilities at one participant can potentially affect multiple institutions and customers.This makes cyber resilience an enterprise-wide and ecosystem-wide responsibility, requiring financial institutions to build safeguards proactively rather than reconstruct them after an incident.RBI steps up fraud intelligenceFraud prevention is emerging as another key challenge as digital payments become faster and more ubiquitous. Social engineering, account takeovers, mule accounts and technology-enabled scams mean that security needs to cover not just payment infrastructure but also the customer’s interaction with it. Advt Murmu said fraud increasingly needs to be treated as an ecosystem problem, requiring cooperation among banks, payment system operators, fintech companies, telecom providers and law enforcement agencies.The RBI is developing the Digital Payments Intelligence Platform through the Reserve Bank Innovation Hub. Its MuleHunter.ai initiative is already using artificial intelligence and machine learning to identify mule accounts that channel proceeds of fraud.The central bank has also issued a discussion paper focused on curbing digital payment fraud, particularly authorised push payment frauds, with responses currently under examination. Advt Speed must not come at cost of customer protectionThe acceleration of digital payments has also compressed the time available to detect, stop or reverse fraudulent transactions. Murmu said transaction monitoring and appropriate authentication remain non-negotiable, while customer awareness is increasingly important.The RBI’s focus is extending beyond payment security to how customers are treated when systems flag suspicious activity. A genuine customer whose payment or account is blocked must be able to regain access quickly, making recovery and resolution an important measure of the payment system’s effectiveness. Advt “The barrier of success for a payment system, then, is not only the number of transactions it processes each second. It is the trust embedded in each of those transactions,” Murmu said.AI, digital lending bring new accountabilityMurmu also flagged the next layer of risks as artificial intelligence moves from systems that analyse and recommend towards systems that can plan and act with limited human intervention.AI is already being used in fraud detection, customer service, risk assessment and compliance, but agentic AI could execute actions across interconnected systems at machine speed. This raises questions around accountability, customer interests, manipulation of AI agents and supervision of automated systems.The RBI has constituted a committee on the responsible and ethical enablement of AI in the financial sector, with areas such as algorithmic bias, explainability and data privacy forming part of the framework.The same principle applies to digital lending, where technology may change how credit is delivered but does not dilute the responsibility of the regulated lender. Murmu stressed that borrowers must know who is lending to them, the cost of the loan and its terms.“Technology can distribute a service, but it cannot distribute responsibility,” he said.Inclusion now shifts from access to usageMurmu also said India’s next phase of digital finance must focus on inclusion through usage rather than simply access. The Financial Inclusion Index has improved, but those still outside the formal digital ecosystem are increasingly the hardest to reach.Limited connectivity, low digital literacy, disability, language constraints and lack of smartphones can unintentionally exclude users from digital finance.He called for multiple modes of access, including UPI 123PAY, offline payments, Aadhaar-enabled payment systems, voice interfaces and local-language solutions, arguing that inclusion must be treated as a design requirement rather than an afterthought.Click here to read our coverage of the Global FinTech Fest 2026.

Advt RBI’s defence involves a trade-off, easing pressure on the rupee and imported inflation while potentially increasing costs for banks, companies and borrowers across the financial system._________________________The Reserve Bank of India’s stepped-up defence of the rupee is beginning to create fresh costs across the financial system, with banks facing higher costs for providing currency hedges, importers paying more to protect themselves against currency volatility and borrowers facing the prospect of tighter financial conditions as the central bank keeps its focus on the rupee.The RBI has introduced a series of measures to curb dollar demand and reduce pressure on the rupee, including a special dollar window for three state-run oil companies and a 20% Foreign Exchange Risk Reserve requirement on specified foreign-exchange derivative contracts. It has also tightened rules governing certain derivative transactions and reduced the threshold for transactions that can be undertaken without proof of underlying exposure.The measures have helped the rupee recover from its recent lows. The currency gained about 2% last week, its strongest weekly performance since March, although it remained under pressure on Monday and closed at Rs94.925 to the dollar.For banks, the most immediate impact is likely to come from the new reserve requirement on eligible foreign-exchange derivatives. Under the framework, banks have to maintain an amount equivalent to 20% of the rupee notional value of specified contracts in their RBI current accounts.The requirement applies to eligible contracts involving current-account transactions and certain hedging of banks’ own positions, subject to the thresholds and exemptions specified by the RBI. The central bank subsequently issued clarifications after the initial rules disrupted trading in the forward market. Advt The requirement increases the amount of funds that banks have to set aside against eligible transactions. Banks have therefore sought changes to the mechanism, including replacing the cash requirement with a fee.For importers, the implications are more immediate. Companies that have future dollar payments normally use forward contracts and other derivatives to lock in the rupee cost of their imports. The new requirement raises the cost for banks of carrying some of these positions and can therefore increase the price at which currency protection is offered to clients.The impact is particularly relevant for companies with large dollar exposures, including businesses importing crude oil, machinery, components and other raw materials. A higher cost of hedging can add to operating costs at a time when many companies are already dealing with elevated commodity and financing costs. Advt The RBI has simultaneously sought to reduce one of the largest sources of dollar demand by opening a special window through which Indian Oil, Bharat Petroleum and Hindustan Petroleum can meet their dollar requirements directly through the central bank's arrangement.The move is designed to reduce the need for these companies to buy dollars in the spot market and thereby ease pressure on the rupee. It does not eliminate the underlying demand for dollars but shifts where that demand is met.The measures have also affected the foreign-exchange forward market. Banks temporarily became more cautious about quoting dollar-rupee forward contracts after the new rules were introduced, while forward premiums rose sharply. The RBI subsequently clarified which transactions would be covered by the new reserve requirement and which would be excluded.Borrower impact Advt The broader impact on borrowers will depend on how the rupee defence affects monetary and liquidity conditions.The RBI raised the repo rate by 25 basis points to 5.50% in October and changed its policy stance to calibrated tightening. The rate increase was the first since February 2023. While the rate decision was part of the broader monetary policy response and cannot be attributed solely to the rupee, tighter financial conditions could eventually increase borrowing costs.The transmission will vary across borrowers and lenders. Companies and households with floating-rate loans are more exposed to changes in benchmark rates, while banks with a larger proportion of floating-rate assets could see lending yields reprice faster than funding costs.The currency measures therefore create a complicated trade-off. Defending the rupee can reduce the risk of a disorderly depreciation and limit the inflationary impact of a weaker currency, particularly when imported commodities such as crude oil remain expensive.But the cost of that defence is increasingly visible elsewhere in the financial system.Banks have to manage the additional reserve requirement and potentially higher transaction costs. Importers face more expensive currency protection. Companies with substantial foreign-currency exposures have to reassess their hedging strategies. Borrowers, meanwhile, face the possibility of tighter monetary conditions if currency and inflation pressures persist.The RBI has succeeded in taking some immediate pressure off the rupee. The more difficult task now is to contain currency volatility without allowing the cost of defending the currency to spread too widely across banks, businesses and borrowers.

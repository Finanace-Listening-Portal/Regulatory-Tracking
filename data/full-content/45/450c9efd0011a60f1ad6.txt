Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); Lupin Investment Private Limited Vs DCIT (ITAT Mumbai) Summary : The Mumbai C Bench of the Income Tax Appellate Tribunal decided cross-appeals for AY 2010-11 against the CIT(A)-48, Mumbai’s order dated 1 September 2016. The original assessee, Pranik Landmark Associates, was a real estate partnership that was dissolved and succeeded by Rahas Mercantile Private Limited, which subsequently merged with Lupin Investment Private Limited. The firm returned income of ₹153,33,50,024, and the assessment under Section 143(3) determined income of ₹218,50,19,981. The Revenue disputed the classification of gains from the transfer of development rights. The surviving grounds in the assessee’s appeal concerned the cost of improvement, indexation and interest under Section 234C. Nirlon Ltd. joined the partnership on 1 October 1995 and agreed to bring in a saleable area of 3,50,000 sq. ft. at Goregaon, Mumbai, as its capital contribution. The firm developed The Hub shopping mall and the Raheja Sherwood residential project. The balance development potential of 27,129.22 sq. metres, including floor space index (FSI) and loaded transferable development rights (TDR), remained work-in-progress at ₹14,28,75,235 until 31 March 2007. Note 3 to the audited accounts recorded that the firm transferred the balance rights from work-in-progress to investments on 1 April 2007 because it intended to develop the remaining area for leasing. The firm also loaded additional TDR during FY 2007-08 and claimed ₹63,43,50,899 as the cost of improvement. Knight Frank helped identify the Lodha Group. Three Lodha Group companies joined the firm on 3 March 2010, and the two existing partners retired on the same date. The continuing partners took over the business, including goodwill and intangibles. The CIT(A) recorded that the development rights were the firm’s only asset on that date and that the transfer took place for ₹220 crore. The assessee paid Knight Frank brokerage of ₹1,13,32,737. It initially treated the transaction as a slump sale under Section 50B and offered long-term capital gains of ₹141,15,13,538 after deducting net worth and brokerage. A separate short-term gain of ₹12,17,31,785 on sale of premises under Section 50 was outside these appeals. The Assessing Officer found no transfer of an undertaking, so the assessee withdrew the slump-sale claim and submitted a revised computation under Section 45 showing long-term capital gains of ₹141,14,41,129. The officer ultimately assessed ₹205,71,24,765 as short-term capital gains after excluding the period when the rights were held as stock-in-trade and disallowing the entire improvement and brokerage claims. The CIT(A) applied Section 45(1), reckoned the holding period from actual acquisition and directed long-term treatment. The appellate authority allowed ₹62,69,29,767 of improvement costs supported by agreements and allowed the brokerage under Section 48(i). It sustained the remaining ₹74,21,132 disallowance for want of evidence. The Revenue argued that stock-in-trade falls outside the definition of capital asset in Section 2(14) and that Explanation 1 to Section 2(42A) did not bring the earlier holding period into account. On that basis, the period from 1 April 2007 to 3 March 2010 was under 36 months. The assessee argued that the Revenue had used the original acquisition date for cost purposes but the conversion date for the holding period. It also submitted that the Finance Act, 2018 amendments on inventory conversion applied from 1 April 2019 and relied on CIT v. J.H. Gotla in support of its approach to statutory interpretation. The Tribunal treated 1 October 1995 as the undisputed acquisition date. It relied on the Bombay High Court’s decision in Jannhavi Investments, which treated the actual acquisition date as controlling for acquisition cost when stock-in-trade is converted into a capital asset. It also considered Keshavji Karsondas on the continuing relevance of the original acquisition date. Applying that reasoning to the period of holding, the Tribunal held that the conversion into investment did not restart the holding period. The rights had been held for more than 36 months, so the gain was long-term. Both Revenue grounds were dismissed. The Tribunal restored the disputed ₹74,21,132 to the Assessing Officer for the limited purpose of verifying payment and nexus with the development rights. The amount comprised architects’ professional fees of ₹55,70,748, scrutiny charges of ₹10,59,400, non-agricultural tax on vacant land of ₹1,75,660 and property tax of ₹6,15,324. The officer must give the assessee a hearing and allow only the expenditure established to be deductible under Section 48. On the additional grounds, the Tribunal applied Section 250(5) and held that the omission to claim indexation earlier was neither wilful nor unreasonable. The issue arose when the CIT(A) classified the gain as long-term and required no fresh facts. The Tribunal directed computation of the indexed cost of acquisition using AY 1996-97 and of the indexed cost of improvement in accordance with law, subject to verification and a hearing. It also directed recomputation of the ₹1,88,25,976 interest charged under Section 234C by reference to tax due on returned income. A Section 154 rectification application was pending before the Assessing Officer. The Revenue’s appeal was dismissed, and the assessee’s appeal was partly allowed for statistical purposes. Cases Discussed Commissioner of Income Tax v. Jannhavi Investments (P.) Ltd. — [2008] 304 ITR 276, decided 8 January 2008 (Bombay High Court). The Tribunal relied on its holding that conversion of stock-in-trade into a capital asset does not change the actual acquisition date for computing acquisition cost. Keshavji Karsondas v. Commissioner of Income Tax — [1994] 207 ITR 737, decided 30 September 1993 (Bombay High Court). The Tribunal discussed the holding that acquisition cost under Sections 48 and 49(2) is determined by reference to the original acquisition of the land. Commissioner of Income Tax v. J. H. Gotla, Yadagiri — [1985] 156 ITR 323, decided 29 August 1985 (Supreme Court). The assessee cited this decision in support of an equitable interpretation where a literal reading would lead to injustice. FULL TEXT OF THE ORDER OF ITAT MUMBAI These two appeals filed by assessee and Revenue are against the order of the ld. CIT(A)-48, Mumbai, vide Order No. CIT(A)-48/IT- 340/DCCC-42/2014-15 dated 01.09.2016 passed against the assessment order by DCIT, CC-42, Mumbai u/s. 143(3) of the Income- tax Act, 1961 (‘the Act’) dated 22.03.2013, for Assessment Year 2010- 11. 2. Revenue has raised the following grounds of appeal in ITA No.7042/Mum/2016: “1. On the facts and in the circumstances of the case and in law, the Ld. CIT(A) erred in holding that the period of holding of the capital asset (i.e. development rights) prior to its conversion as capital asset should also be taken into account to determine the nature of capital asset and, hence, the capital gains arising on transfer of development rights by the assessee firm be taxed as long term capital gains.” “2. On the facts and in the circumstances of the case and in law, the Ld. CIT(A) erred in failing to appreciate that the development rights were held by the assessee as stock-in-trade and they were converted into capital asset only on 01.04.2007 and transferred on 03.03.2010 and as the period of holding of the capital asset was less than 36 months, the gains on transfer of development rights was correctly assessed as short term capital gains by the Assessing Officer.” The appellant prays that the order of CIT(A)-48, Mumbai on the above grounds be set aside and that of the Assessing Officer be restored. 2.1 Assessee has raised the following grounds of appeal in ITA No. 6455/Mum/2016: The appellant objects to the order dated 1 September 2016 passed by the Commissioner of Income-tax (Appeals) – 48, Mumbai on the following among other grounds: Validity of assessment order (1) The learned Commissioner (Appeals) erred in upholding the assessment order made on the dissolved firm which was dissolved on 3 March 2010. Chargeability of capital gains (3) The learned Commissioner (Appeals) erred in not adjudicating the ground of appeal mentioned below and holding that it is repetitive in nature: “11) In any event, the Ld. DCIT failed to appreciate that no income chargeable to tax under the head “capital gains” arose pursuant to the Deeds of Admission and Retirement dated 3 March 2010.” (4) The learned Commissioner (Appeals) ought to have directed that the capital gain on transfer of development rights ought not to have been taxed in the hands of the retiring partners. Computation of capital gains (5) The learned Commissioner (Appeals) erred in not adjudicating the ground of appeal mentioned below and holding that it is repetitive in nature: “12) Without prejudice, the learned DCIT erred in computing the capital gains at Rs. 205,71,24,765” Additional grounds of appeal (6) The learned Commissioner (Appeals) erred in not admitting the following additional grounds of appeal: “1. That the cost of acquisition of aforesaid capital asset shall further be substituted by indexed cost of acquisition. 2. That the indexed cost of acquisition shall be substituted by taking assessment year 1996-97 as the year of acquisition. 3. That the appellant craves leave to add, alter, amend, substitute and/or modify in any manner whatsoever the foregoing ground of appeal at or before the hearing of the appeal.” (7) The learned Commissioner (Appeals) ought to have appreciated that the grounds of appeal being raised are purely legal issues and do not require production or appraisal of fresh details or facts. (8) Without prejudice, the Commissioner (Appeals) ought to have appreciated that the issue of indexation of the cost of acquisition is covered by the Ground No. 12 of the appeal filed before the learned Commissioner (Appeals). Interest under section 234C (9) The learned Commissioner (Appeals) erred in not directing the learned Assessing Officer to delete the interest of Rs. 1,88,25,976 charged under section 234C. General (10) Each one of the above grounds is without prejudice to the other. 2.2. Ld. Counsel for assessee in the appeals by the assessee did not press ground nos. 1, 3, 4 and 5 at the time of hearing. These grounds are accordingly dismissed as not pressed. Ground nos. 10 and 11 are general in nature and need no adjudication. Ground nos. 6 to 8 depend on the outcome of Revenue’s appeal. 2.3. Surviving grounds are ground nos. 1 and 2 of Revenue and ground nos. 2 and 6 to 9 of assessee. Both appeals arise out of the same order of ld. CIT(A). Accordingly, both are taken up together for adjudication by passing this consolidated order. We take up Revenue’s appeal first as ground nos. 6 to 8 of assessee depend on it. 3. Assessee is a partnership firm. Its PAN is AAAFP2410K. It was engaged in the business of holding and leasing of properties, dealing and investment in properties and sale thereof, letting of spaces for hoardings, advertisements and sales promotion. Assessee has since been dissolved and succeeded by Rahas Mercantile Pvt. Ltd. That entity has merged with Lupin Investments Pvt. Ltd. A revised Form No. 36 has been filed on 17.04.2023 under cover of letter dated 10.04.2023. Assessee filed its return of income electronically on 30.09.2010, reporting total income at Rs. 153,33,50,024/-. Assessment was completed under section 143(3) with total income assessed at Rs. 218,50,19,981/-. 3.1. Assessee was originally constituted in 1995 between Pranik Shipping and Services Limited and Landmark Builders Private Limited for real estate development. By Deed of Partnership dated 01.10.1995, Nirlon Ltd. was admitted as a partner. It was agreed that Nirlon Ltd. would bring saleable area of 3,50,000 sq. ft. at Goregaon Mumbai as its capital contribution. A development agreement dated 28.07.2003 was executed by Nirlon Ltd. in favour of the then existing partners of assessee. It vested in assessee the development rights over the entire saleable area of 3,50,000 sq. ft. Assessee constructed a shopping mall known as ‘The Hub’ having built-up area of 1,11,800 sq. ft. It also developed a residential project ‘Raheja Sherwood’ in joint venture with Raheja Universal Limited. Assessee was left with balance development rights having minimum development potential of 27,129.22 sq. mts. comprising FSI and already loaded TDR. These rights were carried as work-in-progress till 31.03.2007 at Rs. 14,28,75,235/-. On 01.04.2007 they were converted into investment. This is supported by Note 3 of the audited accounts for year ended 31.03.2008, relevant to Assessment Year 2008-09, which is extracted below for ready reference: “3. The Firm was developing a project at Goregaon. The development and sale of the Hub- Commercial Mall and residential flats have been effected. As regards balance plot remaining undeveloped, it is decided to build construction thereon for lease and earning rentals. Accordingly, w.e.f. 01/04/2007, the undeveloped area is transferred from work-in-progress to investments in the books of account. This investment has been revalued as on 01/04/2007 to reflect the value as on that date and the increase on revaluation is credited to the partners’ account in profit sharing ratio.” 3.2. Assessee continued to own these development rights till 03.03.2010. It loaded further TDR in FY 2007-08 (AY 2008-09) at a purchase cost of Rs. 63,43,50,899/- which it claims as cost of improvement. Assessee submitted that conversion into investment was accepted in its assessments for Assessment Years 2008-09 and 2009- 10 made u/s 143(3). Assessee appointed Knight Frank around December 2009 to identify a buyer. A deal was finalised with Lodha Group. Vide Deed of Admission dated 03.03.2010 three companies of that group were admitted as partners. They are Infratech Builders and Agro P. Ltd., Lodha Land Developers P. Ltd. and Microtech Constructions P. Ltd. Their profit-sharing ratio was 50%, 37% and 12%, respectively. Share of existing partners Synchem Chemicals (India) P. Ltd. and Novamed Pharmaceuticals P. Ltd. was revised to 0.90% and 0.10%, respectively. Vide Deed of Retirement of even date, these two existing partners retired. The entire business undertaking together with goodwill and all intangibles was taken over by the remaining and continuing partners for which a separate agreement of even date was also executed. Ld. CIT(A) recorded that assessee thereby transferred its balance development rights fact of which was not disputed and that these rights were the only asset of assessee on that date. The consideration was Rs. 220,00,00,000/-. Assessee paid brokerage of Rs. 1,13,32,737/- to Knight Frank vide debit note dated 05.03.2010. 3.3. In its return, assessee treated the transaction as a slump sale u/s 50B. It reduced net worth of Rs. 77,71,53,725/- and brokerage of Rs. 1,13,32,737/- from consideration of Rs. 220,00,00,000/-. It thus offered long term capital gain of Rs. 141,15,13,538/-. It also reported short term capital gain of Rs. 12,17,31,785/- on sale of premises u/s 50. The latter is not in dispute before us. 4. Ld. AO held that there was no transfer of an undertaking. Section 50B was therefore held inapplicable. Assessee withdrew its claim of slump sale. It offered long term capital gain without prejudice u/s 45 at Rs. 141,14,41,129/- on a revised working. Ld. CIT(A) has extracted this working in its order. The same is reproduced below for ready reference: Particulars Rs. Full value of conside ration received on the date of transfer, i.e.,3/3/2010 220,00,00,000 Less: Cost of acquisition (date of acquisition 1/10/1995 – Nirlon brought land as capital contribution in the Firm), excluding revaluation amount + cost of TDR and purchase related expenses in January 2006 and October 2006 14,28,75,235 Purchase cost of TDRs and purchase related expenses in F.Y.2007-08 (i.e., Rs.63,58,32,066 – Rs.18,79,365 + Rs.2,17,176 + Rs.1,81,022 treated as cost of improvement 63,43,50,899 77,72,26,134 Net capital gains under sec 45 142,27,73,866 Less: Brokerage paid under sec 48(i) 1,13,32,737 Long-term capital gain 141,14,41,129 4.1. Ld. AO observed that assessee had used a colourable device and remarked that the gains were taxable as business income. Without prejudice, he then computed capital gains and finally assessed them under that head. On the period of holding, ld. AO held that period to be reckoned is the period for which an asset is held as a capital asset. Stock-in-trade is excluded from the definition of capital asset in section 2(14). Ld. AO therefore held that the period for which the asset was held as stock-in-trade is to be excluded. He drew support from absence of any clause in Explanation 1 to section 2(42A) which includes the period prior to conversion. He further held that the capital asset was held from 01.04.2007 to 03.03.2010 and thus, for less than 36 months. The gain was held to be short term. 4.2. Ld. AO denied deduction of Rs. 63,43,50,899/- claimed as cost of improvement and held that the claim was made for the first time without revising the return and was unsubstantiated and uncorroborated. He also denied brokerage of Rs. 1,13,32,737/- and held that no middleman is needed for distribution of assets among partners. The short-term capital gain was computed as under: Particulars Amount in Rs. Sale Consideration 220,00,00,000/- Less: Deduction u/s.48 (i) Cost of acquisition – Rs. 14,28,75,235/- (ii) Cost of improvement – NIL (iii) Cost of brokerage/commission – NIL Rs. 14,28,75,235/- Short Term Capital Gain 205,71,24,765/- 5. Ld. CIT(A) upheld the assessment made in the name of the dissolved firm. This is not in dispute before us. On the period of holding, ld. CIT(A) noted that the correct provision for taxing the gain is section 45(1) and not section 50B or section 45(4). Ld. CIT(A) noted that conversion of the asset was accepted in Assessment Year 2008-09 and held that period of holding is to be reckoned from the date of acquisition and not from the date of conversion. Ld. CIT(A) held that contrary decisions relied on by ld. AO could not be followed due to judicial discipline and directed ld. AO to treat the gain as long term capital gain. 5.1. On cost of improvement, ld. CIT(A) held that the claim was not new. Net worth of Rs. 77,71,53,725/- offered u/s 50B comprised these expenses. He further held that section 48 mandates deduction of cost of improvement. Ld. CIT(A) found the claim to be as under: Nature of Expenses Amount (Rs.) Cost of TDR purchased from Dynamix Realty 57,66,05,952 Expenses of Stamp Duty incurred on the above 1,72,98,200 Cost of FSI purchased from Nirlon Ltd. (to be allotted against road set back) 3,30,25,615 Professional Fees 55,70,748 Scrutiny Charges 10,59,400 NA Tax on vacant land 1,75,660 Property Tax 6,15,324 TOTAL 63,43,50,899 5.2. Ld. CIT(A) found the first three items supported by agreements. These are agreement dated 26.02.2008 with Dynamix Realty for TDR of 14,880 sq. mts. for Rs. 57,66,05,952/- and agreement dated 26.02.2008 with Eversmile Construction Co. Pvt. Ltd. duly stamped with duty of Rs. 1,72,98,200/- and agreement dated 11.10.2006 with Nirlon Ltd. for transfer of 2,501.50 sq. mts. of FSI. Ld. CIT(A) directed ld. AO to allow Rs. 62,69,29,767/-. No evidence was found for professional fees, scrutiny charges, NA tax and property tax. Disallowance of Rs. 74,21,132/- on these four items was sustained. Ld. CIT(A) directed ld. AO to allow brokerage of Rs. 1,13,32,737/- u/s 48(i). Ld. CIT(A) held that genuineness of brokerage was not doubted and that ld. AO had himself held that a transfer took place which was assessed u/s 45(1). 6. Both parties are aggrieved. Revenue challenges the finding that gain is long term. Assessee challenges the sustained disallowance of Rs. 74,21,132/- and rejection of additional grounds on indexation and the levy of interest u/s 234C. 7. Ld. CIT DR relied on the assessment order and invited attention to section 2(14) which defines the term ‘capital asset’. Ld. CIT DR submitted that stock-in-trade is excluded from this definition. Period for which development rights were held as stock-in-trade cannot therefore be counted as period for which a capital asset was held. Ld. DR further submitted that Explanation 1 to section 2(42A) has no provision for including such period. He supported the findings of ld. AO who had relied on decisions of non-jurisdictional benches and submitted that the gain was rightly assessed as short term capital gain. 7.1. On assessee’s appeal, ld. CIT DR supported the order of ld. Assessing Officer and submitted that ld. CIT(A) rightly sustained disallowance of expenses for want of evidence. Ld. DR also supported rejection of additional grounds by ld. CIT(A). 8. Per-contra, Ld. Counsel for assessee relied on the order of ld. CIT(A) and filed submissions. Ld. Counsel submitted that the sole issue in Revenue’s appeal is the period of holding of development rights. Ld. Counsel submitted that the issue is covered in favour of assessee by decisions including those of Hon’ble jurisdictional High Court. These hold that for computing capital gains what is relevant is the cost of acquisition on the date of acquisition and not the cost on the date on which the asset is treated as a capital asset. 8.1. Ld. Counsel submitted that amendments to sections 2(42A) and 28(via) and 49(9) relating to conversion of stock-in-trade into capital asset were made by Finance Act 2018 with effect from 01.04.2019. They are not applicable for the year under consideration. Ld. Counsel submitted that the prospective amendments themselves show the legislative intent in such cases. Ld. Counsel relied on CIT v. J.H. Ghotla [1985] 156 ITR 323 (SC) for the principle that a construction resulting in equity rather than injustice is to be preferred to a literal construction. 8.2 Ld. Counsel further submitted that Revenue has taken contrary positions. For cost of acquisition, Revenue has taken the cost as on the date of acquisition in Financial Year 1995-96, relevant to AY 1996-97. For period of holding, Revenue has taken the date of conversion, namely 01.04.2007. He contended that the period of holding must be reckoned from the date of acquisition and not from the date of conversion. 9. We have heard both the parties and perused the material on record. We have also given our thoughtful consideration to the submissions made as well as the judicial precedents referred before us. 9.1 We first take up Revenue’s appeal. Facts are not in dispute. Development rights were acquired on 01.10.1995. They were held as stock-in-trade till 31.03.2007. They were converted into investment on 01.04.2007 and transferred on 03.03.2010. Conversion into investment is not doubted. Ld. AO has himself assessed the gain under the head capital gains. Revenue has not challenged the date of acquisition in its grounds. Dispute is confined to one question. It is, whether period of holding is to be reckoned from 01.10.1995 or from 01.04.2007. The consequence is simple, the period from 01.04.2007 to 03.03.2010 is less than 36 months, the gain would then be short term. The period from 01.10.1995 to 03.03.2010 is more than 36 months, the gain would then be long term. 9.2. Relevant definitions are in section 2(42A) and section 2(29A). They are extracted below for ready reference: 2(42A) “short-term capital asset” means a capital asset held by an assessee for not more than thirty-six months immediately preceding the date of its transfer; 2(29A) “long-term capital asset” means a capital asset which is not a short-term capital asset; 9.3. Section 2(42A) speaks of a capital asset held by an assessee. Ld. AO read it as an asset held as a capital asset. Ld. AO excluded the period of stock-in-trade because section 2(14) excludes stock-in-trade from capital asset. Ld. CIT DR has advanced the same argument before us. We find that Hon’ble Bombay High Court in the case of CIT vs. Jannhavi Investments Ltd. [2008] 304 ITR 276 (Bom) has examined this very question in the context of conversion of stock-in-trade into capital asset. In this case shares were acquired in 1977. They were held as stock-in- trade till 06.11.1987 and then converted into capital asset. The question was whether assessee could adopt fair market value as on 01.04.1981 as cost of acquisition. Hon’ble High Court held that what is relevant is the cost of acquisition and not the cost on the date on which the asset was treated as a capital asset. Cost of acquisition is the cost on the date of actual acquisition. There was no acquisition on the date of conversion. Hon’ble Court, while giving its judgment, took note of the decision of the same court in the case of Keshavji Karsondas v. CIT [1994] 207 ITR 737 (Bom). It noted that the amendment of 1993 by the Finance Act, 1992, referred to by the Revenue, by which the mode of computation of income chargeable under the head ‘capital gains’ had changed and the concept of ‘index cost of acquisition’ had been introduced, does not in any way nullify or dilute the ratio laid down in the said judgment of Keshavji Karsondas (supra). 9.4. We perused the judgment in the case of Kesavji Karsondas (supra) to take note of the finding arrived at by the Hon’ble Court. From para 4 of this judgment, the conclusion drawn by the Hon’ble Court states that it did not find merit in the contention of the assessee that the cost of acquisition should be fixed with reference to the date when it became the capital asset by virtue of the amendment made to the Act, i.e. 01.04.1970. Hon’ble Court opined very clearly that the cost of acquisition does not change. It is the date when the asset was actually acquired by the assessee or his grandfather, as in this case, and the said date cannot change. According to the Hon’ble Court, the date of acquisition of the land for the purposes of Section 48 read with Section 49(2) was the date when the land in question was acquired by the grandfather of the assessee prior to 1941. 9.5. In view of the above undisputed factual matrix duly fortified by the biding precedence of Jannhavi Investments Ltd. (supra) by the Hon’ble jurisdictional High Court, we hold that period of holding is to be reckoned from 01.10.1995. In our considered view, cost of acquisition of an asset and its date of actual acquisition shall go hand in hand. Event of subsequent conversion has no effect on both. Development rights were held for more than 36 months. The gain is long term capital gain. Ld. CIT(A) rightly directed ld. AO to treat it so. We uphold the order of ld. CIT(A) on this issue. Ground nos. 1 and 2 of Revenue are dismissed. 10. We now take up assessee’s appeal. Ground no. 2 challenges the sustained disallowance of Rs. 74,21,132/-. This comprises professional fees of Rs. 55,70,748/-, scrutiny charges of Rs. 10,59,400/-, NA tax on vacant land of Rs. 1,75,660/- and property tax of Rs. 6,15,324/-. Ld. CIT(A) denied them as no evidence was submitted. We note that these items relate to amounts paid to government departments and professional fees paid to architects. Their payment and their nexus with the development rights need verification. We therefore restore ground no. 2 to the file of ld. AO for the limited purpose of verification. Assessee shall furnish evidence in support thereof. Ld. AO shall allow the expenses to the extent found to be incurred in relation to the capital asset in accordance with section 48 and decide after giving assessee an opportunity of being heard. Ground no. 2 is allowed for statistical purposes. 11. Ground nos. 6 to 8 arise as Revenue’s appeal is dismissed. Assessee seeks indexation for cost of acquisition since the year of acquisition is Assessment Year 1996-97. Ld. CIT(A) declined to admit these grounds. In this regard, section 250(5) governs the matter. It is extracted below for ready reference: “250(5) The Commissioner (Appeals) may, at the hearing of an appeal, allow the appellant to go into any ground of appeal not specified in the grounds of appeal filed by him, if the Commissioner (Appeals) is satisfied that the omission of that ground from the form of appeal was not wilful or unreasonable.” 11.1. The test is whether omission was wilful or unreasonable. Before ld. Assessing Officer, the gain was assessed as short term. Indexation arises only for long term capital gain under the second proviso to section 48. The question of indexation arose only when ld. CIT(A) held the gain to be long term. The claim is a statutory consequence of that finding. It is a purely legal issue needing no fresh facts. Assessee had also challenged computation of capital gains in ground no. 12 before ld. CIT(A). We therefore, find that the omission was neither wilful nor unreasonable. Ld. CIT(A) erred in rejecting the additional grounds. 11.2. We have held the gain to be long term. Assessee is accordingly, entitled to indexation. Ld. AO shall compute indexed cost of acquisition by taking Assessment Year 1996-97 as year of acquisition. Indexed cost of improvement shall be computed in accordance with law and ld. AO shall verify the figures and give effect to this order after giving assessee an opportunity of being heard. Ground nos. 6 to 8 are allowed for statistical purposes. 12. Ground no. 9 relates to interest of Rs. 1,88,25,976/- levied u/s 234C. Ld. CIT(A) has directed the ld. AO to charge this interest only in accordance with the provision. Ld. CIT(A) noted that a rectification petition dated 24.04.2013 u/s 154 was pending and that ld. AO had not given details of computation. Interest u/s 234C is to be computed by reference to tax due on returned income and not on assessed income. We affirm the direction of ld. CIT(A). Ld. AO shall compute interest u/s 234C on returned income while giving effect to this order. Ground no. 9 is allowed for statistical purposes. 13. Before we part with this order, we record that all other contentions raised before us not specifically dealt with above are either subsumed in the findings arrived at hereinabove or are otherwise not material for the disposal of these appeals. 14.

Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); Astemo India Private Limited Vs Additional Commissioner (AE) (Rajasthan High Court) GST on Secondment: Rajasthan High Court Directs Astemo India to Statutory Appeal, Leaves Taxability Open Can a taxpayer bypass the GST appellate mechanism by describing a disputed employment arrangement as a jurisdictional issue? Does the same officer issuing the show cause notice and passing the adjudication order establish bias? The Rajasthan High Court examined these questions in Astemo India Private Limited v. Additional Commissioner (AE), decided on 4 November 2026. The Court declined to entertain the writ petition because the controversy required examination of disputed facts and documentary evidence. Crucially, it did not decide whether the secondment arrangement was taxable. 1. The Dispute: Employment or Import of Manpower Services? Astemo India, a manufacturer of motor vehicle components, received Japanese expatriate employees from its overseas parent under a secondment arrangement. The Department treated the arrangement as an import of manpower supply services, attracting IGST under the reverse charge mechanism. The adjudication order confirmed IGST of ₹11,19,39,126, denied input tax credit of ₹5,23,25,805, imposed an equal tax penalty and directed payment of interest. The disputed period extended from July 2017 to March 2023. The company maintained that the expatriates were its own employees. Accordingly, their services fell within Entry 1 of Schedule III, concerning services by an employee to the employer in the course of employment. The distinction was fundamental: the company disputed the very existence of a taxable supply. 2. Separate Employment Contracts Were Not Conclusive The company relied on individual employment contracts, operational control and deduction of tax under section 192 of the Income-tax Act. It argued that the overseas salary component was paid in Japan merely for convenience and subsequently reimbursed. The Department disputed this account. It maintained that the overseas company retained employment links and partial control, and that reimbursement represented consideration for services. The Court identified a significant evidentiary gap. The individual contracts relied upon were dated July 2022 and January 2023, while the demand commenced in July 2017. The cost-allocation memorandum itself dated from April 2022. Consequently, those documents could not, without further evidence, establish the employment arrangement throughout the earlier years. 3. Northern Operating Systems Requires Examination of Substance Both sides relied on the Supreme Court’s decision in Northern Operating Systems. The High Court explained that the relevant exercise involves examining the entire arrangement and actual conduct. Questions include who selected employees, paid remuneration, exercised control, retained employment links and administered service benefits. A contractual label or isolated clause could not settle these matters. The Court therefore treated the controversy as a mixed question rooted in facts, requiring payroll records, debit notes, remittances, service records and evidence of actual working arrangements. It did not determine which party’s factual version was correct. 4. A Jurisdictional Argument Cannot Eliminate Factual Disputes Astemo argued that the absence of a taxable supply deprived the Department of jurisdiction. However, establishing that proposition required acceptance of its disputed claim that a genuine employer–employee relationship existed. The Court recognised exceptions permitting writ intervention despite an alternative remedy, including breach of natural justice, lack of jurisdiction, enforcement of fundamental rights and a challenge to statutory validity. Nevertheless, merely presenting an appellate grievance as a jurisdictional objection was insufficient. The taxpayer had to demonstrate that an exception genuinely applied. 5. Same Officer Issuing Notice and Order: Is That Bias? The company argued that the officer who issued the notice could not fairly adjudicate it. The Court rejected this objection on the facts presented. Section 74 entrusted the proper officer with issuing the notice and determining liability after considering the taxpayer’s representation. The statutory combination of functions did not, by itself, establish bias. No personal interest or malice was alleged. The Court distinguished a tentative view in a notice from a closed mind during adjudication. Whether the officer properly considered the reply could be examined in appeal. 6. Disagreement With Reasoning Is Not Denial of Hearing The company had submitted replies and further representations. Its complaint was substantially that the officer rejected its submissions or addressed them inadequately. The Court treated this as a challenge to the quality of reasoning, rather than denial of an opportunity to be heard. It also rejected as speculative the apprehension that the appellate authority would mechanically follow Northern Operating Systems. The statutory appellate authority was required to examine the matter independently and provide reasons. 7. Valuation, ITC and Suppression Remain Open The company also raised Rule 28 valuation, the relevant circulars, ITC limitation, absence of suppression and non-issuance of Form GST DRC-01A. The Court found that these issues also required examination of records. It expressly preserved the merits for the appellate authority. It permitted an appeal within 30 days of uploading the judgment, protected that appeal against rejection on limitation within the stipulated period, and restrained coercive recovery during that window. Adjustment of earlier deposits towards pre-deposit remained open. 8. Practical Significance The decision emphasises evidence covering the entire disputed period, rather than reliance on selected contracts. Its immediate holding concerns the proper forum for resolving contested facts. It should not be reported as a final ruling that Astemo’s secondment arrangement attracts GST. Question that arises is whether dispute turns on facts that the parties contest? If it does, can a writ court resolve those facts on affidavits? DISCUSSION AND ANALYSIS 19. No doubt, the power under Article 226 is wide. An alternate remedy does not take it away. But the power is discretionary. Courts have long restrained themselves where the statute gives an effective remedy. 20. A Constitution Bench of Hon’ble Supreme Court laid down this rule in Thansingh Nathmal v. Superintendent of Taxes, Dhubri.31 The High Court should not, in a writ, decide questions of fact that the statutory authorities are competent to decide. A party should not be allowed to bypass the machinery created by a taxing statute. In Titaghur Paper Mills Co. Ltd. v. State of Orissa,32 the Supreme Court held that where a statute creates a 30 AIR 1964 SC 477. 31 AIR 1964 SC 1419. right or liability and also gives a special remedy, that remedy must be availed. 21. At the same time, Hon’ble the Supreme Court has described fiscal statutes with their own machinery of adjudication and appeal as self contained codes. In Union of India v. Guwahati Carbon Ltd.,33 it said so of the Central Excise Act. It held that the statutory route must be followed. The CGST Act is a statute of the same kind. It provides for adjudication, first appeal, appeal to a Tribunal and then appeal on law. We may like to say more about this scheme below in the succeeding part. 22. Whirlpool recognised four exceptions. A writ may lie despite an alternate remedy for enforcing a fundamental right, for a breach of natural justice, against an order wholly without jurisdiction, or to challenge the vires of a statute. These are exceptions. They are not a licence. A party must show that its case truly falls within one of them. It is not enough to give a ground of appeal the name of an exception. 23. There are sound reasons why the rule of undertaking the journey of statutory self contained adjudicating process should ordinarily be not short circuited. The same is applied more strictly in tax cases. We set them out, because they explain why the exceptions are kept narrow. 24. First, the statutory authorities deal with these questions every day. They know the returns, the forms and the accounting entries. They are better placed to read a payroll or a debit note than a writ court is. Second, the hierarchy produces a full record of findings. When a question of law finally reaches this Court under Section 117, it comes with the facts already found. The Court then decides law on a settled factual base. Third, the statute strikes a deliberate balance between the taxpayer and the revenue. An appeal under Section 107 requires a pre deposit of the admitted amount and a part of the disputed tax. Once that is paid, recovery of the balance stands stayed. A writ petition entertained as a matter of course would let a taxpayer step around that balance. Fourth, if every disputed demand could come straight to this Court, the specialised forums would stand idle. This Court would become a court of first instance on facts. 25. We may also add here that none of this means that a writ can never lie in a tax matter. But, all it means that the taxpayer must bring a real exception. A dressed up ground of appeal will not do. 26. A writ petition is decided on affidavits. There is no trial. Witnesses are not examined. Documents are not proved in the ordinary way. Each side swears to its own version. For that reason the writ court does not ordinarily embark on resolving seriously disputed facts. 27. Both sides rely on Northern Operating Systems34. It is therefore useful to see what kind of enquiry that judgment calls for. We do so only to understand the method so as to figure out if it can be applied. 28. Hon’ble the Supreme Court was dealing with employees seconded by overseas group companies to an Indian company. It did not decide the case on the label the parties had used. It looked at the substance of the arrangement. It asked who, in reality, was the employer. It read the agreements as a whole. It looked at who selected the employees, who paid them, who bore the cost in the end and on whose rolls they remained for social security and retirement benefits. On that cumulative reading, it held that the overseas companies had supplied manpower to the Indian company. 29. What is relevant thus is the methodology adopted for deployment of human resource. The answer in each case depends on the whole arrangement, read with the actual conduct of the parties. A single clause or a single contract does not decide it. That tells us that the deployment by way of secondment is, in every case, a mixed question rooted in facts. 30. Adverting now to the facts in hand, it is borne out that the petition is built on one central assertion. The seconded employees are the petitioner’s own employees. Everything else flows from it. The Department’s case rests on the opposite assertion. The employees remain the employees of the Overseas Company, which supplies their services to the petitioner. Each side reads the same documents to support its own version. Aforesaid tabulated differences cannot possibly be termed as mere differences of legal opinion. They are differences about what happened, what the documents say and how the parties actually behaved over nearly six years. We take them one at a time. We do so only to see whether they are genuinely disputed. We do not resolve them. 32. First, the employer. The petitioner relies on two individual contracts. One is of July 2022. The other is of January 2023. The demand begins in July 2017. Nothing in the pleadings tells us on what terms each employee worked in the earlier years. Nor do we know how many employees were seconded in the period, or whether each of them signed a similar contract. The Memorandum is itself dated 01.04.2022. So for almost five of the six years in dispute, the very document on which the petitioner’s case rests did not exist. What governed those years is a pure question of fact. It cannot be answered by an affidavit that speaks of two contracts in 2022 and 2023. 33. Second, who bears the salary. The petitioner reads Clauses 2(a) to 2(c) of the Memorandum to mean that it alone bears the cost. The Department finds that the employees remain on the payroll of the Overseas Company. Both views are possible on a bare reading of extracts. The answer lies in the payroll records, the debit notes, the bank remittances and the books of both companies. Who in fact paid, how much, when and under what head, is a matter of evidence. It is not a matter of interpretation alone. 34. Third, control. Here too the petitioner points to Clauses 2(h), 4 and 5(f). The order finds that the Overseas Company retains partial control. Practical questions thus are; who appointed the employee? Who could recall or dismiss him? Who fixed his duties and assessed his work? Who granted leave? Was he integrated in the business of the Indian entity? A contract clause gives one part of the answer. The actual practice of the parties gives the rest. Labels in a contract do not settle the true nature of the relationship. As already noted, that exercise needs the full record. It cannot be done on extracts annexed to a writ petition. 35. Fourth, lien and continuity of service. The petitioner says that the employees here have kept no lien abroad. Whereas, the impugned order finds that the employees here also retain a lien with the Overseas Company. Whether they do is a question of fact. It depends on the service records, the terms of return to Japan and how seniority and benefits were treated. 36. Fifth, income tax. The petitioner says tax was deducted under Section 192 on the whole salary. Yet it had to explain a gap in the Form 26AS figures on 16.10.2023. The existence of that gap, and the adequacy of the explanation, are themselves matters of fact. They bear on the very claim that the entire payment was treated as salary. 37. Sixth, the petitioner’s own conduct. It paid Rs. 6,00,66,553/- in April 2023. It raised self invoices. It took credit. It has paid every month since. It says all this was under protest. The Department says the challan shows no protest. Whether the payment was under protest, and what weight that conduct carries, can only be decided on the record. We express no view either way. We note it only to show that the dispute cannot be resolved on disputed facts. 38. Seventh, the remaining grounds also depend on facts. Whether there was suppression depends on what was disclosed, when and to whom. Whether the self invoices are invoices for the purpose of Rule 28 depends on why and how they were raised. Whether the credit was within time depends on the dates of the invoices, the periods they relate to and the date of the annual return. Whether the absence of Form GST DRC 01A caused any prejudice depends on the stage of the proceedings and on the replies the petitioner in fact filed. 39. The petitioner asks us to accept its reading of these documents and hold that there is no supply. That reading comes to us through its own affidavit. An affidavit filed by a party in support of its own case is, by its nature, self serving. It tells us what the deponent says the documents mean. It does not prove that they mean it. The Department’s version, on the other hand, is recorded in a reasoned order passed after notice, replies and hearing. If we were to prefer the petitioner’s affidavit to that order, we would be reappreciating evidence, which we would rather refrain in exercise of extraordinary writ jurisdiction and best leave it to the competent authorities of the revenue. This is not a task for a writ court sitting on affidavits and extracts. It is the ordinary work of a fact finding authority. 40. The statute gives the competent statutory authorities the tools for this work. Under Section 70, the proper officer may summon any person to give evidence and produce documents. Such proceedings are deemed to be judicial proceedings. The appellate authority under Section 107(11) may make such further inquiry as may be necessary. It may then confirm, modify or annul the order. It cannot remand the case to the adjudicating authority. So it must find the facts itself and decide. 41. Rule 112 of the CGST Rules permits additional evidence before the appellate authority in the cases it specifies. The authorities can call for the full payroll, examine the officers of both companies and test each document. A writ court has none of these practical advantages. It would have to decide on whatever each side chose to annex. 42. It follows that the dispute cannot be resolved here without taking evidence and weighing it. That is the work of the competent authority. It must look at the material on record and return its own findings of fact. If the petitioner remains aggrieved by those findings, the statute gives it a further remedy. 43. Furthermore, the CGST Act provides a complete hierarchy for disputes of this kind. We set it out to show that the petitioner’s (i) Sections 73 and 74 provide for notice, reply, hearing and determination by the proper officer. (ii) Section 107 provides a first appeal to the appellate authority. Under Section 107(6), the appellant must pay the admitted amount and a part of the disputed tax as pre deposit. On such payment, recovery of the balance stands stayed by force of Section 107(7). Under Section 107(11), the appellate authority may confirm, modify or annul the order after further inquiry. Under Section 107(12), it must state the points for determination, the decision and the reasons. (iii) Section 112 provides a further appeal to the Goods and Services Tax Appellate Tribunal. The Tribunal is the final forum on facts. It is a specialised body with judicial and technical members. (iv) Section 117 provides an appeal to the High Court from an order of a State Bench of the Tribunal, where the case involves a substantial question of law. Section 118 provides an appeal to the Supreme Court from orders of the National or Regional Benches. (v) Section 75(2) provides that where an appellate authority, Tribunal or court finds that the charge of fraud, wilful misstatement or suppression is not established, the tax shall be determined as if the notice had been issued under Section 73. 44. This scheme reflects a clear legislative choice. Facts are to be found by the adjudicating authority, the appellate authority and the Tribunal. The High Court comes in at the end, and only on a substantial question of law. If we entertain this petition and decide the facts ourselves, we would turn that scheme upside down. The High Court would become the first forum on facts. The appellate authority and the Tribunal would be bypassed altogether. 45. The scheme is also fair to the taxpayer. The pre deposit is a fraction of the disputed tax. Once it is paid, the balance cannot be recovered while the appeal is pending. The petitioner has already deposited Rs. 6,00,66,553/-. It is not left without protection if it goes to appeal. Indeed, it is better protected there, since the statute itself stays recovery. A writ petition offers no such automatic protection. 46. Moving on to the assertion as to whether there is a breach of natural justice because the same officer issued the notice and passed the order. This is the ground pressed most strongly to bring the case within Whirlpool. We cannot accept it. 47. Section 74(1) empowers the proper officer to serve the show cause notice. Section 74(9) directs the proper officer to consider the representation and determine the tax, interest and penalty. The statute itself places both functions on the proper officer. This is the design of the law. It is not a lapse by the officer. 48. The rule against bias strikes at personal interest or a real likelihood of prejudgment. No personal interest is alleged against issued the notice and then decided it. Where the statute itself authorises the same authority to do both, that alone is not bias. If it were, every adjudication under Sections 73 and 74 would fall. The petitioner has not challenged the vires of these provisions. 49. There is also a difference between a tentative view and a closed mind. An officer who issues a notice forms only a prima facie view. The notice says so in its very name. It calls upon the noticee to show cause. Forming such a view is not prejudgment. It is what the statute requires before any person is called upon to answer. The question is whether the officer kept his mind open to the reply. That is tested by reading the final order, which is exactly what an appellate authority does. 50. The petitioner was heard. It filed replies on 01.05.2023 and 13.02.2024. It filed further submissions on 11.10.2024 and 12.11.2024. The petitioner itself says that the order reproduces its submissions. Its real grievance is that the officer did not accept them, or did not deal with them well enough. That is a complaint about the quality of reasoning. It is a ground of appeal. It is not a denial of hearing. 51. The fear that the appellate authority will not apply its mind is speculative. A writ court cannot assume that a statutory authority will act unlawfully. The appeal under Section 107 is not an appeal from Caesar to Caesar’s wife. It lies to a separate authority. That authority must give reasons under Section 107(12). Its order is open to a further appeal before the Tribunal under Section 112. A substantial question of law can then come to this Court under Section 117. If the appellate authority were to decide mechanically, the remedy lies within that very hierarchy. The hierarchy is complete and effective. 52. As an upshot, though we decline to entertain the petition, but the time spent in this Court ought not to be counted against petitioner when it goes to appeal. 53. The petitioner has also deposited Rs. 6,00,66,553/- in April 2023 and has paid the disputed tax every month since. Whether those amounts can be adjusted towards the pre deposit is a matter for the appellate authority and same is left open. CONCLUSION 54. We make it clear that we have not expressed any opinion on the merits. We have referred to the rival versions only to show that they are disputed. Our observations are only for deciding whether the writ petition should be entertained. They shall not influence the appellate authority. 55. The writ petition is dismissed on the ground of availability of an efficacious alternate remedy. To ensure that the petitioner is not prejudiced, we issue the following directions.

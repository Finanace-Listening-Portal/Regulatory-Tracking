Jaydip Jayantilal Joshi (ICAI Disciplinary Committee) “India’s No.1 Cement” Certificate: ICAI Removes CA’s Name for Three Months, Imposes ₹1 Lakh Fine Summary: The Disciplinary Committee of the Institute of Chartered Accountants of India (ICAI), Bench-II, by an order dated 3 October 2026, directed that CA. Jaydip Jayantilal Joshi (Membership No. 170300), associated with M/s Joshi & Shah, Chartered Accountants, be reprimanded, that his name be removed from the Register of Members for three months, and that he pay a fine of ₹1,00,000 within 60 days from receipt of the order. The punishment followed findings pronounced on 29 August 2026, arising from a complaint filed by Shri Vijay Bhutda. The Committee held the respondent guilty of professional misconduct under Clause (7) of Part I of the Second Schedule to the Chartered Accountants Act, 1949. The proceedings concerned a certificate dated 5 July 2021 issued in favour of UltraTech Cement Limited, supporting its claim as “India’s No.1 Cement”. The central issue was whether an auditor who had examined quantitative indicators such as sales value and cement manufacturing capacity could endorse a broadly expressed claim that might also be understood as implying qualitative superiority. The Committee concluded that a chartered accountant’s certificate must be precise, unambiguous and founded upon adequate verifiable evidence, particularly where it is capable of influencing stakeholders and members of the public. The certificate stated that the respondent had examined published results, publicly available information and additional industry-related information for major cement-producing companies in India. On the basis of this examination, he certified that UltraTech Cement Limited had the largest sales value among major cement companies operating in India and the largest cement manufacturing capacity. The certificate then concluded that, in view of those findings, the company’s claim as “India’s No.1 Cement” was fair, based on independent verification of facts, figures and available information. The complainant questioned whether the scope of the respondent’s professional examination justified that concluding statement. According to him, “No.1 Cement” was a broad expression capable of conveying superior cement quality, whereas the respondent’s verification concerned sales and production-related indicators. The complaint consequently questioned whether the certificate inadequately described the scope of the work performed and whether the respondent had exceeded the limits of his professional competence. The complainant also relied upon the statutory and technical framework governing cement standards. He referred to the role of the Bureau of Indian Standards (BIS) and the mandatory certification requirements applicable to different categories of cement. His contention was that certification of market position in terms of sales or manufacturing capacity was distinguishable from certification of product quality, conformity with technical standards or overall superiority. He submitted that the certificate should have explicitly confined the conclusion to the largest cement-selling or manufacturing entity, rather than endorsing a general claim concerning the product. The complainant additionally argued that the certificate did not adequately specify the peer companies against which the claim had been evaluated. Because the certificate could be relied upon by members of the public and used in promotional communications, the complainant considered clarity of its terminology a substantive professional requirement. The respondent denied certifying the quality of UltraTech’s cement. He maintained that he had examined annual reports, published financial results and industry information, and had compared the company’s sales value and manufacturing capacity with those of major competing cement companies. His comparative exercise included UltraTech Cement Limited, ACC Limited, Ambuja Cements Limited and Shree Cement Limited, with reference to performance over multiple financial years. He argued that the certificate must be read in its entirety, because its first two substantive statements specifically referred to sales value and manufacturing capacity. According to him, the subsequent conclusion that the company’s “No.1” claim was “fair” rather than “correct” constituted a reasonable conclusion from the quantitative examination, not an endorsement of product quality. He maintained that no words such as “best” or “highest quality” appeared in the certificate and that no reasonable reader should interpret it as a technical evaluation of the cement. The Disciplinary Committee of the Institute of Chartered Accountants of India (ICAI) has reprimanded CA. Jaydip Jayantilal Joshi, ordered removal of his name from the Register of Members for three months, and imposed a ₹1 lakh fine for professional misconduct concerning a certificate supporting UltraTech Cement Limited’s claim as “India’s No.1 Cement.” The Committee held that the certificate’s language was vague, ambiguous and capable of conveying an impression wider than the quantitative parameters examined. It emphasised that certificates issued by chartered accountants must be precise and supported by verifiable evidence. The order dated October 3, 2026, follows findings dated August 29, 2026, in the complaint filed by Shri Vijay Bhutda. Background of the Case The respondent, associated with M/s Joshi & Shah, Chartered Accountants, issued a certificate dated July 5, 2021, concerning M/s UltraTech Cement Limited. The certificate stated that, based on examination of published results, public-domain information and additional information supplied, the company had the largest sales value and largest cement manufacturing capacity among major cement companies operating in India. It then concluded that, in view of those findings, the company’s claim as “India’s No.1 Cement” was fair, based on independent verification of facts, figures and available information. The complaint questioned whether verification of sales and manufacturing capacity justified certification of such a broadly worded claim. Complainant’s Allegations The complainant argued that “India’s No.1 Cement” could communicate qualitative superiority, whereas the respondent had examined financial and operational figures. He contended that the certificate did not adequately restrict its conclusion to being the largest cement-selling or manufacturing company. He also questioned the respondent’s competence to certify product quality and referred to the role of Bureau of Indian Standards certification. According to the complainant, a certificate intended for reliance by others should clearly identify its scope and limitations, leaving no room for an interpretation extending beyond the work performed. Respondent’s Defence: Certification Was Quantitative The respondent maintained that his work concerned sales value and manufacturing capacity, rather than cement quality. He submitted that he had studied annual reports and compared financial and operational information over three years. The comparative material included figures for UltraTech Cement, ACC, Ambuja Cements and Shree Cement. He stressed that the certificate described the claim as “fair,” rather than “correct”, and that its concluding statement had to be read with the preceding references to sales and capacity. The respondent also relied on the UDIN description, which identified the subject as highest sales value and manufacturing capacity in the cement industry. He denied using expressions such as “best” or certifying product quality. Verification Work Acknowledged by Committee The Committee acknowledged that the respondent had performed certain verification procedures, including examination of annual reports and comparative analysis of selected companies over multiple years. It recognised that this exercise was quantitative in nature, dealing with financial and operational parameters. However, it distinguished between the scope of those procedures and the breadth of the conclusion expressed in the certificate. The existence of comparative work did not, in its assessment, resolve the concern over ambiguous certification. Broad Conclusion Could Convey a Wider Impression The Committee rejected the defence that the certificate could only be understood as referring to quantitative measures. It held that the “number one” conclusion did not clearly confine itself to the precise parameters examined. Although the respondent admitted that he was neither competent nor engaged to certify cement quality, the wording could convey a broader impression to the public. The Committee emphasised that a chartered accountant’s certificate carries substantial public credibility. Its drafting must therefore demonstrate precision and clarity, particularly where stakeholders or members of the public may rely on it. Reliance on analogies, industry usage or contextual interpretation did not discharge that responsibility. Supporting Evidence and Professional Guidance The Committee referred to the Guidance Note on Reports or Certificates for Special Purposes (Revised 2016). It found that the respondent had not placed adequate supporting evidence or documentation on record to substantiate the certification as issued. The Committee held that certificates must be precise, unambiguous and founded on verifiable and demonstrable evidence. It concluded that the respondent failed to exercise the due diligence and care expected of a chartered accountant. At the punishment stage, the respondent disputed these conclusions, reiterated the verification performed and sought leniency, referring to the COVID period and his being newly qualified at the time. Findings Confined to Auditor’s Conduct The Committee expressly clarified that the proceedings concerned the respondent’s role and professional responsibility in issuing the certificate. Its findings did not determine technical or business-related matters concerning UltraTech Cement Limited, including the quality of its cement. Punishment Imposed The respondent was held guilty under Clause (7) of Part I of the Second Schedule to the Chartered Accountants Act, 1949. The Committee imposed a reprimand, three-month removal from membership and ₹1,00,000 fine, payable within 60 days from receipt of the order.

Summary: Business failure often ends with exhausted cash, but its causes usually emerge much earlier through weak demand, unsustainable pricing, uncontrolled expansion, poor management and delayed decisions. This article identifies twelve recurring warning signs and offers practical responses that business owners can incorporate into a monthly review. It explains why rising revenue does not necessarily indicate financial health, why profitability must be examined by product, customer and contract, and why a rolling cash forecast and regular receivables review matter. It also highlights the importance of timely accounts, clear responsibilities, internal controls, compliance discipline and borrowing supported by a credible repayment plan. Beyond financial indicators, the article examines resistance to market changes and workplace cultures that prevent bad news from reaching decision-makers. Nokia’s experience illustrates how fear and internal politics can obstruct an organisation’s response even when warning signals are visible. A green, amber and red scoring system helps owners identify deteriorating conditions, assign responsibility and act while options remain available. Progress can be measured through consistent reviews, faster responses, fewer surprises and honest reporting. The article also discusses the growing role of business data in identifying stress and notes restructuring options subject to eligibility. Its central message is that optimism works best alongside accurate information: owners should recognise uncomfortable facts early, challenge assumptions and take corrective action before a cash crisis forces their hand. Seventy per cent said they ran out of capital; 43% said their product did not fit the market well enough. Most named more than one reason. That overlap contains the central idea of this article: Running out of money is typically how a failure ends, not how it begins. The real error happened months or years before — a product buyers did not need badly enough, a price that never covered costs, an expansion the cash could not support. The money ran out at the finish. The decision behind it was taken at the start. Companies seldom die of the issue that finally kills them. They die of the earlier issue nobody dealt with. What history teaches: physicians and the art of early signs Medical traditions in many cultures — the Greek Hippocratic writings, India’s Ayurvedic texts and classical Chinese medicine — all stressed the importance of spotting small early signs before an illness became serious. A physician’s skill lay not only in healing but in noticing. Business owners need exactly that skill. Once a issue is obvious to everyone, the cheapest remedies have typically run out. The Twelve Early Warnings These twelve warnings show up again and again before a business fails. Use them as a monthly health check and give each one a colour: green (healthy), amber (keep watching) or red (act now). 1. Customers owe you nothing Founders become attached to ideas; markets pay for solutions. A product can be clever and elegant and still fail because buyers don’t need it enough to pay for it. Warning sign: Compliments but no payments. Pilots that never become contracts. “Everyone is our customer.” Fix: Prove demand with money rather than praise — deposits, paid pilots, repeat orders. Treat feedback as data, not as an insult. 2. Revenue can disguise a broken model Discounts, heavy advertising, loose credit and costly delivery can buy sales that never become profit. The more such a business sells, the faster it burns cash. Warning sign: Sales up, margins down. Fix: Know the true profitability of every product, customer and contract. If each extra sale contributes too little, growth won’t rescue you. 3. Cash ignores your forecast Wages are not paid out of projected revenue. Customers pay late. Stock sits on shelves. Suppliers and lenders don’t wait. Warning sign: “Next month’s collections will sort it out” — heard each month. Fix: Maintain a rolling 13-week cash forecast and review overdue receivables weekly. 4. Growth can turn into a burden New branches, larger teams and bigger premises bring commitments that remain even when sales fall short. Warning sign: Expansion driven by ambition rather than evidence. Fix: Before any major expansion, test the returns, working capital, break-even point, payback period and a realistic downside. 5. Poor management cannot be outgrown Some founders control everything; others delegate without accountability. Both fail. Warning sign: Each decision waits for the owner — or no one owns decisions at all. Fix: Set clear decision rights, measurable responsibilities and regular reviews. 6. Bad numbers lead to bad decisions Late accounts and unreconciled balances can make an ailing business look healthy until the cash has gone. Warning sign: Profit figures that arrive months after the period closes. Fix: Close the books each month and reconcile bank, receivables, payables, inventory, loans and statutory dues. 7. Trust is not a control Fraud frequently comes from a trusted employee, supplier or partner. The ACFE’s Occupational Fraud 2026 study of 2,402 cases in 143 countries and territories found that a typical fraud ran for around a year before it was detected, and that tips were the most common way it came to light. Warning sign: One person who prepares, approves and reconciles payments. Fix: Separate those duties, verify any change of bank details independently and provide a safe channel for concerns. 8. Compliance is not paperwork to put off Neglected tax, employment, licensing and data obligations bring penalties, disputes and loss of trust — and pull management attention away exactly when it’s needed most. Warning sign: Notices answered late; returns filed without reconciliation. Fix: A year-round compliance calendar with named owners and evidence. 9. Debt can postpone a decision without fixing the problem Borrowing can fund worthwhile investment. It can also conceal losses while interest piles up. Warning sign: New loans taken to repay old ones; statutory dues used as working capital. Fix: Borrow only when there’s a believable source of repayment. If solvency is in question, get professional advice early — while options still exist. 10. Markets move; businesses must move with them Customers, competitors, technology, regulation and costs never stand still. Yesterday’s formula has an expiry date. Warning sign: “This is how we have always done it.” Fix: Review customers, competitors, prices and technology regularly. Test small, measure honestly and change course when the evidence says so. 11. Culture decides what reaches the owner When people fear blame, mistakes are hidden. When appearances are rewarded, bad news moves slowly. Warning sign: The owner always hears last. Fix: Reward people who raise issues early. Separate honest mistakes from misconduct. Hold every level equally accountable. 12. Act while choices remain Distress typically builds from several linked weaknesses — slipping margins, unreliable information, rising debt, slow collections and fading confidence among stakeholders. The UK Insolvency Service and insolvency practitioners worldwide make the same point: acting early keeps options open that acting late closes for good. Warning sign: Waiting until a bank refuses, a customer leaves or a regulator steps in. Fix: Review the business each month. Ask what is getting worse, which assumptions no longer hold and what has to change now. Quick recap The short version: failure unfolds in visible stages; owners who score the twelve warnings each month see it coming while it’s still inexpensive to stop. A case worth remembering Nokia once led the global mobile phone market. When touch-screen smartphones appeared in 2007, many people inside and outside the company could see the danger. Researchers who later studied its decline, including Timo Vuori and Quy Huy, found that fear and internal politics made middle managers reluctant to pass bad news upwards, while senior leaders underestimated the threat. In 2013, Nokia sold its devices business to Microsoft. The lesson is not that Nokia lacked talent or information. It’s that warnings that can’t travel upwards are as useless as warnings that don’t exist — precisely warning number 11. Signs of progress An early-warning system only matters if it changes what people do. Track four indicators: Discipline — the twelve warnings are scored each month on a fixed date, without postponement. Speed of response — the days between a warning turning amber and a named person acting on it. Aim for under two weeks. Fewer surprises — issues are found by your own review rather than by your bank, auditor, customer or a tax notice. Honest scoring — some warnings show amber or red. Twelve months of solid green typically means nobody is looking hard enough. The decade ahead Warning signals will come increasingly from data rather than gut feel. Accounting systems, bank feeds, GST returns and payroll records already contain most of the signals described here, and AI tools will soon flag them automatically. Lenders and tax authorities are using the same data to assess companies from outside. That brings a new risk. When outsiders can see your warning signs in real time, ignoring them costs more. A bank may cut limits, or a tax system may raise a query, before the owner has even noticed. The owners who thrive will be those who read their own signals first — and act before anyone else needs to ask. Frequently asked questions “How frequently should we review the warning signs?” Monthly for most businesses, and weekly for cash during hard periods. “Which warning sign is most important?” A persistent gap between profit and cash, and growing overdue receivables, are among the earliest and most reliable signals. “Who should be responsible for the review?” The owner or CEO, supported by the finance head, with the results shared across the leadership team. Where things stand (October 2026): Indian companies under strain can explore informal restructuring with lenders, the RBI’s frameworks for stressed accounts and — for MSMEs — the pre-packaged insolvency route under the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, subject to eligibility. Comparable options exist elsewhere, such as administration in the UK and Chapter 11 in the US. Take professional advice before signing new guarantees or paying selected creditors. The toughest pushback — and a reply The pushback: “Founders have to be optimists. If I spend my time studying warning signs, I will lose the nerve to take risks. Each great company looked finished at some point.” There’s truth in this. Building a business demands belief that runs ahead of the evidence, and many successful firms survived near-fatal moments. The reply: The goal is not pessimism; it’s visibility. A pilot who checks the instruments is not afraid of flying — she is making sure she can keep flying. Courage that ignores the fuel gauge is not courage. It’s a bet placed with other people’s money, jobs and trust. Look honestly at the warnings, then decide boldly. The two go together. Common mistakes to avoid Treating rising revenue as proof of good health. Looking at the financial statements only once a year. Explaining away the same warning month after month. Surrounding yourself only with people who agree with you. Waiting for a crisis to force decisions you could take calmly today. Your 30-minute exercise: the traffic-light review Draw a grid with the twelve warnings as rows and three columns: Green, Amber, Red. Score your business honestly, then answer three questions: 1. Which red item, fixed this quarter, would remove the most risk? 2. Who owns it, and what is the first step? 3. Which amber item is drifting towards red? Repeat this on the first working day of each month and keep each sheet. After a year, you will see your business’s true story — not the one you were hoping for. The Business Owner’s Reality Check Before your next expansion, investment or major commitment, ask: Do buyers genuinely value what we sell — and pay enough for it? Do our products, customers and contracts produce sustainable returns? Could we meet our obligations if collections slowed or sales fell? Are our financial reports accurate, timely and useful? Would we catch fraud, errors and compliance failures early? Could the business function without constant intervention from the founder? What happens if our largest customer, key employee, main supplier or funding source disappears? Which warning signs are we ignoring because the truth is uncomfortable? Key takeaways Running out of money is typically the final event in a failure, not the original mistake. Twelve recurring warnings — from soft demand and flawed models to quiet cultures and delayed decisions — appear well before the end. Rate them each month with a straightforward green, amber and red review. A warning that never reaches decision-makers is as dangerous as one that never existed. Optimism and honesty work together: look plainly first, then act with confidence. Strong businesses meet issues like everyone else. They just see them sooner, respond faster and learn before the damage is permanent.

Summary: Receiving an income tax notice does not automatically mean wrongdoing, but ignoring it or replying incorrectly can lead to tax demands, penalties and litigation. This article explains ten mistakes taxpayers should avoid: missing notices or deadlines, misreading statutory provisions, failing to verify authenticity, overlooking AIS and Form 26AS discrepancies, submitting vague or inconsistent explanations, uploading incomplete evidence, choosing the wrong remedy, failing to attend hearings, mishandling demands and neglecting records or contact details. It distinguishes common communications under Sections 139(9), 142(1), 143(1), 143(2), 148, 148A, 154 and 245 of the Income-tax Act, 1961, while highlighting the need to check which law governs a particular assessment year following the Income-tax Act, 2025. Taxpayers should authenticate notices through the official portal, reconcile information with returns and books, provide point-wise evidence-backed replies, seek extensions where appropriate, preserve acknowledgements and obtain professional advice for complex scrutiny or reassessment matters. A practical first-48-hours checklist offers a structured way to respond promptly and minimise avoidable disputes. The first reaction is often panic, followed by one of two poor responses: ignoring it in the hope that it goes away, or replying in a hurry without understanding what is being asked. Both can turn a routine query into a demand, a penalty or a prolonged dispute. The reality is more reassuring. A large number of notices are routine. They flag a mismatch between the return and the data the Department already holds, ask for a missing document, or point out a defect that can be cured quickly. Even notices that signal scrutiny or reassessment can be handled well when the response is accurate, timely and properly supported. What decides the outcome is usually not the notice itself, but the quality of the reply. This article sets out ten mistakes that taxpayers and, at times, professionals make while responding, and how to avoid each of them. A Quick Note on the Legal Framework For assessment years up to 2025-26, notices continue to be issued under the Income-tax Act, 1961, and the section numbers mentioned in this article refer to that Act. The Income-tax Act, 2025 applies from 1 April 2026 with renumbered provisions, but proceedings for earlier years are generally governed by the old Act. It is therefore important to read the notice carefully to see which Act and which section it invokes. The mistakes discussed below apply regardless of the section, though the time limits and procedures differ from one notice to another. Mistake 1: Ignoring the Notice or Delaying the Reply This is the most common and the most costly error. Notices are served electronically on the e-filing portal and on the registered email address and mobile number, and many taxpayers simply never check them. Silence is not treated as a neutral stance. If a taxpayer does not respond to a notice seeking information, the officer may proceed to complete the assessment on the basis of available material, often to the taxpayer’s disadvantage. Non-compliance can also attract penalty, and a best judgment assessment made in the absence of a reply is usually harsher than one made with full information. The right approach is to open the notice the day it is received, note the due date and begin working on the response immediately. Even a notice that looks minor deserves a documented reply. Mistake 2: Not Reading the Notice Carefully Taxpayers frequently react to the word “notice” rather than to its contents. Yet every notice carries specific details that shape the correct response: the section under which it is issued, the assessment year, the exact query or discrepancy, the documents sought, the mode of response and the last date. A notice under section 143(1) is an intimation of processing, a notice under section 139(9) points out a defective return, a notice under section 142(1) seeks information, a notice under section 143(2) begins scrutiny, and a notice under section 148 or a show cause under section 148A relates to income alleged to have escaped assessment. Each calls for a different reply. A reply that answers the wrong question, or addresses a different year, wastes time and may be treated as no reply at all. Read the notice twice, mark the specific points raised, and draft the response to answer those points and nothing else. Mistake 3: Not Verifying That the Notice Is Genuine Fraudulent emails and messages pretending to be from the Income Tax Department are common. They may ask for payment to a personal account, request login credentials, or carry links to look-alike websites. Responding to such a message can lead to financial loss and identity theft. At the same time, assuming that every unfamiliar notice is fake is equally risky, because a genuine notice that goes unanswered carries real consequences. The safe habit is to log in to the official e-filing portal directly by typing its address into the browser, and check the pending actions and notices there. Genuine communications carry a Document Identification Number, and the notice can be matched with the record on the portal. The Department does not ask taxpayers to share passwords or one-time passwords over email or phone, and a payment demand should always be matched against the portal before any money is sent. Mistake 4: Replying Without Reconciling the Data A large share of notices arise from mismatches between the return and the information the Department holds, which is visible in Form 26AS, the Annual Information Statement and the Taxpayer Information Summary. Interest income, dividend, securities transactions, high-value deposits, foreign remittances and property transactions are common triggers. Taxpayers often respond from memory, saying that “everything was disclosed”, without checking whether it actually was. Before replying, download the current AIS and Form 26AS, compare each reported entry with the return and the books, and identify exactly where the difference lies. Sometimes the Department’s data is wrong, for instance because of a reporting error by a bank or because a transaction was reported against the wrong PAN. In that case the taxpayer can submit feedback on the AIS and attach evidence. Sometimes the taxpayer has indeed missed an item, and the better course is to acknowledge it and deal with the tax consequence rather than defend an incorrect position. Mistake 5: Giving Vague, Inconsistent or Over-Informative Explanations The quality of the written explanation matters as much as the documents. Generic statements such as “the income has already been disclosed” or “all details are correct” convince no one. Equally damaging is an explanation that contradicts the return, the books or an earlier reply. The officer reads each submission in light of the earlier ones, and inconsistency invites deeper enquiry. Two cautions apply. First, never offer an explanation you cannot support with a document. An unproven claim, such as a loan from a relative or a gift without a trail, can result in the amount being added as unexplained credit. Second, do not volunteer unrelated information. A reply should answer what has been asked, factually and politely, in clear paragraphs, with each point linked to its supporting evidence. A short, structured reply that tracks the questions in the notice almost always works better than a long narrative. Mistake 6: Submitting Incomplete or Unorganised Documents Even a correct position can fail for lack of proof. Taxpayers sometimes upload a single blurred photograph of a bank statement, or a bundle of unlabelled files, and assume that the officer will piece the story together. The officer will not, and a document that cannot be read or linked to the query carries little weight. Documents should be clear, complete and labelled in the order of the points in the reply. Where the query concerns a loan, include the loan agreement, bank statements showing the movement of funds and the lender’s confirmation and identity proof. For a cash deposit, show the source through sale records, withdrawals or other evidence. For a property sale, provide the agreement, the computation of capital gains and the proof of reinvestment if an exemption is claimed. A short index at the beginning of the submission helps the officer and also helps the taxpayer, who can later see what was filed. Mistake 7: Using the Wrong Route or the Wrong Remedy Taxpayers often respond through the wrong channel or invoke the wrong remedy. Most notices now require a response on the e-filing portal under the relevant e-proceeding, and replies sent by ordinary email may not be recorded. In other cases, a taxpayer who receives an intimation with a small demand files a revised return when the correct step is a rectification request, or files a rectification request when the issue really needs an appeal. The distinction matters. A defect notice under section 139(9) must be cured within the time allowed, which is ordinarily 15 days, failing which the return may be treated as invalid. An apparent mistake in an order processed under section 143(1) can be corrected through a rectification application under section 154. A proposal to adjust a refund against an outstanding demand under section 245 allows only a short window, usually 30 days, to agree or disagree. Where the dispute is on merits, an appeal to the Commissioner (Appeals) is available within the prescribed period. Choosing the right remedy at the right time saves months of effort. Mistake 8: Missing the Deadline and Not Seeking Time or Attending the Hearing Every notice carries a response date. Some taxpayers miss it because they were gathering documents, because a professional was unavailable, or because they did not realise the deadline had been set so short. Others reply on time but skip the hearing scheduled through video conferencing, or fail to join at the stated time. If more time is genuinely needed, the sensible step is to request an extension or adjournment before the deadline, with a short and truthful reason, and to submit whatever part of the information is ready. Waiting until after the date has passed leaves the officer free to proceed. When a personal hearing is fixed, attend it, keep the submissions and documents ready, and be prepared to answer questions on the points raised in the notice. A taxpayer who participates is far better placed than one who is absent. Mistake 9: Paying the Demand Blindly, or Ignoring It Altogether When a notice or order raises a demand, taxpayers tend to go to one of two extremes. Some pay immediately without checking the computation, even when the demand is wrong because of a credit for tax deducted at source that was not given, or an advance tax payment that was not matched. Others ignore the demand in the belief that it is unfair, and then face interest, recovery action and adjustment of future refunds. The better course is to verify the computation. Check whether all tax credits, advance tax and self-assessment tax have been correctly considered, and whether the interest and any penalty are properly calculated. If the demand is wrong, respond on the portal with the appropriate disagreement and evidence, seek rectification, or appeal within time. Where an appeal is filed and the demand is disputed, it is possible to request a stay of recovery, and it is usual for the taxpayer to be asked to pay a part of the disputed amount while the appeal is pending. A disputed demand should be handled actively, and never left to sit. Mistake 10: Neglecting Records, Contact Details and Professional Help The last mistake is less about the reply and more about preparation. Many notices go unseen because the email address or mobile number on the portal is outdated, or because the account is managed by a former adviser who no longer monitors it. Others cannot be answered properly because the taxpayer did not preserve the underlying documents. The law expects records to be kept for several years, and a notice can come long after the transaction was entered into.Keep the contact details on the portal current, and log in periodically to check pending actions. Maintain a simple and organised file for each year with the return, acknowledgement, computation, bank statements, investment proofs, and agreements for major transactions. Keep a copy of every reply filed and every acknowledgement received. Finally, know when to seek help. A simple mismatch can be handled personally by a careful taxpayer, but notices involving scrutiny, reassessment, large additions, search or survey follow-up, or foreign assets are best handled with a qualified Chartered Accountant or tax counsel from the start. A professional engaged at the first notice can often prevent errors that cost far more to correct later. A Practical Checklist for the First 48 Hours Download the notice and read it fully. Note the section, the assessment year, the specific query and the due date. Confirm that it is genuine. Log in to the e-filing portal directly and match the notice with the record there. Pull the data. Download the AIS, Form 26AS and the filed return for the relevant year and compare them. Collect the evidence. Gather the documents that support each point, and arrange them in the order of the query. Decide the remedy. Choose between a reply, a rectification, a revised return, a request for time or an appeal, depending on what the notice requires. Draft and review. Prepare a short and factual reply, check it for consistency with the return and earlier submissions, and file it on the portal before the deadline. Keep proof. Save the acknowledgement and a complete copy of what was submitted. Conclusion Receiving an income tax notice is not an accusation, and in most cases it is an invitation to explain or correct something. The mistakes described in this article, namely ignoring the notice, misreading it, trusting it blindly, replying without reconciliation, giving weak explanations, filing poor documents, choosing the wrong remedy, missing deadlines, mishandling the demand and neglecting records, have one thing in common. Each of them is avoidable with a little discipline. Stay calm, act promptly, read carefully, verify the facts, support every statement with evidence and use the remedy the law provides. A taxpayer who follows these steps will resolve most notices without drama, and will be well prepared even for the few that turn into a longer dispute.

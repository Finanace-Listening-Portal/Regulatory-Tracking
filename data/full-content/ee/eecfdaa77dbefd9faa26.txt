Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); In re Pawan Kumar Rajgaria (Calcutta High Court) Summary : The Calcutta High Court, in Pawan Kumar Rajgaria Vs State of West Bengal, granted bail to the petitioner in a criminal case involving allegations of monetary cheating, forgery of National Company Law Tribunal (NCLT) orders, misrepresentation concerning a One Time Settlement (OTS) Scheme and organised crime under Section 111 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 (BNS). The petitioner was arrested in connection with Dankuni Police Station Case No. 278 of 2026, registered on 17 July 2026, involving offences under Sections 316(2), 318(4), 336(3), 338 and 351(2) of the BNS, with Section 111(2)(b) subsequently added. The dispute arose from an agreement containing elements of property development and investment, together with an arbitration clause. According to the petitioner, the original allegation concerning non-payment of ₹10 crore was subsequently inflated to ₹32 crore in the FIR. The defence maintained that the underlying controversy was substantially contractual and civil in nature and did not justify continued incarceration. The petitioner further disputed the allegations concerning suppression of NCLT proceedings and communication of forged tribunal orders. His counsel submitted that the alleged communications had not resulted in any advantage being obtained and challenged the invocation of Section 111 BNS on the ground that the essential ingredients of organised crime were absent. Particular reliance was placed on the statutory requirements concerning an organised crime syndicate and the applicable Standard Operating Procedure, including the procedure relating to an Organised Crime Assessment Note. Conversely, the de facto complainant contended that forged judicial documents constituted serious criminal misconduct even where no direct benefit was established. The complainant also alleged subsequent discovery of additional forged signatures and relied on Supreme Court precedents concerning criminal antecedents and document forgery. The State opposed bail by characterising the petitioner as a history-sheeter allegedly involved in serious offences across different jurisdictions. It also referred to a show cause notice issued in connection with alleged GST violations and argued that the petitioner’s conduct amounted to an economic offence falling within the ambit of organised crime under Section 111 BNS. The prosecution additionally relied on allegations concerning forged NCLT orders and misrepresentation of an OTS arrangement. The principal question before the High Court was whether these allegations, considered alongside the material collected during investigation, justified the continued detention of the petitioner pending trial. The High Court found that the FIR did not prima facie disclose the essential ingredients of organised crime under Section 111 BNS. It observed that the provision contemplates offences having a wider social impact and that ordinary cheating involving a monetary amount cannot, merely on that account, be classified as an economic offence within the meaning of the provision. The Court emphasised that the existence of an organised crime syndicate and the accused’s involvement in such a syndicate were indispensable requirements. These elements were absent from the allegations contained in the FIR. Importantly, the Court held that mere issuance of a show cause notice under GST law does not automatically render a person a history-sheeter, reaffirming the presumption of innocence until conviction by a competent court. On the question of continued custody, the Court observed that the investigating authorities had already seized the petitioner’s electronic device allegedly used for communicating forged NCLT orders. The allegations of forgery and other criminal misconduct would require proof during trial and could not themselves establish the necessity of indefinite pre-trial detention. The Court also found no material demonstrating that the petitioner had previously been connected with another serious offence or was the kingpin of any criminal racket. Although the complainant relied on judicial precedents concerning the seriousness of forged court documents and criminal antecedents, the High Court distinguished their application to the circumstances of the bail application. More than 40 days had elapsed since arrest without a charge-sheet being filed, leaving uncertainty regarding commencement of the criminal trial. The Court further observed that the remedy for non-payment of contractual dues lay within the realm of civil disputes and did not, by itself, warrant refusal of bail. Accordingly, the High Court allowed the bail application subject to a bond of ₹20,000 with two sureties of an equivalent amount each, including one local surety. The petitioner was directed not to leave the territorial jurisdiction of Karaya Police Station except for specified court-related or investigative purposes, to report to the Investigating Officer once every fortnight, and to surrender his passport. He was also prohibited from contacting, threatening or inducing witnesses or tampering with evidence. The ruling reinforces the distinction between contractual monetary disputes, allegations requiring determination at criminal trial, and the statutory requirements of organised crime. It also clarifies that a GST show cause notice, without supporting evidence of criminal antecedents, cannot independently justify branding an accused as a history-sheeter or refusing bail. The petitioner in the present application for bail was arrested on allegations of offences committed under Sections 316(2)/318(4)/336(3)/338/351(2) of the BNS, 2023 with Section 111(2)(b) of the said Sanhita being added subsequently. 2. Learned senior counsel appearing for the petitioner/accused person contends that the allegations made are of a civil nature and emanate from an agreement having ingredients of a development agreement and investment, which contains an arbitration clause. 3. By placing reliance on the application under Sections 175(3) of the BNSS, which was the genesis of the FIR, it is contended that the same does not contain any plausible link/chain of events connecting the petitioner with any serious crime whatsoever. It is argued that although the allegations involve cheating with regard to the non-payment of an amount of Rs.10 crore, the said amount was inflated subsequently in the FIR to Rs.32 crore. 4. Learned senior counsel next contends that the alleged suppression of an NCLT proceeding at the relevant point of time does not make any material difference insofar as the jural relationship and the transactions of the parties are concerned. 5. Learned counsel next contends that the de facto complainant also alleged that certain forged orders of the NCLT (National Company Law Tribunal) were communicated by the accused/petitioner to the de facto However, since no advantage was taken at any point of time in view of such forged communications, the mere communication of such orders do not constitute an offence in any event. 6. Learned senior counsel places reliance on a Standard Operative Procedure (SOP) in respect of organized crime under Section 111 of the BNS and argues that the basic ingredients thereof are not satisfied by the FIR. Moreover, as per the said provisions and the SOP, the Organized Crime Assessment Note (OCAN) has to be proved by an officer not below the rank of DSP and/or ACP, which was not done in the present case, since the same was forwarded at the behest of the Investigating Officer. 7. Learned senior counsel places reliance on a judgment of a learned Single Judge of this Court in CRR 4132 of 2009 (Shri Suresh Kumar Singhania vs. State of West Bengal & Anr.) to argue that the principle of „caveat emptor‟ is also applicable in a criminal proceeding. 8. In reply, the learned senior counsel for the de facto complainant cites Zeba Khan vs. State of U.P. and Others reported at (2026) SCC OnLine SC 188 for the proposition that the criminal antecedents of an accused person and the fact as to whether the accused is a history-sheeter is to be taken into consideration while adjudicating an application for bail. Learned senior counsel also cites Naveen Singh vs. State of Uttar Pradesh & Another reported at (2021) 6 SCC 191 in support of the proposition that even if a document of court is forged and/or manipulated without the accused person extracting any benefit of the same, such a fact constitutes a criminal offence and is liable to be punished seriously. 9. Learned senior counsel further submits that during the course of investigation, subsequent materials have come up to show that the accused/petitioner also perpetrated other offences, including the forgery of signatures in other agreements. 10. It is next argued by the de facto complainant that from a perusal of the FIR, it would be evident that a strong case has been made out against the petitioner, sufficient to entail rejection of bail. 11. Learned Additional Public Prosecutor, appearing for the State, places reliance on the materials before the Court as well as the case diary to seek to impress upon the Court that the petitioner is a history-sheeter and serious offences have been revealed in different jurisdictions against the petitioner. It is further contended that due to serious GST violations, a show-cause notice has also been issued to the petitioner. 12. Learned counsel further argues that in view of the grave economic offences perpetrated by the petitioner, the petitioner comes within the purview of “organized crime” as contemplated under Section 111 of the BNS. It is further submitted that the petitioner has taken the benefit of forgery of the NCLT order as well as misrepresentation regarding the OTS (One Time Settlement) Scheme. 13. Upon hearing learned counsel for the parties, it transpires that from the contents of the FIR, which is the premise of the present investigation, prima facie ingredients of “organized crime” as contemplated of Section 111 are not made out. To constitute the said offence, there has to be a wide social impact of the crime, not merely against individuals in personam but against the society at large. The nature of offences listed in sub-section (1) of Section 111 itself is sufficient indication of the level of the crimes which come within the ambit of the said provision. Ordinary cheating of a monetary amount cannot come within the expression “economic offence” as incorporated in the said provision. 14. As per the Explanation to sub-section (1) of Section 111 as well as the SOP furnished before the Court, the existence of an organized crime syndicate and the involvement of the accused in such syndicate is a sine qua non to come within the purview of organized crime, which is entirely absent in the allegations made as per the FIR. Mere issuance of a show-cause notice under the GST Act does not ipso facto render a person a “history-sheeter”. It is well-settled that a person has to be deemed innocent till convicted by a competent court of law. 15. Even otherwise, the State and/or the de facto complainant have failed to impress upon this Court as to any circumstance to justify further incarceration of the petitioner. The Court is apprised that the electronic device of the petitioner, from which the communications of forged orders were allegedly made, has already been seized from the petitioner by the investigating authorities. 16. It is absurd to say that the petitioner will preserve carefully in his custody the hard copies of such communications for the investigating officers to merrily go and seize the same, that too, after more than a month has elapsed from the apprehension of the petitioner. 17. Whatever is the nature of the allegations against the petitioner, those are to be proved on trial by evidence. However, in the facts of the instant case, this court does not find any reason to further keep the petitioner in custody. 18. Insofar as the proposition laid down in Zeba Khan (supra) is concerned, nothing has been brought before this Court to show that the petitioner has been previously connected to any other serious offence or that the petitioner is actually the “kingpin” of any racket involving criminal activities. 19. Thus, it would be too high a proposition to label the petitioner a “history-sheeter” and refuse bail on such ground, at least at this stage. 20. Insofar as the proposition laid down in Naveen Singh (supra) is concerned, the allegation of forgery of orders of the NCLT has to be established by the State in the criminal trial which is to be faced by the petitioner upon furnishing of a charge-sheet. 21. Since even after the expiry of more than 40 days from arrest no charge-sheet has been filed, although relevant materials have apparently been seized from the petitioner, it is uncertain as to when the criminal trial will commence. 22. That apart, the remedy in respect of non-payment of the dues of the de facto complainant travels into the realm of a civil dispute and per se does not justify refusal of bail. 23. Taking into consideration the aforesaid circumstances, CRM (M) 2939 of 2026 is allowed on contest, granting bail to the petitioner on condition that the petitioner shall furnish bond of Rs.20,000/- with two sureties of like amount each, one of whom must be local, to the satisfaction of the learned Additional Chief Judicial Magistrate, Serampore, Hooghly, and on further condition that the petitioner shall not leave the territorial jurisdiction of the Karaya Police Station, within which the petitioner resides, except for the purpose of compliance of any direction passed by any court in any criminal proceeding and/or by the Investigating Officer, during the period of investigation and shall make himself available once in a fortnight before the Investigating Officer in charge of the investigation, until further orders. Furthermore, the petitioner shall also surrender his passport in terms of the undertaking given in the present bail application to the Investigating Officer subject to the outcome of the investigation and the criminal trial, if any. 24. The petitioner shall not seek to induce, threaten, and/or contact any of the witnesses/prospective witnesses or any person connected with the criminal trial which might ensue from the FIR and/or try to tamper with the evidence in any manner whatsoever. 25.

Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); Principal Commissioner of CGST & Central Excise, Lucknow Vs Wast Industries (CESTAT Allahabad) Summary: The Customs, Excise and Service Tax Appellate Tribunal (CESTAT), Allahabad, dismissed the Revenue’s appeal against Wast Industries concerning alleged clandestine manufacture and removal of scented chewing tobacco. The dispute arose from investigations conducted by the Directorate General of GST Intelligence (DGGI), Ghaziabad Regional Unit, into the activities of Wast Industries and K.G. Pan Products Pvt. Ltd. The Department alleged that substantial quantities of chewing tobacco and pan masala had been manufactured, transported and sold without accounting for the transactions or discharging applicable tax liabilities. The proceedings involved demands of Basic Excise Duty (BED) of ₹24,86,275 and National Calamity Contingent Duty (NCCD) of ₹13,37,31,674 under Show Cause Notice No. 112/2022-23 dated 4 May 2023. The investigation covered searches at several premises connected with the manufacturers, their alleged distributors and transporters. The Department relied principally on four categories of material: 30 loose handwritten sheets recovered from a Varanasi godown; WhatsApp messages and images obtained from a mobile phone during a Delhi search; Tally accounting data retrieved from a laptop at Prayagraj; and records relating to transportation of printed packaging laminates recovered during an investigation involving a transporter. According to the Department, these records established unaccounted procurement of packing materials, clandestine production, movement of finished goods and corresponding duty evasion. The Department also relied on statements of persons allegedly connected with the manufacturers, distributors and dealers. The adjudicating authority had partly confirmed the demands while dropping BED of ₹14,33,722 and NCCD of ₹8,07,40,439. Both the Department and the noticee challenged the adjudication. The Commissioner (Appeals), Lucknow, by order dated 13 February 2025, rejected the departmental appeal and allowed the noticee’s appeal. The Revenue consequently approached the Tribunal, contending that the appellate authority had wrongly discounted documentary and electronic evidence, witness statements and records indicating clandestine transactions. It maintained that the absence of formal invoices, signatures or written agreements should not defeat an investigation into activities that were deliberately concealed. Examining the Varanasi records, the Tribunal observed that the 30 handwritten sheets did not identify Wast Industries, the author of the entries, the precise goods involved or their commercial particulars. Expressions such as “Bora”, “Punch” and numerical combinations could not independently establish the description, quantity, origin or taxable movement of chewing tobacco. The Tribunal also found no satisfactory verification demonstrating that repeated entries had not been counted more than once. It emphasised that substantial fiscal liabilities could not be computed by successively assuming that unidentified figures represented goods, that the goods were chewing tobacco, that they were manufactured by Wast Industries and that they had been cleared without payment of duty. Regarding WhatsApp messages and images recovered during the Delhi search, the Tribunal identified deficiencies in the documentation of electronic extraction, preservation and identification. The Panchnama did not satisfactorily explain the particular messages retrieved, the accounts involved, the extraction procedure, the integrity of the electronic material or the subsequent preservation of the printouts. The Tribunal considered the statutory safeguards governing electronic evidence under Section 36B of the Central Excise Act, 1944 and found that the evidentiary foundation had not been satisfactorily established. Independently, the Department had failed to connect the handwritten slips appearing in the images with any authorised representative of Wast Industries or with actual identifiable consignments. The Tribunal similarly rejected the Department’s attempt to establish clandestine clearances through third-party Tally records retrieved at Prayagraj. The laptop had not been recovered from the manufacturer’s factory or registered premises, and the accounts were maintained under the name “Jai Bajrang Bali”. Questions concerning the laptop’s identification, ownership, custody and integrity remained unresolved. The Tribunal distinguished a possible marketing or distribution relationship from participation in a clandestine supply chain. Statements concerning the marketing of particular brands did not, without supporting evidence, establish the unaccounted manufacture and clearance of the quantities alleged in the show cause notice. The Tribunal further considered deficiencies in the supply of relied-upon documents, including the statement dated 30 December 2022 and the corresponding Panchnama. It held that a statement relied upon to interpret electronic records or calculate duty liability had to be furnished to the noticee. A summary of the statement in the show cause notice could not substitute for the document itself. The Tribunal referred to Kothari Filaments Vs Commissioner of Customs (Port), Kolkata in discussing the requirement that material relied upon in quasi-judicial proceedings be disclosed to the affected party. It also explained that failure to retract a statement did not automatically make the statement conclusive evidence of clandestine manufacture by another person. On the allegation concerning unaccounted printed laminates, the Tribunal examined the Department’s reliance on transport records and assumptions regarding the conversion of packing materials into finished chewing tobacco. It found that the proposed duty liability depended upon successive presumptions concerning the actual recipient of the laminates, their use in manufacture, the quantity of finished products and their subsequent clandestine removal. The Tribunal reiterated that the evidentiary chain must establish relevant facts such as unaccounted procurement of raw materials, additional production, electricity consumption, labour, packing, transportation, buyers and receipt of sale proceeds. Third-party documents, without sufficient corroboration, could not establish these constituent elements. Relying on the principles discussed in Continental Cement Company Vs Union of India, Oudh Sugar Mills Ltd. Vs Union of India and other judicial decisions, the Tribunal held that allegations of clandestine manufacture and removal must rest on positive, cogent and reliable evidence rather than market assumptions, unverified calculations or private records alone. It found that the Revenue had not demonstrated perversity or legal infirmity in the concurrent findings of the adjudicating authority and Commissioner (Appeals). Accordingly, the Tribunal upheld the appellate order and dismissed the Revenue’s appeal. The decision underscores that electronic records and third-party statements may justify investigation, but a substantial excise duty demand requires a reliable evidentiary connection between the material recovered and the alleged taxable clearances. We have carefully considered the impugned order, the SCN, the relied upon documents, the grounds of appeal, the written submissions made by the Respondent and the records relating to the searches conducted at Varanasi and Delhi. The principal issue requiring consideration is whether the allegation and quantification of alleged clandestine/unaccounted clearances attributed to M/s Wast Industries can legally be sustained on the basis of the loose handwritten sheets recovered during search at Varanasi, the printouts of WhatsApp messages/images, allegedly retrieved from the mobile phone of Shri Salil Bhardwaj during the search proceedings at Delhi, data retrieved from the laptop recovered at the premises at Allahabad and data recovered in the file from the vehicle at residential premises of Shri Sujeet Kumar Singh. Now, we proceed to discuss the issues one by one sequentially. 5. We first take up the demand in question which has been substantially quantified by treating the entries appearing in 30 loose handwritten sheets recovered during the search at Varanasi as representing receipts/loading/unloading of goods allegedly supplied by M/s Wast Industries. We have therefore examined the said documents as mentioned in the SCN as scanned image. 6. Ld. Commissioner (Appeals), on examination of the said loose sheets, has noticed that these are unsigned handwritten papers containing certain dates, numbers, references to loading/unloading of “Bora” and certain payment-related figures. Significantly, nowhere on these sheets is the name of M/s Wast Industries mentioned. The documents do not bear the signature of any director, partner, employee, authorised representative or other person connected with either of the aforesaid manufacturers. There is also no indication on the face of these documents to show that they were prepared by, for, or under instructions from M/s Wast Industries. 7. We further notice that the nature of the goods allegedly represented by the entries is itself not disclosed. Neither “Pan Masala” nor “Chewing Tobacco” is mentioned on the pages relied upon by the Department. There is no description of brand, MRP, pouch size, weight, number of pouches contained in a bag, type of packing, value per bag or any other identifiable commercial particular from which it could reasonably be concluded that the numbers recorded therein represented Pan Masala or Chewing Tobacco manufactured by the Respondent. Certain pages make reference to figures such as “150”, “150/75”, “120/60” and similar numerical expressions. At page No.11, certain entries pertaining to August, 2021 have been referred to and the expression “Punch” appears at the top corner. Similar entries are stated to appear at page No.14 for September, 2021 and at page Nos.18 and 21. However, the mere presence of the expression “Punch” or numerical expressions such as “150/75” or “120/60”, without any contemporaneous evidence explaining what the figures denote, cannot by itself establish the description, quantity or origin of the goods. An interpretation assigned by the investigating agency cannot substitute proof of what the author of the document actually intended those figures to represent. 8. We are of the opinion that before a fiscal liability can be computed from private papers, the Department must first establish that every entry represents an independent transaction and that duplicated/repeated entries have not been counted more than once. The record does not satisfactorily demonstrate such verification. Thus, apart from the question of admissibility and nexus, the very arithmetical basis of quantification is rendered doubtful. It is also noticeable that the Panchnama does not identify with precision the particular location within the searched premises from which these 30 loose sheets were recovered. When unsigned and unidentified loose papers are sought to be made the principal foundation for a substantial demand against persons whose names do not appear thereon, the circumstances of their recovery, authorship and custody become material. The omission assumes greater significance when there is no independent evidence demonstrating that the documents belonged to or were maintained on behalf of M/s Wast Industries. 9. In the present matter, no satisfactory evidence has been brought on record demonstrating corresponding procurement of unaccounted raw material by M/s Wast Industries, additional consumption of electricity, utilisation of additional labour, excess manufacture, stock discrepancy commensurate with the alleged clearances, transportation of the particular goods from the factory to the alleged destination, identification of vehicles/drivers for individual alleged transactions, receipt of the goods by identified buyers or flow-back of unaccounted sale consideration. In such circumstances, the loose sheets can at best constitute material requiring further investigation; they cannot, standing by themselves, constitute proof of taxable clandestine clearances. 10. We, therefore, find no legally sustainable basis for converting the unidentified number of “Bora” recorded in the loose sheets into quantities of Pan Masala/Chewing Tobacco attributable specifically to M/s Wast Industries and thereafter computing tax upon such assumed quantities. Each stage in this exercise involves an assumption: first, that the entry represents goods; secondly, that the goods are Chewing Tobacco; thirdly, that they were manufactured by the Appellants; fourthly, that the numerical entry represents a particular packing/quantity; and fifthly, that the goods were cleared without payment of tax. A fiscal demand cannot be sustained by stacking one unproved assumption upon another. 11. It is settled that a serious charge of clandestine manufacture and removal cannot be sustained merely upon assumptions drawn from private papers without sufficient corroborative evidence. The Hon’ble Allahabad High Court in Continental Cement Company vs. Union of India, 2014 (309) E.L.T. 411 (All.), emphasised that clandestine removal is a serious charge which has to be established by sufficient and tangible evidence and that relevant circumstances such as procurement of raw material, manufacture, electricity consumption, labour, transportation, buyers and flow of sale proceeds assume importance. 12. We now turn to the second category of material, namely the photocopies/printouts of WhatsApp messages/images allegedly retrieved from the mobile phone of Shri Salil Bhardwaj during search proceedings at Delhi on 27.09.2021 and collectively relied upon as RUD-14 in the SCN. The Panchnama records in substance that during the course of search certain WhatsApp messages/images were retrieved from the mobile phone of Shri Salil Bhardwaj and were confronted to him during his statement. However, the Panchnama does not disclose the particulars which are essential for establishing the provenance and integrity of the electronic material subsequently relied upon for determination of tax liability. There is no clear contemporaneous narration regarding which particular WhatsApp chats were accessed; the mobile numbers/accounts between which the communications took place; the total number of messages/images retrieved; the relevant dates and timestamps; the process or software through which the material was extracted; whether the extraction was undertaken directly from the device or through another computer; whether any forensic image or clone of the device was prepared; whether any hash value or other integrity marker was generated; the place where printouts were taken; or how the retrieved electronic material and its printouts were thereafter preserved. The importance of these omissions becomes apparent from Section 36 B of Central Excise Act, 1944. In relation to electronic/computer-generated evidence, Section 36 contemplates statutory safeguards regarding identification of the document, the manner in which it was produced and particulars of the device involved in its production. The record and the reasoning contained in the impugned order do not satisfactorily establish observance of these safeguards in respect of the WhatsApp printouts relied upon for quantification. 13. Even independently of the technical requirements governing electronic evidence, the basic question of authorship and nexus remains unanswered. The printouts contain images of handwritten slips, but the persons who wrote those slips have not been identified in relation to the individual entries. The handwriting is stated to be of more than one person. There is no material establishing that any of those handwritten slips were prepared by an employee, director, manager or authorised representative of M/s Wast Industries. Equally, none of the printouts relied upon has been shown to bear the signature or endorsement of any director, manager, employee or authorised representative of the said manufacturers acknowledging the correctness of the entries. No evidence has been brought on record to demonstrate that the messages/images were created at the instance of, sent under instructions from, or approved by any responsible official of M/s Wast Industries. 14. It is further noticed that the photocopies of the WhatsApp printouts forming part of RUD-14 do not bear the signatures of the panch witnesses. We would not, merely from absence of signatures, conclusively hold that the Panch witnesses were not present at any stage of the proceedings. However, in the absence of any narration in the Panchnama describing the retrieval and printing process, the absence of signatures of the panch witnesses on the printouts deprives the Department of an important contemporaneous assurance that the particular printouts relied upon in the SCN were the same material actually retrieved and witnessed during the search. Thus, there are two distinct defects in the Department’s reliance upon the Delhi material. The first relates to provenance, extraction and preservation of the electronic record; the second relates to the substantive nexus of the contents with M/s Wast Industries. Electronic evidence does not acquire a higher evidentiary status merely because it is found in a mobile phone. The Department was still required to establish the identity of the persons involved, the meaning of the entries, the connection of those entries with the Appellants, and the actual movement of identifiable taxable goods. These fundamental links cannot be supplied by conjecture. 15. We are conscious that clandestine transactions are ordinarily not recorded in statutory books and that the Department is entitled to rely upon circumstantial evidence. However, circumstantial evidence must nevertheless form a complete and coherent chain leading to the conclusion alleged. It cannot consist merely of unidentified figures on unsigned sheets, followed by assumptions regarding the commodity, manufacturer, packing and quantity. The standard applicable in departmental adjudication is not identical to the standard governing a criminal prosecution. Nevertheless, where serious fiscal consequences and penalties are sought to be imposed on the allegation of deliberate suppression and clandestine clearance, the evidence must possess sufficient probative value to establish the allegation on a preponderance of probability based upon reliable material. A probability itself must arise from proved circumstances and not from a succession of conjectures. On an overall appreciation of the evidence, we find that the Department has failed to establish a dependable evidentiary bridge between the anonymous/unsigned loose sheets recovered at Varanasi, the handwritten images contained in the WhatsApp printouts allegedly retrieved at Delhi, and actual unaccounted manufacture and clearance of Chewing Tobacco by M/s Wast Industries. Consequently, the quantification made by converting the figures appearing in such loose sheets/WhatsApp images into quantities of Chewing Tobacco and attributing the same to the Respondent is based substantially upon inference and presumption rather than proved transactions. Such quantification cannot be sustained in law. Once the principal quantification of alleged clandestine clearances founded upon the aforesaid documents fails, the consequential demand of tax attributable thereto cannot survive. Interest, being consequential to the principal tax liability, also cannot survive to that extent. Likewise, penalty founded upon the allegation of deliberate clandestine clearance/suppression cannot be sustained where the foundational allegation itself remains unproved. 16. Now coming to the search conducted at Prayagraj, the principal controversy in the present appeals is whether the alleged clandestine manufacture and clearance of Chewing Tobacco by M/s Wast Industries can be sustained primarily on the basis of Tally data retrieved from an HP laptop found during search of the premises situated at 397B, Dasrath Market, Mewa Lal Bagia Tiraha, Naini, Prayagraj, coupled with the statements of Shri Satish Chandra Srivastava, Shri Prateek Bansal and certain dealers of the Prayagraj region. 17. The Revenue has challenged the concurrent findings of the Adjudicating Authority and the Commissioner (Appeals), essentially contending that the electronic records maintained in the fictitious name of “Jai Bajrang Bali”, when read with the statements of Shri Satish Chandra Srivastava, Shri Prateek Bansal and the dealers, constitute sufficient evidence to establish that the transactions reflected therein were unaccounted supplies of Pan Masala and Chewing Tobacco manufactured by the respondent manufacturers. We find, however, that the grounds raised before us substantially reiterate the very allegations contained in the SCN without satisfactorily answering the fundamental defects in the evidence noticed by both the Authorities below. It is not in dispute that the laptop in question was not recovered from the factory or registered premises of M/s Wast Industries. It was found at the premises at Naini, Prayagraj, which was admittedly occupied by persons other than the Respondent manufacturer. Neither the Tally ledgers nor the sale and purchase registers retrieved therefrom bear the name of M/s Wast Industries. On the contrary, the accounts are stated to have been maintained under the name of a purportedly fictitious concern, namely “Jai Bajrang Bali”. 18. Much emphasis has been placed by the Revenue upon the statement dated 08.12.2021 of Shri Prateek Bansal. According to the Revenue, Shri Bansal admitted that Shri Amar Tulsiyan was his maternal uncle, Shri Deepak Khemka was his relative and that he was looking after marketing of Suddh Plus and Panchmukhi brands in the Prayagraj region. He also allegedly stated that he coordinated delivery of goods to dealers and, in certain cases, collection of payments. 19. Even if the aforesaid statement is taken at its face value for the present purpose, it may establish that Shri Prateek Bansal had some connection with marketing or distribution of the brands. It does not automatically establish that he was managing a parallel clandestine supply chain involving unaccounted clearances of the magnitude quantified in the SCN. A lawful marketing or distribution relationship and participation in clandestine removals are two entirely different propositions. The latter requires independent evidence. 20. We find no appointment letter, agency agreement, remuneration record, commission payment, correspondence, email, WhatsApp communication from the Respondent manufacturers, bank record, stock transfer record, delivery instruction or other contemporaneous document establishing that Shri Prateek Bansal had been entrusted by either Respondent with control over an unaccounted distribution network. 21. Revenue has argued that no formal agreement should be expected because M/s Wast Industries are comparatively small industries and persons engaged in clandestine activity deliberately avoid formal documentation. We are unable to accept such reasoning. The absence of documentary evidence cannot itself be converted into evidence of clandestine clearance by presuming that documents were deliberately not created because the activity was clandestine. Such reasoning is circular: clandestine activity cannot first be assumed in order to explain absence of evidence and thereafter the same absence of evidence be relied upon to prove clandestine clearances. 22. Equally significant is the fact noticed by the Adjudicating Authority that statements of persons connected with the manufacturers, including Shri Amar Tulsiyan and Shri Pradeep Rungta, did not acknowledge the relationship with Shri Prateek Bansal in the manner suggested by the investigation. The investigation was required to resolve this contradiction by independent documentary evidence instead of selecting only those statements which supported the proposed allegation. 23. Revenue has further submitted that certain dealers in the Prayagraj region admitted purchase of accounted as well as unaccounted Pan Masala/Tobacco through Shri Prateek Bansal. However, generalized statements regarding purchase of accounted and unaccounted goods cannot validate every entry in a private Tally account extending over a period of more than three years. We notice that Revenue itself relies upon the assertion that certain dealers made payments by RTGS/NEFT directly into accounts connected with the manufacturers. Payments made through identified banking channels cannot automatically be characterized as consideration for clandestine clearances. It was necessary to compare such payments with the statutory books of both the seller and purchaser and establish that a particular banking transaction represented consideration for goods not covered by an invoice. No such comprehensive reconciliation has been demonstrated. 24. We next consider the evidentiary foundation of the laptop data itself. The Adjudicating Authority has noticed discrepancies between the Panchnama and FORM GST INS-02 dated 08.12.2021, including the difference in the serial number/identification particulars of the laptop. It has also been noticed that there is no satisfactory record regarding sealing of the laptop and its subsequent custody. 25. These are not insignificant procedural irregularities in the facts of the present case. Where tax and duties running into several hundred crores of rupees are proposed to be determined principally from data contained in one electronic device, correct identification of the device from which such data originated and preservation of its integrity throughout the period of custody are fundamental. Section 36 of the Central Excise Act specifically deals with admissibility of computer printouts and electronically stored information. Section 36 contemplates a certificate identifying the document containing the statement, describing the manner in which it was produced and giving appropriate particulars of the device involved in production of the document. 26. In the present case, the objections regarding the identity of the laptop, the discrepancy in serial numbers, absence of satisfactory evidence regarding ownership and operation of the laptop and absence of proper evidence regarding sealing and preservation acquire greater importance when examined in the light of Section 36. The mere fact that an investigating officer himself operated a computer and printed certain pages cannot make the underlying information self-proving when the statutory safeguards governing electronic evidence and the foundational facts concerning the device remain unestablished. 27. We may also refer to the recent decision of the Kolkata Bench of this Tribunal in Rashmi Cement Limited vs. Commissioner in Excise Appeal No.75100/2025, decided on 20.08.2025. While dealing with allegations of clandestine clearances based upon private records and electronic data, the Tribunal held that electronic documents recovered from computers/pen drives without the prescribed statutory certification could not be relied upon and further reiterated that third-party private records, in the absence of corroboration regarding production, buyers, transportation and flow of consideration, were insufficient for establishing clandestine manufacture and removal. The same approach has been reiterated recently in Bhardwaj Steels Private Limited vs. Principal Commissioner, CGST & Central Excise, Ranchi, in Excise Appeal No.75188/2022 Final Order Nos.75497-75498/2026 dated 16.04.2026, where this Tribunal again considered the evidentiary requirements relating to electronic records and reiterated that clandestine removal cannot be established merely from private/electronic records without independent evidence completing the chain of manufacture, removal, buyers, transportation and consideration. 28. The Adjudicating Authority has also specifically noticed that the investigation did not determine whether the laptop belonged to Shri Hemant Kumar, Shri Satish Chandra Srivastava, the office establishment, Shri Prateek Bansal or any other person. No purchase invoice or other ownership document appears to have been sought. This assumes relevance because the person responsible for creation and regular maintenance of the data itself has to be satisfactorily identified before the contents can be attributed to other persons. 29. The Revenue’s assertion that the laptop belonged to Shri Satish Chandra Srivastava is itself insufficient unless supported by contemporaneous record. More importantly, even ownership of the laptop by Shri Satish Chandra Srivastava would not prove that entries therein were made upon instructions of M/s Wast Industries. Ownership of the device, authorship of the entries and truth of the transactions reflected therein are separate evidentiary stages. 30. We further find considerable force in the observation of the lower authorities that no primary documents were recovered showing the source from which the alleged sale and purchase figures were fed into the Tally software. No corresponding order sheets, invoices, delivery slips, goods receipt notes, transport receipts, cash books or payment sheets constituting the source documents for the entries were recovered. Revenue’s explanation is that the information was communicated primarily through telephone calls. If that was indeed the Department’s case, it was open to the investigating agency to correlate at least representative entries with call detail records, messages, transport instructions, statements of drivers, receipt of goods by dealers or the corresponding payment trail. No such meaningful correlation has been demonstrated. 31. A computer entry is only a representation of information entered into a system. Unless the source and meaning of the data are established, existence of the entry does not establish the truth of every commercial transaction represented by it. The Tally data could certainly constitute an investigative lead, but an investigative lead cannot itself become the final proof of a clandestine clearance unless the subsequent investigation validates it. Another important aspect concerns the Panchnama dated 30.12.2022 and the statement of Shri Prateek Bansal dated 30.12.2022. The Adjudicating Authority noticed that the Panchnama, under which the material used for computation was allegedly generated, was not itself made a relied upon document and that the statement dated 30.12.2022 was also not actually supplied in the set of RUDs. 32. Revenue seeks to explain that the statement dated 30.12.2022 was intended to be RUD-25, but because of an inadvertent scanning error RUD-24 was scanned twice, while the contents of the statement were substantially reproduced in paragraph 14.6 of the SCN. 33. We are unable to treat this as an inconsequential clerical error. If a statement is relied upon to interpret the data, determine conversion factors or otherwise form the basis of quantification, the noticee is entitled to the statement itself and not merely the investigating officer’s summary of the statement in the SCN. The exact questions put to the witness, the precise answers given, qualifications or explanations contained therein and the context in which the answers were recorded may have a direct bearing on the defence. The Supreme Court has emphasized in Kothari Filaments vs. Commissioner of Customs (Port), Kolkata 2009 (233) E.L.T. 289 (S.C.) that a quasi-judicial Authority cannot base its decision upon material known only to the authority and not furnished to the affected party. A later explanation in departmental appeal that a document was omitted through inadvertence does not retrospectively provide the noticee the opportunity which was required to be available while meeting the SCN. 34. The position is even more significant regarding the Panchnama dated 30.12.2022. Even assuming that the resulting printouts were placed at RUD-37, the Panchnama recording the circumstances in which those printouts were generated constitutes an important part of the chain of evidence. If the quantification is based upon a subsequent extraction, compilation or printing exercise conducted more than a year after the original seizure, the procedure followed during such exercise cannot be treated as irrelevant. 35. Revenue has laid considerable emphasis upon the fact that Shri Satish Chandra Srivastava and Shri Prateek Bansal did not retract their statements. We agree that absence of retraction is a circumstance which may be taken into account while assessing the evidentiary value of a statement. However, non-retraction does not elevate a third-party statement into conclusive proof of clandestine manufacture and removal by another person. Even an un-retracted statement has to be appreciated in the context of the entire evidence. More importantly, it cannot cure a defective electronic record, establish unidentified source documents, explain discrepancies in the identification of the laptop or substitute evidence of production, transportation and receipt of consideration. 36. In the present case, statements relied upon by Revenue can at best establish a marketing/distribution connection between certain persons and the brands manufactured by the Respondent. To translate such connection into a finding that Pan Masala valued at approximately Rs.191.90 crores and Tobacco valued at approximately Rs.28.40 crores were clandestinely manufactured and removed by the respective Respondent requires considerably more evidence. 37. The jurisdictional Allahabad High Court in Continental Cement Company vs. Union of India, 2014 (309) E.L.T. 411 (All.) held that clandestine removal is a serious charge which requires sufficient and tangible evidence and that, in the absence of evidence regarding matters such as raw material, electricity consumption, actual sale/removal and flow-back of consideration, the demand cannot be sustained merely upon assumptions and presumptions. The recent decisions of this Tribunal in Rashmi Cement Limited and Bhardwaj Steels Private Limited (supra) reiterate the same principle and specifically emphasize that private records/electronic data have to be corroborated through evidence regarding excess raw materials, production, buyers, transport and receipt of consideration before they can sustain a charge of clandestine removal. 38. In the present case, the investigation has not demonstrated corresponding procurement of unaccounted areca nut, tobacco, packing material or other principal inputs required for manufacture of the very substantial quantity of goods alleged to have been clandestinely cleared. No corresponding excess consumption of electricity has been established. No production study has been undertaken to demonstrate that the installed machinery and available working hours could accommodate the alleged accounted as well as unaccounted production. 39. There is also no evidence regarding deployment of additional labour, production shifts, packing operations or generation of manufacturing waste corresponding to such substantial suppressed production. These omissions become particularly material because the demand is not confined to a few identified clandestine transactions. The entire Tally record for a period extending from April, 2018 to November, 2021 has effectively been converted into suppressed turnover of the Respondent manufacturers. 40. Similarly, no transaction-wise evidence regarding outward transportation from the factories of the Respondents to Prayagraj has been produced. The investigation has not demonstrated, with respect even to representative transactions, the identity of the vehicles, drivers, transporters, loading particulars, freight payments, bilty/GR or delivery acknowledgements linking the alleged entries with actual removal of goods from the Respondents’ factories. Equally, there is no satisfactory evidence of the flow-back of the enormous consideration allegedly generated from the unaccounted clearances to either M/s Wast Industries or their responsible officers. No parallel cash book belonging to the Respondents, unexplained bank deposits, cash recoveries or other financial evidence corresponding to the alleged clandestine turnover has been identified. 41. It is significant that the demand against M/s Wast Industries is based upon alleged clandestine clearance of Tobacco with consequential duties/taxes/cess exceeding Rs.13 crores approx. The magnitude of a demand does not alter the applicable standard of proof; however, where hundreds of crores of rupees of liability is extrapolated from electronic records maintained at a third-party premises, it becomes all the more necessary for the investigation to test and corroborate the data through representative transactions. We find no satisfactory exercise whereby selected ledger entries have been traced from factory production to loading, transportation, receipt by the dealer and ultimate flow-back of consideration to either manufacturer. 42. The Department cannot merely proceed on the reasoning that, because some dealers dealt in the Respondents’ brands and Shri Prateek Bansal was associated with marketing of those brands, the entire database maintained under the fictitious account “Jai Bajrang Bali” must necessarily constitute the Respondents’ suppressed turnover. This involves precisely the type of inferential leap which the settled law relating to clandestine removal does not permit. 43. The last issue is related to clandestine supply of laminate to the Respondents. The entire case of the Revenue is founded upon File No.17, which was allegedly resumed during the course of search conducted at the residential premises of Shri Sujeet Kumar Singh, Director of M/s BTCPL. According to the Department, the said file contained particulars relating to the transportation of laminate allegedly supplied by M/s MSPL. Significantly, the entire demand has been built upon the premise that every entry recorded in the said file is genuine and accurately reflects the actual movement of goods. Proceeding on this premise, the Department has further presumed that the laminate mentioned in the said file was in fact transported, reached the factory premises of the Respondent, was duly received by the Respondent, consumed in the manufacture of chewing tobacco, and that the entire quantity of laminate so received was converted into finished goods. The Department has thereafter gone a step further by presuming that every pouch allegedly manufactured out of such laminate was clandestinely removed without payment of duty. Thus, the entire chain of conclusions drawn in the SCN rests exclusively upon the authenticity, correctness and evidentiary value of File No.17. Unless the entries contained in the said file are first established to be genuine, reliable and duly corroborated by independent evidence connecting them with the Respondent, the entire foundation of the Revenue’s case necessarily fails, as every subsequent inference recorded in the SCN is merely a consequential presumption flowing from the correctness of the said third-party document. 44. The principal document relied upon by the Department, namely File No.17, was admittedly neither recovered from the premises of the Respondent nor maintained by the Respondent in the ordinary course of its business. It is an undisputed fact that the said file was allegedly resumed during the search conducted at the residential premises of Shri Sujeet Kumar Singh, who claimed to be the owner of M/s BTCPL. The Department has sought to fasten liability upon the Respondent solely on the basis of the entries contained in the said third-party document without first establishing its authenticity, authorship and evidentiary value. Significantly, no evidence whatsoever has been brought on record to establish as to who prepared the entries contained in File No.17, when such entries were made, under whose instructions they were recorded, whether they formed part of the regular books or records of M/s BTCPL, whether they were maintained contemporaneously in the ordinary course of business, or whether the Respondent had ever accepted, acknowledged or acted upon the correctness of such entries. Equally, the Department has failed to establish the authorship of the entries through any independent evidence or by examining the person who actually prepared or maintained the document. It is a settled principle of law that a private document recovered from a third party does not automatically become admissible evidence against an assessee merely because the assessee’s name appears therein. Unless such document is independently proved, corroborated and linked with the assessee through legally admissible evidence, no civil or penal liability can be fastened upon the assessee merely on the basis of such third-party records. 45. The other crucial evidence is the sole statement of Shri Sujeet Kumar Singh recorded by the officers of DGGI on 25.06.2021. In the said statement, Shri Sujeet Kumar Singh himself categorically stated that he had sold M/s BTCPL along with approximately 140 trucks, to Shri Keshav Chandra Patra and Shri Madan Mohan Jena for a consideration of ₹6.5 crores, subject to the condition that the purchasers would continue paying the instalments of the vehicles. He further stated that as the purchasers failed to honour their commitments and defaulted in payment of the instalments, he subsequently took over the affairs of the company again. Surprisingly, he also admitted that no written agreement whatsoever had been executed in respect of such an alleged sale, stating only that the parties were known to each other. 46. These assertions, which formed the very foundation of the Revenue’s reliance on Shri Sujeet Kumar Singh’s statement and the documents allegedly recovered from him, were accepted by the investigating officers without any verification whatsoever. No investigation was undertaken to ascertain whether any such transaction of sale had in fact taken place. The records maintained by the Ministry of Corporate Affairs5 which could have conclusively established the shareholding pattern, directorship and management of M/s BTCPL during the relevant period, were admittedly never examined. It is inconceivable that a private limited company could be transferred merely through an oral understanding without execution of any agreement or without transfer of shares in accordance with the provisions of the Companies Act. The investigation is completely silent on these vital aspects. 47. If, as claimed by Shri Sujeet Kumar Singh, the shares of M/s BTCPL had in fact been transferred to Shri Madan Mohan Jeena and Shri Keshav Chandra Patra, then, in law, Shri Sujeet Kumar Singh would cease to have any control over the affairs, records or management of the company and would have no locus standi to speak on behalf of the company or produce its internal records. Conversely, if no such transfer of shares had taken place, his assertion regarding sale of the company itself stood falsified. Astonishingly, the Department made no efforts to verify either of these possibilities. Such a fundamental issue, going to the very root of ownership and custody of the records, remained completely uninvestigated. 48. Equally significant is the fact that although Shri Sujeet Kumar Singh alleged that Shri Madan Mohan Jeena and Shri Keshav Chandra Patra had defaulted in payment and that he had taken over the company again, the Department never examined why, despite such alleged default, Shri Jeena and Shri Patra continued to remain Directors of M/s BTCPL at the time of search. These glaring contradictions were never confronted to either Shri Madan Mohan Jeena or Shri Keshav Chandra Patra. No statement of either of them was recorded under Section 70 of the CGST Act, 2017, despite the fact that they were admittedly the Directors of the company and were the most competent persons to explain the management, custody of records and transportation activities of M/s BTCPL during the relevant period. Their complete non-examination leaves a substantial gap in the investigation and seriously undermines the credibility of the Revenue’s case. 49. The investigation is equally silent regarding the manner in which Shri Sujeet Kumar Singh was functioning after the alleged sale of the company. If Shri Jeena and Shri Patra were continuing as Directors, they would naturally be responsible for the day-to-day affairs of the company and for maintaining its statutory and commercial records. In such circumstances, the Department has not explained under what authority Shri Sujeet Kumar Singh was allegedly in possession of confidential records pertaining to the financial year 2020-21, particularly when those records were allegedly recovered from a vehicle parked in the parking area allotted to his residence. No investigation was carried out to ascertain whether any resolution of the Board of Directors, authorization or other legal authority existed enabling Shri Sujeet Kumar Singh to retain or access such records. These vital questions were neither put to Shri Sujeet Kumar Singh during his statement dated 25.06.2021 nor was he subsequently summoned to explain these glaring inconsistencies. The investigation, therefore, leaves unanswered the fundamental question regarding the lawful custody, origin and authenticity of the documents allegedly recovered from him. 50. The Revenue has further sought to explain the absence of any name, signature, initials or identifying mark of M/s BTCPL or its employees on pages 1 to 251 of File No.17 by contending that it was a confidential record maintained for covert transportation of printed laminates to various Pan Masala manufacturers. We find ourselves unable to accept such a sweeping assertion in the absence of any supporting evidence. Merely describing a document as “confidential” cannot dispense with the legal requirement of proving its authorship, authenticity and reliability. If the document was indeed maintained by M/s BTCPL in the ordinary course of its business, the Department was required to establish, through cogent evidence, who prepared it, under whose directions it was maintained, how it formed part of the business records of the transporter and what was the basis for treating the entries therein as truthful and reliable. The complete absence of any identifying particulars, signatures, initials, company stamp or other indicia of authorship only heightens the suspicion regarding the origin and authenticity of the document. A document of unknown authorship and uncertain provenance cannot acquire evidentiary sanctity merely because the Department chooses to describe it as a confidential record. Such an explanation, unsupported by any investigation or independent corroboration, is wholly insufficient to confer evidentiary value upon File No.17 or to sustain a demand involving serious civil and penal consequences. 51. Viewed from any angle, therefore, File No.17 remains an unverified third-party private document of doubtful origin and authorship, unsupported by any independent corroboration and recovered from a person whose own legal status, authority and relationship with M/s BTCPL remained unexplored. In the absence of a proper investigation establishing its authenticity and connecting it with the Respondent through reliable and legally admissible evidence, no adverse inference can be drawn against the Respondent on the basis of such a document alone. The very foundation upon which the Revenue has built its case is thus rendered legally unsustainable. 52. We find that the very foundation of the Revenue’s case suffers from a serious evidentiary infirmity. The demand of duty and NCCD aggregating to approximately ₹13 crores has not been founded upon any direct, independent or corroborative evidence establishing clandestine manufacture or removal by the Respondent. On the contrary, the entire demand has been constructed upon a chain of assumptions and presumptions originating from the entries contained in File No.17, admittedly recovered from the possession/premises of a third party. Even if, for the sake of argument, it is assumed that the entries contained in the said file pertain to transportation of printed laminates, such entries, by themselves, could not constitute evidence of clandestine manufacture and clearance of excisable goods by the Respondent. The investigation is conspicuously silent regarding the actual receipt of laminate at the Respondent’s factory. No unloading records, gate registers, weighbridge slips, inward registers, warehouse records, acknowledgement of receipt, inventory records or stock reconciliation statements have been relied upon. No officer or employee responsible for receiving the alleged consignments has been examined. No transporter has stated that the disputed consignments were actually unloaded at the Respondent’s premises. Thus, even the alleged delivery of laminate remains unproved. It is a trite law that an alleged movement of goods reflected in a third-party record cannot, by itself, establish actual delivery. Likewise, delivery cannot establish receipt, receipt cannot establish consumption, consumption cannot establish manufacture and manufacture cannot establish clandestine clearance. Every one of these stages constitutes an independent fact in issue requiring independent proof. The Department has failed to establish even the first link in this chain. 53. We further notice that, as recorded in paragraph 20.3 of the SCN, the officers, purportedly acting upon a suggestion made by Shri Rungta in his statement dated 29.11.2022, proceeded to determine the ratio of laminate allegedly consumed by M/s KGPPL and M/s Wast during the months of December, 2020, March, 2021, April, 2021 and May, 2021. On the basis of such exercise, a ratio of 13:7 was arrived at. Thereafter, the Department applied the said ratio to the aggregate quantity of 12,82,156.670 kgs of laminate and, purely by arithmetical apportionment, attributed 4,48,988.04 kgs of laminate to M/s Wast Industries. Significantly, there is no independent evidence demonstrating that the said quantity was actually supplied to, received by, or consumed in the factory of the Respondent. The attribution itself is, therefore, founded not upon proof of actual receipt or consumption but upon an assumed ratio derived from selected periods. 54. Having first presumed the quantity of laminate allegedly attributable to the Respondent on the basis of the aforesaid 13:7 ratio, the Department thereafter proceeded to make a further assumption that the entire quantity so attributed was necessarily utilised for manufacture of tobacco pouches, and on that basis calculated the number of pouches allegedly manufactured and clandestinely cleared. Thus, the quantification of the alleged clandestine production is not based upon any actual production record, seizure of unaccounted finished goods, evidence of receipt of raw materials, statements of buyers, transport documents relating to finished goods, or financial trail. It is, in substance, an extrapolation built upon an earlier extrapolation. Such a methodology converts an unverified assumption regarding allocation of laminate into a further presumption regarding manufacture, and thereafter into yet another presumption regarding clandestine clearance. 55. The allegations become even more untenable when the Department proceeds to raise an independent demand of approximately Rs.13 Cr. on Scented Chewing Tobacco. Admittedly, there is not a single piece of evidence collected during investigation regarding clandestine manufacture or clearance of tobacco. No investigation has been conducted regarding procurement of raw tobacco, kimam, flavours, menthol, edible lime, catechu or any other raw material required for manufacture of chewing tobacco. No supplier of tobacco has been examined. No purchase records have been scrutinized. No evidence has been produced regarding clandestine procurement of tobacco raw materials. In fact, the investigation in relation to tobacco is completely non-existent. 56. Similarly, there is no evidence regarding excess electricity consumption, deployment of additional manpower. Such massive production could never have been achieved without engaging a substantially larger workforce. Yet no attendance registers, wage records, overtime payments, contractor details, labour deployment records or statements of employees have been relied upon. The investigation proceeds as if hundreds of crores of pouches could be manufactured without any human intervention. The Department has also failed to establish transportation of the alleged clandestinely manufactured finished goods. No transporter has admitted transportation of such finished products. No vehicle carrying clandestine goods has ever been intercepted. No lorry receipts, e-way bills, loading slips or destination records have been produced. No buyer has been identified. No dealer has confirmed receipt of clandestinely manufactured goods. No market survey has been conducted. No sale proceeds have been traced. No flow-back of funds has been established. No unaccounted cash or investments have been discovered. Thus, every essential ingredient which the law requires to prove clandestine manufacture and removal is completely absent. 57. What emerges from the record is that the Department has attempted to establish one presumption upon another. The first presumption is that File No.17 correctly records transportation of laminate. The second presumption is that such laminate reached the Respondent’s factory. The third presumption is that it was received by the Respondent. The fourth presumption is that it was entirely consumed in manufacture. The fifth presumption is that the resulting goods were clandestinely removed. It is well settled that a presumption cannot be founded upon another presumption. Where the foundational fact itself remains unproved, every consequential inference necessarily collapses. 58. The Hon’ble Supreme Court and various High Courts have consistently held that allegations of clandestine manufacture and removal cannot be sustained on probabilities, assumptions, market perceptions or mathematical calculations. They must rest upon positive, cogent and independent evidence establishing every constituent element of the alleged clandestine activity. The present case falls woefully short of this settled legal standard. The Department has, in effect, sought to levy approximately ₹13 crores of duty by replacing legal proof with assumptions based on alleged market practice. Such an approach is fundamentally inconsistent with the scheme of the Central Excise Act, violates the settled principles governing burden of proof and cannot be countenanced in law. 59. Accordingly, we are of the considered view that the entire demand relating to Scented Chewing Tobacco is founded upon conjectures, successive presumptions and unverified assumptions rather than legally admissible evidence. The findings recorded by the Adjudicating Authority, as affirmed by the Commissioner (Appeals), therefore call for no interference, and the Revenue’s appeal deserves to be dismissed on this ground alone. 60. The Adjudicating Authority examined the entire evidence threadbare and concluded that the Department had failed to establish clandestine manufacture through legally admissible corroborative evidence. The Commissioner (Appeals), after independent appraisal of the record, concurred with those findings. The Revenue has not demonstrated that these concurrent findings suffer from perversity, misreading of evidence or disregard of any material record. 61. An appellate forum ordinarily does not interfere with concurrent findings of fact unless such findings are shown to be manifestly erroneous. No such circumstance exists in the present case. 62. After considering the evidence in its entirety, we find that the Revenue has failed to establish the essential ingredients necessary for sustaining an allegation of clandestine manufacture and removal. The investigation has proceeded substantially on assumptions arising from a third-party transport record without establishing the complete chain of evidence required in law. The Hon’ble Supreme Court in Oudh Sugar Mills Ltd. vs. Union of India, 1978 (2) E.L.T. (J 172) (S.C.) has similarly laid down the principle that a finding based merely upon assumptions and presumptions, unsupported by tangible evidence, cannot sustain a demand. The principle is directly relevant here because the essential link between the numerical entries and the alleged taxable clearances has not been independently established. Reference may also appropriately be made to Common Cause (A Registered Society) vs. Union of India, wherein the Hon’ble Supreme Court cautioned against treating entries contained in loose sheets, not forming part of regularly maintained books of account, as substantive proof without reliable supporting material. Although that decision arose in a different statutory setting, the evidentiary principle that loose, unverified entries cannot acquire conclusive character merely because figures are recorded thereon remains relevant while appreciating such material. 63. More recently, in M/s Prabhat Zarda Factory Overseas vs. Commissioner, CGST, Noida, in Excise Appeal No.70638-70640/2025 Final Order No.70543-70545/2025 decided on 06.08.2025, the Tribunal reiterated in a matter concerning allegations of clandestine removal that third-party/private records cannot by themselves sustain the charge in the absence of corroboration regarding excess procurement of raw material, production, electricity consumption and other affirmative evidence. We find the principle particularly relevant to the facts before me. 64. In view of the foregoing discussion, we are of the considered opinion that Revenue has failed to establish the necessary chain beginning with unaccounted procurement of raw material, excess manufacture, packing and removal from the Respondents’ factory and extending to transportation, delivery to the identified recipient and eventual flow-back of the sale proceeds. In the absence of such corroboration, the demand founded upon the third-party electronic records remains based substantially upon assumptions and inferences. 65. We, therefore, find no perversity or legal infirmity in the findings recorded by the Commissioner (Appeals). The impugned appellate order is legal, proper and calls for no interference. 66. The appeal filed by the Revenue is, therefore, dismissed.

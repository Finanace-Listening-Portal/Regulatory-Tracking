(Delhi High Court) Summary: The Delhi High Court, comprising Justice Anil Kshetarpal and Justice Shail Jain, adjudicated a batch of 22 appeals preferred by the Commissioner of Customs (Export) under Section 130 of the Customs Act, 1962, challenging the common Final Order Nos. 59488–59509/2024 dated 25 November 2024 passed by the Customs, Excise and Service Tax Appellate Tribunal (CESTAT), New Delhi. The dispute concerned allegations of fraudulent availment of customs duty drawback and export promotion benefits by five exporters of readymade garments, namely M/s G.D. Manglam Exim Pvt. Ltd. (subsequently renamed M/s Welldone Exim Pvt. Ltd.), M/s Yogmaya Traders Pvt. Ltd., M/s Sidh Designer Pvt. Ltd., M/s Konark Exim Pvt. Ltd. and M/s DSM International. The Revenue alleged that export values had been artificially inflated and that overseas remittances received from unrelated third parties were presented as genuine export proceeds. The principal legal controversy involved the powers of Customs authorities to examine the genuineness of export transactions and recover allegedly irregularly obtained incentives, even where the exported goods had previously been assessed and payments had entered India through banking channels. The underlying investigation concerned 13,008 consignments of readymade garments exported principally to the United Arab Emirates during the period 2008–09 to March 2014. The exporters had claimed drawback under Section 75 of the Customs Act and benefits under the Focus Product Scheme and Focus Market Scheme administered through the Directorate General of Foreign Trade. The Directorate General of Revenue Intelligence (DRI) investigated allegations that the declared export values substantially exceeded the actual commercial values of the goods. According to the Revenue, the exporters procured garments from domestic suppliers at relatively low prices and declared substantially higher FOB values in their shipping bills to obtain increased drawback and duty credit benefits. The investigation relied upon export documentation, overseas customs records, supplier enquiries, statements of persons connected with the transactions and banking information. In the lead case concerning G.D. Manglam Exim Pvt. Ltd., the proceedings referred to 842 shipping bills filed through several ports during 2013. The Department contended that the discrepancies reflected a coordinated arrangement rather than isolated errors in export valuation. A central component of the investigation related to substantial third-party remittances received by the exporter entities. According to the Revenue, payments exceeding USD 76 million had been received through the International Banking Branch of Punjab National Bank from entities located in Hong Kong, the British Virgin Islands and the UAE, although the shipping documents identified different overseas buyers. The investigation also referred to transfers through accounts of entities such as M/s Ganapati Trading Company and other firms allegedly connected with diamond trading and the exporters. Certain overseas consignees reportedly denied purchasing the goods or stated that the consideration paid by them was considerably lower than the amount declared in the Indian shipping bills. The DRI alleged that a wider arrangement involving movement of money through multiple bank accounts, overseas intermediaries and purported diamond transactions enabled funds to be presented as export realisations. The Revenue accordingly disputed whether the remittances represented genuine sale proceeds attributable to the goods covered by the relevant shipping bills. The allegation of overvaluation was supported by comparisons between Indian export declarations and documents obtained from overseas customs authorities. The Revenue relied upon information from destinations including Dubai, Spain, Greece, the Netherlands, Belize, Poland and Georgia, contending that the value declared before Indian Customs was in certain cases between two and twenty times the corresponding overseas import value. The investigation also examined invoices, shipping bills, domestic procurement records and particulars of suppliers whose business premises were allegedly found vacant or otherwise inconsistent with the declared transactions. Sample verification of consignments through Dubai was relied upon to allege excess drawback of Rs.1,52,54,678 in the cases covered by the material received. These allegations formed part of the basis for show cause notices proposing recovery of drawback under the applicable Drawback Rules, recovery of duty foregone under Section 28AAA, confiscation, interest and penalties. The dispute was thus framed by the Revenue as one concerning deliberate misdeclaration and irregular export incentives, rather than merely the correctness of assessable value. Another important issue concerned the Bank Realisation Certificates (BRCs) issued by Punjab National Bank certifying receipt of export proceeds. Following information received from the DRI, the bank cancelled 1,650 BRCs on grounds relating to non-compliance with RBI requirements governing third-party remittances. The relevant regulatory framework included RBI Circular No. 70 dated 8 November 2013 and Circular No. 100 dated 4 February 2014, which prescribed conditions concerning third-party payments, documentary arrangements and export declarations. G.D. Manglam Exim Pvt. Ltd. challenged the cancellation before the Delhi High Court and subsequently instituted a civil suit against the bank. By judgment dated 13 March 2020, the competent civil court declared the cancellation of the BRCs null and void. Meanwhile, the Commissioner of Customs (Export), by Order-in-Original dated 30 May 2023, confirmed demands and consequential action in respect of consignments associated with the cancelled BRCs, while dropping proposals relating to other consignments where the available evidence was considered insufficient. Both the Department and the affected exporters and individuals challenged different portions of that adjudication order before the CESTAT. The CESTAT, by its common order dated 25 November 2024, dismissed the Revenue’s appeals and allowed the appeals preferred by the exporters and concerned individual noticees. It considered that the mere receipt of remittances from persons other than the overseas buyers did not automatically establish non-realisation of export proceeds. It also reasoned that questions concerning foreign exchange remittances were governed by the regulatory framework administered by the Reserve Bank of India and that the Customs authorities could not independently reject banking receipts without sufficient legal foundation. On the issue of overvaluation, the Tribunal emphasised the requirement of determination or redetermination of the value of export goods under Section 14 of the Customs Act and the Customs Valuation (Determination of Value of Export Goods) Rules, 2007. Regarding Section 28AAA, it considered the absence of independent invalidation of duty credit scrips and proceedings against their subsequent users. The Tribunal further relied upon the civil court decree concerning cancellation of BRCs. The Revenue challenged these conclusions as reflecting an incorrect appreciation of the statutory provisions and the cumulative evidence gathered during investigation. On the preliminary issue of maintainability, the High Court rejected the exporters’ contention that the appeals were barred under Section 130 because the dispute involved valuation of goods. The Court distinguished disputes requiring determination of assessable value from disputes in which allegedly inflated values constitute evidence of fraudulent availment of customs benefits. Referring particularly to the Supreme Court’s decision in M/s Alupro Building Systems Pvt. Ltd. Vs Commissioner of Central Excise, Bangalore-II, 2026 INSC 582, the Court explained that the statutory exclusion from High Court appellate jurisdiction applies where the question has the required direct and proximate connection with the rate of duty or value of goods for assessment purposes. In the present appeals, the Revenue was not seeking a fresh determination of assessable value. Instead, the question concerned whether the exporters had improperly obtained drawback and export incentives through misdeclaration and disputed remittances. The Court distinguished the valuation-based precedents relied upon by the respondents and held that the appeals raised substantial questions of law within the jurisdiction conferred by Section 130. Examining the third-party remittances and Section 75, the High Court held that the Customs authorities were not deprived of jurisdiction to examine the genuineness of export realisations merely because foreign exchange regulation falls within the domain of the RBI under the Foreign Exchange Management Act, 1999. The second proviso to Section 75(1) requires attention to the realisation of sale proceeds in respect of exported goods. Consequently, actual receipt of foreign currency through banking channels cannot, in every case, conclusively establish that the amount represents genuine consideration for the corresponding exports. The Court distinguished the permissibility of third-party payments under the applicable regulatory framework from the separate question whether particular remittances were genuinely connected with the exported goods. It considered that the CESTAT had failed to examine adequately the collective significance of the overseas enquiries, banking records, alleged circular movement of funds, statements and discrepancies in export documentation. The Court therefore found the Tribunal’s approach to the evidence and the statutory jurisdiction of Customs authorities legally unsustainable. The High Court also considered the evidentiary foundation of the adjudication proceedings and the objections concerning cross-examination. It noted that the Department’s case did not rest exclusively upon statements recorded during investigation but included banking records, SWIFT messages, foreign customs documents, overseas enquiries and other documentary material. Referring to Section 138B of the Customs Act and the Supreme Court’s decision in Kanungo & Company Vs Collector of Customs, the Court explained that cross-examination is not an automatic or inflexible requirement in every customs adjudication merely because statements have been recorded. At the same time, it did not accept that cross-examination could invariably be denied. Its necessity depends upon the role of the statements in establishing the allegations, the availability of independent corroborative material and the prejudice caused to the affected noticee. The Court considered that the CESTAT was required to examine the evidentiary record and procedural objections in their proper statutory context rather than treating the alleged shortcomings as conclusively invalidating the proceedings without such examination. Regarding the civil court decree and recovery under Section 28AAA, the High Court rejected two further aspects of the CESTAT’s reasoning. Applying the principles explained in Razia Begum Vs Sahebzadi Anwar Begum and Deccan Paper Mills Co. Ltd. Vs Regency Mahavir Properties Pvt. Ltd., it held that a declaratory civil decree ordinarily operates between the parties and persons legally claiming through them. As the Customs Department was not a party to the proceedings between the exporter and Punjab National Bank, the decree declaring cancellation of BRCs invalid did not independently extinguish Customs authorities’ statutory powers or conclusively determine the genuineness of the underlying transactions. With respect to Section 28AAA, the Court observed that the statutory conditions concerning instruments obtained through collusion, wilful misstatement or suppression of facts must be examined on the available evidence. However, the absence of proceedings against every subsequent user of a duty credit instrument, or the absence of its separate invalidation, could not by itself defeat proceedings against persons allegedly responsible for obtaining it improperly. The Court found that the Tribunal had failed to apply the proper statutory tests. In its final determination, the Delhi High Court allowed the Revenue’s batch of appeals and set aside CESTAT Final Order Nos. 59488–59509/2024 dated 25 November 2024, restoring the Commissioner’s Order-in-Original dated 30 May 2023 in all other respects. The Court nevertheless maintained a specific exception concerning Shri Nitin Gupta, Director of M/s Oak Shipping Services Pvt. Ltd., against whom a penalty had been imposed on allegations relating to false landing certificates. The CESTAT had recorded that he had not been served with the necessary notice and relevant documents or afforded an effective opportunity of hearing. Applying Section 124 of the Customs Act and the Supreme Court’s decision in Kothari Filaments & Anr. Vs Commissioner of Customs (Port), Kolkata & Ors., the High Court held that these mandatory safeguards could not be displaced by the seriousness of the allegations. Accordingly, the penalty imposed upon Shri Nitin Gupta remained set aside, while the adjudication order was otherwise restored. The judgment establishes the importance of examining the genuine nexus between export proceeds and goods, the statutory conditions for recovering export benefits, and compliance with mandatory procedural safeguards in customs proceedings.

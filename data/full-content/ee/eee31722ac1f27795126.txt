However, with credit growth already running ahead of deposit growth, competition for deposits could intensify over the next few quarters as the liquidity cushion is absorbed. The immediate rate hike is therefore likely to support banks' margins before it translates into higher funding costs.The RBI raised the repo rate by 25 basis points to 5.50 per cent on Wednesday, its first rate increase in nearly four years, and shifted its policy stance to calibrated tightening. The move changes the direction of the interest-rate cycle, but the transmission to deposit rates is unlikely to be immediate. Advt State Bank of India chairman C S Setty expects deposit rates to remain unchanged for the next two to three months, citing sufficient liquidity in the banking system. The recent inflow of foreign-currency non-resident (FCNR) deposits has provided banks with an additional liquidity cushion, reducing the immediate need to raise rates to attract deposits.The rate hike could instead provide some near-term relief to banks' net interest margins. A significant proportion of floating-rate loans is linked to external benchmarks, allowing lending rates to reprice relatively quickly when the policy rate changes. Deposit rates, in contrast, are typically repriced more gradually. This could temporarily widen the spread between lending yields and funding costs.Deposit pressure could emerge laterThe bigger question for banks is what happens after the current liquidity surplus is absorbed. Credit growth is already running faster than deposit growth, with the latest data showing bank credit expanding 18.1 per cent against 17.3 per cent growth in deposits. If this gap persists, banks will eventually need to compete more aggressively for deposits to fund loan growth.That could push up fixed deposit rates, particularly for selected tenures where banks need to attract fresh retail deposits. The extent of the increase, however, will depend on how quickly liquidity tightens and how aggressively banks want to pursue credit growth. Advt The FCNR(B) inflows have temporarily eased this pressure, but they are not a permanent source of domestic funding. As this liquidity is deployed and the banking system's funding requirements increase, deposit mobilisation could become more important.The RBI's move also changes the broader environment for savers. A 25-basis-point repo hike does not automatically translate into a similar increase in fixed deposit rates because banks price deposits according to their own funding requirements, liquidity and competitive position. Banks with comfortable liquidity can continue to keep deposit rates unchanged even when the policy rate rises. Advt For borrowers, the transmission could be faster. Loans linked to external benchmarks can see lending rates move relatively quickly, while existing fixed-rate deposits continue to carry their contracted rates until maturity. This creates a period in which banks can benefit from higher lending yields without an immediate corresponding increase in their deposit costs.What happens to FD rates?Fixed deposit rates could therefore move higher, but probably not immediately across the banking system. Banks that face greater funding pressure are likely to be the first to adjust rates, particularly if credit growth remains strong and deposit mobilisation weakens. Advt The repo rate hike also signals that the RBI is no longer focused on easing monetary conditions. If inflation remains elevated and further rate increases become necessary, banks would face greater pressure to reprice deposits as the policy cycle moves further towards tightening.For now, however, the evidence points to a lag between the RBI's rate action and deposit-rate increases. Banks have enough liquidity to avoid an immediate deposit-rate war, but the combination of strong credit growth and slower deposit growth could make deposits more expensive in the months ahead.For depositors, the message is therefore mixed: the RBI's rate hike improves the outlook for higher FD rates, but the first beneficiaries are likely to be banks rather than savers.

Summary: E-Waste Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR) registration is a regulatory requirement for producers of covered electrical and electronic equipment under the E-Waste (Management) Rules, 2022, which came into force on 1 April 2023. The Central Pollution Control Board administers registration and compliance through an online portal. The rules may apply to own-brand manufacturers, brand owners using contract manufacturers, importers and businesses selling imported or used equipment. Product coverage is determined by Schedule I, while specified exclusions include waste batteries, plastic packaging, qualifying micro enterprises and radioactive waste. Registration is only the starting point: producers must maintain accurate product and sales records, meet recycling targets, obtain eligible EPR certificates from registered recyclers, create consumer awareness and submit prescribed returns. The general recycling targets are 60 per cent for 2023–24 and 2024–25, 70 per cent for 2025–26 and 2026–27, and 80 per cent from 2027–28, subject to the applicable calculation basis and separate provisions for newer producers and used-equipment imports. Businesses should verify equipment classification, weights, registration particulars, certificate transactions and statutory deadlines. Hazardous-substance restrictions and environmental compensation provisions also require attention. Producers dealing with batteries or packaging must assess those separate compliance regimes independently. Regular reconciliation across finance, procurement, product and compliance teams helps identify reporting errors, demonstrate fulfilment of obligations and reduce regulatory risk. Products such as computers, mobile phones, household appliances and other electronic devices eventually become waste. Improper disposal can release hazardous substances, harm human health and cause the loss of valuable materials that could otherwise be recovered through recycling. Extended Producer Responsibility, commonly known as EPR, makes producers responsible for meeting prescribed obligations relating to the end-of-life management of covered products. E-Waste EPR registration connects producers with the regulatory system administered by the Central Pollution Control Board, or CPCB. Compliance requires continued attention to recycling targets, product information, consumer awareness and statutory reporting. What Is E-Waste EPR Registration? E-Waste EPR registration is the registration obtained by a covered producer through the designated CPCB online portal under the E-Waste (Management) Rules, 2022. It enables the regulator to identify the producer, record its covered electrical and electronic equipment and administer the applicable recycling obligations. The producer registration certificate must be distinguished from an EPR recycling certificate. The registration certificate establishes the producer’s registered status, while eligible EPR certificates generated through registered recyclers are used towards fulfilling recycling targets. Obtaining registration does not, by itself, complete the producer’s compliance requirements. Legal Provisions Governing E-Waste EPR Registration The E-Waste (Management) Rules, 2022 were notified under the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986 and came into force on 1 April 2023. These rules replaced the E-Waste (Management) Rules, 2016 and introduced a centralised online system for registration, recycling obligations, certificate transactions and reporting. The principal rules must be read together with subsequent amendments, applicable CPCB guidelines and regulatory directions. Rule 4 addresses registration, while Rule 6 specifies the responsibilities of producers. Schedules III and IV contain provisions relating to recycling targets. Other provisions cover matters such as hazardous substances, environmental compensation and verification of compliance. Who Is a Producer Under the E-Waste Rules? The definition of a producer extends beyond businesses that manufacture electronic equipment in their own factories. A business may qualify as a producer when it manufactures and offers covered equipment for sale under its own brand, sells equipment produced by another manufacturer under its own brand, offers imported equipment for sale or imports used electrical and electronic equipment. The selling method does not determine whether the rules apply. A business may sell through physical stores, distributors, dealers or online platforms and still fall within the definition. Producer status should therefore be assessed by examining the actual business activities, product coverage and branding arrangements. Manufacturers Selling Under Their Own Brand A manufacturer that produces covered electrical or electronic equipment and offers it for sale under its own brand should assess its obligations as a producer. Its responsibilities may extend beyond managing waste generated within the factory to meeting the applicable obligations associated with equipment introduced into the market. Where a business performs both manufacturing and producer activities, it should examine the registration requirements for each relevant category. Its internal compliance process should distinguish manufacturing waste records from information used to determine producer recycling obligations. Brand Owners Using Contract Manufacturers A business may engage another company to manufacture electronic products and then sell those products under its own brand. Such an arrangement can bring the brand owner within the definition of a producer even though it does not operate a manufacturing facility. The agreement between the brand owner and the contract manufacturer should clearly allocate responsibility for providing product specifications, equipment weights and supporting compliance information. However, a commercial agreement cannot remove statutory obligations imposed on an entity by the rules. Importers of Electrical and Electronic Equipment Businesses offering imported covered equipment for sale in India should assess their producer registration and EPR obligations. The assessment should consider the product description, equipment category and the nature of the import transaction. Importers should maintain consistent records of imported models, quantities and weights. EPR registration also needs to be considered separately from customs requirements, product certification and any other approvals applicable to the equipment. Products Covered Under E-Waste EPR Registration Product coverage is determined by Schedule I of the E-Waste (Management) Rules, 2022. The schedule covers a wide range of electrical and electronic equipment, including specified information technology equipment, consumer appliances, lighting equipment, electrical tools, medical devices and laboratory instruments. Businesses should classify products according to the applicable legal description and equipment code. A commercial name alone may not provide sufficient information for classification. Covered components, consumables, parts and spares must also be considered where applicable. A product register can help maintain consistency. It should identify the model, brand, technical function, relevant equipment category and supporting weight information. The register should be reviewed whenever new products are introduced or existing models are substantially modified. Exclusions From the E-Waste Rules The rules provide exclusions for specified categories, including waste batteries governed by the Battery Waste Management Rules, packaging plastics governed by the Plastic Waste Management Rules, qualifying micro enterprises and radioactive waste governed by the relevant atomic energy legislation. A business should verify whether an exclusion applies to its particular circumstances. MSME registration alone should not be treated as a general exemption for all micro, small and medium enterprises. The applicable classification and the legal conditions must be examined. An exclusion from the e-waste rules does not necessarily mean that no waste management obligations apply. For example, batteries and plastic packaging may attract separate responsibilities under their respective rules. Registration Obligations of Producers Covered producers must obtain registration through the designated portal before carrying on activities for which registration is required. The application should correctly identify the legal entity, relevant business activities, covered equipment and other prescribed information. A producer should also ensure that its registration continues to reflect its operations. Changes in product categories, business particulars or other relevant information should be reviewed against the applicable amendment procedure. Where the entity falls within more than one regulated stakeholder category, the registration requirements for those categories should be assessed separately. E-Waste EPR Registration Process Applicability Assessment The first stage is to determine whether the business falls within the producer definition and whether its equipment is covered by Schedule I. This assessment should consider manufacturing arrangements, branding, imports and the sale of used equipment. A proper applicability review reduces the risk of registering in the wrong category or excluding covered product lines. Businesses operating through several legal entities should assess the activities of each entity separately. Product Classification Each covered product should be mapped to the appropriate equipment description and code. Classification should be supported by technical information, particularly where a product combines several functions or is marketed under a name that differs from the terminology used in the rules. Consistent classification is necessary because product information influences registration, reporting and the assessment of obligations. The same classification approach should be used across procurement, finance and compliance records. Preparation of Information and Documents The producer should assemble the business identification records, authorisations, product details, sales information and declarations required by the current application procedure. Import-related information should also be prepared where relevant. Before submission, the producer should check whether the legal name, address and identification details are consistent across the supporting records. Sales quantities and product weights should be supported by a clear and reproducible calculation. Submission Through the CPCB Portal The application is submitted through the designated CPCB online system in the relevant stakeholder category. The applicant must complete the prescribed information, upload supporting documents and follow the applicable payment and declaration requirements. The submission should receive an internal review before filing. This is particularly useful where application information has been collected from different departments or external suppliers. Scrutiny and Clarifications CPCB may seek clarification or additional information while examining an application. The applicant should monitor the application and respond within the period specified in the communication. Responses should address the actual query and include supporting evidence. If a correction affects other application figures or product information, those related entries should also be reviewed for consistency. Review After Registration After registration is granted, the producer should check the certificate and portal information for accuracy. The business should understand its applicable target position, reporting responsibilities and registration conditions. An internal compliance calendar should then be established. Responsibility should be assigned for maintaining data, monitoring certificate transactions, filing returns and reviewing changes in business operations. Documents Required for Registration The exact document requirements depend on the current CPCB application procedure and the applicant’s activities. Producers should generally keep their incorporation or constitution records, business identification information, authorised representative details, product descriptions, brand information and relevant sales records ready for review. Importers should also organise the applicable import identification and transaction records. Where equipment weights are reported, the producer should retain the basis used to establish those weights. Prescribed declarations and undertakings should be prepared in the required format. The objective is to create a reliable connection between the application and the underlying business records. Documents should support the submitted information rather than merely demonstrate that the business exists. Recycling Targets for Producers The rules prescribe phased recycling targets. Under the general target schedule, the target is 60 per cent for financial years 2023–24 and 2024–25, 70 per cent for 2025–26 and 2026–27, and 80 per cent from 2027–28 onwards. These percentages apply to the relevant waste-generation basis determined under the applicable provisions. They should not automatically be calculated against current-year turnover or current-year sales. Producers must establish the correct base quantity before applying the relevant percentage. For example, where the applicable waste-generation quantity is 100 metric tonnes and the relevant target is 70 per cent, the recycling obligation would be 70 metric tonnes. This illustration assumes that the underlying waste-generation quantity has already been correctly determined. Targets for Newer Producers Schedule IV contains separate provisions for qualifying producers whose period of operation is shorter than the average life of their products. The applicable calculation therefore requires consideration of when the producer started selling the equipment and the relevant historical sales information. Under the published target provisions, the applicable target from financial year 2025–26 onwards for qualifying newer producers is 20 per cent of sales from two financial years earlier. Producers should confirm that Schedule IV applies to their circumstances before using this calculation. Imports of Used Equipment Imports of used electrical and electronic equipment are subject to a separate target provision of 100 per cent of the imported quantity. Businesses dealing in used equipment should assess this requirement carefully rather than applying the general target percentage. The EPR assessment must also be distinguished from the legal permissibility of the import. Registration under the e-waste rules does not independently authorise an import that requires permission or is restricted under another law. Fulfilment Through EPR Certificates Producers fulfil their applicable recycling obligations through eligible EPR certificates obtained from registered recyclers through the prescribed portal mechanism. The producer should ensure that the certificates are suitable for its obligations and that the transaction is correctly reflected in the system. Commercial documents should be reconciled with portal records. A payment receipt or recycler agreement alone should not be treated as sufficient evidence that the producer’s obligation has been settled. The certificate quantity, eligibility, relevant period and portal status should all be checked. Producers should monitor progress during the year. Early reconciliation provides time to resolve transaction errors, obtain missing information and address any shortfall before the applicable deadline. Quarterly and Annual Return Filing Quarterly and annual returns are an essential part of producer compliance. They enable the regulator to assess reported business information and the fulfilment of obligations through the online system. Producers should follow the applicable statutory timelines together with any valid extension issued for the relevant reporting period. An extension for one financial year should not be assumed to apply to another. Similarly, a return-filing extension should not automatically be treated as an extension of the recycling target deadline. Before filing, the producer should reconcile the return with sales records, product information and certificate transactions. The final submission and acknowledgement should be retained with the supporting records. Consumer Awareness Obligations Producers are required to create awareness through appropriate communication channels. These activities can include educational content on websites, user information, advertisements, packaging communication and customer outreach. Effective awareness material should explain the importance of separate e-waste handling and guide users towards appropriate disposal channels. The communication should be understandable and relevant to the products sold by the business. Producers should retain dated copies of awareness material and records of the activities undertaken. This makes it easier to demonstrate compliance during a review. Restrictions on Hazardous Substances Rule 16 addresses the reduction of specified hazardous substances in covered electrical and electronic equipment, subject to applicable exemptions and amendments. Producers should assess product composition requirements alongside their recycling obligations. Relevant supplier declarations, technical specifications and testing evidence should be maintained where appropriate. These records should identify the products or models they support. Product compliance should also be reviewed when suppliers, components or materials change. A declaration obtained for an earlier model may not adequately support a modified product. Record Maintenance and Internal Controls Reliable records are necessary to support registration information, target calculations and statutory returns. Producers should maintain a traceable connection between reported quantities and the underlying sales, import and product records. A practical internal process should identify who prepares the information, who reviews it and who approves the filing. Finance teams can verify transaction records, product teams can confirm classification and weights, and compliance teams can coordinate the reporting process. Periodic reconciliation helps detect inconsistent weights, missing product lines and differences between accounting and portal information. Resolving these issues early reduces the likelihood of repeated errors across reporting periods. Separate Compliance for Batteries, Packaging and Products E-waste registration does not replace other obligations that may apply to a business. Batteries and plastic packaging require separate assessment under their respective waste management rules. Depending on the equipment and business activities, product certification, import permissions and facility-related environmental approvals may also be relevant. Each requirement should be assessed according to its own legal provisions. A compliance register can identify the applicable requirement, responsible legal entity, relevant product and reporting or renewal date. This helps businesses maintain a complete view of their responsibilities. Environmental Compensation and Regulatory Action Failure to comply with the e-waste rules may attract environmental compensation and other action under the applicable provisions. CPCB has issued guidelines for assessing environmental compensation, and compliance may be examined through verification, inspections and audits. Producers should therefore be able to substantiate the information submitted and the obligations claimed as fulfilled. Unsupported figures, inaccurate declarations and incomplete portal transactions can create difficulties during scrutiny. Where an error or shortfall is identified, the business should determine the affected period, establish the correct position and take the required corrective steps. Paying an amount demanded by the regulator should not be assumed, by itself, to complete every outstanding compliance obligation. Common Mistakes Producers Should Avoid A frequent mistake is treating registration as the end of the compliance process. Producers may obtain a certificate but fail to maintain records, monitor targets or submit returns. Other problems arise from incorrect product classification, inconsistent equipment weights and the use of the wrong calculation base. Businesses may also assume that contract manufacturers carry all responsibilities or that every MSME is exempt. These issues can be reduced by reviewing applicability before launching products, assigning an internal compliance owner and reconciling information throughout the year. Regular coordination between finance, procurement, product and compliance teams makes the obligations easier to manage. Conclusion E-Waste EPR registration is an important step for producers entering India’s regulated electronic waste management system. However, obtaining registration is only the beginning. Producers must continue to classify their products correctly, maintain accurate sales records, fulfil applicable recycling targets, create consumer awareness and submit required returns within the prescribed timelines. These responsibilities help ensure that discarded electrical and electronic equipment reaches appropriate recycling channels. Integrating EPR compliance into everyday business operations makes these obligations easier to manage. Regular data reconciliation, verification of recycling certificates and careful review of filings can help businesses identify errors early and maintain reliable compliance records. Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ’s) Q1. What is E-Waste EPR registration? Ans. E-Waste EPR registration is registration with the Central Pollution Control Board for producers of covered electrical and electronic equipment. It enables the administration of their recycling and reporting obligations under the E-Waste (Management) Rules, 2022. Q2. Who needs E-Waste EPR registration in India? Ans. Covered own-brand manufacturers, businesses selling equipment made by others under their own brand, and businesses offering imported equipment for sale may require registration. Importers of used electrical and electronic equipment must also assess their obligations. Q3. Is E-Waste EPR registration required for online sellers? Ans. Online selling does not create an exemption. An online seller must obtain registration if its activities fall within the definition of a producer and its products are covered by the rules. Q4. Does using a contract manufacturer remove the brand owner’s responsibility? Ans. A brand owner selling covered equipment manufactured by another company may still qualify as a producer. Outsourcing manufacturing does not automatically transfer the brand owner’s statutory EPR obligations. Q5. What documents are required for E-Waste EPR registration? Ans. Applicants generally need business identification records, authorisation documents, product and brand details, relevant sales information and prescribed declarations. The exact requirements depend on the current CPCB procedure and the applicant’s activities. Q6. What are the main obligations after obtaining registration? Ans. Producers must fulfil applicable recycling targets, create awareness and submit quarterly and annual returns. They should also maintain accurate supporting records and comply with applicable hazardous substance restrictions. Q7. What is the general e-waste recycling target for FY 2026–27? Ans. The general Schedule III recycling target for FY 2026–27 is 70 per cent of the applicable waste-generation quantity. Separate calculation provisions apply to qualifying newer producers and imports of used equipment. Q8. How do producers fulfil their EPR recycling obligations? Ans. Producers fulfil applicable obligations through eligible EPR certificates obtained from registered recyclers using the prescribed online portal mechanism. Certificate transactions must be correctly recorded and reflected in the relevant compliance filings. Q9. Does E-Waste EPR registration cover batteries and plastic packaging? Ans. Waste batteries and plastic packaging are governed by separate waste management rules. A business dealing with these materials should independently assess its registration and compliance obligations under those rules. Q10. What happens if a producer fails to comply with E-Waste EPR requirements? Ans. Non-compliance may attract environmental compensation and other regulatory action under the applicable provisions. Obtaining registration alone does not excuse a failure to meet recycling targets or submit required returns.

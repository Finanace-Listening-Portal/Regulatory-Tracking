Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); Prem Prakash Vs Union of India Through Directorate of Enforcement (Supreme Court of India) Summary: The Supreme Court of India, in Prem Prakash Vs Union of India Through Directorate of Enforcement, delivered an important judgment on 28 August 2024 concerning bail under the Prevention of Money Laundering Act, 2002 (PMLA), the admissibility of statements recorded under Section 50, the evidentiary value of co-accused statements and the constitutional protection of personal liberty under Article 21. A Bench comprising Justice B.R. Gavai and Justice K.V. Viswanathan considered an appeal challenging the Jharkhand High Court’s order dated 22 March 2024 in B.A. No. 9863/2023, which had rejected the appellant’s application for regular bail in ECIR Case No. 5 of 2023. The proceedings concerned allegations of money laundering arising from purportedly fraudulent land transactions in Ranchi. The Supreme Court examined whether the material relied upon by the Directorate of Enforcement (ED) established reasonable grounds for believing that the appellant was involved in money laundering and whether the stringent twin conditions prescribed under Section 45 of the PMLA had been satisfied. The Court also addressed the significant question whether statements recorded by ED officers under Section 50 against an individual already in judicial custody in another proceeding investigated by the same agency could be used as incriminating evidence against that individual. Reaffirming that bail is the rule and jail is the exception, the Court held that statutory restrictions on bail must be interpreted consistently with constitutional guarantees and that the prosecution must establish the necessary factual foundation before the statutory presumption under Section 24 can operate. The prosecution originated from FIR No. 399 of 2022, registered on 8 September 2022 at Sadar Police Station, Ranchi, alleging offences including cheating, forgery, criminal breach of trust and criminal intimidation under the Indian Penal Code. The allegations concerned approximately one acre of land situated at Plot No. 28, Khata No. 37, Village Gari, Cheshire Home Road, Ranchi. According to the complainant, the accused persons had fraudulently prepared documents, including a power of attorney and sale deeds, to transfer ownership of the property. The Directorate of Enforcement registered ECIR No. 5 of 2023 on 7 March 2023 because the underlying allegations included scheduled offences under Sections 420 and 467 of the IPC. Although Prem Prakash was not named in the original FIR or initially identified in the ECIR, he was subsequently arraigned as Accused No. 8 in the prosecution complaint. The ED alleged that the land had been transferred to Punit Bhargava for ₹1,78,55,800 and subsequently sold to Bishnu Kumar Agarwal for ₹1.80 crore through two sale deeds dated 1 April 2021. It was further alleged that ₹1,78,20,000 was paid to Punit Bhargava’s proprietorship concern, Shiva Fabcons, and that ₹1,01,57,400 was thereafter transferred to M/s Jamini Enterprises, a firm allegedly controlled and beneficially owned by Prem Prakash. The prosecution claimed that the appellant had facilitated the transactions, influenced registration and mutation proceedings, and participated in a conspiracy involving forged land records. Prem Prakash had already been in judicial custody since 25 August 2022 in another ED proceeding, ECIR No. 4 of 2022, when his arrest in the present case was recorded on 11 August 2023. His bail application was rejected by the Special Court on 20 September 2023 and subsequently by the Jharkhand High Court, leading to the appeal before the Supreme Court. The Supreme Court first examined the scope and operation of Section 45 of the PMLA, which prescribes two conditions for granting bail when the Public Prosecutor opposes the application: the Court must be satisfied that there are reasonable grounds for believing that the accused is not guilty of the alleged offence and that the accused is unlikely to commit an offence while on bail. Referring to Vijay Madanlal Choudhary Vs Union of India, the Court explained that these conditions restrict judicial discretion but do not impose an absolute prohibition on bail. The statutory test requires a judicial assessment of reasonable grounds based on available material rather than a conclusive determination of innocence. The Court further relied upon Manish Sisodia Vs Directorate of Enforcement, particularly the decision dated 9 August 2024, which reiterated that bail ordinarily constitutes the rule and incarceration the exception. The Supreme Court emphasised that this principle reflects Article 21 of the Constitution, which protects life and personal liberty except through a valid and reasonable procedure established by law. The presence of stringent statutory bail conditions does not reverse the constitutional presumption in favour of liberty. Referring also to Ramkripal Meena Vs Directorate of Enforcement and Javed Gulam Nabi Shaikh Vs State of Maharashtra, the Court observed that prolonged pretrial detention, particularly where trial is unlikely to conclude within a reasonable period, may justify granting conditional liberty despite the rigours of Section 45. Detention before conviction must not become punishment without trial. The Court therefore held that the twin conditions must be applied through a balanced assessment of the prosecution material, the accused’s circumstances, the duration of custody and the constitutional right to a speedy trial. The Court next clarified the limited nature of judicial scrutiny at the bail stage and the importance of establishing foundational facts under Section 24 of the PMLA. Drawing upon Vijay Madanlal Choudhary and Ranjitsing Brahmajeetsing Sharma Vs State of Maharashtra, it held that a court considering bail is not required to conduct a detailed trial or meticulously weigh every item of evidence. The expression ‘reasonable grounds for believing’ requires an assessment of broad probabilities based upon the material collected during investigation. The prosecution is not required to prove guilt beyond reasonable doubt at this stage, but it must disclose sufficient material to demonstrate a genuine case against the accused. The Court identified three foundational requirements relevant to the presumption under Section 24: first, the commission of criminal activity relating to a scheduled offence; second, the derivation or acquisition of property as a result of that criminal activity; and third, the direct or indirect involvement of the person concerned in a process or activity connected with that property as proceeds of crime. Only after the prosecution establishes these foundational circumstances does the statutory presumption arise and the burden shift to the accused to rebut it. The Supreme Court stressed that where the prosecution opposes bail, the investigating agency’s counter-affidavit should clearly identify the material relied upon to establish these elements. General allegations, unsupported assumptions or the mere invocation of the statutory presumption cannot substitute for the necessary factual foundation. The Court accordingly examined whether the ED’s evidence genuinely connected Prem Prakash with the alleged forgery, acquisition of proceeds of crime or laundering activity. A particularly significant part of the judgment concerned the admissibility of statements recorded under Section 50 of the PMLA while an accused was already in judicial custody in another case investigated by the same agency. The ED relied upon statements recorded from Prem Prakash on several dates in August 2023, including 3 August and 4 August 2023, when he was already in judicial custody in ECIR No. 4 of 2022 but had not yet been formally arrested in ECIR No. 5 of 2023. The Supreme Court considered the interaction between Section 50 of the PMLA, Section 25 of the Indian Evidence Act, 1872, and Article 20(3) of the Constitution, which protects against compelled self-incrimination. Although Vijay Madanlal Choudhary had held that ED officers are not police officers merely by virtue of their powers under the PMLA, it had also recognised that the applicability of evidentiary protections to particular custodial statements may require examination on a case-by-case basis. Referring to Rajaram Jaiswal Vs State of Bihar and Nandini Satpathy Vs P.L. Dani, the Court emphasised the vulnerability of a person already deprived of liberty and the possibility of coercive circumstances arising from the investigating agency’s dominant position. The Court held that an accused who is in custody under the PMLA in one proceeding cannot be treated as a person exercising free choice merely because the agency records a statement concerning a different ECIR. The formal distinction between the two proceedings does not eliminate the circumstances of custody. Consequently, the Supreme Court held that where an accused is already in custody under the PMLA, a statement recorded under Section 50 by the same investigating agency is inadmissible against its maker, irrespective of the particular case in which custody was originally imposed. Applying this principle, the Court concluded that Prem Prakash’s custodial statements could not be relied upon against him in the present ECIR. The Supreme Court also scrutinised the statements of other individuals relied upon by the ED, including Afshar Ali, Rajdeep Kumar, Md. Saddam Hussain and Punit Bhargava. Regarding the statement of co-accused Afshar Ali, the Court applied the principles governing confessions of co-accused under Section 30 of the Indian Evidence Act, particularly those explained in Kashmira Singh Vs State of Madhya Pradesh. It held that a co-accused’s statement does not constitute substantive evidence capable of independently establishing the prosecution’s case. The prosecution must first examine the other evidence and may, where legally permissible, use such a statement only to lend assurance to otherwise credible evidence. The Court found that Afshar Ali’s statement did not prima facie establish Prem Prakash’s involvement in preparing forged sale deeds or knowingly laundering proceeds of crime. Similarly, Rajdeep Kumar’s statement primarily indicated that he worked for the appellant and had met individuals connected with the land transaction. The statement of Saddam Hussain did not materially advance the prosecution’s case against Prem Prakash, while Punit Bhargava’s statement principally described the appellant’s involvement in advising or facilitating the purchase and sale of the property. The Court noted that the statements did not sufficiently establish that Prem Prakash knew of the alleged forgery or participated in the laundering of criminal proceeds. Importantly, Bishnu Kumar Agarwal, the subsequent purchaser of the land, had already been granted bail by an order that had attained finality. The Court also observed that the ED had not placed sufficient material on record demonstrating the basis for alleging that Prem Prakash held a beneficial interest in M/s Jamini Enterprises. The mere movement of ₹1,01,57,400 into that firm’s account, without adequate material establishing the appellant’s beneficial ownership and involvement in laundering activity, was insufficient in the circumstances to justify continued detention. On an overall examination of the prosecution material, the Court found reasonable grounds for believing, for the limited purpose of bail, that the appellant was not guilty of the offences alleged under Sections 3 and 4 of the PMLA. The Supreme Court allowed Prem Prakash’s appeal, set aside the Jharkhand High Court’s order dated 22 March 2024 and directed his release on bail in ECIR No. 5 of 2023. The Court held that both conditions under Section 45 of the PMLA were satisfied: the available material provided reasonable grounds for believing that the appellant was not guilty of the alleged money-laundering offence, and there was no sufficient basis to conclude that he was likely to commit an offence if released on bail. The Court considered the ED’s reliance on another pending ECIR concerning alleged illegal stone-mining activities in Jharkhand but held that the merits of the bail proceedings in that separate case would be examined independently. The mere pendency of that proceeding did not justify denial of bail in the present matter. The Court also noted that Prem Prakash had remained in custody for more than one year and that the trial had not commenced. Allegations regarding the misuse of facilities in jail were considered insufficient to deny bail in the circumstances, although any violation of prison rules could be addressed by the competent authorities. The Supreme Court directed that the appellant be released upon furnishing bail bonds of ₹5 lakh with two sureties of the same amount. He was required to surrender his passport before the Trial Court, report to the Investigating Officer every Monday and Thursday between 10 a.m. and 11 a.m., and refrain from influencing witnesses or tampering with evidence. The Court expressly clarified that its observations were confined to deciding the bail application and would not prejudice the trial, which must proceed independently on the evidence. The judgment reinforces the principle that constitutional liberty, fair investigation, evidentiary safeguards and meaningful judicial scrutiny remain essential even in prosecutions under stringent economic-offence legislation such as the PMLA. (Supreme Court); 2022 SCC OnLine SC 929 — Relied upon to explain the twin conditions under Section 45, the foundational facts required under Section 24, the scope of bail-stage scrutiny and the evidentiary implications of statements recorded under Section 50 of the PMLA. Manish Sisodia Vs Directorate of Enforcement (Supreme Court); Criminal Appeal No. 3295/2024; 09/08/2024 — Followed for the principle that bail is the rule and jail the exception, and that prolonged incarceration without commencement or timely completion of trial raises serious concerns under Article 21. Ramkripal Meena Vs Directorate of Enforcement (Supreme Court); SLP (Crl.) No. 3205/2024; 30/07/2024 — Referred to concerning prolonged custody, delay in trial and the circumstances in which conditional liberty may be granted despite the statutory restrictions under Section 45 of the PMLA. Javed Gulam Nabi Shaikh Vs State of Maharashtra & Another (Supreme Court); 2024 SCC OnLine SC 1693 — Relied upon for the constitutional right to speedy trial and the principle that prolonged pretrial imprisonment cannot become punishment without conviction. Ranjitsing Brahmajeetsing Sharma Vs State of Maharashtra & Another (Supreme Court); (2005) 5 SCC 294 — Considered regarding the limited nature of judicial scrutiny at the bail stage, assessment of broad probabilities and the requirement to balance statutory restrictions against the presumption of innocence. Rajaram Jaiswal Vs State of Bihar (Supreme Court); AIR 1964 SC 828 — Discussed concerning the interpretation of the expression ‘police officer’ under Section 25 of the Indian Evidence Act and the safeguards applicable to potentially incriminating statements. Nandini Satpathy Vs P.L. Dani & Another (Supreme Court); (1978) 2 SCC 424 — Relied upon for the protection against compelled self-incrimination under Article 20(3), including the relevance of custodial circumstances and the right against self-accusation in related investigations. In Re Elukuri Seshapani Chetti (Madras High Court); ILR 1937 Mad 358 — Referred to for the principle that a confession made during investigation of one offence does not become admissible merely because it concerns another offence. Kodangi Vs Emperor (Madras High Court); AIR 1932 Mad 24 — Relied upon regarding the inadmissibility of confessional statements made during investigation of one offence when sought to be used in another criminal proceeding. Kashmira Singh Vs State of Madhya Pradesh (Supreme Court); 1952 SCR 526 — Relied upon for the principle that a co-accused’s confession is not substantive evidence against another accused and can, at most, lend assurance to independent evidence.

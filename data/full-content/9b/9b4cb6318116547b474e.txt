Summary: CBIC Circular No. 258/04/2026-GST dated 26 October 2026 provides comprehensive administrative guidance on GST demand, adjudication and first-appeal proceedings. This article examines the statutory distinction between Sections 73, 74 and 74A, emphasising that allegations of fraud, wilful misstatement or suppression must be supported by specific facts rather than inferred from a tax mismatch or non-payment alone. It discusses the role of pre-show cause notice intimation in Form GST DRC-01A, the essential particulars and evidence that a show cause notice should disclose, the proper quantification of demands, and the limits of adjudication beyond the notice. The analysis also addresses parallel Central and State GST proceedings under Section 6, limitation and service of notices, mandatory personal hearings under Section 75(4), adjournments, and the requirement for reasoned speaking orders. It explains why rectification under Section 161 cannot substitute for appellate review and why first appellate authorities must independently examine submissions, evidence and statutory grounds. Particular attention is given to third-party statements, electronic evidence, supplier non-existence allegations, the doctrine of prejudice and the treatment of pending proceedings. Practical checklists help taxpayers and GST litigation professionals identify jurisdictional, evidentiary and procedural defects at the notice, adjudication and appeal stages. The central message is that GST liability must be established through lawful, evidence-based and fair decision-making, not mechanical confirmation of investigation findings. Introduction: GST Adjudication Must Be a Reasoned Legal Process, Not a Mechanical Exercise The effectiveness of any taxation system depends not merely on the power of the authorities to assess and recover taxes but also on the fairness, transparency and legality of the procedures adopted for determining tax liabilities. Under the Goods and Services Tax regime, demand proceedings constitute one of the most significant areas of interaction between taxpayers and tax administration. Proceedings initiated under Sections 73, 74 and 74A of the Central Goods and Services Tax Act, 2017, can result in substantial demands of tax, interest and penalties. In cases involving allegations of fraud, wilful misstatement or suppression of facts, the consequences may extend beyond monetary demands to investigation, prosecution and reputational concerns. Over the years, numerous disputes have arisen from procedural deficiencies in GST proceedings, including vague show cause notices, mechanical invocation of fraud-related provisions, failure to examine taxpayers’ submissions, denial of effective personal hearings, and orders that merely reproduce the allegations contained in the notice. Against this background, CBIC’s guidelines described in Circular No. 258/04/2026-GST seek to reinforce the legal standards governing demand, adjudication and appellate proceedings. The central principle underlying these guidelines is straightforward: A GST demand must be founded on ascertainable facts, supported by evidence, justified by applicable statutory provisions and confirmed only through a fair adjudicatory process. The circular is significant not because it creates an entirely new adjudication framework, but because it consolidates and emphasises safeguards already embedded in the CGST Act and developed through judicial interpretation. Importantly, instructions issued under Section 168(1) must operate within the statutory framework. They cannot override the Act, Rules or binding judicial decisions. 2. Understanding the Statutory Architecture of GST Demand Proceedings Before examining the individual clarifications, it is necessary to understand the distinction between the principal demand provisions. Section 73: Non-Fraud Cases Section 73 governs determination of tax not paid or short paid, tax erroneously refunded, or input tax credit wrongly availed or utilised, for periods to which that section applies, where the circumstances do not involve fraud, wilful misstatement or suppression of facts to evade tax. The provision recognises that a tax discrepancy may arise for several reasons, including incorrect interpretation, computational mistakes, classification disputes, valuation differences or genuine compliance errors. A tax shortfall does not necessarily imply dishonest conduct. Section 74: Cases Involving Fraud, Wilful Misstatement or Suppression Section 74 addresses similar tax discrepancies where the statutory ingredients of fraud, wilful misstatement or suppression of facts to evade tax are present. The distinction is substantive, not merely procedural. Invocation of Section 74 carries more serious penal consequences and requires the department to establish the factual foundation for the alleged conduct. The existence of a tax demand and the existence of fraudulent intent are two separate questions. Both require examination. Section 74A: Common Demand Framework From FY 2024–25 Section 74A introduces a common statutory framework for determining demands for FY 2024–25 onwards, while retaining different consequences depending on whether fraud, wilful misstatement or suppression of facts is involved. Under the framework described in the supplied circular summary, the notice-issuance period is 42 months from the due date of the annual return for the relevant financial year, subject to the statutory rules applicable to the particular demand. However, a common procedural framework does not eliminate the need to distinguish between fraudulent and non-fraudulent conduct. The nature of the allegation remains important for determining the applicable penalty and other statutory consequences. Why This Distinction Matters A demand may be legally sustainable while an allegation of fraud is not. For example, an incorrect classification of goods may result in a differential tax liability. However, unless additional facts establish the requisite statutory ingredients, the mere classification dispute does not automatically justify treating the transaction as fraudulent. Similarly, a mismatch between GSTR-3B and GSTR-2A or GSTR-2B may warrant examination, but the mismatch itself cannot conclusively establish collusion or deliberate suppression. The legal character of a demand must be determined by the underlying facts, not by the seriousness of the amount involved. 3. Pre-Show Cause Notice Intimation Under Rule 142(1A) Rule 142(1A) permits the proper officer to communicate details of tax, interest and penalty as ascertained by the officer in Part A of FORM GST DRC-01A before issuance of a formal notice under the applicable demand provisions. The objective of this mechanism is to provide an opportunity for early clarification or voluntary compliance. Purpose of DRC-01A A pre-notice intimation can serve several functions: Informing the taxpayer of the discrepancy identified by the department. Allowing reconciliation of records before formal proceedings. Providing an opportunity to explain factual or computational differences. Facilitating voluntary payment where the liability is accepted. Reducing avoidable litigation. For example, an apparent difference between outward supplies reported in GSTR-1 and tax discharged in GSTR-3B may arise from amendments, timing differences, credit notes or reporting errors. A preliminary communication may enable the taxpayer to explain the difference without requiring a formal adjudication. Is DRC-01A Mandatory? The distinction between a mandatory statutory requirement and an administrative recommendation is important. Rule 142(1A), in its amended form, uses discretionary language. Therefore, the mere absence of DRC-01A should not automatically be treated as rendering every subsequent SCN invalid. The circular’s recommendation to issue such intimations wherever feasible reinforces good administrative practice, but the legal consequence of non-issuance must be evaluated with reference to the applicable statutory text and facts. Further, a DRC-01A intimation is not a substitute for a properly drafted SCN. 4. Show Cause Notice: The Foundation of Adjudication A show cause notice is not a mere procedural formality. It is the foundational document that informs a person of the allegations against them and provides an opportunity to answer those allegations before an adverse decision is taken. The validity of subsequent adjudication depends substantially on whether the notice adequately identifies the proposed action and the factual and legal grounds supporting it. Essential Components of a Valid SCN A properly drafted notice should ordinarily contain the following: A. Identification of the Noticee The notice should identify the person against whom proceedings are proposed, including relevant registration details, address, jurisdiction and tax period. B. Background of Proceedings The notice should explain whether the demand arises from audit, scrutiny, assessment, investigation, intelligence inputs or another statutory process. This enables the taxpayer to understand the context in which the alleged discrepancy was identified. C. Statement of Material Facts The notice must disclose the transactions, omissions, discrepancies or conduct forming the basis of the proposed demand. A general allegation that a taxpayer has availed ineligible ITC, without identifying the relevant transactions and basis of ineligibility, may not provide an effective opportunity of defence. D. Evidence Relied Upon Where the department relies on invoices, statements, reports, electronic records, third-party information or investigation findings, the material forming the basis of the allegation should be identified and disclosed in accordance with applicable law. E. Applicable Legal Provisions The notice should explain the statutory provisions allegedly violated and their connection with the facts. Merely reproducing the language of a section is not equivalent to demonstrating its applicability. F. Quantification of Demand The calculation of tax, interest and penalty should be intelligible and capable of verification. Where appropriate, the notice should provide tax-period-wise, financial-year-wise, supplier-wise or transaction-wise details. G. Opportunity to Respond The notice must communicate the procedure and time available for filing a reply, along with the consequences of non-compliance. Illustrative Example: Allegation of Fake ITC Consider a notice alleging that a taxpayer has availed ₹4 crore of fraudulent ITC from non-existent suppliers. An adequate notice should identify the suppliers, relevant invoices, tax periods, alleged discrepancies and the material relied upon to question the genuineness of the transactions. Where fraud is alleged against the recipient, the department must also explain the factual basis for attributing the relevant conduct to that recipient. Simply stating that the suppliers were found non-existent during an investigation may be insufficient to establish every ingredient of the proposed demand or penalty. The recipient’s evidence and the legal requirements of Section 16 must be examined alongside the department’s material. 5. A Show Cause Notice Cannot Reflect a Predetermined Conclusion A fundamental requirement of natural justice is that the adjudicating authority must approach the matter with an open mind. The SCN communicates a proposal; it does not represent a final determination of liability. Accordingly, the language of the notice should distinguish between allegations requiring examination and conclusions that can be reached only after considering the taxpayer’s defence. Expressions such as “it appears” or “it is proposed” may appropriately convey the provisional nature of the allegation. However, the use of cautious language alone does not cure substantive deficiencies. A notice may use the words “it appears” repeatedly and still be defective if it fails to disclose the underlying facts, evidence or legal basis. The real test is whether the taxpayer has been given a meaningful opportunity to understand and rebut the proposed case. The supplied circular summary refers to Nice Enterprises v. Deputy Commissioner ST and G.R. Infra Project Ltd. v. State of Madhya Pradesh in this context. The principle emerging from the discussion is that the essential foundation of a demand must exist in the notice itself. An adjudication order cannot ordinarily be used to construct an entirely new case that was never put to the taxpayer. 6. Fraud, Wilful Misstatement and Suppression Cannot Be Presumed One of the most important aspects of the circular concerns the mechanical invocation of fraud-related provisions. The distinction between an ordinary tax dispute and a case involving deliberate evasion is fundamental to GST litigation. A. Fraud Requires a Factual Foundation Fraud involves deceptive conduct and cannot be established merely because the department disagrees with the taxpayer’s interpretation of law. A finding of fraud must be supported by relevant facts and evidence. For instance, the existence of a disputed ITC claim does not, by itself, establish that the recipient knowingly participated in a fraudulent arrangement. B. Wilful Misstatement Is Different From an Incorrect Statement A statement may be incorrect without being wilfully incorrect. The expression “wilful” introduces an element of deliberate conduct. An error arising from misunderstanding, computational mistake or a bona fide interpretational dispute should not automatically be equated with intentional misrepresentation. C. Suppression Must Be Examined in Its Statutory Context The CGST Act contains a specific explanation concerning suppression for the purposes of the demand provisions. Therefore, arguments concerning suppression must be tested against the statutory definition, the relevant disclosure obligations and the requirement of intent to evade tax where applicable. The question is not simply whether some information was omitted. The authority must examine what information was required to be disclosed, whether it was disclosed, the circumstances of any omission and whether the statutory ingredients of the fraud-related provision are established. D. Mere Non-Payment Is Not Proof of Fraud A taxpayer may fail to discharge tax because of an interpretational dispute, financial difficulty, accounting error or other circumstances. Such non-payment may give rise to a tax demand, but it does not automatically establish fraudulent intention. E. Scrutiny Discrepancies Are Not Conclusive Evidence A mismatch identified through return scrutiny is a starting point for verification. It is not necessarily proof of fraudulent conduct. For example, a difference between purchase records and auto-populated ITC statements may require examination of invoices, reporting periods, supplier compliance and statutory conditions. The department must evaluate the explanation and supporting records before drawing conclusions. Litigation Significance Where the SCN invokes Section 74 or the fraud-related consequences under Section 74A without identifying supporting material, the taxpayer should specifically challenge the factual foundation of those allegations. The defence should separately address: The underlying tax or ITC dispute. The allegation of fraud, wilful misstatement or suppression. The proposed penalty and its statutory basis. These are related but distinct issues. 7. Repeated Fraud Allegations for Successive Tax Periods The supplied circular summary also addresses repeated SCNs alleging fraud or suppression on substantially identical facts. This raises an important conceptual issue. Where the department has already examined a particular factual arrangement and issued a notice, it may be difficult to justify automatically repeating the same allegation of deliberate suppression in later periods without considering what was already disclosed or known. However, this principle should not be interpreted as an absolute prohibition on subsequent notices. GST is a period-based tax system. Transactions, disclosures and statutory obligations may differ between periods. The legality of a later notice depends on the applicable limitation provisions, the facts of that period, the nature of the alleged conduct and whether independent grounds exist. The central question is whether the department has established the necessary statutory ingredients for the period under examination, rather than merely repeating an earlier allegation. 8. Separate Quantification of Fraud and Non-Fraud Demands A single investigation may reveal different categories of discrepancies. Some may involve ordinary interpretational disputes, while others may be alleged to involve deliberate evasion. The department should not treat every transaction as fraudulent merely because certain transactions are under suspicion. Where the applicable law permits different demand categories, the notice should clearly distinguish the factual and legal basis for each category. Example Suppose an investigation identifies: ₹80 lakh relating to an alleged classification error. ₹1.20 crore relating to disputed ITC from suppliers whose registrations were cancelled. ₹50 lakh relating to invoices alleged to have been issued without actual supply. Each category may require a different factual enquiry. The department should examine the legal conditions applicable to each demand and separately justify any allegation of fraudulent conduct. For FY 2024–25 onwards, the Section 74A framework makes this distinction particularly relevant to penalty consequences. A common notice mechanism cannot justify an undifferentiated finding of fraud. 9. Cross-Empowerment and Parallel Proceedings Under Section 6 Section 6 of the CGST Act deals with authorisation of State tax and Union territory tax officers and contains restrictions intended to prevent overlapping proceedings on the same subject matter. Section 6(2)(b) is particularly relevant where proceedings have already been initiated by an officer under the corresponding State or Union territory GST law. What Constitutes the Same Subject Matter? This is often the central issue in jurisdictional disputes. The fact that two authorities are examining the same taxpayer or financial year does not necessarily mean they are examining the same subject matter. The enquiry must consider the transactions, allegations, statutory issues and scope of the respective proceedings. For example, a State tax audit examining a particular ITC arrangement and a subsequent central investigation into the same arrangement may raise a different jurisdictional question from proceedings concerning unrelated transactions. Practical Importance Where parallel proceedings are initiated, the taxpayer should examine: The date and nature of the first proceeding. The authority that initiated it. The precise subject matter covered. Whether the later proceeding substantially overlaps. The statutory basis asserted for the subsequent investigation. The supplied summary refers to the Supreme Court’s decision in Armour Security (India) Ltd. v. Commissioner, CGST. The application of that decision must be assessed in light of its actual ratio and the specific proceedings involved. The broader principle is that cross-empowerment should not become a mechanism for unnecessary duplication or conflicting adjudication. 10. Timely Issuance and Proper Service of Show Cause Notices Limitation is a substantive safeguard in tax litigation. Demand proceedings must be initiated and completed within the periods prescribed by the applicable statutory provisions. The circular’s emphasis on issuing notices sufficiently in advance of limitation deadlines is significant from an administrative perspective. A notice issued close to the expiry of limitation may create practical difficulties for meaningful response and adjudication, although late issuance within the statutory period is not automatically invalid merely because it is close to the deadline. Service Under Section 169 Section 169 prescribes recognised modes of service, including specified physical and electronic methods. Proper service matters because it can determine when the taxpayer becomes aware of the proceedings and when the period for responding begins. Disputes may arise where notices are uploaded to the portal, sent to registered email addresses, delivered to incorrect addresses or otherwise communicated through disputed methods. Key Legal Distinction The validity of service and the adequacy of opportunity are related but separate questions. A notice may be served through a statutorily recognised mode, yet subsequent proceedings may still raise natural justice concerns if the taxpayer is denied a meaningful opportunity to respond. Conversely, a taxpayer’s failure to monitor a prescribed communication channel does not automatically invalidate service. The factual record and statutory requirements must be carefully examined. 11. Personal Hearing Under Section 75(4): A Substantive Right Section 75(4) provides that an opportunity of hearing shall be granted where a request is received in writing from the person chargeable with tax or penalty, or where an adverse decision is contemplated against such person. The provision is particularly important because it recognises that an effective adjudicatory process cannot always be completed through written submissions alone. Personal Hearing Is Not Merely a Formality A meaningful hearing enables the taxpayer to: Explain complex factual circumstances. Clarify documentary evidence. Address questions raised by the adjudicating authority. Distinguish judgments relied upon by the department. Respond to proposed adverse inferences. Draw attention to legal or computational errors. A hearing should provide a real opportunity to present the case. Adverse Orders and the Requirement of Hearing The statutory language makes the contemplated adverse decision independently relevant. Therefore, the absence of an express request for personal hearing does not necessarily relieve the authority of its obligation under Section 75(4) where an adverse decision is proposed. This issue has been the subject of significant litigation. Adjournments Under Section 75(5) Section 75(5) permits adjournment where sufficient cause is shown, subject to the statutory restriction on the number of adjournments. The provision requires the authority to exercise discretion reasonably. Repeated or unjustified adjournments may undermine timely adjudication, but mechanically rejecting a genuine request may also compromise fairness. Meaningful Hearing Versus Mechanical Compliance A personal hearing notice is not an end in itself. The circumstances of the hearing matter. For example, issuing multiple notices within an extremely short interval, providing inadequate time to respond or recording a hearing without genuinely considering the submissions may raise questions about whether the statutory opportunity was effective. At the same time, not every procedural irregularity necessarily results in automatic invalidation. The nature of the breach, statutory requirements and relevant judicial principles must be considered. 12. Speaking Orders: The Duty to Give Reasons A speaking order is one that discloses the reasoning connecting the material on record with the conclusions reached by the adjudicating authority. Reasoned decision-making is essential for transparency, accountability and effective appellate review. What Should an Adjudication Order Address? A well-reasoned order should ordinarily identify: The issues for determination: What legal and factual questions require adjudication? The department’s allegations: What is the precise case proposed in the SCN? The taxpayer’s defence: What submissions, explanations and documents have been furnished? The evidence: What material supports or contradicts the allegations? The applicable law: Which statutory provisions and judicial principles govern the dispute? The findings: Why is each material contention accepted or rejected? The conclusion: What tax, interest or penalty, if any, is determined and on what basis? Merely Reproducing the SCN Is Not Enough An adjudication order cannot be treated as reasoned simply because it repeats the allegations contained in the notice. The adjudicating authority must independently examine the reply and evidence. For instance, where a taxpayer produces invoices, e-way bills, transport records, stock registers and banking evidence in an ITC dispute, the order should address the relevance and evidentiary value of those documents. A conclusion that the documents are “not satisfactory” without explaining why may be vulnerable to challenge. Judicial Precedents Must Be Considered Where a taxpayer relies on a relevant judgment, the authority should examine its applicability. The authority may distinguish the judgment where the facts or legal context differ, but should provide reasons rather than ignore the citation. This is particularly important where the decision concerns a statutory interpretation or procedural safeguard directly relevant to the dispute. A reasoned order is not necessarily a lengthy order. It is an order that answers the material questions necessary to justify the decision. 13. Adjudication Cannot Travel Beyond the Show Cause Notice Section 75(7) contains an important restriction on the scope of adjudication. The amount of tax, interest and penalty demanded in the order cannot exceed the amount specified in the notice, and no demand can be confirmed on grounds other than those specified in the notice. This provision preserves the taxpayer’s right to know and answer the case being made against them. Example Suppose an SCN proposes denial of ITC solely on the ground that the supplier’s registration was retrospectively cancelled. During adjudication, the authority cannot simply replace that allegation with an entirely different ground, such as alleged non-receipt of goods, without complying with the applicable statutory requirements. The taxpayer must have an opportunity to address the actual grounds on which liability is proposed. Distinction Between Evidence and New Grounds Additional analysis of an existing allegation is not necessarily the same as introducing a new ground. The distinction depends on whether the essential case remains within the notice or whether the order introduces a materially different basis of liability. This requires careful comparison of the SCN, reply, evidence and adjudication findings. 14. Rectification Under Section 161: Correction Is Not Review Section 161 permits rectification of errors apparent on the face of the record, subject to the statutory conditions and time limits. Its purpose is to correct identifiable errors without reopening the entire adjudication. Examples of Potential Rectifiable Errors Depending on the circumstances, rectification may be appropriate for: An obvious arithmetical mistake. A clerical error in the order. An apparent transcription mistake. Another error that can be established from the existing record without a detailed reconsideration of disputed issues. What Rectification Is Not Section 161 should not be treated as a general power to reconsider the merits of a completed adjudication. Where the issue requires fresh appreciation of disputed evidence, re-evaluation of legal arguments or substitution of one possible interpretation for another, the appropriate remedy may lie in appeal or other proceedings rather than rectification. The distinction is particularly important because rectification applications should not be assumed to suspend or extend appellate limitation unless the law provides that consequence. 15. Appeal Proceedings: The First Appellate Authority Must Independently Examine the Dispute The appellate stage is not intended to be a mechanical endorsement of the adjudicating authority’s conclusions. An appeal provides a statutory opportunity to challenge the legality, factual findings and procedural fairness of the order. Scope of Appellate Examination The appellate authority should examine: Whether the SCN disclosed adequate grounds. Whether the demand was correctly quantified. Whether the statutory provision was properly invoked. Whether the taxpayer’s evidence was considered. Whether the adjudicating authority complied with natural justice. Whether the order exceeded the scope of the notice. Whether the conclusions are supported by the record. Speaking Appellate Orders Section 107(12) requires the appellate order to state the points for determination, the decision thereon and the reasons for the decision. An appellate order should therefore engage with the material grounds raised by the appellant. A general observation that the original order is correct, without dealing with substantive grounds, may not satisfy the statutory requirement of reasoned appellate adjudication. Additional Evidence The treatment of additional evidence must be examined with reference to the applicable appellate framework and principles of fairness. The appellant should explain why the evidence is relevant and why it was not produced earlier, where that explanation is material. The appellate authority must also ensure that reliance on additional material is consistent with procedural fairness. Enhancement and New Adverse Findings The appellate authority’s powers are governed by Section 107, including the applicable procedural safeguards. Where enhancement or another adverse action is contemplated, the taxpayer must receive the opportunity required by law. The appellate process should not become a mechanism for introducing adverse findings without appropriate notice. 16. Evidentiary Standards in GST Investigation and Adjudication Although the circular primarily concerns procedural fairness, its principles have significant implications for evidence-based litigation. Many GST disputes depend on electronic records, third-party statements, supplier data and documents gathered during investigation. The existence of evidence and the legal sufficiency of evidence are different questions. Third-Party Statements Statements recorded during investigation may form part of the department’s material. However, the legal relevance, reliability and permissible use of such statements must be examined in the context of the applicable statutory provisions and procedural safeguards. Section 136 of the CGST Act specifically deals with the relevancy of statements made and signed in response to summons under Section 70 for proving the truth of their contents in prosecutions, subject to specified conditions. Its wording must be carefully distinguished from provisions such as Section 9D of the Central Excise Act. Where adjudication relies heavily on third-party statements, the taxpayer may need to examine the circumstances in which the statements were recorded, their consistency with documentary evidence and whether procedural fairness requires an opportunity to challenge them. Electronic Evidence Electronic material may include emails, digital ledgers, ERP records, portal information, electronic invoices and communications. Its evidentiary value depends on factors such as authenticity, integrity, relevance and applicable legal requirements. A screenshot or electronic extract should not automatically be treated as conclusive proof of every allegation derived from it. Supplier Declared Non-Existent A supplier being found non-existent at the time of investigation raises legitimate questions that require examination. However, the date of inspection, relevant supply period, nature of the transaction and recipient’s evidence may all be material. The department must establish the statutory basis for the proposed ITC denial or penalty. Likewise, the recipient must establish compliance with the conditions applicable to its ITC claim. Neither the department’s allegation nor the taxpayer’s possession of invoices should be treated as automatically decisive in every case. 17. Natural Justice and the Question of Prejudice A recurring issue in procedural litigation is whether every violation of a procedural requirement automatically invalidates the resulting order. The answer depends on the nature of the requirement, the applicable statutory language and judicial principles. Some procedural safeguards are expressly mandated by the statute. Other complaints may require examination of whether the taxpayer was deprived of a meaningful opportunity to defend the case. For example, failure to grant a hearing where Section 75(4) requires one raises a different legal issue from a minor clerical defect that causes no meaningful uncertainty. The doctrine of prejudice may become relevant, but it should not be used mechanically to excuse disregard of express statutory requirements. Natural justice is not satisfied merely by recording that an opportunity was given. The opportunity must be meaningful in the circumstances of the case. 18. Practical Implications for Taxpayers and GST Litigation Professionals The circular provides an opportunity to reassess how GST notices and orders are examined. A taxpayer should not restrict the reply to disputing the amount of tax. The validity of the proceedings themselves may require separate examination. A. At the SCN Stage Examine whether: The proper officer has jurisdiction. The notice is within limitation. The allegations are sufficiently specific. The relied-upon material has been identified. The proposed demand is properly quantified. Fraud-related provisions have a factual foundation. Parallel proceedings exist on the same subject matter. The taxpayer has been given a meaningful opportunity to respond. B. During Adjudication Ensure that: Replies are comprehensive and supported by documents. Important legal grounds are specifically raised. Requests for relied-upon documents are properly recorded. Personal hearing requests are made and documented. Written submissions and relevant judgments are placed on record. Any procedural deficiency is promptly brought to the authority’s attention. C. After Receipt of the Order Review whether: The order deals with the material submissions. The findings are supported by evidence. The adjudicating authority has travelled beyond the SCN. Fraud or suppression has been independently established where alleged. Interest and penalty have been computed correctly. The order contains sufficient reasons. The appropriate appellate or other statutory remedy is available within limitation. A structured review of these issues can help identify defects that may otherwise be overlooked when attention is confined to the substantive tax demand. 19. Can These Guidelines Be Applied to Pending Proceedings? An important practical question concerns the application of the circular to notices and adjudication proceedings already pending on the date of its issuance. The answer requires distinguishing between: Existing statutory obligations. Clarifications of established legal principles. Administrative instructions governing future conduct. Any express transitional or prospective directions contained in the circular. Where the circular reiterates requirements already imposed by Sections 75, 107 or other statutory provisions, those obligations do not arise for the first time merely because CBIC has issued a clarification. For example, the requirement to grant a hearing under Section 75(4) and the restriction on confirming demands beyond the SCN under Section 75(7) are statutory safeguards. However, it would be incorrect to assume that every administrative direction in a new circular automatically invalidates earlier proceedings. The precise language, legal character and applicability of each instruction must be examined. 20. Are CBIC Circulars Binding on Adjudicating and Appellate Authorities? Circulars issued under Section 168(1) are intended to secure uniformity in implementation of the GST law and bind the departmental officers to whom they apply within the statutory framework. However, administrative instructions cannot override the Act or Rules. Nor can a circular displace a binding judicial interpretation. The position of independent appellate and judicial authorities must be distinguished from that of departmental officers acting under administrative instructions. Accordingly, while a circular may be an important source for challenging inconsistent departmental conduct, the stronger legal foundation often lies in the underlying statutory provision and applicable judicial precedent. For litigation purposes, it is advisable to rely on the circular together with the relevant statutory requirement rather than treating the circular as an independent source of every taxpayer right. 21. The Larger Significance: From Demand Confirmation to Evidence-Based Adjudication Adjudication The most important message emerging from the guidelines is that tax administration must distinguish between detecting a discrepancy and legally establishing liability. An investigation may identify suspicious transactions. A scrutiny proceeding may reveal inconsistencies. An audit may identify possible short payment. But none of these preliminary findings, by itself, dispenses with the obligation to establish the relevant statutory ingredients through a lawful adjudicatory process. Similarly, the seriousness of an allegation does not reduce the taxpayer’s procedural protections. Indeed, where fraud, wilful misstatement or suppression is alleged, the need for careful examination of facts and evidence becomes even more important. An adjudicating authority is required to determine liability independently, not merely confirm the conclusions reached during investigation. The authority must consider both incriminating and exculpatory material, address the taxpayer’s substantive defence and explain the reasons for its findings. The appellate authority, in turn, must provide genuine scrutiny of the disputed decision. This approach benefits not only taxpayers but also tax administration. Properly drafted notices, well-supported demands and reasoned orders are more likely to withstand judicial scrutiny, while reducing avoidable remands, repetitive litigation and uncertainty. 22. Conclusion CBIC’s guidelines on demand, adjudication and appeal proceedings represent an important reaffirmation of the principles that should govern GST enforcement. The key principles may be summarised as follows: First, a show cause notice must disclose the case that the taxpayer is required to answer. Vague allegations and mechanical reproduction of statutory language cannot substitute for facts and evidence. Second, fraud, wilful misstatement and suppression must be established through the relevant statutory ingredients. They cannot be presumed merely from non-payment, mismatches or adverse investigation findings. Third, personal hearing and consideration of the taxpayer’s defence are essential elements of fair adjudication. Procedural compliance must be meaningful rather than mechanical. Fourth, adjudication orders must be reasoned and must remain within the scope of the notice. The taxpayer cannot be made liable on an entirely new basis introduced for the first time in the final order. Fifth, appellate authorities must independently examine the grounds of appeal and provide reasoned findings. The appellate process is an important statutory safeguard against erroneous demands. For GST litigation professionals, these principles reinforce the importance of challenging not only the quantum of demand but also the jurisdiction, legal foundation, evidentiary basis and procedural validity of the proceedings. Ultimately, the legitimacy of GST enforcement depends on the quality of the decision-making process. A legally sustainable GST demand is not one that is merely quantified and confirmed.

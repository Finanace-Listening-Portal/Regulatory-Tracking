Summary: This research paper compares traditional Indian community-based mediation with the safeguards contemplated by modern statutory mediation. Traditional approaches involving village elders, panchayats and respected community members emphasise dialogue, accessibility, cultural familiarity and preservation of social relationships, but may also be affected by unequal power, discrimination and social hierarchy. The paper examines Section 89 of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908, the Mediation Act, 2023, and the Supreme Court decisions in Salem Advocate Bar Association v. Union of India, Afcons Infrastructure Ltd. v. Cherian Varkey Construction Co. (P) Ltd. and Patil Automation Pvt. Ltd. v. Rakheja Engineers Pvt. Ltd. It discusses party autonomy, neutrality, confidentiality, pre-litigation mediation, mediated settlement agreements and the model of community mediation described in Sections 43 and 44 of the Act. Rather than treating traditional and modern methods as competing systems, the authors propose a Hybrid Community Mediation Model combining local access, cultural and linguistic sensitivity, trained neutral mediators, safeguards for vulnerable parties and technology-enabled participation. The paper argues that a future-ready Indian mediation system should retain the reconciliatory strengths of traditional practice while requiring fairness, consent and legal accountability. The discussion of provisions under the Mediation Act describes its enacted framework; whether each provision is operative depends on the relevant commencement notification. Introduction Mediation is an alternative method of dispute resolution in which a neutral third person, known as a mediator, helps two or more parties resolve their dispute through discussion and mutual agreement. Unlike a judge or arbitrator, a mediator does not impose a decision on the parties. The parties themselves decide whether to accept a settlement. Although mediation has gained formal recognition in the Indian legal system only in recent decades, the idea of settling disputes through discussion and compromise has existed in Indian society for a long time. Traditional Indian communities often relied on village elders, panchayats and respected members of society to help settle disputes. These mechanisms were generally based on dialogue, compromise, reconciliation and the maintenance of social relationships. Traditional dispute resolution was particularly connected with the local community. The people involved usually understood the language, customs and social relationships of the parties. Therefore, the objective was often not only to settle the immediate dispute but also to restore peace between the parties. Modern mediation operates differently because it functions within a recognised legal framework. Principles such as neutrality, confidentiality, voluntary participation, fairness and enforceability are given importance. Section 89 of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 was an important development in introducing alternative dispute resolution into the formal court system. The Supreme Court decisions in Salem Advocate Bar Association v. Union of India and Afcons Infrastructure Ltd. v. Cherian Varkey Construction Co. (P) Ltd. further developed the role of mediation in India. The enactment of the Mediation Act, 2023 has provided a more comprehensive statutory framework. It recognises different forms of mediation, including pre-litigation, institutional, online and community mediation. Therefore, the present research does not seek to decide whether traditional mediation or statutory mediation is better. Instead, it examines how the useful elements of traditional Indian practices can be combined with the safeguards of modern law to create a future-oriented mediation system. Arguments Based On The Research Question Traditional Indian practices have continuing value Traditional Indian dispute-resolution practices have several features that can still be useful. They were generally community-based, flexible and accessible. Local elders and community members were familiar with the language, customs and circumstances of the parties. An important feature was the focus on reconciliation. In disputes involving family members, neighbours or people from the same community, maintaining the relationship may be as important as resolving the immediate disagreement. Traditional practices therefore provide an important lesson that dispute resolution should sometimes focus on restoring relationships rather than simply determining who is right or wrong. However, traditional systems should not be copied exactly as they existed in the past. Some may have been influenced by social hierarchy, gender inequality or unequal power within the community. Therefore, useful traditional principles must be adapted to modern legal and constitutional values. Modern statutory mediation provides important safeguards Modern mediation provides protections that may not have existed in traditional systems. A trained mediator is expected to remain neutral, and participation is based on consent. Confidentiality allows parties to communicate more freely. Modern law also provides a framework for recognising and enforcing settlements. The Mediation Act, 2023 provides provisions relating to mediated settlement agreements, confidentiality, online mediation, institutional mediation and community mediation. These safeguards are important because mediation should be a voluntary process and should not become a means through which a stronger party pressures a weaker party. The future should combine both approaches Traditional and modern mediation should not necessarily be treated as competing systems. Traditional mediation can contribute community participation, local knowledge, cultural understanding and accessibility. Modern statutory mediation can provide neutrality, accountability, confidentiality and legal protection. The basic principle of the proposed approach can therefore be stated as: Traditional strengths + Modern legal safeguards = Future-ready Indian mediation. Relevant Case Laws Salem Advocate Bar Association v. Union of India (2003 and 2005) The Supreme Court considered the amendments relating to Section 89 of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 and recognised the importance of alternative dispute resolution mechanisms. The Court also considered the practical implementation of ADR mechanisms. These decisions were important because they helped establish mediation and other ADR methods as part of the Indian justice system. The cases demonstrate that courts are not the only means through which disputes can be resolved and that settlement-based mechanisms have an important role in reducing unnecessary litigation. Afcons Infrastructure Ltd. v. Cherian Varkey Construction Co. (P) Ltd. (2010) In this case, the Supreme Court examined Section 89 CPC and discussed the nature of mediation. The Court explained that a mediator assists parties in identifying issues, reducing misunderstandings and exploring possible solutions. The mediator does not impose a decision on the parties. This principle has a connection with traditional Indian mediation because traditional community-based settlement also placed importance on discussion and compromise rather than simply imposing a decision. Patil Automation Pvt. Ltd. v. Rakheja Engineers Pvt. Ltd. (2022) The Supreme Court considered pre-institution mediation under the Commercial Courts Act. The decision highlighted the importance of attempting mediation before certain commercial disputes are taken to court. The case reflects the changing role of mediation in India, where settlement is increasingly encouraged at an early stage instead of waiting until litigation becomes lengthy and expensive. Analysis Of Legal Provisions The Mediation Act, 2023 is the most important legislation for the present study. The Act seeks to promote and facilitate mediation, encourage institutional mediation, provide for enforcement of mediated settlement agreements, recognise community mediation and facilitate online mediation. Section 3 – Meaning of Mediation Section 3 defines mediation as a process in which parties attempt to reach an amicable settlement with the assistance of a mediator. The mediator does not have the authority to impose a settlement. This provision is important because it protects the principle of party autonomy. The parties remain in control of the outcome. Pre-litigation Mediation The Act provides a framework for pre-litigation mediation. This allows parties to attempt settlement before starting formal legal proceedings. There is an important similarity here with traditional dispute resolution. Traditional communities often attempted to resolve disputes locally before they developed into larger conflicts. Modern pre-litigation mediation gives this basic idea a formal legal structure. Mediated Settlement Agreements Another major feature of modern mediation is the legal recognition of mediated settlements. Unlike a purely informal community settlement, a settlement reached through the statutory mediation framework can receive legal recognition and enforcement according to the Act. This gives parties greater legal certainty. Community Mediation The most important provision for this research is Chapter X of the Mediation Act, 2023, which deals with community mediation. Section 43 provides for community mediation in disputes that may affect peace, harmony and tranquillity among residents or families of an area or locality, subject to the requirements of the Act. Section 44 provides for a panel of three community mediators and allows the panel to adopt a suitable procedure for resolving the dispute. These provisions are important because they create a connection between India’s traditional community-based dispute resolution and the modern statutory framework. However, there is also an important limitation. A settlement reached through community mediation does not have the same legal status as a mediated settlement agreement that can be enforced as a judgment or decree of a civil court. Therefore, the legal consequences of community mediation must be clearly understood by the parties. Proposed Future Model – Hybrid Community Mediation Based on the research question, this paper proposes a Hybrid Community Mediation Model. The objective of this model is to combine the positive features of traditional community mediation with the safeguards provided by modern law. 1. Community Accessibility Mediation centres should be made available closer to local communities so that people can resolve suitable disputes without immediately approaching courts. 2. Cultural and Linguistic Sensitivity Parties should be able to communicate in a language they understand. Mediators should also understand the cultural and social background of the dispute while remaining neutral. 3. Trained Community Mediators A mediator should not be selected only because he or she is a respected person in the community. Proper training in mediation techniques, neutrality, communication and confidentiality should be required. 4. Protection of Vulnerable Parties The process must ensure that weaker parties are not forced into settlements. The mediator should ensure that participation is voluntary and that parties understand the consequences of settlement. 5. Use of Technology Online and hybrid mediation can be used when physical participation is difficult. Technology can allow parties from different locations to participate in the same mediation process. The proposed model can therefore be represented as: Community participation + Trained mediators + Legal safeguards + Technology = Hybrid Community Mediation. This approach would preserve the useful features of traditional dispute resolution without bringing back practices that may conflict with equality, dignity or individual rights. Conclusion The development of mediation in India shows that the idea of resolving disputes through dialogue, compromise and community participation is not new. Traditional Indian practices provided valuable lessons about reconciliation, accessibility, flexibility and the importance of maintaining social relationships. Modern statutory mediation has developed these ideas within a formal legal structure. It provides safeguards such as neutrality, confidentiality, voluntary participation, professional standards and legal recognition of settlements. The Mediation Act, 2023 is particularly important because it recognises community mediation and therefore creates a connection between traditional community-based dispute resolution and modern legal mediation. The future of mediation in India should therefore not be seen as a choice between tradition and statute. Instead, the strengths of both systems can be brought together. Traditional practices can provide cultural understanding, local participation and accessibility, while modern law can provide the safeguards necessary to protect fairness and individual rights. The Hybrid Community Mediation Model proposed in this paper provides one possible approach. It combines community accessibility with trained mediators, legal safeguards and technology. At the same time, it recognises that not every traditional practice is suitable for modern society. Practices involving discrimination, coercion or unfair treatment must not be carried forward. Thus, the future of Indian mediation can be understood as “tradition informed by modern law.” By combining India’s traditional experience of community dispute resolution with the safeguards of modern statutory mediation, India can develop a system that is accessible, culturally sensitive, legally secure and capable of meeting the changing needs of society.

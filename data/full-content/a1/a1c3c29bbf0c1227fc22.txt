(Karnataka High Court) Transfer Pricing: Karnataka HC Upholds Exclusion of Comparables, Rejects Revenue’s Challenge to ₹200 Crore Turnover Filter Introduction The Karnataka High Court dismissed the Revenue’s transfer pricing appeal against Trident Microsystems India Pvt. Ltd., upholding the Tribunal’s exclusion of comparable companies. Applying the principles stated in SAP Labs India Private Limited v. Income Tax Officer, the Court reiterated that comparability analysis is essentially a factual and data-driven exercise. Interference requires a demonstrated defect, such as perversity, rather than disagreement with the Tribunal’s conclusion. The Court also reaffirmed the rational basis for a ₹200 crore upper turnover filter. The Revenue failed to demonstrate perversity or establish that the disputed comparables performed functions similar to those of the assessee. Background of the Appeal The Revenue filed the appeal under Section 260A of the Income Tax Act, 1961, challenging the Bengaluru Tribunal’s order dated 14 August 2020 in IT(TP)A No. 2234/Bang/2016. The proceedings concerned AY 2012-13 and, specifically, the Tribunal’s directions to exclude certain companies from the transfer pricing comparables. The High Court admitted the appeal on 19 February 2025, framing three substantial questions of law. These concerned the exclusion of Genesys International Corporation Ltd., application of a turnover filter, and whether the Tribunal had disregarded the TPO’s analysis under Rule 10B. The respondent company was served but remained unrepresented before the High Court. Revenue’s Challenge to the Tribunal’s Findings The Revenue questioned the exclusion of Genesys International Corporation on grounds of functional dissimilarity. It contended that the TPO had applied the required tests and correctly found the company’s functions comparable to those of the assessee. It also argued that there was no direct relationship between turnover and profit margins of companies engaged in software development services. On that basis, it challenged exclusion through a turnover filter. The third question alleged that the Tribunal had ignored material considered by the TPO, despite the officer having satisfied the requirements of Rule 10B. These objections required the Court to examine whether the Tribunal’s findings disclosed perversity or another sustainable basis for interference. Reliance on SAP Labs Decision The High Court relied on the coordinate Bench’s judgment dated 28 August 2026 in ITA No. 10/2011 and connected appeals concerning SAP Labs. Among the principles reproduced was that Chapter X constitutes a complete statutory framework governing transfer pricing, including determination of the arm’s length price, documentation and assessment of international transactions. The taxpayer bears the initial burden of determining the ALP and maintaining prescribed documentation. The TPO may interfere with that determination only upon satisfaction of the conditions under Section 92C(3). The precedent further explained that the TPO cannot reject the taxpayer’s comparables merely to replace them with a standard departmental set. Selection and exclusion must satisfy Rule 10B and rest on relevant facts and data. ₹200 Crore Turnover Filter Found Rational The Court reiterated that, in the absence of a statutory prescription fixing lower or upper turnover limits, the Tribunal’s adoption of a ₹200 crore upper turnover filter could not be regarded as arbitrary. The SAP Labs principles recognised that turnover, brand value, economies of scale, bargaining power and ownership of intangibles can materially influence comparability and profitability. Accordingly, the filter had a rational and logical foundation. Its application involved a factual determination that could not be disturbed without specifically demonstrating perversity. The ruling does not prescribe ₹200 crore as a statutory ceiling for every transfer pricing analysis. It recognises the legal sustainability of the Tribunal’s approach in the context considered by the Court. Functional Analysis Supported Exclusion On Genesys International Corporation, the Court found that the Revenue had failed to demonstrate perversity or explain how its functions were similar to those of the respondent assessee. For the remaining questions, the Court noted that the Tribunal had considered the functionality of the companies and undertaken an examination of their functions, assets and risks. On that material, the Tribunal had correctly concluded that the comparables were functionally dissimilar and directed their exclusion. The Revenue also failed to identify any instance where the Tribunal had relied on irrelevant material or excluded relevant material while reaching its decision. Decision and Key Takeaway The High Court answered all three substantial questions of law against the Revenue and in favour of the assessee. The appeal was accordingly dismissed. The decision reinforces the importance of company-specific evidence in transfer pricing disputes. A challenge to exclusion must address the actual functional analysis and identify a demonstrable error in the Tribunal’s reasoning. The judgment also confirms that merely asserting compliance with the TPO’s filters does not establish perversity in a contrary appellate finding. Here, the Tribunal’s analysis of functional comparability and FAR factors remained undisturbed because the Revenue failed to substantiate its objections. FULL TEXT OF THE JUDGMENT OF KARNATAKA HIGH COURT This appeal is filed under Section 260A of the ITA Act, 1961 is directed against 14.08.2020 in IT(TP)A No.2234/BANG/2016 passed by the Income Tax Appellate Tribunal, ‘A’ Bench, Bangalore for the assessment year 2012-13. 2. The above appeal was admitted for hearing on the following Substantial Questions of Law by order dated 19.02.2025: “1. Whether on facts and the circumstances of the case and in law, Tribunal’s order can be said as perverse in nature as Tribunal has directed Transfer Pricing officer to exclude M/s.Genesys International Corporation Ltd on the ground of functional dissimilarity though the Transfer Pricing Officer after application of required tests rightly held that comparable-company’s functions are similar to that of assessee? 2. Whether on the facts and in the circumstances of the case, the Tribunal is not justified in law in not appreciating that there is no direct relationship between turnover of comparable and the margin earned by the companies engaged in Software Development Services and as such excluding of comparable-companies on ground of turnover filter is unwarranted? 3. Whether on the facts and in the circumstances of the case, the Tribunal is right in law in directing Transfer Pricing Officer to exclude comparable’s when TPO has satisfied all required tests set out in Rule 10B and as such, the order passed by Tribunal is perverse as Tribunal has ignored the materials brought on record by TPO?” 3. The above appeal relates to transfer pricing and particularly exclusion of comparables as directed by the ITAT. 4. In the matter of transfer pricing, the Co-ordinate Bench of this Court in ITA No.10/2011 and connected appeals between the Sap Labs India Private Limited and The Income Tax Officer and others, by judgment dated 28.08.2026 on considering the relevant judgments of the Hon’ble Apex, has recorded the following findings: i. The Court concludes that Chapter X of the Income-tax Act, 1961 constitutes a self-contained code governing transfer pricing, providing a complete statutory framework for determination of the Arm’s Length Price (ALP), maintenance of documentation, and assessment of international transactions. ii. The initial burden of determining the ALP and maintaining the prescribed documentation rests upon the taxpayer, while the Transfer Pricing Officer can interfere with the taxpayer’s determination only upon satisfaction of the conditions stipulated under Section 92C(3) of the Act. iii. The selection or exclusion of comparables is essentially a factual and data-driven exercise, and the TPO cannot reject the taxpayer’s comparables merely to substitute them with a standard departmental set. Such determination must strictly conform to the requirements of Rule 10B of the Rules. iv. The Tribunal’s adoption of an upper turnover filter of Rs.200 crores is rational and legally sustainable, as turnover, brand value, economies of scale, bargaining power and ownership of intangibles materially influence comparability and profitability. v. An RPT filter of 15% is ordinarily preferable, though a higher threshold of 20% or 25% may be adopted only upon recording a specific finding that sufficient comparable companies satisfying the lower threshold are unavailable. vi. Foreign exchange gain or loss can be treated as an operating item only when it has a direct nexus with the international transaction. Where such nexus is absent, it cannot form part of the operating revenue or operating cost. vii. The burden of proof varies according to the nature of the dispute. While the taxpayer bears the initial burden of substantiating the ALP, once the TPO rejects the taxpayer’s determination and substitutes comparables, the burden shifts to the TPO to justify such inclusion or exclusion. viii. There is no legal prohibition against inclusion or exclusion of comparables at the appellate stage, provided the subsequently relied upon data is reliable and the proposed comparables satisfy the requirements of the Act and the Rules. ix. The tolerance of ±5% prescribed under Section 92C of the Act is merely a permissible variation and not a standard deduction, and transfer pricing adjustment becomes necessary whenever the variation exceeds the prescribed statutory limit. x. Working Capital Adjustment is a comparability adjustment and not a selection filter, and its grant depends entirely upon the facts of each case. Findings on such adjustment, like the selection of comparables, ordinarily remain findings of fact and do not warrant interference unless shown to be contrary to the Act or vitiated by perversity. 5. In so far as Substantial Question of Law No.1, the same is covered by the decision of the Co-ordinate Bench in Sap Labs (supra). The Co-ordinate Bench has held that in the absence of any statutory prescription fixing either a lower or an upper turnover limit, the adoption by the Tribunal of an upper turnover filter of Rs.200 crores cannot be said to be arbitrary. On the contrary, the said criteria is founded on a rational and logical basis. Such a determination being essentially a finding of fact, cannot be interfered with unless perversity is specifically prayed and demonstrated. In the instant case, the appellants-Revenue has failed to demonstrate the perversity and how the Assessee-Genesys International Corporation Limited performs similar function as that of the respondent-Assessee. Therefore, the said question is answered against the appellants-Revenue and in favour of the respondent-Assessee. 6. Insofar as Substantial Questions of Law Nos.2 and 3, with regard to exclusion of comparables, is also covered by the decision in Sap labs supra. It is specifically held that the selection or exclusion of comparables is essentially a factual and data driven exercise, and that TPO cannot reject the taxpayer’s comparables merely to substitute them with a standard departmental set. Such determination must strictly comply to the requirements of Rule 10B of the Rules. The Tribunal on taking note of the functionality of the comparable companies and the respondent-Assessee company and also taking note of the functions, assets and risks assumed by the comparable companies as well as the respondent-Assessee company, has rightly come to the conclusion that comparables are functionally dissimilar and rightly directed exclusion of those comparables. The appellants-Revenue has failed to point out any perversity in the order of the Tribunal. Moreover, the appellants have not pointed out considering either irrelevant material or excluding relevant material while passing the impugned order. 7. Thus, the Substantial Questions of Law are answered against the appellants-Revenue and in favour of the respondent-Assessee. Accordingly, the appeal stands dismissed.

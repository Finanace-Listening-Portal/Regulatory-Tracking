Summary: An Indian citizen relocating to Dubai does not automatically become a non-resident Indian (NRI) for income-tax purposes upon obtaining a UAE residence visa, Emirates ID or employment contract. Residential status is determined separately for each Indian tax year under the applicable income-tax legislation, based principally on the number of days spent in India and the purpose of departure. Under the Indian residential-status provisions, an individual is generally resident if present in India for 182 days or more, or for at least 60 days during the relevant year and 365 days during the preceding four years. However, an Indian citizen leaving India for genuine employment abroad may benefit from the special 182-day threshold. Entrepreneurs establishing Dubai businesses must separately establish whether this concession applies, as merely obtaining an investor visa or incorporating a UAE company is insufficient. Special provisions also govern visiting NRIs, including the 120-day rule for specified higher-income individuals, resident but not ordinarily resident (RNOR) status and deemed residence where Indian income exceeds ₹15 lakh. Importantly, residential status under FEMA may change from the time of departure for employment or business, even when Indian income-tax residence continues for the year. UAE domestic tax residence and eligibility under the India–UAE Double Taxation Avoidance Agreement require independent examination. Individuals relocating to Dubai should therefore evaluate their departure date, travel history, employment or business arrangements, Indian-source income, banking compliance and treaty position before claiming non-resident status. Moving to Dubai does not make an Indian citizen an NRI on the date the UAE residence visa is issued. Nor does obtaining an Emirates ID, renting an apartment or incorporating a Dubai company automatically change the person’s Indian tax status. For Indian tax purposes, residential status is determined separately for every tax year. The decisive factors normally include the number of days spent in India, the reason for leaving India, income from Indian sources and, in certain situations, whether the person is liable to tax in another country. The same individual may also have a different residential status under the Foreign Exchange Management Act. This distinction affects bank accounts, overseas investments, property transactions and other foreign-exchange matters. For anyone relocating from India to Dubai, the practical question is therefore not simply, “When did I move?” It is, “Under which law, for which period and for what purpose am I claiming to be a non-resident?” NRI is a Legal Status, Not a Visa Category The expression “NRI” is commonly used for any Indian citizen living abroad. That everyday description can be misleading when tax or banking decisions are involved. A person may be: A UAE resident for immigration purposes; A resident or non-resident under Indian income-tax law; A person resident outside India under FEMA; and A UAE tax resident under domestic UAE rules or the India–UAE tax treaty. These classifications serve different purposes and do not always change on the same date. An Indian citizen may receive a Dubai employment visa in August but remain an Indian tax resident for that entire Indian tax year because of the number of days already spent in India. At the same time, the person may become a person resident outside India under FEMA after leaving to take up employment in Dubai. This is why “NRI from the date of departure” is not a reliable conclusion for every purpose. The Indian Tax Year Must Be Considered First India now uses the expression “tax year” for periods beginning on or after 1 April 2026 under the Income-tax Act, 2025. The tax year ordinarily runs from 1 April to 31 March. Residential status must be calculated separately for each such year. For periods beginning before 1 April 2026, the Income-tax Act, 1961 continues to govern the residence analysis. For tax years beginning on or after that date, the Income-tax Act, 2025 applies. Although the new legislation has reorganised and simplified the statutory framework, the principal individual residence tests and important concessions for Indian citizens have broadly continued. The date of assessment or filing does not decide which legislation applies. The relevant question is the period in which the income was earned. The Basic Indian Residence Tests Under the general rule, an individual is treated as resident in India for a tax year if either of the following conditions is satisfied: The individual is present in India for 182 days or more during that tax year; or The individual is present in India for at least 60 days during that year and for at least 365 days in total during the four preceding tax years. If neither condition is satisfied, the individual will generally be non-resident for that tax year, subject to special provisions. Every day or part of a day spent in India should be examined carefully. Arrival and departure dates can affect the calculation, particularly when the person is close to a statutory threshold. Passport stamps alone may not always provide a convenient complete record because immigration processes are increasingly electronic. Relocating individuals should retain tickets, boarding passes, official travel history and a year-wise day-count working. Special Rule When an Indian Citizen Leaves for Employment Abroad A significant concession applies when an Indian citizen leaves India during the tax year to take up employment outside India. In this situation, the ordinary 60-day test is replaced by a 182-day threshold. The individual will generally become non-resident for that tax year if the stay in India is less than 182 days. This rule is particularly relevant to an employee who resigns from an Indian job and relocates to Dubai to work for a UAE employer. Consider an Indian citizen who has historically lived in India and moves to Dubai for employment partway through the year. If the individual spent fewer than 182 days in India during the relevant Indian tax year, the individual may qualify as a non-resident for that year. If the individual had already spent 182 days or more in India before departure, the person would ordinarily remain an Indian resident for that entire tax year, even though the Dubai employment, residence visa and accommodation continued for the rest of the year. The result is determined for the complete tax year. India does not generally divide an individual’s domestic residential status into resident and non-resident portions merely because the person moved during the year. Why the Departure Date Matters For an employee planning a Dubai relocation, a difference of a few days can change the Indian tax result. Suppose an individual has lived in India continuously from 1 April and leaves for a genuine Dubai employment assignment later in the year. The days spent in India before departure must remain below 182 if the person intends to rely on the special departure rule. A departure late in September may produce a different result from a departure in early October. The exact calculation will depend on the calendar, travel times and whether the arrival or departure day is treated as a day of presence in India based on the underlying facts and records. A relocation should not be scheduled using a rough “six-month” estimate. The tax law applies a day count, not an approximate number of months. Before finalising travel, the individual should prepare a date-specific residence computation and leave a reasonable buffer for emergency visits to India. Does Leaving India to Start a Dubai Business Receive the Same Treatment? This is one of the most important issues for Indian entrepreneurs. The statutory concession expressly addresses an Indian citizen leaving India for employment outside India and certain crew members. An entrepreneur moving to Dubai to establish or operate a business should not automatically assume that every business relocation will receive the same treatment as overseas employment. The actual arrangement must be examined. Relevant considerations may include: Whether the person has taken up genuine employment with a UAE company; Whether an employment contract exists; Whether remuneration is actually paid; Whether the company has substantive business operations; Whether the person is merely a shareholder or investor; Whether the individual is carrying on an independent business or profession; and Whether the relocation is supported by genuine commercial conduct. A founder who incorporates a Dubai company, appoints himself as manager and obtains an investor visa should not assume that the label “manager” conclusively establishes overseas employment. If the special departure concession is unavailable, the normal 60-day-plus-365-day test may apply. A person who has lived in India for several preceding years could then remain Indian resident even after spending substantially fewer than 182 days in India during the relocation year. Entrepreneurs should therefore obtain a residence analysis before choosing the departure date or structuring their relationship with the UAE company. Example: Employee Relocating to Dubai Assume Rohan, an Indian citizen, has always lived and worked in India. He accepts a genuine employment position with a Dubai company and leaves India during the tax year. If Rohan’s total presence in India for that tax year is below 182 days, the special rule for leaving India for employment may allow him to qualify as non-resident. His Dubai employment contract, visa, salary records, travel history and evidence of actual work in the UAE should be retained. If Rohan returns frequently to India and his total presence reaches 182 days, he may become Indian resident for the complete tax year. The fact that his principal job and home are in Dubai would not, by itself, override the domestic day-count test. Example: Founder Establishing a Dubai Company Assume Meera leaves India to establish a consultancy company in a Dubai free zone. She is the sole shareholder and controls the business. She obtains an investor visa and describes herself as the company’s general manager. Meera spends 150 days in India during the relocation year and had spent more than 365 days in India during the preceding four years. If her departure does not qualify as leaving India for employment, the ordinary residence test may apply. Her stay of at least 60 days in the current year, combined with the preceding four-year presence, could make her an Indian resident despite spending most of the remaining year in Dubai. Her position requires a factual and legal examination. It should not be decided merely from the designation printed on her UAE visa or trade license. Visiting India After Becoming an NRI Different rules can apply after an Indian citizen or person of Indian origin becomes non-resident and subsequently visits India. A visiting Indian citizen or person of Indian origin is generally protected by an extended threshold instead of the ordinary 60-day test. However, the threshold can be reduced to 120 days where specified Indian income exceeds ₹15 lakh and the other statutory conditions are satisfied. Broadly, a visiting person may become resident if: Presence in India reaches 182 days during the tax year; or In relevant higher-income cases, presence reaches at least 120 days during the year and at least 365 days during the four preceding years. The ₹15 lakh test generally considers income other than income from foreign sources as defined for this purpose. It should not be confused with total worldwide earnings. An NRI living in Dubai who has substantial rent, interest, capital gains or business income in India should therefore not use the 182-day threshold without checking whether the 120-day rule applies. When a 120-Day Resident May Be RNOR An Indian citizen or person of Indian origin who becomes resident under the special 120-day visiting rule may be treated as resident but not ordinarily resident, commonly abbreviated as RNOR. RNOR is a resident category, not a form of NRI status. However, its scope of taxation may be narrower than that applicable to a resident and ordinarily resident. The distinction can be significant for a Dubai-based individual with: UAE salary; Profits from a Dubai business; Income from overseas investments; Foreign bank accounts; or Directorships in foreign companies. The person should not assume that becoming resident necessarily brings every item of foreign income into Indian taxation. The source of income, place of control, nature of the activity and RNOR provisions must be examined. Equally, the RNOR label should not be used casually. It has prescribed statutory conditions and must be established from the individual’s residence history. Deemed Residence for Indian Citizens Indian law also contains a deemed-residence rule intended to address certain Indian citizens who are not liable to tax in another country by reason of residence, domicile or a similar criterion. Broadly, an Indian citizen may be deemed resident where: Specified Indian income exceeds ₹15 lakh; and The person is not liable to tax in any other country or territory by reason of domicile, residence or another similar criterion. A person covered by this provision is generally treated as RNOR rather than resident and ordinarily resident. The rule does not mean that every Indian citizen living in the UAE automatically becomes a deemed Indian resident because the UAE does not levy personal income tax in the same manner as India. The expression “liable to tax” is a legal concept. It is not always the same as actually paying tax. The person’s UAE residence position, local legislation, treaty eligibility and supporting documentation may all be relevant. Dubai-based business owners with Indian income exceeding the threshold should specifically review this rule rather than relying on the general statement that “there is no personal income tax in the UAE.” Income-Tax Residence and FEMA Residence Are Different The Income-tax Act determines how an individual is taxed. FEMA residence regulates foreign-exchange transactions and related financial arrangements. Under FEMA, a person’s intention and reason for leaving India can be decisive. A person who leaves India: To take up employment outside India; To carry on a business or vocation outside India; or For another purpose indicating an intention to stay outside India for an uncertain period, may become a person resident outside India for FEMA purposes from the change in circumstances associated with the departure. This means that an individual relocating permanently to Dubai for employment or business may become non-resident under FEMA even though the person remains Indian tax resident for the relocation year due to the income-tax day count. The familiar 182-day figure under FEMA should not be applied mechanically. FEMA uses the preceding financial year as part of its definition but also contains important purpose-and-intention exclusions. Why FEMA Status Matters After Moving to Dubai Once a person becomes resident outside India for FEMA purposes, practical changes may be required in relation to: Resident Indian savings accounts; NRO and NRE accounts; Fixed deposits; Demat and brokerage accounts; Indian property transactions; Repatriation of funds; Investments in Indian entities; Existing overseas assets; Guarantees and loans; and Continuing interests in Indian companies or partnerships. A resident savings account should not simply continue unchanged after the account holder becomes non-resident under FEMA. The individual should inform the bank and arrange the appropriate redesignation. Tax residence and bank-account eligibility must therefore be reviewed separately. UAE Tax Residence Is Another Independent Test A person who becomes non-resident in India is not automatically a UAE tax resident. The UAE has its own domestic tax-residence framework. Depending on the circumstances, the analysis may consider: Physical presence in the UAE; Usual or principal place of residence; Centre of financial and personal interests; UAE employment or business; and Availability of a permanent home. The India–UAE DTAA contains a treaty-specific definition. For an individual seeking UAE residence under that treaty, presence in the UAE for an aggregate period of at least 183 days in the relevant calendar year is particularly important. This creates another timing difference. Indian tax residence is tested using the Indian tax year running from April to March, whereas the treaty’s UAE individual residence condition refers to the relevant calendar year. An individual could be non-resident under Indian domestic law but fail to satisfy the treaty’s UAE residence condition for that calendar year. A UAE visa or domestic Tax Residency Certificate should not be assumed to resolve every treaty question automatically. Dual Residence and the India–UAE Treaty In some cases, the domestic laws of both India and the UAE may initially regard an individual as resident. The India–UAE DTAA contains tie-breaker principles for determining treaty residence. These ordinarily consider, in sequence: Where a permanent home is available; Where personal and economic relations are closer; Habitual abode; Nationality; and Competent-authority resolution where necessary. The centre of vital interests requires a wider factual review than counting days. Relevant factors may include the location of: Spouse and dependent children; Main home; Employment or active business; Senior management responsibilities; Investments; Bank accounts; Social and personal connections; and Day-to-day economic activity. An individual who spends time in Dubai but leaves the family, principal home and active business management in India may face a more complicated treaty-residence analysis. What Changes Once the Individual Becomes Non-Resident? A non-resident is generally taxed in India on income received or deemed to be received in India and income accruing, arising or deemed to accrue or arise in India. Common taxable Indian income may include: Rent from Indian property; Capital gains from Indian assets; Interest from taxable Indian accounts or deposits; Dividends from Indian companies; Salary for employment exercised in India; Business income connected with an Indian operation; and Fees, royalties or other income deemed to arise in India. Becoming an NRI does not make Indian income tax-free. However, genuine foreign income earned and received outside India may fall outside the Indian tax scope of a non-resident, subject to the source rules and any specific deeming provisions. For a founder controlling a Dubai business, an important question is where the income-earning activity is performed and where the business is controlled. Invoicing through a UAE company does not automatically turn India-connected income into foreign income. Dubai Salary Credited to an Indian Account A common concern is whether UAE salary becomes taxable in India merely because it is transferred to an Indian bank account. A distinction should be made between first receipt of income and a later remittance of money already received abroad. If salary is first received in a UAE bank account and later transferred to India, the remittance does not ordinarily change the original place of receipt. However, if the employment income is directly received in India, or the services are performed in India, the analysis may differ. The individual’s residential status, employment location, payroll arrangement and first point of receipt should all be documented. Practical Steps During the Relocation Year An Indian moving to Dubai should complete the following review before and after departure: Prepare a day count for the current and preceding tax years. Establish the exact purpose of departure: employment, business, profession, investment or family relocation. Confirm whether the special 182-day departure rule applies. Review whether the ordinary 60-day-plus-365-day condition could apply. Examine the ₹15 lakh visiting and deemed-residence provisions where relevant. Determine whether the person will be resident, RNOR or non-resident. Review FEMA residence separately. Inform Indian banks and financial institutions of the status change where required. Convert or redesignate bank and investment accounts appropriately. Review Indian property, company interests, directorships and partnership positions. Retain the UAE employment contract, trade license, visa, Emirates ID, lease, salary records and travel history. Examine UAE domestic and India–UAE treaty residence independently. Consider advance tax and Indian return-filing obligations for continuing Indian income. Recalculate the day count before every visit to India. Documents That Should Be Retained A defensible residence position is built from consistent evidence. The individual should preserve: Passport copies; Official entry-and-exit report; Airline tickets and boarding passes; UAE residence visa and Emirates ID; Dubai employment contract; Salary certificates and payslips; UAE bank statements; Residential tenancy contract; Utility records; UAE company documents, where applicable; Evidence of the individual’s actual role and remuneration; Indian income computations; Indian and UAE Tax Residency Certificates, where required; and A detailed year-wise travel calendar. Documents should reflect the real arrangement. A paper employment contract will carry limited value if the individual continues to perform the work and manage the business from India. Common Mistakes After Moving to Dubai Frequent errors include: Assuming NRI status begins when the UAE visa is stamped; Counting six months instead of calculating exact days; Ignoring the reason for leaving India; Treating an investor visa as conclusive evidence of foreign employment; Applying the employee departure concession to every business owner; Forgetting the 60-day-plus-365-day test; Ignoring the 120-day rule for high-income visits; Assuming no UAE personal income tax automatically triggers deemed Indian residence; Treating FEMA and income-tax residence as identical; Continuing to use resident Indian bank accounts; Assuming all Indian income becomes exempt; Believing foreign salary is taxable merely because it is later remitted to India; Failing to consider India–UAE treaty residence; Managing a Dubai company entirely from India; and Keeping no reliable travel records. Conclusion An Indian moving to Dubai becomes an NRI for Indian income-tax purposes only when the applicable residence conditions are satisfied for the relevant tax year. A UAE visa, Emirates ID, employment contract or Dubai company license cannot independently determine that status. For an Indian citizen genuinely leaving to take up employment in Dubai, staying in India for fewer than 182 days during the relocation year may permit non-resident status. The position can be more complex for founders, investors and professionals moving to operate their own businesses because the ordinary residence tests may still require examination. FEMA status can change on a different basis, particularly where the person leaves India for employment, business or an uncertain period. UAE domestic residence and India–UAE treaty residence must also be tested separately. The safest approach is to review the departure purpose, exact travel dates, Indian income, UAE arrangements and continuing connections before the move. In cross-border residence matters, a few days—or an incorrectly assumed legal test—can change the tax treatment of an entire year.

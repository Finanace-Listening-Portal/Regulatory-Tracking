Summary: When a borrower renegotiates a financial liability with its existing lender, Ind AS 109 does not permit the accounting result to be selected from the commercial label used in the amendment agreement. The entity first determines whether the exchange or modification creates substantially different terms. The quantitative test compares the present value of cash flows under the new terms—including only qualifying borrower-lender fees—discounted at the original effective interest rate with the present value of the remaining original cash flows; a difference of at least 10% indicates substantially different terms and derecognition. A result below 10% does not always end the analysis because a fundamental qualitative change may still require judgment. Derecognition produces extinguishment accounting and a new liability; a non-substantial modification keeps the liability but remeasures its gross carrying amount using the original effective interest rate and recognises a modification gain or loss. This guide separates those outcomes, explains fee treatment, presents a worked control framework and identifies the evidence needed for financial reporting and audit review. An exchange between an existing borrower and lender of debt instruments with substantially different terms, or a substantial modification of the terms of an existing liability, is accounted for as extinguishment of the original liability and recognition of a new liability. The analysis is therefore performed on enforceable cash flows and rights, not on descriptions such as rescheduling, waiver, refinancing or one-time settlement. Before running any percentage calculation, identify the liability or portion affected. A restructuring can involve partial repayment, conversion of a portion into equity, release of one borrower, addition of a guarantee, revised security and a modification of the remaining balance. A payment or equity settlement may extinguish one part while the residual debt is separately tested. Combining all elements into one spreadsheet can conceal the accounting for the settled portion. Legal memo and credit-risk analysis Build the 10% test from contractual cash flows and the original EIR Compare present values rather than undiscounted totals Paragraph B3.3.6 treats terms as substantially different when the discounted present value of cash flows under the new terms, including qualifying fees paid net of qualifying fees received, is at least 10% different from the discounted present value of the remaining original cash flows. The discount rate is the original effective interest rate of the liability. Using the renegotiated coupon, a current borrowing rate or an undiscounted principal comparison can change the conclusion and is not the prescribed quantitative test. Use the correct comparison date and remaining original schedule The cash-flow model should be prepared at the effective date of the modification. The denominator is based on remaining contractual cash flows of the original liability from that date, discounted at the original EIR. The new-side model uses modified contractual cash flows. Accrued interest, instalments paid on the effective date, balloon payments, floating-rate conventions and prepayment terms should be handled consistently and traced to the agreement. A borrower includes only fees paid or received between the borrower and lender, including fees paid or received by either party on the other’s behalf. Third-party legal, valuation or advisory fees are not inserted into the 10% calculation merely because they were necessary to negotiate the restructuring. The accounting after classification is separate. If the modification is an extinguishment, costs or fees incurred are recognised as part of the gain or loss on extinguishment, subject to the treatment of costs directly attributable to the newly recognised liability. If the exchange or modification is not an extinguishment, costs or fees adjust the carrying amount of the liability and are amortised over the remaining term through the effective-interest mechanism. A fee matrix should therefore record counterparty, purpose, whether included in the test and where ultimately recognised. Invoice descriptions are not sufficient evidence A bank may deduct an arrangement amount from proceeds, while a lawyer’s invoice may include work performed for both borrower and lender. The team should identify who legally incurred the fee, who received it and whether one party paid it on the other’s behalf. Split mixed invoices on a supportable basis. Do not classify every bank debit as a lender fee or every professional invoice as a third-party fee without examining the contract and invoice. A result below 10% may still require qualitative assessment Ind AS Transition Facilitation Group clarification has noted that failure to meet the quantitative threshold does not necessarily prove that a modification is non-substantial in every case. The 10% test captures changes in contractual cash flows; it may not capture a fundamental change in currency, subordination, conversion rights, collateral, recourse, embedded derivatives or other legal features. Whether a qualitative change is substantial is judgmental and depends on facts. The qualitative memorandum should be focused. It should identify changes not already reflected in the cash-flow model, explain why they alter or do not alter the economic substance of the obligation, and record relevant specialist advice. A conclusion should not be reverse-engineered to obtain a preferred profit outcome. Conversely, listing routine covenant adjustments without explaining their effect does not establish derecognition. Derecognition creates a new liability and an extinguishment result When the terms are substantially different, derecognise the old financial liability and recognise the new liability under Ind AS 109. The difference between the carrying amount extinguished and the consideration paid—including non-cash consideration and qualifying costs or fees—is recognised in profit or loss. The new liability is measured at fair value on initial recognition, and a new effective interest rate is established for subsequent amortised-cost accounting where applicable. The old carrying amount and new fair value need independent support The old carrying amount should reconcile to the general ledger immediately before modification, including unamortised transaction costs, premium or discount and accrued interest as appropriate. The new liability’s fair value cannot automatically be assumed to equal its face amount. Market terms, borrower credit, security, maturity and cash-flow features may require a valuation. The gain or loss bridge should separately show settlement consideration, new-liability fair value and costs. Tax treatment does not follow the accounting label automatically. An accounting extinguishment gain, waiver, fee or equity settlement may have different current-tax, deferred-tax, withholding and minimum-tax consequences. Record tax advice and temporary-difference analysis separately instead of forcing the tax result into the Ind AS 109 journal entry. A non-substantial modification keeps the liability but records a day-one effect If the modification is not substantial, the borrower does not derecognise the liability. Ind AS 109 requires recalculation of the gross carrying amount as the present value of renegotiated contractual cash flows discounted at the original effective interest rate. The difference from the pre-modification carrying amount is recognised as a modification gain or loss in profit or loss. This is not merely a prospective adjustment to future interest expense. Qualifying costs or fees then adjust the carrying amount and are amortised over the remaining term. The original EIR continues, adjusted through the carrying amount and fee treatment rather than replaced with the modified coupon. The revised amortisation schedule should reproduce the post-modification carrying amount, allocate interest consistently and reconcile every subsequent period to the ledger. Frequently Asked Questions on Ind AS 109 Debt Modification Is a 10% difference always required for derecognition? A difference of at least 10% establishes substantially different terms under the quantitative test, but a below-10% result may still require qualitative analysis of fundamental changes. Which discount rate is used in the 10% test? Use the original effective interest rate of the existing liability, not the modified coupon or current market rate. Are legal and advisory fees included in the 10% calculation? Not merely because they relate to the transaction. The test includes only borrower-lender fees and amounts paid by either on the other’s behalf. What happens when the test indicates substantial modification? The old liability is derecognised, a new liability is recognised, and the extinguishment difference is recorded in profit or loss. Is a below-10% modification accounted for prospectively only? No. The liability is remeasured using revised cash flows discounted at the original EIR, with a modification gain or loss recognised on the modification date. Does a new coupon become the new EIR after a non-substantial modification? No. The original EIR remains central to the remeasurement and subsequent amortised-cost schedule, subject to the prescribed fee treatment. Can the tax treatment be copied from the Ind AS journal? No. Taxability, deductibility, withholding and deferred tax require a separate analysis under applicable tax law. Key Takeaways Identify the obligation and any settled or converted portion before testing the remaining liability. Apply the 10% test with contractual cash flows, original EIR and only qualifying borrower-lender fees. Do not ignore a fundamental qualitative change merely because the quantitative result is below 10%. Derecognition creates a new liability; non-substantial modification retains and remeasures the old one.

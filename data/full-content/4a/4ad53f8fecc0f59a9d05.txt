Table of Contents▸ Diligent Media Corporation Limited Vs Datalink Multi Trading Private Limited (NCLT Mumbai) Images Images Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); Diligent Media Corporation Limited Vs Datalink Multi Trading Private Limited (NCLT Mumbai) Summary : The National Company Law Tribunal (NCLT), Mumbai Bench-I, approved the resolution plan submitted by Real Value Infotech Projects Private Limited for Datalink Multi Trading Private Limited under Sections 30(6) and 31(1) of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016 (IBC). The plan, dated 13 September 2024 and supplemented by three addenda, received 100% approval from the Committee of Creditors (CoC). The insolvency proceedings originated from a petition filed by operational creditor Diligent Media Corporation Limited. Subsequently, DC Play Distribution Private Limited became the sole financial creditor after admission of its ₹25 crore claim. The Tribunal examined the resolution plan’s compliance with statutory requirements, the valuation of corporate assets, the treatment of creditors, the implementation mechanism and the reliefs sought by the successful resolution applicant. The approved plan involved direct consideration of ₹26.50 lakh and an aggregate stated plan value of ₹60,89,744, including ₹34,39,744 held in the corporate debtor’s IDBI Bank account. The financial proposal allocated ₹25 lakh towards CIRP costs, ₹50,000 towards operational creditors and ₹1 lakh as direct payment to the unsecured financial creditor. The existing bank balance was also to be transferred to that creditor. Additionally, the resolution applicant proposed to distribute 25% of recoveries from trade receivables, after deducting recovery expenses, to the unsecured financial creditor. No payment was allocated towards statutory dues because no corresponding claims had been admitted. The Tribunal clarified that references to a secured financial creditor in the plan documents must be interpreted as references to the sole unsecured financial creditor. A significant issue concerned the valuation of substantial assets appearing in the corporate debtor’s financial statements. The Tribunal observed that short-term loans and advances exceeding ₹50 crore and investments in optionally convertible debentures and other instruments exceeding ₹90 crore had been assigned nil values. It repeatedly sought explanations regarding the identity and status of recipient companies, their possible relationships with the corporate debtor, and recovery efforts. The Resolution Professional furnished additional affidavits, MCA records and information concerning communications with the entities. The applicant explained that some entities were under liquidation or non-functional, that the amounts were considered unrecoverable or time-barred, and that impairment provisions had been recorded in the financial statements. However, the loans and investments were to remain in the corporate debtor’s books without being written off under the resolution plan. The Tribunal also examined the identity of the sole financial creditor because the corporate debtor’s earlier balance sheet recorded ₹25 crore in optionally convertible debentures against Bigule Tech Private Limited rather than DC Play Distribution Private Limited. The Resolution Professional explained that Bigule Tech had merged with Satcab Subscriber Services Private Limited, subsequently renamed DC Play Distribution Private Limited. The Tribunal referred to the Delhi NCLT’s merger order dated 20 January 2022 and accepted the explanation concerning succession to the relevant assets and liabilities. It additionally examined the difference between the recorded bank balance and the amount valued, attributable to an unencashed Axis Bank demand draft of approximately ₹1.73 lakh. On statutory compliance, the Tribunal considered the Resolution Professional’s Form-H certificate, due diligence materials and the successful resolution applicant’s eligibility under Section 29A of the IBC. It found that the plan satisfied Section 30(2) and the applicable CIRP Regulations. Referring to the Supreme Court’s decision in K. Sashidhar v. Indian Overseas Bank, the Tribunal reiterated that its jurisdiction over a CoC-approved resolution plan is confined to examining statutory compliance rather than substituting its commercial judgment for that of the creditors. Regarding reliefs and concessions, the Tribunal applied the principles laid down in Ghanshyam Mishra and Sons Private Limited v. Edelweiss Asset Reconstruction Company Limited and referred to Municipal Corporation of Greater Mumbai v. Abhilash Lal. It clarified that claims, including statutory dues relating to the period preceding approval, which are not included in the approved resolution plan stand extinguished in accordance with the governing legal principles. However, the resolution applicant must comply with applicable procedures concerning corporate filings, capital restructuring, licences and statutory approvals. The Tribunal further held that the carry-forward of business losses and unabsorbed depreciation would remain subject to the Income-tax Act, 1961, and that the Income Tax Department could examine their eligibility under the applicable provisions. Accordingly, the NCLT approved the resolution plan with effect from 3 November 2026, making it binding on the corporate debtor, creditors, governmental authorities, guarantors and other stakeholders covered by Section 31 of the IBC. The moratorium under Section 14 ceased from the date of approval. The Tribunal directed the implementation of the plan, necessary corporate filings, communication of records to the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Board of India, and periodic reporting on implementation. The decision illustrates that unanimous CoC approval, supported by compliance with statutory requirements, can result in approval of a resolution plan even where substantial book assets have been impaired, while tax benefits and regulatory concessions remain subject to applicable law. In K Sashidhar v. Indian Overseas Bank & Others (in Civil Appeal No.10673/2018 decided on 05.02.2019) the Hon’ble Apex Court held that if the CoC had approved the Resolution Plan by requisite percent of voting share, then as per Section 30(6) of the Code, it is imperative for the Resolution Professional to submit the same to the Adjudicating Authority (NCLT). On receipt of such a proposal, the Adjudicating Authority is required to satisfy itself that the Resolution Plan as approved by CoC meets the requirements specified in Section 30(2) of the Code. The Hon’ble Apex Court further observed that the role of the NCLT is ‘no more and no less’. The Hon’ble Apex Court further held that the discretion of the Adjudicating Authority is circumscribed by Section 31 of the Code and is limited to scrutiny of the Resolution Plan “as approved” by the requisite percent of voting share of financial creditors. Even in that enquiry, the grounds on which the Adjudicating Authority can reject the Resolution Plan is in reference to matters specified in Section 30(2) of the Code when the Resolution Plan does not conform to the stated requirements. 55.

Summary: The Ghaziabad State Bench of the Goods and Services Tax Appellate Tribunal, in a decision dated 1 October 2026 involving Gainwell Commosales Private Limited, dismissed a departmental appeal concerning a penalty of Rs. 11,88,000 imposed under Section 129(3) of the UPGST Act, 2017. The dealer had successfully challenged the penalty before the first appellate authority, following which the Department filed an appeal under Section 112(3). The Tribunal held that the disputed amount was below the Rs. 20 lakh monetary threshold prescribed under Section 120 and the Uttar Pradesh State Tax Commissioner’s litigation policy. It found that the Revenue had neither pleaded nor established any applicable exception permitting an appeal below the prescribed limit. Importantly, the Tribunal distinguished the Commissioner’s statutory authorisation to file an appeal from the separate requirement of establishing a recognised exception to the monetary threshold. A routine authorisation, without case-specific consideration supporting invocation of an exception, was held insufficient. Consequently, the appeal was dismissed at the admission stage without examining the underlying penalty dispute on merits. The ruling reinforces the importance of monetary-limit objections, statutory appeal conditions and administrative litigation discipline. However, its reasoning requires careful consideration because the penalty was described as disputed tax in certain passages, and exceptions relating to recurring legal questions or statutory interpretation may still permit departmental appeals involving smaller amounts. The decision therefore supports threshold scrutiny of departmental appeals without establishing an absolute prohibition on all appeals below Rs. 20 lakh. The order, passed on 01.10.2026 by a Division Bench comprising Dr. Sanjay Kumar Chandhariyavi, Member (Judicial) and Smt. Sungita Sharma, Member (Technical), in Filing No. 2026109301001667, is among the first reasoned rulings of the Tribunal on the litigation policy framed under Section 120 of the UPGST Act, 2017. The significance lies less in the amount than in the principle. For the first time the Tribunal has said, in clear terms, that an authorisation under Section 112(3) is not a licence to bypass the monetary limit, and that where the Department relies on the Commissioner’s residual power, the record must show that the Commissioner applied his mind to that particular case. For every dealer who has won before the first appellate authority and now faces an APL-07 filed by the Department, this order opens a threshold objection that deserves to be taken at the very first hearing. The Facts in Brief The dealer, Gainwell Commosales Private Limited (GSTIN 09AAFCG8736M1ZX, Ecotech II, Greater Noida), had a consignment on vehicle No. UP14KT1118 detained, and a penalty of Rs. 11,88,000 was imposed under Section 129(3). The dealer’s first appeal (AD090725066501Y) was allowed and the penalty set aside by the appellate order bearing APL-04 Reference No. ZD090825014597Y. The Department, through its officer Sandeep Tiwari, carried the matter to the Tribunal in FORM GST APL-07, stating that the appeal was filed under Section 112(3). The Authorised Representative pressed for admission and a hearing on merits. The Tribunal declined to reach the merits, examined maintainability first, and dismissed the appeal at the threshold. Section 120(1) of the UPGST Act empowers the Commissioner, on the recommendations of the GST Council, to fix monetary limits for the purpose of regulating appeals and applications filed by officers of the State tax. Sub sections (2) and (3) protect the Revenue: not filing in one case does not bar an appeal in another case on the same issue, and no party can plead that the Department acquiesced merely because it did not appeal. Sub section (4) directs the Tribunal to have regard to the circumstances in which an appeal was not filed. The provision is modelled on Section 268A of the Income Tax Act, 1961, under which the ITAT and the High Courts have for years dismissed departmental appeals below the CBDT limits. Across the country, the limits followed the 53rd GST Council meeting of June 2024. For CGST, the Board issued Circular No. 207/1/2024-GST dated 26.06.2024 fixing Rs. 20 lakh for the Tribunal, Rs. 1 crore for the High Court and Rs. 2 crore for the Supreme Court. In Uttar Pradesh, the Commissioner issued Circular No. 2425008 dated 05.07.2024 prescribing Rs. 20,00,000 for departmental appeals before GSTAT. The framework was carried forward by Circular No. 252/2026-27/State Tax dated 22.05.2026, which adds review through Zonal Law Committees. The circular also lays down how the amount is to be measured. Where tax is disputed, only the tax and cess count, and interest and penalty are left out. Where only penalty is disputed, the penalty is the measure. Where interest, penalty or late fee is disputed without tax, their aggregate counts. Composite orders are taken together. The exceptions, set out in Clause 4, permit an appeal irrespective of amount where: a provision of the CGST, UPGST, IGST or Compensation Act has been held unconstitutional; a rule or regulation has been held ultra vires the parent Act; a notification, circular, order or instruction has been held contrary to the Act or Rules; the matter concerns valuation, classification, refund, place of supply, or a recurring issue or one involving interpretation of law; strictures, adverse comments or costs have been passed against the Government, Department or its officers; the Commissioner considers it necessary to contest a case or class of cases in the interest of justice or revenue. Finally, Section 112(3) requires the Commissioner to call for the record, satisfy himself on the legality or propriety of the appellate order, and by order direct a subordinate officer to apply to the Tribunal on the points specified in that order. Under Section 112(4) such an application is heard as an appeal, and therefore carries all the conditions that govern appeals. What the Tribunal Held The Bench framed seven issues and answered them in a straight line. The findings may be stated as five propositions. Admission is not a formality. Relying on Ganga Bai v. Vijay Kumar, (1974) 2 SCC 393 and Anant Mills Co. Ltd. v. State of Gujarat, (1975) 2 SCC 175, the Tribunal held that an appeal is a creature of statute and may be made subject to conditions. Filing an appeal does not by itself confer a right to a hearing on merits. Section 112 must be read with Section 120. The Department’s right of appeal under Section 112 is not absolute. It is regulated by the monetary limit instructions issued under Section 120, and the Tribunal is entitled to test compliance before admitting. The burden lies on the Revenue. Once the amount is shown to be below Rs. 20 lakh, it is for the Revenue to identify the exception, plead the facts supporting it and show its basis. An exception cannot be presumed from the mere wish to appeal. Authorisation is not an exception. Permission to file and compliance with the limit are separate matters. Where the residual power of the Commissioner is invoked, the record must contain his order or recorded opinion showing that he considered this particular case and found it worth contesting despite the limit. A bare statement that the appeal is filed with the Commissioner’s approval is not enough; otherwise every small appeal would pass on routine sanction and the policy would be defeated. Maintainability comes before merits. An arguable case on merits does not overcome the binding policy. The Tribunal therefore refused to examine the Section 129 question at all. The Bench drew support from Ranadey Micronutrients v. Collector of Central Excise, (1996) 10 SCC 387 on the binding effect of circulars on the Department, from Director of Income Tax v. S.R.M.B. Dairy Farming (P) Ltd. on the need to curb needless government litigation, and from Commissioner of Commercial Tax v. Vikram Cement (decided 05.02.2026), where the Supreme Court is stated to have applied the Rs. 20 lakh GST limit to pending appeals as well. The appeal was dismissed at the threshold, with an express clarification that nothing in the order decides the correctness of the first appellate order on merits. A Critical Look at the Reasoning The order is right in its result and strong on its central point, but it has soft spots that the Department will look for and that counsel citing it should know about. Where it is strongest. The reading of Section 112(3) is the heart of the order and it is sound. The sub section does not speak of permission; it speaks of the Commissioner calling for the record, satisfying himself and specifying the points for the Tribunal. An order that does all this would naturally record why a case below the limit must still be fought. Demanding that record is not a technicality; it is simply asking the Department to show that the statutory satisfaction exists. The approach also matches settled income tax practice under Section 268A, where low tax effect appeals are routinely dismissed unless the Department establishes an exception. The amount is mislabelled. In paragraphs 5 and 16(ii) the Tribunal calls Rs. 11,88,000 the “disputed tax demand”. It is nothing of the kind. A Section 129(3) order imposes penalty, and the correct route under the circular is the clause applying where only penalty is disputed. The conclusion does not change, since the penalty is in any case below Rs. 20 lakh, but the slip gives the Department an easy line of attack and should be explained away rather than repeated when the order is cited. The figure also appears as “?11,88,000” and “Rs. _?11,88,000”, a rupee symbol lost in conversion. It is a pleading based dismissal. The ratio is that the Revenue neither pleaded nor proved an exception. It is not a finding that Section 129 matters fall outside the exceptions. That distinction matters, because exception (iv) covers any recurring issue or one involving interpretation of the Act, Rules or circulars. Detention penalties frequently turn on precisely such questions, for instance whether a technical lapse in an e way bill attracts Section 129 at all in the face of Circular No. 76/50/2018-GST, or whether intent to evade is a necessary ingredient. A better drafted APL-07 that pleads exception (iv) with reasons, and annexes the Commissioner’s order, may well survive admission. Dealers should not treat this order as closing the door on every small departmental appeal. The source of the Tribunal’s power deserves a firmer footing. Section 120 regulates the filing of appeals by officers; it does not in terms direct the Tribunal to dismiss. The order bridges the gap through Section 112(4) and the binding effect of circulars on the Department under Ranadey Micronutrients. That is a defensible bridge, and Section 120(4), which tells the Tribunal to have regard to the circumstances of non filing, shows the legislature contemplated the Tribunal taking the policy into account. A more explicit reliance on the parallel Section 268A jurisprudence would have made the reasoning harder to dislodge. Citations need checking before reuse. Several authorities are cited without reporter references: Vikram Cement, S.R.M.B. Dairy Farming, State of Maharashtra v. Greatship (India) Ltd. and Commissioner of Customs v. Balaji Overseas. Greatship is better known for the rule on alternative remedies in writ jurisdiction than as authority for appeals being creatures of statute, and the figure attributed to Balaji Overseas sits oddly with a low tax effect disposal. Counsel relying on these passages should verify the reported text before quoting them in a pleading. Practically, the order is likely to be final. Any further challenge by the Department would be an appeal under Section 117 to the High Court. If the State’s High Court limit follows the CBIC figure of Rs. 1 crore, a Rs. 11.88 lakh penalty is far below it, and the Department would face the same objection one level higher. Conclusion The Ghaziabad Bench has given the monetary limit real teeth. By separating the Commissioner’s authorisation from compliance with the limit, and by placing the burden of proving an exception squarely on the Revenue, it has turned an administrative circular into an effective filter at the Tribunal’s door. The order has its flaws: the penalty is wrongly described as tax, some authorities are cited loosely, and the dismissal rests on the Department’s failure to plead rather than on any holding that small Section 129 matters can never qualify. None of these disturbs the core holding. For taxpayers who have won before the first appellate authority, the message is that a modest demand should not have to be defended twice. For the Department, it is a reminder that the State is expected to litigate by its own declared policy, and that a policy which binds when it helps must also bind when it restrains.

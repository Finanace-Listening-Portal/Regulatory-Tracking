Summary: The article examines the entitlement to interest under Section 56 of the CGST Act, 2017, where a refund claim initially rejected by the proper officer is subsequently allowed by an Appellate Authority, Appellate Tribunal or Court. The author argues that interest at 6% becomes payable immediately after the expiry of sixty days from receipt of the original complete refund application and continues until actual disbursement, notwithstanding the filing of a consequential refund application following appellate relief. The proviso to Section 56 separately provides for enhanced interest at 9% where a refund arising from an order that has attained finality remains unpaid beyond sixty days from receipt of the consequential application. According to the author, the proviso cannot extinguish the interest entitlement arising under the main provision. Through practical illustrations, the article explains the distinction between the two interest rates and their respective periods of applicability. It also discusses the GST Appellate Tribunal, Vijayawada, decision in Shapoorji Pallonji and Company Private Limited Vs Commissioner (State Tax), Guntur, dated 11 November 2026, as reported in the article, which recognised the original refund application as the relevant starting point for calculating interest at 6%, subject to statutory conditions. The author emphasises the legal fiction contained in the Explanation to Section 56 and advocates a harmonious interpretation of Sections 54 and 56 to prevent taxpayers from losing interest merely because their admissible refund claims were initially rejected. The said Section is extracted below for ready reference. 56. Interest on delayed refunds.— If any tax ordered to be refunded under sub-section (5) of section 54 to any applicant is not refunded within sixty days from the date of receipt of application under sub-section (1) of that section, interest at such rate not exceeding six per cent. as may be specified in the notification issued by the Government on the recommendations of the Council shall be payable in respect of such refund from the date immediately after the expiry of sixty days from the date of receipt of application under the said sub-section till the date of refund of such tax: Provided that where any claim of refund arises from an order passed by an adjudicating authority or Appellate Authority or Appellate Tribunal or court which has attained finality and the same is not refunded within sixty days from the date of receipt of application filed consequent to such order, interest at such rate not exceeding nine per cent as may be notified by the Government on the recommendations of the Council shall be payable in respect of such refund from the date immediately after the expiry of sixty days from the date of receipt of application till the date of refund. Explanation.––For the purposes of this section, where any order of refund is made by an Appellate Authority, Appellate Tribunal or any court against an order of the proper officer under sub-section (5) of section 54, the order passed by the Appellate Authority, Appellate Tribunal or by the court shall be deemed to be an order passed under the said sub-section (5). Departmental Interpretation of the Sixty-Day Period 2. In several cases, the Department has taken the view that once the appellate order is passed, the taxpayer has to file a fresh refund application and that the Department gets another period of sixty days to sanction the refund. If the refund is sanctioned within those sixty days, the Department rejects the claim for interest altogether, notwithstanding the fact that the taxpayer had originally filed the refund application much earlier and was deprived of the amount because of the rejection order. This kind of interpretation raises an important question that whether the Department, by initially rejecting a legally admissible refund can extinguish the interest liability which would otherwise have arisen under Section 56 of the CGST Act, 2017?. SGST/UT GST laws too have similar provisions. Refund of Accumulated ITC Under Section 54(3) 3. It may be noted that Section 54(3) of the CGST Act,2017 permits a registered person to claim refund of unutilised input tax credit arising on account of an inverted tax structure in addition to refund due to exports under bond/LUT, subject to the statutory conditions. For instance, let us consider a case of a manufacturer procuring inputs attracting GST at 18% and supplying the finished goods attracting GST at 5%. The accumulation of input tax credit on account of the higher rate of tax on inputs may make the taxpayer eligible for refund, subject to the restrictions and formula prescribed under Rule 89(5) of the CGST Rules,2017. Similarly, a service provider or works contractor may accumulate credit where the applicable input and output tax rates result in an eligible inverted duty structure. The taxpayer files Form GST RFD-01 with the prescribed supporting documents. Section 54(7) requires the proper officer to issue an order under Section 54(5) within sixty days from receipt of an application complete in all respects. Section 56 provides for 6% interest where the refund is not made within the prescribed period of sixty days. Thus, the said sixty-day period assumes importance in view of its mention both in the main provision of Section 56(1) and in the proviso to the said Section. Legal Fiction Under the Explanation to Section 56 4. The legal fiction contained in the Explanation to Section 56 needs due consideration and importance in this context. It provides that where an order of refund is made by an Appellate Authority, Appellate Tribunal or any Court against an order of the proper officer under Section 54(5) of the CGST Act,2017, such appellate or judicial order shall be deemed to be an order passed under Section 54(5). The legislative intention appears to be that an order allowing refund in appeal should receive the same legal recognition as an order passed by the proper officer determining the refund under Section 54(5) initially. It may be noted that the provision does not declare the Appellate Authority or the Tribunal or the Court to be a proper officers. Instead, it creates a deeming fiction regarding the character of the refund order approved by those appellate fora. The effect of this fiction cannot be diluted by treating the appellate order as merely a direction to commence an entirely new refund proceeding, thereby wiping out the consequences of the original application to pay 6% interest if the refund is sanctioned after sixty days of filing the refund application under Section 54(1) read with Rule 89(1) of the CGST Rules,2017. Distinction Between 6% and 9% Interest on Delayed GST Refunds 5. It is necessary to read Section 56 in its entirety, including its proviso and the Explanation. The main provision provides for interest at a rate not exceeding 6% where the refund ordered under Section 54(5) is not made within sixty days from receipt of the application under Section 54(1). The proviso deals with a different situation. Where a refund claim arises from an order passed by an adjudicating authority, Appellate Authority, Appellate Tribunal or Court which has attained finality, and the refund is not made within sixty days from receipt of the application filed consequent to such order, interest at a rate not exceeding 9% is payable. Notification No. 13/2017-Central Tax dated 28 June 2017 prescribes 6% under the main provision and 9% under the proviso. The two rates are therefore applicable to two distinct stages of delay. The proviso cannot be interpreted to mean that the taxpayer loses the initial 6% interest already accrued under the main provision merely because the refund is subsequently sanctioned within sixty days of the consequential application after winning in appeal. 6. There is a debate on the rate of applicable interest for delayed refunds following appellate relief, particularly whether the taxpayer is entitled to 6% or 9%. In my view, the distinction between the two provisions is important. Interest at 6% is payable for the delay commencing immediately after the expiry of sixty days from the original refund application, provided it was complete in all respects. If the refund is subsequently allowed in appeal and the consequential application is not processed within another sixty days, the proviso provides for enhanced interest at 9% after expiry of that subsequent sixty-day period. Thus, the 9% rate is not automatically available merely because the refund is sanctioned after sixty days of initial filing of refund application due to the orders passed by the appellate authority/Tribunal/Court allowing the refund appeals. Further, the 9% proviso cannot be used as a reason to deny the 6% interest which had already accrued under the main provision even if the consequential refund is sanctioned within sixty days of consequential refiling of refund application. Practical Illustrations of Interest on Delayed GST Refunds Illustration 1: Refund Paid Within Sixty Days of Consequential Application 7. A simple illustration would clarify the position. Let us assume that a manufacturer procuring inputs attracting GST at 18% and supplying finished goods attracting GST at 5% files an inverted duty structure refund application on 15th April 2025 for Rs. 20 lakh. The proper officer rejects the claim on 10th June 2025, taking an incorrect view regarding refund eligibility. The taxpayer files an appeal and the Appellate Authority allows the refund on 30th April 2026. The taxpayer files a consequential refund application on 10th May 2026. The Department sanctions and pays the principal refund on 5th July 2026, within sixty days of the consequential refund application but does not pay interest though specifically claimed by the applicant. Can the Department deny 6% interest on the ground that the refund was paid within sixty days on 5th July 2026?. In my view, that kind of interpretation not to pay interest would defeat the intent on interest payment contained in main provision of Section 56 in case of delay in refunding the sanctioned amount . Subject to the original application being complete and the refund being ultimately admissible, interest at 6% needs to be calculated from the day immediately following the expiry of sixty days from 15th April 2025 until 5th July 2026, the day on which refund was paid. The fact that the refund was paid within sixty days of the consequential application does not erase the earlier delay beyond sixty days of initial filing of the refund application on 15th April 2025 as per the main provision of Section 56 of the CGST Act,2017. Illustration 2: Refund Delayed Beyond Sixty Days of Consequential Application 8. However, if the consequential refund filed on 10th May 2026 is sanctioned on 10th September 2026 which is after sixty days of consequential filing, the taxpayer would be entitled to 6% interest for the period commencing after the expiry of sixty days from the original application on 15th April 2025, up to the expiry of sixty days from the consequential application on 10th May 2026. Thereafter, if the conditions of the proviso are fulfilled and the appellate order has attained finality, the enhanced interest at 9% becomes payable from the day immediately following the expiry of the latter sixty-day period until actual payment of refund on 10th September 2026. Therefore, the proviso is intended to compensate the taxpayer at a higher rate where the Department continues to delay implementation even after the appellate relief has attained finality. The two periods and rates have to be calculated separately. Recent GSTAT Vijayawada Judgment on Interest on Delayed GST Refunds Facts of Shapoorji Pallonji and Company Private Limited Case 9. The above controversy on interest payment came up for consideration before the Hon’ble GST Appellate Tribunal, Andhra Pradesh State Bench, Vijayawada, in M/s. Shapoorji Pallonji and Company Private Limited vs Commissioner (State Tax), Guntur, common order dated 11 November 2026 in Appeal Nos. APL/7/VJY/2026 and connected appeals. The appellant, engaged in works contracts and allied services, had filed eight refund applications under the inverted duty structure for tax periods from December 2018 to March 2020. The original refund applications were filed between 18 January 2023 and 29 March 2023. The Department rejected the claims by relying on Notification No. 15/2017-Central Tax (Rate), which restricted refund of accumulated credit in respect of specified construction services. The First Appellate Authority, by separate orders dated 23 September 2023, set aside the rejection orders and allowed the refund claims. The taxpayer thereafter filed consequential refund applications on 25 and 26 September 2023. The principal refund amounts were sanctioned during November 2023, but interest was denied on the ground that the refunds had been sanctioned within sixty days of the consequential applications. The appellate authority also endorsed the said view on non-payment of interest. Findings of GSTAT and Judicial Precedents 10. On appeal against the orders of appellate authority rejecting payment of interest, the Hon’ble GSTAT, Vijayawada examined the statutory scheme of Sections 54 and 56, the Explanation to Section 56, Rule 89 and Rule 93 of the CGST Rules,2017. The Tribunal also considered the judgments in Ranbaxy Laboratories Ltd. v. Union of India, SBI Cards and Payment Services Ltd. v. Union of India, Lupin Limited v. Union of India, Qualcomm India Private Limited v. Deputy Commissioner (ST) and Altisource Business Solutions India Pvt. Ltd. v. Union of India. The Tribunal held that a consequential refund application filed pursuant to an appellate order is a procedural requirement for implementing the appellate relief. It does not replace the original refund application for calculating interest under the main provision of Section 56 of the CGST Act,2017. The Explanation to Section 56 gives the appellate order the legal character of an order under Section 54(5) . The Tribunal accordingly held that the taxpayer was entitled to interest at 6% from the day immediately following the expiry of sixty days from the respective original refund applications until actual disbursement, subject to verification of completeness and other statutory conditions. The Department was directed to undertake the calculation separately for all eight claims and complete the exercise within eight weeks from the receipt of the order. Implications of the GSTAT Ruling for Taxpayers and the Department 11. The decision is significant because it addresses the very approach adopted by the Department in rejecting interest claims after granting principal refunds in pursuance of appellate orders. If the Department’s interpretation were accepted, it would create an undesirable situation eternally. A taxpayer files a refund claim, the Department rejects it, the taxpayer spends time and resources pursuing an appeal, and even after succeeding, the taxpayer is told that no interest is payable because the Department has released the amount within sixty days of the consequential application. In effect, the Department would unduly benefit from its own erroneous rejection by not paying interest on refund for the period of delay due to various reasons including the period spent on appeals. There is a genuine concern that if interest is not recognised with reference to the original refund application, there is every likelihood that initially filed refund claims may routinely be rejected, tax payers’ favourable orders appealed, leaving taxpayers to approach the Appellate Authority or GSTAT, High Court or Supreme Court for relief. Such an interpretation would make appellate proceedings an almost compulsory step for obtaining a refund which ought to have been granted at the first stage. It would also undermine the statutory discipline contemplated under Section 54(7) of the CGST Act,2017 to pass refund order within sixty days and paid to the applicant in case of refund under Section 54(3) as provided under Section 54(8) so that interest payment can be avoided. Consistency With Earlier Judicial Decisions 12. It may also be noted that the GSTAT Vijayawada decision is consistent with the reasoning adopted by the Hon’ble Supreme Court’s decision in Ranbaxy Laboratories Ltd. v. Union of India and the judgements from the Hon’ble Telangana and Bombay High Courts, though rendered under the erstwhile Central Excise law to pay applicable interest from the original refund application and not postponed merely because the refund is ultimately sanctioned by an appellate order. However, entitlement to refund and interest is not automatic in every case. It may be stated that a genuine and complete refund claim, subsequently allowed in appeal, should not lose its accrued interest merely because the Department took an incorrect view at the initial stage. Conclusion: Interest Liability Must Be Considered From the Original Refund Application 13. In conclusion, Section 54 and Section 56 must be read harmoniously so that the statutory right to refund and the corresponding right to interest are not defeated by procedural interpretations. The Explanation to Section 56 gives legal recognition to an appellate order allowing refund as an order under Section 54(5). The main provision protects the taxpayer by providing 6% interest after expiry of sixty days from the original refund application, while the proviso provides enhanced interest at 9% if the consequential refund is further delayed beyond sixty days after the filing of consequential refund application after appellate order has attained finality. The recent GSTAT Vijayawada order in Shapoorji Pallonji placing reliance on the judgments of High Courts and Supreme Court, is a welcome development in this direction. The Department may consider granting the applicable interest along with the principal refund from the date after sixty days of initial refund application wherever appellate relief is implemented, instead of compelling taxpayers to file another round of proceedings solely for interest. 14. Therefore, even the 6% interest liability from the date of initial filing of refund needs to be considered carefully while rejecting the refund claims apart from other reasons for rejection of refund claim. ***** (Views expressed are personal views of the author.

Including overseas foreign currency borrowings and external commercial borrowings, total mobilisation under the facility reached $136.4 billion. The banking system's liquidity surplus stood at around Rs10.5 lakh crore on September 9, after touching a record Rs11.6 lakh crore on September 7. Advt The immediate challenge for lenders is therefore not a shortage of funds but how quickly they can convert the additional liquidity into loans or other earning assets without weakening underwriting standards or lending spreads. Setty said the inflows were unlikely to result in abnormal lending and that banks would deploy the liquidity gradually over the next three to four months.The deployment is already taking different forms across banks. ICICI Bank, which mobilised $17.88 billion under the scheme, has also issued standby letters of credit worth $3.63 billion to other banks against such deposits, according to its exchange disclosure.Banks have broadly three avenues for deploying the funds: lending to companies, investing in government securities or replacing higher-cost deposits. But with credit growth already strong, particularly in corporate lending, the ability to deploy the entire inflow quickly without compromising returns remains a question.Different challengesThe issue is also beginning to play out differently across lenders. Axis Bank chief executive Amitabh Chaudhry said the current 18-19 per cent credit growth could moderate to 15-16 per cent for the full year, while noting that FCNR(B) inflows could temporarily push lending above its underlying trajectory. Advt This creates the possibility of stronger competition among banks for high-quality borrowers as lenders seek to put the additional liquidity to work. At the same time, aggressive deployment could put pressure on lending spreads if banks compete on price.HDFC Bank has cut its marginal cost of funds-based lending rates by 5-10 basis points across tenures from September 7. Its one-year MCLR is now 8.35 per cent. The move coincides with the system-wide liquidity surplus, although it should not be directly attributed to FCNR(B) inflows.The scale of the inflows has already required substantial RBI intervention. The central bank absorbed more than Rs6 lakh crore through overnight and 30-day operations on September 7 after the liquidity surplus reached Rs11.6 lakh crore, and has subsequently used other instruments, including foreign exchange sell-buy swaps, to manage the surplus. Advt The FCNR(B) windfall has therefore shifted the banking sector's challenge from raising funds to deploying them efficiently. Setty's three-to-four-month timeline suggests that the impact on credit growth will emerge gradually, while competition for borrowers could determine how much of the funding advantage ultimately translates into stronger bank earnings rather than lower lending spreads.

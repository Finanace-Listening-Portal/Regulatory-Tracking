Vs District Collector (Kerala High Court) Becoming a Christian Nun Does Not Bar Inheritance Under the Indian Succession Act, 1925: Kerala High Court Summary: The Kerala High Court held in Jose A.M. Vs District Collector, Kozhikode and Others that a co-owner who lives near and manages family land cannot claim the entire solatium payable on its compulsory acquisition. The Court upheld equal distribution of the compensation, including solatium, among six siblings. It also held that an unregistered family agreement could not extinguish one sibling’s rights in the property and that another sibling’s entry into a religious order did not disqualify her from inheriting under the Indian Succession Act, 1925. Acquisition Award and Dispute Among the Siblings The acquisition concerned 3.295 acres that had belonged to the father of Jose and respondents 4 to 8. The Special Tahsildar deposited the total award amount of ₹3,74,34,449, including solatium under Section 30 of the Right to Fair Compensation and Transparency in Land Acquisition, Rehabilitation and Resettlement Act, 2013. The dispute over apportionment came before the Land Acquisition, Rehabilitation and Resettlement Authority at Kozhikode on a reference under Section 76 of the Act. In L.A.R. No. 38 of 2024, the Authority directed that the amount on deposit be released in equal shares to Jose and his five siblings. Jose challenged that decision. He argued that his siblings had suffered no pain from the acquisition and should receive no part of the solatium. He also contended that one brother had relinquished his rights under a family settlement and that one sister, having become a nun, was disqualified from receiving any part of the compensation. Managing the Property Did Not Confer a Preferential Right The Court explained that solatium is a statutory component of compensation for compulsory acquisition. Market value measures the financial worth of the land, while solatium addresses the hardship associated with being forced to part with it. That hardship can include the loss of emotional security and attachment to a home or family property. Jose was said to live near the acquired land and to have managed it until the acquisition. His siblings may have moved away from their parental home, but the Court found no basis to infer that they had lost their emotional attachment to the family property or that Jose necessarily suffered more because he lived nearby. His management of the land was an act performed on behalf of all the co-owners. It gave him no special right to the entire solatium, and the Court upheld its inclusion in the amount to be shared equally. Family Agreement Required Registration to Extinguish Property Rights Jose relied on Ext. P3, an agreement dated 29 August 2009, to argue that the fourth respondent had relinquished his rights in their father’s properties. The Court examined what the document itself was intended to do. An instrument that creates or extinguishes rights in immovable property requires registration. A memorandum prepared simply to record an earlier family arrangement can be treated differently because the memorandum itself effects no change in those rights. The Court found that Ext. P3 was the instrument through which the brother’s rights were sought to be extinguished. It therefore required registration under Section 17 read with Section 49 of the Registration Act. As the agreement was unregistered, the Court declined to give effect to the alleged relinquishment. The fourth respondent consequently remained entitled to his share of the acquisition compensation. Entry Into a Religious Order Did Not Bar Succession Jose also argued that the sixth respondent had incurred civil death by becoming a nun and had therefore lost her right to share in the compensation. The Court relied on Molly Joseph Vs George Sebastian, in which the Supreme Court held that legislation prevails over a conflicting personal law, usage or custom. The Indian Succession Act, 1925 contains no special exclusion governing inheritance or succession by a Christian priest or nun. The Court also relied on its Division Bench decision in M.S.G.R. Xavier Chullikkal Vs C.G. Raphel, which held that joining a religious order and renouncing worldly pleasures does not automatically deprive a person of property acquired through intestate or testamentary succession. It noted that the same view had been followed in Mariyam alias Beatrice Vs Fr. Joseph Mattam. The sixth respondent’s religious vocation therefore supplied no ground to deny her a share in the award. High Court Upholds Equal Distribution of the Award Justice G. Girish dismissed W.P.(C) No. 4681 of 2026 on 13 November 2026, leaving the Authority’s direction for equal distribution in force. All six siblings remained entitled to equal shares of the deposited award, including solatium. The Court’s decision turned on their rights as co-owners, the absence of an effective relinquishment by the fourth respondent and the statutory protection of the sixth respondent’s succession rights. Jose’s role in looking after the property gave him no separate entitlement to the solatium component. Cases Discussed: Mariyam @ Beatrice Vs Fr. Joseph Mattam (Died) (Kerala High Court); R.S.A. No. 62 of 2024; 2024 KHC 123; 07/02/2024. FULL TEXT OF THE ORIGINAL JUDGMENT/ORDER Ext.P5 judgment rendered by the Land Acquisition Rehabilitation and Resettlement Authority (Additional District Court-IV), Kozhikode, in L.A.R No.38/2024 in a reference under Section 76 of the Right to Fair Compensation and Transparency in Land Acquisition, Rehabilitation and Resettlement Act, 2013 (for short ‘the LARR Act’), is under challenge in this writ petition filed by the ‘A’ party in that proceedings. 2. The issue is related to the apportionment of shares of the amount of Rs.3,74,34,449/- deposited by the second respondent herein before the above Court as the total award amount, including solatium in terms of Section 30 of the LARR Act, in connection with the acquisition of 3.295 Acres of land which belonged to the father of the petitioner and respondents 4 to 8. The petitioner would contend that the respondents 4 to 8, who are his siblings, are not entitled to any share of solatium since they have not undergone any pain and sufferings of the acquisition of the land belonging to their father. Another contention raised by the petitioner is that the 4th respondent had entered into Ext.P3 agreement in the form of family settlement, relinquishing his right over the properties of his father, and hence he is not entitled for a share of the compensation amount. The petitioner would further contend that the 6th respondent has become a nun who attained the status of ‘civil death’, and hence she is not entitled to a share of the compensation. The Rehabilitation and Resettlement Authority repelled all the above contentions, and passed the impugned judgment directing the release of the amount in deposit equally among the petitioner and the respondents 4 to 8. The aforesaid judgment is under challenge in this writ petition. 3. Heard the learned counsel for the petitioner, the learned Government Pleader representing the respondents 1 to 3 and the learned counsel for respondents 4 to 8. 4. It is true that the term ‘solatium’ has got the meaning of compensation/damages allowed for injury to the feelings and mental sufferings. Wharton’s Law Lexicon characterises ‘solatium’ as an expression of solace or a compassionate allowance given to a person to mitigate the hardship, inconvenience or emotional pain caused by an involuntary act, such as the forced taking of their property. In P.Ramanatha Aiyar’s Law Lexicon (Indian context), ‘solatium’ is defined as a solace for loss, which emphasises that in compulsory land acquisition, solatium is a statutory addition to the market value of the property, awarded as a matter of right, to soothe the feelings of an owner, who is forced to part with his land against his will. Judicial precedents view solatium as a necessary component of just compensation, acknowledging that losing property involves more than just a loss of real estate, it often involves a loss of livelihood, community and emotional security. Unlike market value, which covers the financial worth of the land, solatium is intended to address non-pecuniary losses, such as the distress of being forced to leave one’s home or ancestral property. 5. In the present case, the property which was acquired admittedly belonged to the father of the petitioner and respondents 4 to 8. In that sense, it is the ancestral property of the petitioner and respondents 4 to 8. It may be true that the respondents 4 to 8 might be residing away from the acquired property pursuant to their shifting from their parental home during the course of their life. But, that does not mean that their emotional attachment with their ancestral property would have been lost by such shifting. So also, the petitioner, who is said to be residing near to the acquired property, and who had been managing the affairs of the said property till its acquisition, cannot be expected to have suffered an emotional trauma or suffering more than the emotional trauma or suffering undergone by respondents 4 to 8 in connection with the above acquisition of the land belonging to their father. This is especially so, since the management and looking after of the property by the petitioner as a co-owner of the property could only be termed as an act done as a representative of all the co-owners of that property. There is no basis for the contention that the aforesaid act of the petitioner taking care of the ancestral property for and on behalf of the co-owners of that property would confer him any special right to have exclusive allotment of solatium. Therefore, the challenge raised by the petitioner in the above regard is bereft of merit. 6. The contention of the petitioner that the 4th respondent has relinquished his rights over the property of their predecessor in view of the execution of Ext.P3 agreement, is prima facie unsustainable since the said document is legally invalid due to non-registration. It is well settled that if the terms of a family arrangement are reduced into writing, it requires registration to have legal validity. Of course, it is true that, after the making of a family arrangement, if a mere memorandum is prepared for the purpose of record, such a memorandum does not require registration since that document by itself does not create or extinguish any rights in immovable properties. As far as Ext.P3 is concerned, it is apparent from the said document that the extinguishment of the right of the 4th respondent over the family property was sought to be created by the said document. That being so, Ext.P3 is a compulsorily registrable document under Section 17 r/w Section 49 of the Registration Act. Therefore, the aforesaid unregistered document cannot be acted upon in any legal proceedings. Needless to say, the contention of the petitioner in the above regard, is totally untenable. 7. As regards the disqualification attributed to the 6th respondent to have a share of the compensation in view of her becoming a nun, it has to be stated that the law is well settled with the decision of the Hon’ble Supreme Court in Molly Joseph v. George Sebastian [1997 (1) KLT 1 (SC)] that the law enacted by the Legislature would prevail and override any personal law, usage or custom. The Indian Succession Act, 1925 does not make any departure in the matter of inheritance or succession of a Christian priest or nun. The proposition of law in the above regard is followed by a Division Bench of this Court in M.S.G.R Xavier Chullikkal and Others v. C.G.Raphel and Others [2017 (3) KHC 193] wherein it has been held that the mere fact that one has entered into a religious order and renounced his worldly pleasures, would not automatically result in the deprivation of property acquired by way of intestate or testamentary succession. The same view has been followed by this Court in Mariyam @ Beatrice v. Fr.Joseph Mattam (Died) [2024 KHC 123]. Therefore, the contention of the petitioner against the entitlement of the 6th respondent to have a share of the compensation amount, is totally unfounded. As a conclusion to the above decision, I find that the present writ petition is devoid of merit. In the result, the writ petition is hereby dismissed.

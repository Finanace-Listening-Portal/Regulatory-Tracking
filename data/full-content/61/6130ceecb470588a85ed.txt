Summary: A systematic investment plan (SIP) is a method of investing fixed or varying amounts at regular intervals, not a mutual fund product or a guarantee of profit. Reviewing an SIP requires more than checking whether its latest one-year return is positive. Investors should assess the underlying scheme’s investment objective, category, benchmark, risk profile, portfolio composition, costs and performance over appropriate market cycles. Rolling returns can reveal how outcomes varied across different entry periods, while risk-adjusted measures can help evaluate whether returns were achieved with disproportionate volatility. The current risk-o-meter, concentration in sectors or securities, portfolio turnover, expense ratio and differences between direct and regular plans deserve attention. A scheme that underperforms its benchmark briefly is not necessarily unsuitable, and one that tops a short-term ranking may not fit an investor’s time horizon or risk capacity. SIP cash flows should be evaluated using an appropriate money-weighted return calculation, while scheme performance comparisons require consistent periods and return conventions. Before stopping, redeeming or switching, investors should examine asset allocation, exit loads, taxation and potential consequences for long-term goals. This article explains a practical framework for reviewing SIP-linked mutual fund investments without mistaking recent market performance for a reliable forecast. Each instalment buys units at the applicable net asset value (NAV), so the investment experience differs from that of an investor who committed the full amount on a single date. A falling market may allow later SIP instalments to acquire more units, but it can also produce losses. Rupee-cost averaging does not eliminate market risk or guarantee an attractive outcome. Distinguish the scheme’s published performance from the investor’s own return. A fund factsheet may present point-to-point returns or compounded annual growth rates for specified periods, while a personal SIP involves a sequence of contributions and potentially withdrawals. Extended internal rate of return (XIRR) is commonly used to estimate the annualised return on irregularly dated investor cash flows, subject to the accuracy of the dates and values entered. Check the Scheme Objective, Category and Investment Strategy Start with the scheme information document and current factsheet. The stated investment objective and permitted investment universe determine what the fund is intended to do. Equity, hybrid, debt and other categories have different risk drivers; comparing them solely by recent returns is misleading. Examine whether the scheme has materially changed its mandate, portfolio approach, fund manager or risk exposure. A fund pursuing concentrated small-cap exposure should not be judged by the same short-term expectations as a diversified large-cap fund or a short-duration debt scheme. Confirm that the scheme’s role still matches the investor’s financial goal. Compare Mutual Fund Returns With the Correct Benchmark Use the benchmark disclosed for the scheme and verify whether it is appropriate for the scheme category and investment strategy. Benchmark comparison should cover consistent periods and return methodologies. A fund that earned 12% is not necessarily outperforming if its relevant benchmark earned substantially more over the same period. Assess performance against both the benchmark and relevant peers, without assuming that a single quarter or year is decisive. Check whether performance differences persist across market environments. A fund may outperform during rising markets but lose more than comparable funds during corrections; both outcomes matter to an SIP investor. Use Rolling Returns Instead of Relying on One-Year Rankings Rolling returns measure performance over multiple overlapping periods, such as successive three-year or five-year windows, rather than one arbitrarily selected start and end date. They help reveal consistency, the range of historical outcomes and the frequency of benchmark underperformance. For example, a three-year rolling-return study can examine each possible three-year holding period within the available history. Investors should review the distribution of results, not only the average. Rolling returns remain historical measures: their interpretation depends on the data period, benchmark, scheme changes and market conditions, and they cannot predict future performance. Assess Volatility and Risk-Adjusted Mutual Fund Performance Return alone is an incomplete measure of investment quality. Volatility, downside drawdowns and the ability to recover after market declines are relevant to investors who may need funds by a particular date. Risk-adjusted disclosures, including measures such as the information ratio where applicable, may provide additional context for benchmark-relative results. No single statistic captures every risk. A fund may have attractive past risk-adjusted numbers because of a favourable market phase or a limited sample. Read the measurement period and methodology before comparing funds. For debt-oriented schemes, interest-rate sensitivity, credit quality and liquidity may matter more than equity-style volatility comparisons. Read the Current Risk-o-Meter and Portfolio Concentration The scheme’s risk-o-meter is a useful disclosed indicator, but it is not a promise about future losses. Review the latest label and consider whether the level of risk is consistent with the investment horizon and the investor’s ability to withstand declines. Look at the top holdings, sector allocations, market-cap exposure and any substantial concentration in a small number of issuers. A diversified fund can still carry meaningful thematic or sector exposure. The same stock may appear in several funds owned by an investor, creating unintended overlap at the overall portfolio level. Examine Portfolio Turnover, Expense Ratios and Plan Type A scheme’s portfolio turnover can indicate how frequently holdings change, although a high or low ratio is not automatically good or bad. Interpret turnover alongside the investment strategy, transaction costs and portfolio disclosures. Check the total expense ratio and whether the units are in a direct or regular plan. Direct and regular plans of the same scheme can have different expense ratios, which may lead to different returns over time. Compare like with like: the same scheme, option, plan type, measurement date and return convention. Lower costs are relevant but should not replace an assessment of suitability and risk. Illustrative SIP Review: Why Recent Rankings Can Mislead Assume an investor contributes ₹5,000 monthly to an equity mutual fund for several years. The scheme ranks near the top of its category over the latest one-year period, but its rolling three-year returns show substantial variation and repeated benchmark underperformance. At the same time, the investor already holds two other schemes with similar large sector exposures. A decision based only on the latest ranking might encourage increasing the SIP. A fuller review would consider the fund’s mandate, overlapping holdings, expenses, risk profile and the investor’s target asset allocation. The appropriate response might be to continue, reduce, redirect or stop future instalments, depending on the circumstances. This is a hypothetical illustration, not a recommendation to act on any named scheme. Review SIPs Against Financial Goals and Asset Allocation A mutual fund should serve a defined purpose: long-term wealth creation, a medium-term purchase, retirement or another goal. The relevant review period depends on when the money will be needed and how much volatility the investor can tolerate. Equity exposure may be unsuitable for money required soon, even if a recent return looks attractive. Portfolio asset allocation and rebalancing can be more important than replacing an underperforming fund every year. Review how equity, debt, cash and other holdings interact. A rising market can increase equity’s share of the portfolio beyond the intended range, requiring a deliberate review rather than an emotional reaction to headlines. Stopping, Pausing, Redeeming or Switching a SIP: Different Decisions Stopping future SIP instalments is not the same as redeeming units already held. A pause affects new purchases, whereas redemption may realise gains or losses and trigger applicable charges and taxes. A switch between schemes or plans can also have transaction and tax consequences depending on its structure. Before redeeming, check exit-load provisions, any lock-in restrictions and the applicable scheme documents. Examine the tax implications of switching mutual funds, including the nature of the scheme, holding period and applicable law. Do not assume that a switch is tax-neutral merely because the proceeds remain invested in mutual funds. Records to Maintain for a Meaningful SIP Performance Review Retain the SIP registration details, instalment dates and amounts, unit allotment statements, scheme factsheets, current and historical portfolio disclosures, benchmark data, expense-ratio information and records of redemptions or switches. For an investor-level return calculation, preserve cash-flow dates, values and the valuation date used for outstanding units. A review note should record the original goal, intended time horizon, acceptable risk level, reasons for any change and the date of the next review. This reduces the likelihood of repeated switching based on short-term market movements. Keep tax statements and transaction confirmations separately so that future return filing does not depend on incomplete portfolio-app summaries. Practical SIP Review Checklist Confirm the financial goal, remaining investment horizon and appropriate asset allocation. Read the current scheme objective, category, strategy and benchmark. Compare consistent-period scheme returns, rolling returns and benchmark-relative performance. Review the latest risk-o-meter, portfolio concentration, overlap and downside exposure. Check risk-adjusted measures, portfolio turnover and direct/regular plan expense ratios. Calculate the investor’s cash-flow-based return using accurate instalment dates and values. Consider exit loads, lock-ins and tax consequences before redemption or switching. Record the rationale for continuing, modifying or stopping the SIP and schedule the next review. Frequently Asked Questions Does an SIP guarantee a profit if investments continue for many years? No. Regular investing spreads purchase dates but does not remove market risk or guarantee a positive return, even over long periods. Should a mutual fund SIP be stopped after one year of underperformance? Not automatically. Examine the scheme’s objective, relevant benchmark, rolling performance, risk exposure and suitability before deciding whether the weakness is material. Why can personal SIP XIRR differ from the scheme’s published return? The investor buys units on multiple dates, whereas a published point-to-point return often assumes a single starting investment. The cash-flow pattern and measurement method therefore differ. Are rolling returns a forecast of future SIP performance? No. They describe the range and consistency of historical outcomes over repeated holding periods. Future market conditions and scheme performance can differ. Is a direct mutual fund plan always the best choice? Direct plans generally have different distribution-cost structures from regular plans, but the appropriate route also depends on an investor’s ability to select and monitor investments and the nature of any advice received. Does cancelling an SIP automatically redeem existing mutual fund units? No. Cancelling future instalments ordinarily stops further purchases; existing units remain invested unless separately redeemed or otherwise transacted. Can switching between mutual fund schemes trigger tax? Yes, depending on the transaction and applicable tax law. Review the nature of the switch, holding period, scheme category, exit load and potential capital gains consequences. Key Takeaway A disciplined SIP review focuses on the suitability and performance of the underlying mutual fund, not simply the latest return ranking. Compare the correct benchmark, study rolling and risk-adjusted results, inspect portfolio risks and costs, and evaluate any change against the investor’s goals, allocation and tax position.

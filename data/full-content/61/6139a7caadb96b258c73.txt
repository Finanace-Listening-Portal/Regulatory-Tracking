Summary: Suspension, cancellation and retrospective cancellation of GST registration can severely disrupt business operations, return filing, tax invoicing and customers’ input tax credit claims. This article examines the statutory safeguards governing these actions under Sections 29 and 30 of the CGST Act, 2017 and Rules 21, 21A, 22, 23 and 25 of the CGST Rules. It identifies six recurring problems in departmental proceedings: defective physical verification, vague show cause notices, denial of personal hearings, reliance on undisclosed third-party data, prolonged suspension and retrospective cancellation without adequate notice or reasons. Drawing upon decisions of the Karnataka, Gujarat, Delhi, Madras, Patna and Telangana High Courts, the article explains the importance of establishing specific grounds, disclosing relied-upon material and providing taxpayers with a meaningful opportunity to respond. It distinguishes temporary closure of business premises from actual non-existence of a business and discusses the consequences of keeping registration suspended without completing cancellation proceedings. Particular emphasis is placed on the discretionary nature of retrospective cancellation and its implications for customers’ input tax credit. The article also discusses the notified system-based framework stated to take effect from 1 April 2027 and provides a comparative table of statutory safeguards, procedural failures and relevant judicial authorities. Practical remedies include filing documentary evidence with replies, requesting personal hearings and revocation of suspension, pursuing statutory revocation or appellate remedies, and approaching the High Court where procedural violations justify judicial intervention. Taxpayers and practitioners are advised to maintain comprehensive business records and verify the applicable statutory provisions, limitation periods and complete judgments before relying on them. It lets him issue tax invoices, claim input tax credit (ITC) and file returns, and it is the basis on which buyers accept his invoices. When it is suspended or cancelled, the effect on the business is immediate. I have seen the same pattern repeatedly in practice. An officer visits the premises and finds it closed or finds nobody. A report follows, built on third-party or analytical data, and the portal then suspends the registration. A show cause notice (SCN) follows that says little. The suspension stays for seven or eight months, during which the taxpayer cannot file returns. Finally, the registration is cancelled from a past date that the notice never mentioned, on the ground that the taxpayer “was not doing business at the registered place”. This article sets out what Section 29, Section 30 and Rules 21, 21A, 22 and 23 actually provide, and then tests each of those practices against the law and the decisions of the High Courts. Where a decision is known to me only from a reported summary, I have said so. Please verify the full text and paragraph numbers before quoting any of it in a reply. 1. The Statutory Framework 1.1 Section 29: Cancellation Section 29(1) lets the proper officer cancel a registration on his own motion, on the taxpayer’s application, or on the death of the proprietor. Section 29(2) lists the grounds for cancellation on the officer’s own motion: Violation of the Act or Rules, where the taxpayer under the composition scheme or otherwise fails to comply. Non-filing of returns for a continuous period (six months for regular taxpayers, and for composition dealers the equivalent default). Not commencing business within six months of registration. Obtaining registration by fraud, wilful misstatement or suppression of facts. The officer may cancel “from such date, including any retrospective date, as he may deem fit”. The same sub-section carries a proviso: the officer shall not cancel the registration without giving the person an opportunity of being heard. Two points follow from the text. First, the power to cancel retrospectively exists, but it is a discretion (“as he may deem fit”), and a discretion must be exercised on reasons. Second, the hearing is a statutory requirement. The officer’s power is therefore a conditional one and not an absolute power. 1.2 Rule 21: The Grounds in the Rules Rule 21 lists situations where registration is liable to be cancelled. These include not conducting any business from the declared place of business, issuing invoices or bills without supply, and violating Section 171 on anti-profiteering. Rule 21(a), the “no business at the declared place” ground, is the one most often used against small dealers. 1.3 Rule 21A: Suspension Rule 21A has two main routes: Suspension on the taxpayer’s own application. Where a registered person applies for cancellation under Rule 20, the registration is deemed suspended from the date of the application or the date from which cancellation is sought, whichever is later, pending the cancellation proceedings. Suspension by the officer. Where the proper officer has reasons to believe that the registration is liable to be cancelled under Section 29 or Rule 21, he may suspend it pending completion of the Rule 22 proceedings. Rule 21A (3) says a registered person whose registration is suspended shall not make any taxable supply during the suspension and is not required to furnish returns. The rule thus has a consequence that is often missed: during suspension, the portal will not let the taxpayer file the monthly return. Rule 21A (4) says the suspension is deemed revoked on completion of the proceedings under Rule 22, and that revocation takes effect from the date on which the suspension began. The rule also lets the officer revoke the suspension during the proceedings where he considers it appropriate. So, the Rules treat suspension as a temporary measure that ends when the cancellation proceeding ends. 1.4 Rule 22: The Procedure Rule 22(1) requires a show cause notice in FORM GST REG-17, stating why the registration should not be cancelled. The taxpayer has seven working days to reply in FORM GST REG-18. If the reply is satisfactory, the officer drops the proceedings by an order in FORM GST REG-20. If it is not, the officer passes an order in FORM GST REG-19, with the effective date of cancellation, within thirty days of the reply. Where a hearing has been asked for or is required, it must be given. 1.5 Section 30 and Rule 23: Revocation A registered person whose registration was cancelled by the proper officer on his own motion may apply for revocation within thirty days of the cancellation order, in FORM GST REG-21. The time may be extended by the senior authority specified in the Act, and the Gauhati High Court has in a recent order allowed a taxpayer two months from its order of 16 November 2026 to file the revocation application. The revocation application route is separate from the appeal under Section 107, and a taxpayer who is refused revocation has the statutory appeal and the writ remedy. 1.6 What Is Changing The CBIC has notified a system-based framework for suspension, cancellation and revocation, with substituted Rules 21A and 22 and a new Rule 23A. They apply from 1 April 2027, so they do not govern a present dispute. Under the new Rule 21A (2), the common portal can suspend a registration for specified return defaults or for Rule 10A violations, and the taxpayer gets thirty days after the intimation in FORM GST REG-31 to furnish the pending returns. If he does not, the registration is deemed cancelled under new Rule 22(5). Suspension by the officer is still deemed revoked on completion of Rule 22 proceedings. The thirty-day cap on a system suspension is a useful contrast to the open-ended suspensions I describe below. 2. The Six Recurring Problems 2.1 The Officer’s Visit That Is Not a Proper Visit Rule 25 of the CGST Rules provides for physical verification of the place of business. The verification The officer’s verification report is often the sole basis for the proceeding. If it records that the premises was “found closed” without any photographs, the taxpayer’s contact number, the visit’s date and time, or the independent witnesses’ names, it cannot carry the weight placed on it. is to be carried out in the presence of the taxpayer, and the report with photographs is uploaded within fifteen working days in FORM GST REG-30. Rule 25 is a verification provision, and it does not by itself create a ground for cancellation. In M/s Flex Enterprises v. Superintendent of Central Tax (Karnataka HC, WP 13708/2026, 12 June 2026), the court quashed cancellations where the verification reports were defective: one report showed an unrelated property and the other was blank. Such reports could not form the “reasons to believe” under Rule 22(1). That is a decision of our own High Court, and I would lead with it in any Karnataka reply. Closure alone also does not justify cancellation. High Courts have held that temporary closure of the premises during a GST survey is not by itself a valid ground, and that a finding of non-existence must be tied to Section 29(2). On the other side, where the premises were non-functional, no books were produced and the landlord said only a small room with a display board existed, the Patna High Court upheld cancellation in JD Enterprises v. State of Bihar (2025). The Telangana High Court did the same in M.A. Enterprises where fake documents were filed for the declared premises. The distinction is between a business that is shut at the moment of the visit and a business that does not exist. Illustration. A scrap dealer in Mysuru moves goods at night and shuts the yard by 3 PM the next day. An officer visits at 4 PM, finds the shutter down, and notes “business not carried on”. The report has no photograph with a date stamp and no attempt to call the proprietor. The dealer has e-way bills for the night movements, a weighbridge register, a stock account under Rule 56(2) and electricity bills showing yard consumption. On these facts the “non-existence” finding is unsupported, and the dealer’s evidence is the answer. 2.2 A Vague Show Cause Notice An SCN that merely reproduces the words of Rule 21(a) or Section 29(2)(b) does not tell the taxpayer what case he must meet. The Gujarat High Court addressed this directly in Aggarwal Dyeing and Printing Works v. State of Gujarat (SCA 18860/2021 with connected matters, 24 February 2022). It found the SCN “vague as anything” and the cancellation order “more vague than the show cause notice” . It held that a notice that restates the statutory ground without specific facts is “a mere formality and an eye wash” because no prudent person can reply to it, and it quashed the notices and orders with liberty to the department to issue fresh, particularised notices . The court also noted that the lead petitioner’s registration had been cancelled retrospectively from the date of registration by an ex-parte order issued twelve days after the SCN. Where the SCN and the cancellation order bear the same date, the High Courts have set the order aside as cryptic, relying on Aggarwal Dyeing. The Delhi High Court has also held that the mere issue of an SCN is not enough to cancel a registration. 2.3 No Opportunity of Hearing The proviso to Section 29(2) is mandatory. A reply to the SCN is not a hearing. Where the taxpayer asks for a personal hearing, or where the reply raises factual disputes, the officer must fix a date and record what happened. A cancellation order that does not say whether a hearing was offered is vulnerable. In Shree Bankey Bihari Trading Co. (Delhi HC, 3 October 2024), the court reaffirmed that an SCN alone is insufficient and that intelligible reasons and an opportunity of hearing are needed . In Deep Plastic Granules Trading Co. v. UoI (Delhi HC, 4 April 2024), where the taxpayer was not found at the declared place, the court still directed a detailed reply to the SCN, a personal hearing, and a decision within two months . 2.4 Third-Party and Analytical Data Departmental analytics often supply the trigger: a supplier’s cancellation, a mismatch between GSTR-1 and GSTR-3B, or a flag from a report on e-way bills. Such material can justify an enquiry. It cannot replace the officer’s own reasons to believe. Two safeguards apply. First, if the department relies on third-party data, it must disclose that data in the SCN or supply it with the notice, so that the taxpayer can answer it. The Delhi High Court noted in Jordan Enterprises v. Union of India (25 February 2026) that the relied-upon material had not been supplied and the order was non-speaking. Second, the order must say why the data leads to the conclusion drawn. In Flex Enterprises, the Karnataka High Court said the verification reports must meet “the statutory test of reasons to believe” . A bare reference to “data analytics” does not meet that test. 2.5 Suspension Kept Pending for Months Rule 21A allows suspension “pending completion of proceedings under Rule 22” . The Rule 22 timeline is short: seven working days for the reply and thirty days for the order after the reply . A suspension that runs for seven or eight months, with no order under Rule 22, is not a “pending” proceeding in any meaningful sense. It operates as a penalty imposed with no adjudication. The Madras High Court has held that automatic suspension of registration pending adjudication of an SCN is unlawful unless justified by specific findings of fraud or evasion. In that matter it quashed the suspension effective 27.02.2026, upheld the SCN, and directed that the registration be restored on conditions: filing pending returns and paying tax in cash without using unverified ITC, as laid down in Tvl Suguna Cut Piece Center . The decision is from a summary and concerns the Tamil Nadu GST Act, so it is persuasive and not binding in Karnataka. The inability to file returns during suspension also creates a trap. Rule 21A(3) excuses the return filing during suspension, but if the registration is later cancelled retrospectively, the officer may treat the period as a period of non-filing, and the taxpayer is left with a liability for late fees and an exposure to the tax on supplies. Where a suspension is prolonged, the taxpayer should write to the officer requesting revocation of the suspension under the Rule 21A provision allowing revocation during the pendency of the proceedings, and keep a copy of every such request. 2.6 Retrospective Cancellation That the Notice Never Proposed Retrospective cancellation is the most damaging step, because it can wipe out the taxpayer’s past supplies from the registration. It also affects his customers’ ITC for the retrospective period, which is why buyers may start receiving notices. The Delhi High Court has repeatedly held that, although Section 29(2) permits retrospective cancellation, the power cannot be exercised mechanically: Royal Enterprises v. Principal Commissioner of GST East Delhi (W.P.(C) 2461/2025, 27 February 2025). The SCN gave no indication that cancellation from a past date was contemplated, and it gave no reasons for one. The court held the retrospective order invalid and directed that cancellation take effect from the date of the SCN, 27 September 2024, instead of 25 November 2021. Garg Candle Works v. Commissioner, Delhi GST (8 May 2025). Cancellation from 1 July 2017 was modified to operate from 14 May 2024 . Bhagvan Singh v. Commissioner of DGST (W.P.(C) 3178/2025, 5 February 2026). The court set aside the order of 12.10.2023 and the SCN of 15.01.2023, and directed restoration of the registration. It held that retrospective cancellation is an exception, not the rule, that the SCN itself must indicate the intention to cancel retrospectively, and that the order must give reasons for the retrospective date. Jordan Enterprises v. Union of India (25 February 2026). The court applied the same principle where the SCN did not propose retrospective action, relied-upon material was not supplied, and the order was non-speaking. V R Impex v. Commissioner of Delhi GST (reported November 2026). The court modified the effective date of cancellation to 4 April 2022 because the SCN did not propose retrospective cancellation and the order did not explain the choice of the earlier date. These are all Delhi decisions. The reasoning rests on natural justice and the text of Section 29(2) and should be persuasive before the Karnataka High Court, but it is not binding there. Mention too that the retrospective date must have some link to the facts, such as the date on which the taxpayer actually stopped business. A date that goes back to the date of registration, with no finding that the business never existed, is hard to defend. 3. Remedies and Practical Steps Reply to the SCN in time under REG-18, with documents to show the business is real: rent agreement, electricity bills, stock records, e-way bills, bank statements, and returns filed. Ask expressly for the verification report, the photographs and any third-party data relied on. Ask for a personal hearing and for a reasoned order. Note in the reply that the SCN does not propose retrospective cancellation, so that the point is on record. Request revocation of suspension in writing while the proceeding is pending, and keep the acknowledgement. This builds the record on delay. If an order is passed, file the revocation application in FORM GST REG-21 within thirty days, or seek the extension, and consider the appeal under Section 107 against the order, which generally has a three-month limitation. Check the pre-deposit position and the statutory time limits before filing. Consider a writ petition before the High Court where the grounds are the ones above: a vague SCN, no hearing, retrospective cancellation without notice, or a suspension that has become indefinite. The Gujarat High Court in Aggarwal Dyeing showed that such relief is available. High Courts have often restored the registration or moved the effective date to the date of the SCN. Look after the downstream position. A retrospective cancellation affects customers’ ITC. Advise buyers to retain their evidence of receipt and payment. The note on Section 16(2)(b) from our earlier article applies directly. I also advise taxpayers not to treat a cancellation order as final merely because the portal shows it. Where the return default was the cause, the pending returns and the dues will have to be cleared. The Gauhati order mentioned above concerned precisely that situation. Conclusion Suspension, cancellation and retrospective cancellation are three distinct powers, and each has its own safeguards. Suspension is a holding measure that must end with the Rule 22 proceeding. Cancellation needs a particularised notice, a hearing and a reasoned order. Retrospective cancellation is permitted by Section 29(2) but must be proposed in the notice and justified in the order, as the Delhi High Court has held repeatedly. A closed shop during one visit, a data mismatch from a third party, or a suspension left pending for months does not meet those tests. The taxpayer’s best protection is a record that shows the business is real: books at the premises, a reachable contact, e-way bills, and a prompt reply to every notice. The best advice for a practitioner is to build the file early. Raise the procedural points at the first reply, take them to the appellate authority or the High Court if the order still issues, and keep every fact straight on the dates.

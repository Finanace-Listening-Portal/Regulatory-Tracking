Vs National Projects Construction Corporation (Supreme Court of India) Arbitral Award Unenforced for Over 24 Years: Supreme Court Seeks Law Commission Study on Execution Reforms Summary: The Supreme Court has held that clarification proceedings cannot be used to expand an earlier judgment on a certified question of law into supervision of continuing execution proceedings. Disputes concerning amounts deposited, withdrawals, adjustment and set-off must be determined by the competent executing court and the appellate court exercising jurisdiction over its orders. In Royal Construction Company Pvt. Ltd. v. National Projects Construction Corporation, the Court also expressed concern that more than 24 years after the arbitral award, the parties continued to litigate over its enforcement. It requested the Law Commission of India to study the framework governing enforcement and execution of arbitral awards and consider reforms for timely recovery. Facts of the Case Royal Construction Company Pvt. Ltd. was the award-holder, while National Projects Construction Corporation was the award-debtor. An arbitral award dated 10 August 2002, arising from an internationally seated arbitration, was rendered in favour of Royal Construction. The awarded amounts were denominated in Iraqi Dinars. After challenges before the Indian courts, the Supreme Court, by its judgment dated 24 February 2015, restored the directions contained in the award. It emphasised the principle of minimal judicial intervention and cautioned against courts substituting their subjective assessment for that of the arbitral tribunal without statutory grounds for interference. The dispute subsequently continued at the execution stage. Dispute over Payment in Indian Currency By an order dated 26 July 2017, the executing court permitted discharge of the award liability in Indian Rupees, applying the exchange rate prevailing on 26 September 1982, identified in that order as the date of the underlying agreement. The award-holder challenged this direction before the Division Bench of the Delhi High Court. The Division Bench set aside the Single Judge’s order and certified a substantial question of law of general importance under Article 134A read with Article 133(1)(a) of the Constitution. The certified question concerned the relevant date for conversion of the awarded sum from US Dollars into Indian Rupees. By its judgment dated 10 October 2023, the Supreme Court held that no occasion arose to determine or fix a conversion date. It also cautioned that the agreement, the arbitral award and its earlier directions should not be disturbed. As explained in the present judgment, payment was to be made in foreign currency. Why the Matter Returned to the Supreme Court During the continuing execution proceedings, the award-debtor contended that the principal amount had already been paid in Indian Rupees in 2009 pursuant to orders of the High Court. By an interim order dated 16 February 2026, the High Court granted the parties liberty to seek clarification from the Supreme Court regarding why this payment had not been brought to its notice earlier. Royal Construction challenged that order. Separately, the award-debtor filed a miscellaneous application seeking modification of the 2023 judgment, contending that the rupee amounts deposited by it had already been withdrawn by the award-holder in 2009. 1. Earlier Adjudication Was Confined to the Certified Legal Question The Supreme Court held that its earlier appellate consideration was limited to answering the substantial question of law certified by the High Court. It had not been called upon to examine factual matters concerning the deposits made or their subsequent withdrawal. Once the certified question had been answered, the scope of those appellate proceedings stood exhausted. A clarification request could not enlarge that adjudication to cover distinct factual disputes arising during execution. 2. Execution Disputes Must Be Decided by the Competent Court The Court rejected the suggestion that it should assume a continuing supervisory role over execution proceedings merely because it had answered the earlier legal question. Questions arising during execution must be determined by the competent executing court, with appellate scrutiny available in accordance with law. Accordingly, the Court did not decide whether the earlier rupee payments fully or partly discharged the award liability. It expressly preserved the award-debtor’s liberty to raise objections and contentions concerning adjustment or set-off before the executing court. Thus, dismissal of the modification application did not amount to rejection of the adjustment or set-off claim on its merits. 3. Delay in Execution Defeats the Purpose of Arbitration The Supreme Court expressed serious concern over the prolonged enforcement proceedings. Although the award had been rendered in 2002, the successful party was still litigating over its enforcement more than 24 years later. The Court explained that the effectiveness of arbitration cannot be assessed solely by how quickly an award is rendered or by the limited grounds available to challenge it. It must also be measured by whether the successful party can realise the benefit of the award within a reasonable time. An award-holder compelled to undertake another prolonged round of litigation for recovery is denied the practical benefit of choosing arbitration. 4. Law Commission Requested to Examine Enforcement Reforms The Court referred to Section 36 of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996, which provides for enforcement of an award in the same manner as a court decree, using the ordinary execution machinery under the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908. It identified several matters requiring focused examination: Identification and disclosure of the award-debtor’s assets. Coordination of different modes of execution. Disposal of repetitive or dilatory objections. Time taken to conclude execution proceedings. Lessons available from enforcement mechanisms in other jurisdictions. The Court requested the Law Commission to consider whether domestic and foreign arbitral awards require distinct enforcement procedures, or whether a common framework with appropriate safeguards would better serve the purpose. The Commission was invited to recommend legislative or procedural measures while preserving legitimate procedural safeguards available to judgment-debtors. These observations constitute a request for study and recommendations. The judgment itself does not introduce a new execution procedure. Decision of the Supreme Court The Supreme Court disposed of the civil appeal in terms of its findings that the earlier adjudication could not be expanded through clarification proceedings. It dismissed the miscellaneous application seeking modification of the judgment dated 10 October 2023, while permitting the applicant to raise adjustment and set-off contentions before the executing court. The Registry was directed to forward the judgment to the Chairman of the Law Commission of India. Practical Takeaway The judgment reinforces that a clarification application cannot replace adjudication of factual disputes by the executing court. Parties seeking credit for earlier deposits or payments must establish their entitlement to adjustment or set-off in the appropriate execution proceedings. Its wider significance lies in recognising that timely enforcement is essential to effective arbitration. A favourable award delivers meaningful relief only when the award-holder can secure recovery within a reasonable period. FULL TEXT OF THE JUDGMENT OF SUPREME COURT Leave granted. 2. Heard Mr. Rishabh Sancheti, learned counsel appearing for the appellant, and Mr. Vikramjit Banerjee, learned Additional Solicitor General appearing for the respondent. 3. The present appeal assails the interim order dated 16th February, 2026, passed by the High Court of Delhi at 16:49:47 IST New Delhi1 in Execution First Appeal (Original Side) No. 17 of 2018, whereby the parties were granted liberty to seek clarification from this Court as to why the fact that the principal amount had already been paid in Indian Rupees in 2009, pursuant to the orders passed by the High Court, had not been brought to its notice on an earlier occasion when this Court had the opportunity to consider and answer the substantial question of law framed by the High Court under Article 134-A read with Article 133(1)(a) of the Constitution of India. 4. The brief facts relevant to the disposal of the present appeal are as follows: 4.1. The appellant is the award-holder, while the respondent is the award-debtor. Pursuant to an internationally seated arbitration, an arbitral award dated 10th August, 2002, was rendered in favour of the appellant, with the amounts awarded denominated in Iraqi Dinars. 4.2. The award was challenged before the Indian courts, culminating in proceedings before this Court. By judgment dated 24th February, 2015, this Court underscored the principle of minimal judicial intervention in arbitral awards, holding that the courts ought not to substitute their own subjective 1 Hereinafter, referred to as “High Court”. satisfaction for that of the arbitral tribunal unless the statutory grounds for interference under Sections 34 and 37 of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996, are made out. Accordingly, this Court restored the directions contained in the award dated 10th August, 2002. 4.3. Thereafter, the appellant initiated execution proceedings before the High Court. By order dated 26th July, 2017, the High Court directed that the award-debtor could discharge its liability in Indian currency, with the conversion to be made at the exchange rate prevailing on the date of the underlying agreement, i.e., 26th September, 1982. 4.4. Aggrieved by the aforesaid order, the appellant preferred an appeal before the Division Bench of the High Court. By order dated 17th December, 2019, the Division Bench set aside the order passed by the Single Judge and certified a substantial question of law of general importance under Articles 134-A and 133(1)(a) of the Constitution of India for consideration by this Court. The question, being germane to the controversy, is reproduced hereinbelow: – “In terms of the agreement dated 29th June, 1982, between the parties and in light of the judgment dated 24th February, 2015 of the Supreme Court of India in Civil Appeal Nos. 2543-44/2015, what should be the relevant date for conversion of awarded sum from USD to Indian rupees?” 4.5. By judgment dated 10th October, 2023, this Court answered the aforesaid question in the negative, holding that no occasion arose for determining or fixing a date for conversion of US Dollars into Indian Rupees (INR). This Court further cautioned that the terms of the agreement, the arbitral award, and the directions contained in its judgment dated 24th February, 2015, ought not to be disturbed. 4.6. Thereafter, the appellant continued to pursue the execution proceedings before the High Court. While those proceedings were pending, the respondent contended that the principal amount had already been paid in 2009 pursuant to the orders passed by the High Court. By an interim order dated 16th February, 2026, the High Court granted liberty to the parties to seek clarification from this Court in that regard. 5. Hence, the appellant is before us. 6. We have perused the material on record and considered the submissions advanced on behalf of the parties. 7. In our considered opinion, the Division Bench has fallen into error in appreciating the scope of the controversy previously adjudicated by this Court. A perusal of the judgment dated 10th October, 2023, makes it evident that this Court, while exercising appellate jurisdiction, was called upon to answer the substantial question of law of general importance certified by the High Court. The scope of consideration was, therefore, confined to answering the question so referred, without undertaking an examination of factual matters beyond its ambit. 8. Having held that no occasion arose for determining or fixing a date for conversion of the amount into Indian Rupees and that payment was to be made in foreign currency, this Court had no occasion to issue any further clarification on factual questions concerning the amounts deposited or their subsequent withdrawal. 9. The order of the executing court was assailed before the Division Bench of the High Court, which, upon certifying a substantial question of law of general importance under Articles 134-A and 133(1)(a) of the Constitution, referred the question to this Court. The jurisdiction exercised by this Court was confined to answering that question. This Court cannot be called upon, in the guise of seeking clarification, to assume a supervisory role over the continuing execution proceedings or to adjudicate upon every issue that may arise therein. It is for the competent court seized of the execution proceedings, and the appellate court exercising jurisdiction over its orders, to determine the questions arising in accordance with law. 10. Once the substantial question of law referred to this Court stood answered, the scope of the earlier appellate proceedings stood exhausted. It would be impermissible to enlarge the scope of that adjudication by seeking answers to questions that are distinct from, and unconnected with, the lis previously decided. Epilogue: 11. Before parting, we deem it appropriate to express our concern at the protracted course which the present proceedings have taken. The arbitration in the present case was invoked when the Arbitration Act, 1940 held the field. We are now approaching three decades under the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996, enacted, inter alia, in furtherance of the UNCITRAL Model Law and with the objective of facilitating the expeditious resolution of disputes through minimal judicial intervention. However, more than twenty-four years after the arbitral award was rendered, the parties continue to litigate over its enforcement. Such a state of affairs strikes at the very rationale underlying the adoption of arbitration as an effective alternative to conventional civil litigation. 12. The efficacy of an arbitral regime cannot be measured merely by the expedition with which an award is rendered or the circumscribed grounds upon which it may be challenged. It must equally be assessed by the ability of the successful party to realise the fruits of the award within a reasonable time. The promise of arbitration remains unfulfilled if a party, having traversed the arbitral process, is compelled to embark upon another protracted course of litigation to secure the benefit of the award. 13. While the statutory framework governing arbitration has sought to address delays in arbitral proceedings, challenges to awards and the grant of stays on their enforcement, the process of actual execution warrants equally serious consideration. Section 36 of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996 provides for enforcement of an arbitral award in the same manner as a decree of the Court, thereby drawing upon the ordinary machinery of execution under the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908. The experience of cases such as the present one calls for a closer examination of whether this framework adequately serves the objectives of arbitration. In particular, the identification and disclosure of the award-debtor’s assets, the coordination of different modes of execution, the disposal of repetitive or dilatory objections, and the time taken to conclude execution proceedings merit focused scrutiny. Comparative legal systems may offer useful insights into more effective and coordinated enforcement mechanisms, while preserving the legitimate procedural safeguards available to judgment-debtors. 14. In these circumstances, we request the Law Commission of India to undertake a focused study of the statutory and procedural framework governing the enforcement and execution of arbitral awards. The study may identify the practical impediments to timely recovery, examine the experience of other jurisdictions, and consider whether domestic and foreign arbitral awards warrant distinct enforcement procedures or a common framework with appropriate safeguards. The Commission may, upon such examination, make such legislative or procedural recommendations as it considers necessary to strengthen the execution regime and ensure that the enforcement of arbitral awards is commensurate with the objectives of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996 and India’s commitment to establishing itself as an arbitration-friendly jurisdiction. 15. The appeal is disposed of in the above terms. 16. The Registry shall forward a copy of this judgment to the Hon’ble Chairman of the Law Commission of India. 17. All pending application(s), if any, stand disposed of. M.A. @ Diary No. 48152 of 2026 in Civil Appeal No. 1991 of 2019 18. M.A @ Dairy No. 48152 of 2026 is taken on board. 19.

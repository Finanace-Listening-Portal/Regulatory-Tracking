Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); Vivek Agarwal Vs DCIT/ACIT (ITAT Dehradun) Section 153A Addition for an Unabated Year Requires Incriminating Material Found in Search Summary: Can a brochure showing six luxury tents support an addition of ₹10.50 lakh as unexplained investment? Can a guest register, coupled with estimated occupancy, establish undisclosed lodging income for a completed assessment year? The Dehradun ITAT answered both questions in favour of the assessee on the facts of the case. It also deleted hospitality income additions for two other years because the same income had already been assessed in the hands of the assessee’s wife under Section 44AD. Search at Camp Bliss and the additions made A search was conducted on 25 April 2018. The material found included booking records, kitchen slips, brochures, photographs and loose papers relating to Camp Bliss, an outdoor hospitality venture with two suites and six Swiss tents. For AY 2015–16, the Assessing Officer made two additions that eventually reached the Tribunal. The first was ₹10.50 lakh towards alleged unexplained investment in the Swiss tents. The second was ₹5,44,500 towards estimated undisclosed income from lodging and food services. The CIT(A) sustained the tent investment addition in full and retained ₹2,67,524 of the hospitality income addition. Brochure showed the tents were available for booking The CIT(A) relied on a brochure depicting six Swiss tents and a booking record showing that the first occupied stay in tent accommodation was on 7 May 2014. The assessee had furnished no purchase bills, bank statements or supplier confirmations to explain the cost or source of the investment. The CIT(A) accepted an estimate based on online market rates. The Tribunal focused on what the search material established about the assessee’s own investment. The brochure and booking record showed that the tents were installed and offered to guests. The Department could trace no purchase bills indicating the alleged investment, and the material relied on did not establish that the assessee had made the investment attributed to him. The Tribunal therefore deleted the ₹10.50 lakh addition. Estimated room occupancy could not sustain the lodging addition The Assessing Officer arrived at 220.5 room-days by dividing the number of guests by two and applying an average tariff of ₹2,600 per night. The assessee pointed out that the working counted fractional rooms, included cancelled bookings and, on some dates, assumed occupancy beyond the camp’s physical capacity of eight accommodation units. The CIT(A) accepted 189 room-days while retaining the tariff of ₹2,600. After allowing 30% for expenses and deducting the income already disclosed, the CIT(A) sustained ₹2,67,524 as undisclosed lodging income. The food component was deleted after accepting that the assessee’s wife managed the catering activity and had offered the income to tax. The Tribunal deleted the surviving lodging addition as well. The revised calculation was still an estimation exercise, and the Tribunal found it insufficient to sustain the addition in the unabated assessment for AY 2015–16. Supreme Court ruling in Abhisar Buildwell applied The Tribunal applied PCIT v. Abhisar Buildwell Pvt. Ltd. (2023) 454 ITR 212 (SC). The Supreme Court held that an addition under Section 153A for a completed or unabated assessment requires incriminating material found during the search or requisition. AY 2015–16 was admittedly unabated on the date of search. The Tribunal accordingly examined whether the records relied on supported the alleged undisclosed investment and lodging income. Same hospitality income assessed in the wife’s hands For AYs 2018–19 and 2019–20, the Tribunal dealt with a separate issue. The lower authorities had estimated hospitality income in the assessee’s hands, while his wife, Rashmi Agarwal, had already been assessed under Section 44AD on the very income in dispute. On those facts, the Tribunal held that the additions resulted in double taxation of the same income and deleted them. All three appeals were allowed. Author’s Comments The order illustrates why the link between the seized record and the amount sought to be taxed matters. A brochure can show what accommodation a business offers. A guest register can show who checked in. To sustain an addition for an unabated year, the Revenue must show how the material supports the alleged undisclosed investment or income in the assessee’s hands. A market quotation can help estimate cost once the investment is established. Here, the evidence did not establish the underlying investment attributed to the assessee. The ruling on the wife’s income turns on the Tribunal’s finding that the same income was assessed twice. In a similar dispute, a year-wise reconciliation of the receipts, the activity carried on by each person and the income already assessed can help establish whether both additions cover the same amount. Cases Discussed: PCIT Vs Abhisar Buildwell Pvt. Ltd. (Supreme Court); (2023) 454 ITR 212 (SC); 24/04/2023; AY: Not specified in the present order. ITBA/APL/S/250/2025-26/1082884589(1), 1082887446(1) and 1082887609(1); respectively, involving proceedings under section 153A of the Income-tax Act, 1961 (hereinafter referred to as ‘the Act’) in former twin cases and under section 143(3) of the Act in the last case. Heard both the parties at length. Case files perused. We proceed assessment year-wise for the sake of convenience and brevity. 2. It transpires during the course of hearing that the assessee’s “lead” appeal ITA 22/DDN/2026 for assessment year 2015-16 raises his twin substantive grounds, inter alia, challenging both the learned lower authorities’ respective assessments and lower appellate findings making unexplained investment addition of Rs. 10.50 lakhs in “swiss tents” and partly sustaining Camp Bliss income addition of Rs.2,76,976/-; respectively; reading as under: “5. During the appellate proceedings the notices were issued u/s 250 of the I.T. Act. The appellant assessee complied to the notices served. I have carefully examined the written submissions of the appellant, assessment order, statement of facts and grounds of appeal. Ground wise adjudication is as under: 5.1 Ground of Appeal No. 1 1. The Assessing Officer erred in law as well as in fact by adding a sum of Rs.2,98,200 as Undisclosed Income from House Property without appreciating the facts of the case and submissions made by the appellate assessee. 5.1.1 During the course of assessment proceedings, the AO observed that income from house property has not been disclosed by appellant truly in its return of income. The AO has made an addition of Rs. 2,98,200/- as notional rent in respect of two properties viz-a- viz shop located at Haldwani and a vacant plot at Damuadunga, by taking annual lettable value(ALV) for the properties in consideration. 5.1.2 During the appellant proceedings, the appellant has submitted the written submission and substantial evidence in support of its claim. The appellant contended that: i) That the Haldwani shop is self-occupied for business purposes, and income from the said business has already been taxed under Section 44AD. ii) The Damuadunga plot is vacant open land. 5.1.3 I have carefully examined the assessment order, appellant’s submissions and facts of the case. The appellant has demonstrated that the Haldwani shop is self- occupied for business purposes and the income from business is offered to tax under the provision of section 44AD of the Act. Also, as per Section 22, any property used for the owner’s business is expressly excluded from taxation under the head “Income from House Property. The AO has not brought any evidence that this property was ever let out or intended to be let. Further the Damuadunga plot is vacant open land and does not constitute a “building or land appurtenant thereto” as contemplated under Section 22. The taxation of notional ‘Annual lettable Value’ on vacant land is contrary to law. 5.1.4 Therefore, in view of above discussion it is held that the AO has adopted arbitrary, unsubstantiated, and notional ALVs, without; municipal valuation, comparable rental instances and independent verification. There is no evidence that any rent was receive d or receivable by the appellant. Accordingly, the AO’s action for making addition is based on assumptions and is ‘devoid of any merit’ and the addition of Rs. 2,98,200/- is deleted. Hence the ground of appeal no. 1 is allowed. 5.2 Ground No. 2 2. The Assessing Officer erred in law as well as in fact by adding a sum of Rs.5,44,500 as Undisclosed Income from Camp Bliss without considering the submission made by the appellate assessee and documents found during the course of search. 5.2.1 Facts of the Case i) That during the search proceedings, documents, registers, kitchen slips, brochures, photographs, and loose papers pertaining to an outdoor hospitality activity named “Camp Bliss” were seized. ii) That during the assessment proceedings, the AO examined a seized check – in register, photographs, and one invoice for a group booking. Based on these, the AO concluded that the appellant assessee was running a lodging & boarding operation and had not disclosed complete receipts from the hospitality business. iii) That the AO estimated occupancy (room- days) by dividing number of persons by 2 and applying an average rate of Rs. 2,600/- per- night. Food receipts were also estimated by the AO. A consolidated addition of Rs. 5,44,500/- was made. 5.2.2 During the appellate proceedings, the appellant submitted written submission and the appellant contended that during the assessment proceedings the appellant has submitted before the AO that the room computation was wrong as fractional rooms and more room s than physically available were counted, cancellations were ignored, fooding income belonged to spouse and was already declared, and the appellant has already offered differential lodging income voluntarily. Further, the booking record reflects that the first occupied stay in the tent accommodation occurred on 07.05.2014, thereby establishing that the tents were already installed during the year under consideration. 5.3.2 During the assessment as well as appellate proceedings, the appellant has failed to furnish any evidence whatsoever; such as purchase bills, bank statements, confirmations from suppliers, or any relevant record to explain the source or actual cost of inve stment in these six Swiss tents. The ad option of an average price of Rs. 75,000 per tent, falling well within the range of Rs.50,000/- to Rs. 90,000/- as found on the internet, is fair & conservative. The appellant failed to substantiate its claim or produce any evidence to rebut the findings of the AO. Therefore, I find no merit in the appellant’s ground. 5.3.4 Accordingly, the addition of Rs. 10,50,000/- represents a reasonable and logical estimation of unexplained investment and is sustained. We wish to make it clear that the assessment year 2015-16 is admittedly an “unabated” one as the date of search herein carried out on 25.04.2018; and, therefore, there that such an addition ought to be based on incriminating material only in light of PCIT Vs. L earned CIT(DR) seeks to buttress the point that the lower authorities herein have gone by various evidence(s) whilst making the impugned twin additions in the assessee’s hands. We find no reason to sustain the same as it has already come on record that the learned lower authorities could not even trace the relevant purchase bills of swiss tents indicating the alleged investment. We thus delete the impugned addition of Rs.10.50 lakhs in very terms. The outcome of the assessee’s latter substantive ground would be hardly any different since based on estimation exercise only. We thus accept the assessee’s instant twin substantive grounds as well as the “lead” appeal ITA 22/DDN/2026 in very terms. 5. Next come the assessee’s latter twin appeals ITA Nos. 19 & 20/DDN/2026 for assessment years 2018-19 and 2019-20. Suffice to say, both the learned lower authorities have estimated his income derived from hospitality business wherein his wife Smt. Rashmi Agarwal already appears to have been assessed under section 44AD of the Act. We thus conclude in this factual backdrop that both the learned lower authorities have erred in law and on facts in assessing the assessee for the very income as an instance of double addition. Deleted accordingly. No other ground or argument has been pressed. 6. These assessee’s three appeal ITA Nos. 22, 19 & 20/DDN /2026 are allowed in the foregoing terms.

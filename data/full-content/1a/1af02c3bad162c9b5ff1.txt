(Supreme Court of India) Case Summary: The Supreme Court of India, in Lifestyle Equities C.V. & Anr. Vs Amazon Technologies Inc., 2025 INSC 1190, explained the circumstances in which an appellate court may grant an unconditional stay of execution of a money decree under Order XLI Rule 5 of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908. The dispute arose from an intellectual property infringement suit in which the Delhi High Court had awarded substantial damages against Amazon Technologies Inc. in relation to the Beverly Hills Polo Club trademark. A Division Bench of the Delhi High Court subsequently stayed the monetary portion of that decree pending appeal without requiring Amazon Technologies to deposit the decretal amount or furnish conventional financial security. Lifestyle Equities challenged the stay before the Supreme Court, contending that deposit or security was a mandatory prerequisite to staying a money decree. The Supreme Court rejected an absolute interpretation of the requirement and held that the appellate court retains limited discretion to grant an unconditional stay where exceptional circumstances justify that relief. It nevertheless emphasised that money decrees should ordinarily not be stayed unconditionally and that such discretion must be exercised sparingly, upon cogent reasons and proper examination of the circumstances. The Special Leave Petition had been dismissed by order dated 24 September 2025, with reasons reserved. The reasoned judgment dated 7 October 2025 explained why the High Court’s interim order did not warrant interference. The proceedings originated from a trademark infringement suit filed by Lifestyle Equities C.V. and its licensee in the Delhi High Court. The plaintiffs claimed proprietary and commercial rights in the Beverly Hills Polo Club trademark, which included a distinctive polo-player device. They alleged that Amazon Technologies Inc. had used a deceptively similar logo in connection with the sale and marketing of products, thereby infringing their registered trademark and associated copyright and causing damage to the goodwill of the brand. The plaintiffs instituted CS(COMM) No. 443 of 2020, seeking permanent injunctive relief, damages, delivery of infringing material and other consequential remedies. The original damages prayer was quantified at approximately ₹2 crore, subject to further determination during the proceedings. On 12 October 2020, the Delhi High Court directed issuance of summons and granted an interim injunction against the allegedly infringing use. The directions contemplated service upon the relevant defendant through electronic means, including email and WhatsApp. The question whether those directions were actually complied with subsequently became important because Amazon Technologies disputed the validity of service and maintained that the proceedings leading to the decree had been conducted without proper notice. The litigation therefore involved not only the monetary implications of a substantial intellectual property decree but also the procedural safeguards governing service of summons and the opportunity to contest a civil suit. Amazon Technologies Inc. was proceeded against ex parte by order dated 20 April 2022. The suit thereafter resulted in a judgment dated 25 February 2025 granting a permanent injunction and substantial damages against Amazon Technologies. The Single Judge awarded compensatory damages of USD 38.78 million, equivalent in the decree to ₹336,02,87,000, comprising USD 33.78 million towards lost royalties and USD 5 million towards increased advertising and promotional expenditure. The decree also awarded costs of ₹3,23,10,966.60 together with court fees, producing an aggregate monetary figure of approximately ₹339.26 crore excluding the additional court-fee component. Interest at five per cent per annum was stipulated if the damages were not paid within the prescribed period. Amazon Technologies challenged the judgment through RFA(OS)(COMM) No. 11 of 2025 and applied for a stay of the decree under Order XLI Rule 5 CPC. The Division Bench stayed the damages and costs portions of the decree without directing a monetary deposit. Instead, Amazon Technologies was required to furnish an undertaking that it would comply with the decree if its appeal ultimately failed. The Division Bench expressly clarified that its observations were preliminary and would not bind the appellate court when deciding the substantive appeal. Before the Supreme Court, Lifestyle Equities argued that the High Court had acted contrary to Order XLI Rule 1(3) and Rule 5(3) and (5) CPC by staying a substantial money decree without requiring deposit or adequate security. The decree-holders maintained that Order XLI Rule 1(3) contemplated deposit of the disputed sum or furnishing of security and that Rule 5(5) prohibited a stay when the required deposit or security was not furnished. They relied particularly upon Sihor Nagar Palika Bureau Vs Bhabhlubhai Virabhai & Co., (2005) 4 SCC 1, to contend that the requirement was mandatory in character. Lifestyle Equities also disputed Amazon Technologies’ claim of defective service, maintaining that the defendant had sufficient knowledge of the proceedings and had deliberately chosen not to participate. It argued that the defendant should have pursued the remedy available under Order IX Rule 13 CPC if its complaint genuinely concerned an ex parte decree passed without proper service. The plaintiffs relied upon Sunil Poddar Vs Union Bank of India, (2008) 2 SCC 326, and other authorities concerning service of summons, knowledge of proceedings and the consequences of non-appearance. They further contended that an undertaking to comply with the decree at a future date was not equivalent to meaningful financial security protecting the decree-holders’ recovery rights. Consequently, they sought an order requiring deposit of the decretal amount with interest as a condition for continuation of the stay. Amazon Technologies opposed the challenge, arguing that the Division Bench had exercised its discretion properly after identifying substantial procedural and legal infirmities in the decree. It contended that valid summons had not been served upon it in accordance with the directions issued by the High Court and that the case had proceeded ex parte despite deficiencies in the service process. It also pointed to the extraordinary increase in the damages figure from approximately ₹2 crore initially claimed to a much larger monetary demand advanced during later proceedings, without corresponding amendment of the pleadings or adequate notice to the defendant. Amazon Technologies maintained that these irregularities raised serious doubts about the sustainability of the damages decree. Reliance was placed upon Malwa Strips Pvt. Ltd. Vs Jyoti Ltd., (2009) 2 SCC 426, which recognised that the requirement concerning deposit or security should not be interpreted as eliminating the appellate court’s discretion in exceptional cases. The defendant argued that an order requiring a deposit exceeding ₹300 crore merely to obtain interim protection against a decree suffering from serious prima facie defects could be disproportionate and unjust. It emphasised that the Division Bench had considered these matters and had required a binding undertaking as a condition of relief. The Supreme Court therefore had to examine whether the statutory framework permitted the discretion exercised by the High Court and whether the particular facts justified its exercise. The Supreme Court examined the legislative history of Order XLI Rule 1(3) and Rule 5(5) CPC, including the amendments introduced through the Code of Civil Procedure (Amendment) Act, 1976. It observed that the legislative process had deliberately distinguished the right to pursue an appeal from the right to obtain a stay of execution. The initial proposal to reject a money-decree appeal for failure to deposit the disputed amount or furnish security was modified following consideration by a Parliamentary Joint Committee. The statutory scheme ultimately preserved the right of appeal while regulating the availability of interim protection against execution. Referring to Kayamuddin Shamsuddin Khan Vs State Bank of India, (1998) 8 SCC 676, and Malwa Strips Pvt. Ltd. Vs Jyoti Ltd., the Supreme Court held that Order XLI Rule 1(3) is directory in relation to maintainability of an appeal; failure to comply with a deposit direction does not, by itself, require rejection of the appeal. The Court further explained that Order XLI Rule 5 must be interpreted as a whole, considering the conditions governing stay and the discretion conferred upon appellate courts. It rejected the contention that the text required deposit of the entire monetary decree in every case as an inflexible prerequisite for relief. At the same time, the Court made clear that the absence of such an absolute rule did not entitle unsuccessful defendants to obtain unconditional stays as a matter of course. The Court identified sufficient cause as the central condition governing the exercise of appellate discretion under Order XLI Rule 5 CPC. Merely filing an appeal does not automatically stay execution. An appellant seeking interim protection must demonstrate why substantial loss may result if execution proceeds, establish that the application has been made without unreasonable delay, and address the question of appropriate security for performance of the decree. The court granting a stay must record cogent and adequate reasons showing why preservation of the existing position is justified. The Supreme Court explained that the statute does not make an absolute distinction between money decrees and other decrees for purposes of the power to grant a stay. Nevertheless, as an established rule of prudence, a money decree should ordinarily not be stayed without requiring deposit or appropriate security, because payment of money is generally capable of restitution if the appeal succeeds. The Court recognised that unconditional stay may be justified in exceptional circumstances, particularly where the decree appears egregiously perverse, affected by patent illegality or facially untenable. The Court further clarified that security is not restricted to a cash deposit and may, depending upon the case, take the form of property, a bond or a suitable undertaking. Such flexibility must be exercised judicially and cannot become an excuse for routine suspension of enforceable money decrees. The Supreme Court also examined the argument based upon Section 36(3) of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996, which addresses stays of enforcement of arbitral awards. The decree-holders submitted that the statutory treatment of unconditional stays in cases involving fraud or corruption supported a restrictive understanding of discretion under Order XLI CPC. The Court considered the relationship between the arbitration provisions and the principles applicable to stays of money decrees, but did not accept the proposition that exceptional relief under Order XLI was necessarily limited to cases of fraud or corruption. The reasoning also addressed the significance of valid service of summons. The Supreme Court observed that a court’s jurisdiction over a defendant ordinarily requires proper service or voluntary submission to the proceedings. Personal service is the preferred mode in an action against a person, while substituted service is an exceptional procedure requiring compliance with applicable legal requirements. The Court referred to the Joint Registrar’s order dated 1 March 2021, which recorded that although the plaint papers had apparently been delivered through speed post and courier, the Registry had reported non-compliance with the earlier direction requiring service by email and WhatsApp. This procedural history supported the Division Bench’s concern about the propriety of treating Amazon Technologies as properly served. The Supreme Court additionally considered the serious preliminary questions surrounding the assessment and award of damages, including the relationship between the pleaded monetary claim and the much larger amount eventually decreed. Ultimately, the Supreme Court declined to interfere with the Delhi High Court Division Bench’s order granting a stay of execution of the damages and costs portions of the decree without a monetary deposit. It held that the Division Bench had examined relevant circumstances and had not exercised its discretion arbitrarily. The substantial prima facie concerns relating to service of summons, the basis of liability and the extraordinary damages assessment provided a foundation for treating the matter as an exceptional case. The Court summarised eleven principles governing the stay of decrees under Order XLI, emphasising that a specific reasoned order is necessary; sufficient cause must be established; substantial loss, delay and security must be examined; and unconditional relief must remain exceptional. It reaffirmed that a deposit is ordinarily prudent but not invariably mandatory and that security can take different legally acceptable forms. The Special Leave Petition stood dismissed under the order dated 24 September 2025, with the detailed reasons furnished on 7 October 2025. The Supreme Court expressly clarified that its observations and those of the Division Bench were preliminary and would not prejudge the merits of the pending regular first appeal. The judgment therefore did not finally set aside or affirm the underlying trademark infringement decree; it upheld only the interim stay pending appellate adjudication. The ruling is significant for civil and commercial litigation because it balances protection of decree-holders against execution-related hardship where a money decree suffers from serious apparent legal defects. Cases Discussed / Relied Upon 1. Sihor Nagar Palika Bureau Vs Bhabhlubhai Virabhai & Co. (Supreme Court); (2005) 4 SCC 1. The petitioners relied upon this judgment to argue that deposit of the disputed decretal amount or furnishing security is a condition precedent to staying execution of a money decree under Order XLI Rule 5 CPC. The Supreme Court examined the decision while distinguishing the ordinary rule requiring financial protection from the discretionary power available in exceptional circumstances. 2. Malwa Strips Pvt. Ltd. Vs Jyoti Ltd. (Supreme Court); (2009) 2 SCC 426. The Supreme Court relied substantially on this judgment for the interpretation of Order XLI Rule 1(3) and Rule 5 CPC. It reaffirmed that the statutory requirement concerning deposit or security is directory insofar as the maintainability of an appeal is concerned and that an appellate court may, in exceptional cases, stay a money decree without requiring the entire amount to be deposited. The judgment was central to the interpretation of the appellate court’s discretionary powers. 3. Kayamuddin Shamsuddin Khan Vs State Bank of India (Supreme Court); (1998) 8 SCC 676. The Supreme Court discussed this decision concerning the consequence of failing to comply with directions to deposit the disputed amount or furnish security in a money-decree appeal. The earlier ruling distinguished the right to maintain an appeal from the right to obtain an order staying execution. It supported the conclusion that non-compliance does not automatically require dismissal of the appeal. 4. Sunil Poddar and Others Vs Union Bank of India (Supreme Court); (2008) 2 SCC 326. The petitioners relied upon this judgment to contend that procedural defects in service should not invalidate proceedings where the defendant had actual knowledge and sufficient opportunity to participate. The Supreme Court distinguished the earlier decision in examining the evidence concerning service upon Amazon Technologies. Related TaxGuru judicial discussion. 5. LT Foods Ltd. Vs Saraswati Trading Company (Delhi High Court); 2022 SCC OnLine Del 3694. This decision was relied upon by the petitioners regarding service of summons, knowledge of proceedings and the consequences of non-appearance. The Supreme Court considered it in addressing the arguments arising from the ex parte decree. 6. Mithilesh Singh Vs Union of India and Others (Supreme Court); (2003) 3 SCC 309. The Supreme Court relied upon this decision concerning statutory interpretation, particularly the principle that every expression employed by the legislature should ordinarily be given effect and that statutory language should not be treated as redundant without justification. 7. Padma Sundara Rao (Dead) and Others Vs State of Tamil Nadu and Others (Supreme Court); (2002) 3 SCC 533. The Court discussed this Constitution Bench decision concerning interpretation of statutory provisions, the rule against supplying a casus omissus and the requirement to construe a statute as a coherent whole. 8. Union of India Vs Deoki Nandan Agarwal (Supreme Court); 1992 Supp (1) SCC 323. The Supreme Court relied upon this precedent for the proposition that courts must interpret legislation rather than rewrite, recast or supplement statutory language. The decision was relevant to interpreting the provisions governing deposits, security and stays of execution.

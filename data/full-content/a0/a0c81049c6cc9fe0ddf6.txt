(District Court, Saket, New Delhi) Professional fees for GST refund litigation cannot be made contingent on success of refund and being credited to the client The District Court, Saket (South District), New Delhi in Rajinder Kumar Sethi v. M/s. Miso Study India Pvt. Ltd. [CS DJ No. 259/2023 dated October 01, 2026] decreed the suit for recovery of professional fees of Rs. 5,25,000/- filed by an Advocate who had represented the assessee in GST refund proceedings up to the first appellate stage, and held that the assessee cannot, by way of an afterthought, make payment of the agreed professional fees contingent upon the refund being credited to its bank account when no such agreement existed between the parties. Facts: Mr. Rajinder Kumar Sethi (“the Plaintiff”), an Advocate, was engaged in January 2021 by M/s. Miso Study India Pvt. Ltd. (“the Defendant”) to pursue a GST refund claim of Rs. 60,61,000/- pertaining to the Financial Years 2017-18, 2018-19 and 2019-20. As per the Plaintiff, the professional fee was agreed at Rs. 5,25,000/- for handling the matter up to the appeal before the Special Commissioner, Department of Trade and Taxes, Government of NCT of Delhi. A Power of Attorney was executed by the Director of the Defendant authorising the Plaintiff to represent the company in the GST refund case. Upon rejection of the refund claim by the Proper Officer, the Plaintiff filed an appeal under Section 107 of the Central Goods and Services Tax Act, 2017 (“the CGST Act”) in March 2021, appeared on various dates of hearing and concluded arguments on October 28, 2022. The appeal was disposed of by the Special Commissioner vide order dated January 30, 2023, which recorded the appearance of the Plaintiff as the Authorised Representative of the Defendant. The Plaintiff raised an invoice dated July 09, 2022 for Rs. 5,25,000/- and followed it up through e-mail, speed post and reminders. The Defendant, vide e-mail dated September 28, 2022, stated that the fee of Rs. 5,25,000/- would be paid only after the case was won and the refund was credited to its bank account, and only if the refund exceeded Rs. 50,00,000/-. The Plaintiff objected to this condition and issued a legal notice dated February 18, 2023. The Defendant, vide reply dated February 20, 2023, reiterated its refusal. Hence, the suit for recovery along with interest at 18% per annum and litigation costs of Rs. 35,000/- was filed. The Plaintiff contended that he was engaged directly by the Defendant, that the fee was a fixed fee, and that a fee contingent upon the result of the case is contrary to the standards of professional conduct applicable to Advocates. The Defendant contended that there was no privity of contract with the Plaintiff, as it had engaged M/s. Relan & Associates, Chartered Accountants, who in turn had engaged the Plaintiff; that the suit was bad for non-joinder of the said firm; that under its arrangement with the firm, fees were payable only upon credit of the refund; that the arrangement was terminated owing to an alleged demand for illegal gratification; and that the Plaintiff, holding only a Power of Attorney and not a vakalatnama, was merely an agent. Issues: Whether the Defendant had directly engaged the Plaintiff for the GST refund proceedings on an agreed professional fee of Rs. 5,25,000/-? Whether the suit was bad for non-joinder of the Chartered Accountant firm as a necessary party? Whether the Plaintiff is entitled to recover the professional fee with interest when the Defendant asserts that the fee was payable only upon the refund being credited to its bank account? Held: The District Court, Saket (South District), New Delhi in CS DJ No. 259/2023 held as under: Observed that, the Power of Attorney executed by the Director of the Defendant specifically authorised the Plaintiff, described therein as a lawyer, to represent the Defendant in its GST refund case, and its execution was admitted by the Defendant’s witness in cross-examination. Thus, the engagement of the Plaintiff by the Defendant stood established. Noted that, the order dated January 30, 2023 passed by the Special Commissioner recorded that the Plaintiff had appeared as the Authorised Representative of the Defendant on various dates and concluded the arguments. Thus, the rendering of services by the Plaintiff was firmly established. Noted that, the Defendant in its own e-mail dated September 28, 2022 acknowledged the fee of Rs. 5,25,000/-, while seeking to attach a condition of payment only after refund. Further, the Defendant’s witness admitted in cross-examination that there was no agreement between the Plaintiff and the Defendant providing that the fee would be paid only after the GST refund was transferred to the Defendant’s account. Observed that, the condition making the fee contingent upon refund or upon winning the case was clearly an afterthought. Relying upon the judgment of the Hon’ble Madras High Court in S. Ramasamy v. State of Tamil Nadu [W.P. No. 32337 of 2012], the Court reiterated that the relationship between a lawyer and a client rests more on trust than on contract, and that once the fee is agreed and the work is completed, the client cannot go back on its promise. Held that, the allegations of bribery raised by the Defendant were not established on record and the Defendant had not been truthful. Accordingly, all the issues, including that of non-joinder, were decided in favour of the Plaintiff. Directed that, the suit be decreed for Rs. 5,25,000/- along with pendente lite and future interest at 6% per annum from the date of filing of the suit till realisation, as against 18% claimed, in view of Section 34 of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 (“the CPC”) and the judgment of the Hon’ble Supreme Court in Central Bank of India v. Ravindra [(2002) 1 SCC 367 dated October 18, 2001]. No order was made as to costs. Our Comments: Although the dispute is a civil recovery suit, the ruling is of direct relevance to every professional engaged in litigation, as it arises from a routine situation, namely a refund matter pursued up to the first appellate stage, an oral fee arrangement, and a client who declines to pay because the refund has not been received. Section 116 of the CGST Act permits a person to appear before a GST officer or the Appellate Authority through an authorised representative, which under Section 116(2) includes, inter alia, an Advocate entitled to practise in any court in India and a Chartered Accountant holding a certificate of practice. Appearance before such authorities is on the strength of a letter of authorisation, and a vakalatnama is not the prescribed mode. The Defendant’s plea that the Plaintiff was a mere agent for want of a vakalatnama therefore does not sit well with the statutory scheme, and the Court, relying on the appellate order recording the Plaintiff as the Authorised Representative, treated the engagement as a professional one. On the question of contingent fees, Rule 20 of the Bar Council of India Rules (Part VI, Chapter II, Section II) provides that an Advocate shall not stipulate for a fee contingent on the results of litigation or agree to share the proceeds thereof. The Hon’ble Supreme Court in In the matter of ‘G’, a Senior Advocate [AIR 1954 SC 557] held that an agreement by an Advocate to receive a share in the proceeds of litigation amounts to professional misconduct. Further, the Hon’ble Supreme Court in B. Sunitha v. State of Telangana [Criminal Appeal No. 2068 of 2017 dated December 05, 2017] held that a claim of fee based on a percentage of the subject matter of litigation is professional misconduct and that such an agreement is opposed to public policy and hit by Section 23 of the Indian Contract Act, 1872. Seen in this light, even if the Defendant had succeeded in proving an arrangement making the Advocate’s fee payable only upon credit of refund, the enforceability of such a condition would itself have been doubtful. A similar restriction applies to Chartered Accountants. Clause (10) of Part I of the First Schedule to the Chartered Accountants Act, 1949 treats charging of fees based on a percentage of profits or contingent upon the findings or results of the professional employment as professional misconduct, except as permitted under Regulation 192 of the Chartered Accountants Regulations, 1988, which carves out limited exceptions. Success-fee arrangements for GST refund matters should therefore be examined against these provisions before being entered into. It is also relevant that the Hon’ble Supreme Court in R.D. Saxena v. Balram Prasad Sharma [(2000) 7 SCC 264 dated August 22, 2000] held that an Advocate has no lien over the files and papers of the client for unpaid fees and that the remedy lies in legal proceedings for recovery. The present case is an illustration of that remedy being successfully invoked. On the other hand, the judgment has certain limitations. First, the issues were decided collectively, and the plea of non-joinder of the Chartered Accountant firm and the alleged back-to-back arrangement between the Defendant and the firm were not separately analysed; where the documentary trail shows engagement only through an intermediary firm, the question of privity may be decided differently. Second, interest was restricted to 6% per annum as against 18% claimed. In terms of the proviso to Section 34 of the CPC read with Explanation II thereto, future interest above 6% may be awarded where the liability arises out of a commercial transaction, that is, one connected with the industry, trade or business of the party incurring the liability, and it is arguable that professional fees for a company’s tax litigation fall within this description. Third, being a judgment of a District Court, it carries persuasive value only and is appealable under Section 96 of the CPC. As a matter of practice, professionals handling refund, adjudication and appellate matters would be well advised to record the engagement in a written engagement letter addressed to and accepted by the client directly, specifying the scope and stage of work, the fixed fee, the milestones for payment, reimbursement of expenses and interest on delayed payment. In the present case, the Plaintiff succeeded largely because the client’s own e-mail acknowledged the quantum of fee and its witness admitted the absence of any contingent arrangement. The present suit has been filed for recovery of Rs 5,25,000/- with interest @ 18 % till its realisation. The facts, as put forth by the plaintiff, are hereby succinctly recapitulated:. a) It was stated that by the plaintiff in his plaint that the Defendant engaged the Plaintiff to handle a GST refund claim of ₹60,61,000/- for FY 2017-18, 2018-19, and 2019-20. ISSUE WISE ANALYSIS & FINDINGS THERETO (a) Issue No.1: Whether the defendant in January 2021, entrusted the plaintiff for claiming GST refund for the financial years 2017-2018, 2018-2019 and 2019-20 and thereby agreed to pay the plaintiff his professional free of Rs 5,25,000/- for handling the case till appeals before the Special Commissioner, Department of Trade and Taxes, Govt of Nct of Delhi? (c) Issue No.3: Whether the plaintiff is entitled for recovery of Rs 5,25,000/- along with future and pendentelite interest @ 18% per annum and a cost of Rs 35,000/- towards the litigation expenses? 24. All the issues are being dealt with collectively. 25. The present suit has been filed by the plaintiff seeking recovery of ₹5,25,000/- towards professional fees incurred by the plaintiff for rendering professional services to the defendant company. In order to establish that the plaintiff was indeed engaged by the defendant company to represent the defendant company before the Commissioner, GST, it would be pertinent to peruse the following extracts of Exhibit DW1/3 i.e. the Power of Attorney in favour of the plaintiff herein: “I Jeong Mun Hong Director of M/s. Miso Study India Private Limited do hereby authorise Mr. Ranjanna Kumar Sethi, lawyer to represent our case for GST refund and/or to do all such acts, execute documents/deeds, make explanation, statements in order to give effect to the said process and other connected matters. These explanation and statements will be binding on us.” 26. The above power of attorney was also admitted by DW1 Ashish Jacob during his cross examination on 01.09.2025 wherein he avowed as thus: “It is correct that Shri Jeong mun Hong was director of the company in the year 2021 and he still holds this post. It is correct that Power of Attorney was signed by Shri Jeong Mun Hong in favour of the plaintiff to handle the GST case. The Power of Attorney marked PW1/3 is now exhibited as Exhibit DW1/3.” 27. As far as the presence of the plaintiff before the GST is concerned, it would be apt to refer to order dated 30.01.2023 passed by the Special Commissioner Delhi GST/VAT wherein it was observed as thus: “During the course of hearing, the AR of the appellant R . K. Sethi Advocate has appeared on various dates of hearing and finally on 28.10.2022 he has concluded his arguments and thereafter the matter was kept for orders accordingly.” 28. Thus the presence of the plaintiff before the GST Commissioner has also been firmly established. 29. As far as to what was the quantum of professional fee is concerned it would also be apt to refer to the emails dated 28.09.2022 viz. Exhibit PW1/8 and PW1/9, wherein it was stated as thus: “I am writing because our record indicate an outstanding balance of amount of Rs 5,25,000/- against our invoice no 075 Dt 09.07.2022. I was under impression that you will make the payment at your end without reminding on this issue. Now Payment is overdue.” 30. Similarly, in email dated 28.09.2026 viz., Ex PW1/9, sent by HR Miso Study to plaintiff Rajinder Sethi, it was written as thus: We will pay your fees 5,25,000/- as per the following condition. We will pay your fees after refund only (when you will win the case and refund was initiated in our bank account than only we can pay your fees same time) As per our commitment we confirmed that we will pay 525000/- as per your fees If the refund amount is more than 50,00,000/- (After refund only we can pay your fees) Kindly try to get the refund as soon as possible and resolve this case as soon as possible.” 31. As far as the quantum of the amount of profession fee is concerned, it would be pertinent to refer to Exhibit PW1/5 and Exhibit PW1/6. These are notices sent by the plaintiff to the defendant dated 09.07.2022. The notices were sent on the official email ID of the defendant company as well as the same was served by way of speed post. The said notices clearly elucidate the fact that the plaintiff had demanded legal and professional fees for the case qua GST refund as ₹5,25,000/-. 32. Furthermore, on 28.09.2022, the plaintiff again wrote an email to the Managing Director of the defendant company claiming the outstanding balance of amount of ₹5,25,000/- against Invoice No. 75 dated 09.07.2022. The said email is Exhibit PW1/8. However instead of paying the fees, the defendant company wrote an email on 28.09.2022 i.e. Exhibit PW1/9 contending that the fees would be paid only after the refund is obtained and the fees would be paid only on the eventuality of the plaintiff winning the case. This reply is absolutely preposterous. In this context it would be apt to refer to the relevant extracts of S Ramasamy Vs State of Tamil Nadu WP no 32337 of 2012 wherein It was held as under: “12.It is relevant to state that the relationship between a lawyer and client is more on trust than on contract. Once a client agrees to pay a certain fee, the Advocate trusts his client and discharges his duties. On completion of the case, the client shall not go back on his promise and question the competence or the fee as excessive. It is prestige and prerogative of the lawyer to value himself and fix the fees at his discretion. No client can assess his talent and estimate his fees. Lawyers have every right to decline the brief, if his services and his dignity is not respected. In order to do social justice or economic justice an Advocate may accept higher fee from an affluent client and lesser or no fees from a poor or downtrodden litigant. It is his discretion to accept the brief and fix the fee. In the case of Law Officers, they agree for a fee schedule. The Advocate General and Additional Advocate General are requested to appear in important and sensitive cases. After accepting to pay a special fee for their services, it is not palatable for Government to state that I will restrict your fee and pay less than what I promised. No client, much less, the mighty Government as a client, which shall be a model for ethical standards, shall fail in their promise. It is relevant to reiterate that the instructions issued by the Chief Secretary to Government that “Generally, the special fees claimed by the Advocate General are not to be questioned.” but in the instant writ petition, it was questioned by the respondent contrary to the instructions which always bind on him. 33. The defendant has sought to insert a condition to make the payment of fee contingent upon refund or winning the case. This is clearly an afterthought, as is evinced by a factum of perusal of cross examination of DW1 dated 01.09.2025 wherein he averred as thus: “It is correct that there is no such agreement between the plaintiff and the defendant company which may provide that professional fee to the plaintiff was to be paid only after the GST money gets transferred into the account of the defendant.” 34. Thus the defendant has not been truthful. The defence as put by the defendant qua bribery charges, have not been established on record and are a pure moonshine. Under these circumstances no case is made out in favour of the defendant. Thus, in view of the above, issue no 1, 2, and 3 are decided in favour of plaintiff and against the defendant. Ergo, in view of the above in extenso discussion, the suit stands decreed in favour of the plaintiff with the following reliefs: a) Plaintiff is entitled to a decree of the sum of Rs 5,25,000/- as a principle amount and on this amount the plaintiff shall also be entitled for interest @ 6 % per annum from the date of filing of suit till the date of decree. b) As far as future interest is concerned, in Central Bank of India v Ravindra, AIR 2001 SC 3095, the Hon’ble Supreme Court held that the grant of pendente-lite and future interest is a subject matter of the discretion of the Court and not to be governed by the agreement between the parties. Keeping in view (a) Section 34 of CPC, 1908, which contemplates grant of interest at the rate of 6% per annum and (b) the fact that in the past decade, the nationalized banks have also granted interest at the rate of 5-6% per annum, on terms deposits, the plaintiff has been granted pendente-lite and future interest at the rate of 6% per annum.@ 6% per annum from the date of filing of suit till realization. 36.

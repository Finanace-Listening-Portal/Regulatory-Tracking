(Delhi High Court) Summary: The Delhi High Court allowed connected petitions filed by Vinod Kumar Dandona and others, including former directors of Educomp Solution Limited, a charitable trust and former Chief Financial Officer Ashish Mittal, challenging the Central Government’s order dated 17.08.2018 directing an SFIO investigation under Section 212(1)(c) of the Companies Act, 2013. The petitioners contended that the order disclosed no reasons establishing the necessity of an SFIO investigation and disregarded statutory inspection reports that had found only compoundable regulatory irregularities, without evidence of fraud, siphoning or diversion of funds. They also alleged that the proceedings originated in a dispute with the Raffles Group and relied on independent audits, banking examinations, police inquiries, insolvency-related scrutiny and SEBI material to challenge the foundation of the investigation. During the petitions’ pendency, SFIO completed its investigation and filed a prosecution complaint alleging financial irregularities and offences under Sections 447, 448 and 449. The respondents argued that the initiating order should be read with the Oversight Committee’s minutes dated 28.06.2018, which referred to statutory inspection reports, complaints, PMO references, Yes Bank’s Fraud Monitoring Report, IFC material and a media article. They submitted that the completed investigation and prosecution complaint should be examined by the Special Court, that competing expert reports required assessment at trial, and that alleged defects or mala fides in the investigation did not justify quashing proceedings where the material disclosed offences. The High Court rejected the preliminary objection based on the earlier dismissal of a co-accused’s petition: that dismissal was on prematurity, with liberty to seek remedies later, and did not adjudicate the legality of the investigation order on merits. Relying on the statutory framework and the principles discussed in Nita Puri, Parmeshwar Das Agarwal, Barium Chemicals and Rohtas Industries, the Court held that an SFIO investigation is a serious statutory action requiring demonstrable consideration of relevant circumstances. Although formation of the Government’s opinion is subjective, the material supporting it and the reasons for assigning the investigation specifically to SFIO must be discernible from the order itself. The impugned order merely reproduced the statutory power and recorded no reasons explaining either the necessity of investigation or the selection of SFIO. Additional reasons subsequently supplied through affidavits, committee minutes or investigation reports could not cure that defect. The Court nevertheless examined the Oversight Committee’s material and found an independent failure to apply its mind. The statutory inspection reports did not substantiate fraud or diversion, and the committee had failed to consider their actual findings and the compoundable nature of the irregularities. The IFC allegations had already been examined in the supplementary inspection and could not be treated as separate independent material. The Yes Bank report did not supply an independent reasoned basis for entrusting investigation to SFIO; no identifiable contemporaneous PMO complaint was established; and the MP’s letter and media article lacked independent probative value sufficient to justify the action. The Court emphasised that specialist authorities must exercise their expertise and independently evaluate the material rather than act mechanically. The Court expressly refrained from deciding whether documents or the media article had been planted by a corporate rival, or whether the investigation involved collusion. It also did not adjudicate the underlying fraud allegations or the petitioners’ factual defences. Its interference concerned the legality of initiating the investigation. Subsequent progress, the collection of evidence and filing of the prosecution complaint could not validate an order passed without satisfying Section 212(1)(c). Accordingly, the Court quashed the order dated 17.08.2018 together with all consequential proceedings emanating from it, allowed both petitions and disposed of pending applications. He submitted that the statutory power under Section 212(1)(c) is not unbridled and the opinion of the Central Government must be founded upon relevant and sufficient material available to it when the order is passed. 31. Reliance is placed upon the judgment passed by the Bombay High Court in Parmeshwar Das Aggarwal v. Union of India, 2016 SCC OnLine Bom 9276, affirmed by the Hon’ble Supreme Court, to submit that the existence of circumstances forming the basis of the statutory opinion must itself be demonstrable. Mere assertion that an opinion has been formed cannot substitute the existence of relevant material. It is further submitted that the Bombay High Court specifically held that an investigation procured at the instance of a corporate rival is vitiated at the threshold. 32. Reliance is also placed upon the judgment passed by a Coordinate Bench of this Court in Nita Puri v. Union of India, W.P.(C) 261/2025, dated 28.08.2025, and the judgment passed by the Hon’ble Supreme Court in Karanartham Viramah Foundation v. Union of India & Anr., SLP (C) Diary No. 4494/2026, vide order dated 27.01.2026, to submit that the material forming the basis of an order under Section 212(1)(c) must satisfy the twin requirements of relevancy and sufficiency and must prima facie disclose circumstances warranting criminal investigation by the SFIO. 33. He submitted that, having regard to the serious consequences of an investigation under Section 212 upon the promoters, directors and managerial personnel, including consequences affecting their rights under Article 21 of the Constitution of India, the Central Government was required to undertake a conscious and objective assessment of the material and record the reasons, necessity and justification for assigning the investigation to the SFIO. 34. He submitted that the impugned order cannot be supplemented by reasons subsequently disclosed in the Oversight Committee minutes, SFIO pleadings or the investigation report. Reliance is placed upon Mohinder Singh Gill v. Chief Election Commissioner, (1978) 1 SCC 405, and the aforesaid decision of the Hon’ble Supreme Court in Karanartham Viramah Foundation (supra), to submit that the validity of the impugned order must be tested on the reasons contained in the order itself and not on fresh reasons or subsequent developments. 35. He submitted that the invocation of Section 212(1)(c) was contrary to the statutory scheme of Chapter XIV of the Companies Act. The reports prepared under Sections 206 and 208 had not disclosed fraud, siphoning or diversion of funds and did not recommend an SFIO investigation. He submitted that as regards diversion of funds for acquisition of Educomp Child Care/Little Millennium and The Millennium School, the Grant Thornton audit, RP’s examination and the EOW proceedings did not disclose any such diversion and, in any event, the shares of ESL in Little Millennium continued to remain with ESL. 43. He submitted that as regards alleged divestment of shares in Vidya Mandir Classes Ltd., the allegation substantially reproduces the allegations contained in the Wire article and was examined by EOW, Delhi, without any cognizable offence being found. The relevant shares were sold by ICICI Bank, which received the consideration, and the bank itself has not been implicated. 44. He submitted that the alleged fraud concerning Mussoorie International School, the charge was subsequently based upon a complaint of Raffles and that, according to the statement of the former SFIO officer before EOW, the documents concerning the transaction were not independently collected by SFIO but were furnished by Raffles. The recovered communications allegedly demonstrate that the investigation into the transaction was pursued at the instance of Raffles. 45. He submitted that as regards the allegations concerning bogus capital advances and land acquisitions, the allegations relate to periods substantially preceding the impugned order and were already examined during the CDR process, forensic audit, statutory inspection, RP’s transaction audit and SEBI proceedings. The SEBI report specifically found no adverse inference regarding the capital advances. The documents subsequently relied upon for these charges were supplied by Raffles and were not independently verified by SFIO. 46. He submitted that the prosecution complaint is therefore founded upon material which either stood exonerated by contemporaneous investigations or was subsequently procured from a corporate rival. The SFIO cannot convert such material into a valid jurisdictional foundation for an order under Section 212(1)(c), particularly when the impugned order itself contains no reasons demonstrating the formation of the requisite opinion. 47. He lastly submitted that the subsequent conduct of the SFIO aggravates, rather than cures, the original jurisdictional defect. The investigation was allegedly conducted in collusion with persons associated with Raffles, confidential investigation material was shared with them, documents supplied by them were incorporated without independent verification and the investigating officer himself has been charge-sheeted in connection with the alleged conspiracy. 48. To buttress the arguments advanced, reliance is placed upon A.M. Mohan v. State, (2024) 12 SCC 181, and Anand Kumar Mohatta v. State (NCT of Delhi), (2019) 11 SCC 706, to submit that proceedings instituted with mala fide intent or for wreaking vengeance may be quashed even after filing of a charge-sheet. 49. Hence, it has been urged that the impugned order dated 17.08.2018, having been passed without demonstrable and sufficient material, without recording the requisite reasons for assigning investigation to the SFIO, in disregard of the exculpatory statutory reports and in circumstances indicating initiation at the instance of a corporate rival, is unsustainable in law. Consequently, all proceedings consequential thereto, including the SFIO Investigation Report and prosecution complaint, are liable to be quashed. 50. Per Contra, Mr. Tiwari, learned Central Government Standing Counsel submitted that the impugned order dated 17.08.2018 ought not to be read in isolation and must be read in conjunction with the minutes of the Oversight Committee dated 28.06.2018, which reflect detailed deliberation upon the material available with the Central Government, on the basis whereof the opinion was formed that an investigation by the SFIO was necessitated in public interest. He submitted that although the deliberations of the Committee find no specific mention in the impugned order, they are implicit therein and form the very basis of formation of opinion under section 212(1)(c) of the Companies Act. 51. On 28.06.2018 the Oversight Committee considered those reports with Shri P.P. He emphasised that the opinion under Section 212 was thus supported by relevant, independent and contemporaneous material. He submitted that the circumstances, application of mind, reasons, justification and necessity for an SFIO investigation were borne out by the Oversight Committee minutes and that, read with the impugned order, they satisfied the test laid down in Nita Puri (supra). 53. He further submitted that a completed statutory investigation and prosecution complaint could not be annulled on that ground alone. The first investigating officer was repatriated on 31.12.2020, before completion and filing of the prosecution complaint. He submitted that the said officer neither prepared nor submitted the final report dated 30.11.2022. The prosecution complaint was filed by the second officer against whom he said no allegation had been made. 54. He further submitted that a filed prosecution complaint cannot be quashed merely because the initiating order was defective or the investigation was allegedly tainted. He submitted that those matters are matters related to defences to be urged during the trial before the Special Court. He relied on State of Bihar v. P.P. Sharma, AIR 1991 SC 1260; Ahmed Noormohmed Bhatti v. State of Gujarat, (2005) 3 SCC 647; State of Maharashtra v. Iswar Piraji Kalpatri (1996) 1 SCC 542; Sheonandan Paswan v. State of Bihar, (l987) 1 SCC 288; and State of Karnataka v. M. He further submitted that the expert, forensic and financial reports cited by the petitioners were evaluative in nature and had to be tested at trial through cross-examination, corroboration and judicial assessment. He submitted that in writ jurisdiction this court could not decide the probative value of competing expert conclusions at the pre-cognizance or cognizance stage. Lastly, referring to each of the charges set out in the prosecution complaint, he submitted that a case of fraud is made out therein, and that notwithstanding any lacuna or fallacy in the impugned order dated 17.08.2018, the prosecution complaint is not liable to be quashed after its filing. 56. By placing reliance on the said material, Mr. Jethmalani, learned Senior Counsel submitted that the prosecution complaint contradicted every contemporaneous independent audit, forensic examination and statutory inquiry concerning the same transactions, including the examinations under Sections 206(5) and 208. He relied on a comparative chart juxtaposing each SFIO charge with the exculpatory finding on that transaction. He contended that the SFIO by one stroke reversed the consistent finding given by several domain experts without any cogent basis with a predisposition to implicate the petitioners. Submissions heard and the material placed on record perused. 59. At the outset, this Court deems it apposite to deal with the preliminary objection regarding the maintainability of the petitions raised by the respondents. 60. It was vehemently contended on behalf of the respondent that the petition needs to be dismissed in limine on the ground that the present proceedings are, in effect, an attempt to re-agitate issues which had already been considered by a Coordinate Bench of this Court while dismissing a similar petition, being, W.P.(C) 14487/2022, which was filed by one of the accused, being, OSN Infrastructure and Projects Ltd. 61. It was contended that, on an earlier occasion, the Coordinate Bench, by order dated 13.10.2022, had dismissed the petition filed by co-accused, wherein similar prayers were sought. It was contended that the grounds and contentions which are raised in the present petition were also taken on an earlier occasion, however, the Coordinate Bench dismissed the said petition giving opportunity to the accused to agitate all grounds at an appropriate time. 62. In the opinion of this Court, the aforesaid objections raised by the respondents are unmerited. As rightly contended by Mr. Jethmalani, learned Senior Counsel, the present petition was filed prior to dismissal of the said petition and the petitioners were also protected by way of an interim order passed by this Court. Further, W.P.(C) 14487/2022 was dismissed by the Coordinate Bench noting that the issues raised were pre-mature and the liberty was granted to the petitioners therein to approach the Court at an appropriate stage. Thus, the dismissal of the said petition filed by the co-accused did not constitute an adjudication on merits so as to attract the bar of res judicata in the present proceedings. 63. Moreover, it remains undisputed that pursuant to filing of the present petition, the investigation proceeded. The Hon’ble Apex Court, in numerous cases, has categorically held that a party who approaches the Court, cannot be disentitled from hearing only because certain circumstances have changed during the pendency of the petition. The legality of the impugned action must be examined in the context of the grounds urged against it and the applicable statutory framework. The subsequent progress of the proceedings cannot, merely by reason of the passage of time, foreclose judicial scrutiny of the original action. 64. It would be apposite, in this regard, to refer to the order dated 13.10.2022 passed by the Coordinate Bench in W.P.(C) 14487/2022, wherein it was noted that the final decision by the Central Government is yet to be taken on the investigation report and therefore, the challenge was premature. It was held as under: – “7. Having heard learned counsels for parties, the Court at the outset notes that in terms of the provisions enshrined in Section 212 of the Act, on conclusion of investigation, the Investigation Report is liable to be transmitted to the Union Government for its consideration. In terms of sub section (14) thereof, the Union Government may after due examination of the said report and upon legal advice that may be obtained, proceed to direct the SFIO to initiate prosecution. Admittedly, that stage is yet to be reached in the present case. In fact, learned counsel appearing for the SFIO apprised the Court that the Union Government is yet to receive the Investigation Report. It is thus manifest that the Union Government is yet to apply to its mind on the report and form an opinion whether prosecution is liable to be initiated. 8. In that view of the matter, this Court is of the opinion that the challenge as laid is clearly premature since as and when the Union Government does get the opportunity to apply its mind and thereafter direct further proceedings to be taken by the SFIO, the petitioner shall have all remedies as are available in law to assail any prosecution that may be lodged as well as to challenge any adverse findings that may stand recorded or contained in the Investigation Report.” 65. A perusal of the aforesaid observations makes it evident that the Coordinate Bench declined to entertain the challenge at that stage because the statutory process had not yet culminated in a decision by the Central Government regarding prosecution. The Court did not adjudicate upon the legality of the order entrusting the investigation to the SFIO. It is apparent that the Court observed that the prosecution is initiated ultimately on perusal of the report and the petitioner can seek recourse by initiating appropriate remedy. 66. Hence, in view of the above, the issue in regard to maintainability of the present Petitions is decided in favor of the petitioners. 67. I had the benefit of not only hearing the extensive arguments advanced by learned counsels representing the parties but also perusing the written submissions filed on their behalf. 68. The foremost issue, forming the fulcrum of arguments advanced by the parties, is whether the power exercised by the Department under Section 212 (1) (c) of the Companies Act, 2013, fulfils the statutory prerequisite governing the exercise of such power, as mandated under the Act and as interpreted by the Courts from time to time. 69. The issue therefore, to be considered at the outset, is whether the impugned order dated 17.08.2018 passed by the Respondent No. 1, in exercise of the power conferred under Section 212 (1)(c) of the Act, thereby entrusting the investigation into the affairs of ESL to the SFIO, is legally sustainable. 70. For the purpose of convenience, the Sections 206, 208, 210 and 212 of the Act, are set out below: – “CHAPTER XIV INSPECTION, INQUIRY AND INVESTIGATION 206. A Coordinate Bench of this Court in Nita Puri (supra), while examining a challenge to an order passed under Section 212(1)(c) of the Act, considered the statutory scheme and the applicable legal principles in considerable detail. The relevant observations are reproduced hereinbelow:- “Reasoning and findings: 19. Having considered the rival contentions of the parties, this court finds that there is merit in the petitioner’s contention that the impugned order cannot withstand the scrutiny of law. The reasons are enumerated as under: (A) The impugned order is in contravention of the dicta laid down in the judgment of the Bombay High Court in Parmeshwar Das Agarwal v. Additional Director (Investigation) [(2016) 199 Comp Cas 353 (Bom); 2016 SCC OnLine Bom 9276.] 20. In the aforesaid case, the Bombay High Court had occasion to examine the statutory scheme of Chapter XIV of the Companies Act, 2013. After taking note of the corresponding provisions in sections 234 and 237 of the Companies Act, 1956 (hereinafter the “Act, 1956”) and considering the judgment of the Supreme Court in Barium Chemicals Ltd. v. Company Law Board [[1966] 36 Comp Cas 639 (SC); 1966 SCC OnLine SC 53; AIR 1967 SC 295.] , and Rohtas Industries Ltd. v. S.D. Agarwal [(1969) 39 Comp Cas 781 (SC); (1969) 1 SCC 325; 1968 SCC OnLine SC 372.] it was held as under (page 380 of 199 Comp Cas): “Thus, the principle is that there has, to be an opinion formed. That opinion may be subjective, but the existence of circumstances relevant to the inference as to the sine qua non for action must be demonstrable. It is not reasonable to hold that the clause permits the Government to say that it has formed an opinion on circumstances which it thinks exist. Since existence of circumstances is a condition fundamental to the making of the opinion, when questioned the existence of these circumstances have to be proved at least prima facie. *** Therefore, a perusal of this section would indicate that the Central Government must form an opinion, that opinion must be that it is necessary to investigate into the affairs of a company. The Central Government can act on the receipt of a report of the Registrar or inspector under section 208 or on intimation of a special resolution passed by a company that its affairs are to be investigated or in public interest. Thus, there is a discretion to order an investigation into the affairs of the company… Section 210 falling in the same Chapter XIV titled inspection, inquiry and investigation contains these two sections. Section 210 confers a discretion in the Central Government to order an investigation into the affairs of the company and that power has to be exercised if there is an order passed by a court or a Tribunal in any proceedings before it to the effect that the affairs of a company ought to be investigated. Thus sub-section (1) of section 210 confers a discretion while sub-section (2) is mandatory in terms. By sub-section (3) and when the Central Government orders an investigation into the affairs of the company, it may appoint one or more persons as inspectors so as to carry out this task and to report thereon in such manner as the Central Government may direct. By section 212 the seventeen sub-sections thereof enable investigation into the affairs of a company by the Serious Fraud Investigation Office. This power is without prejudice to the provisions of section 210. This power is to be exercised if the Central Government is of the opinion that it is necessary to investigate into the affairs of a company by the Serious Fraud Investigation Office. Therefore, the power to investigate into the affairs of company is common to both provisions. In the former there are three clauses (a) to (c) in sub-section (1) of section 210 and the investigation is to be carried out by the Central Government by appointing inspectors and there is a discretion in that behalf. This power is stated to be akin to section 235 of the 1956 Act. The latter enables investigations into the affairs of a company by the Serious Fraud Investigation Office and there is one more clause (d) in sub-section (1) of section 212 where the Central Government can act on a request from any department of the Central Government or a State Government. Therefore, in a given case there could be an action initiated on the request of the Central Vigilance Commission or based on its recommendations. However, by its very title, the investigation under section 212 by the Serious Fraud Investigation Office ought to be on the basis of the opinion of the Central Government that it is necessary to investigate into the affairs of the company by the Serious Fraud Investigation Office. That opinion has to be based on the report of the Registrar or inspector under section 208; on intimation of a special resolution passed by a company that its affairs are required to be investigated; in the public interest or on the request from any department of the Central Government or the State Government. By section 211, the Serious Fraud Investigation Office is established to investigate frauds relating to a company. It is a very special office and headed by a director and consists of such number of experts from the fields enumerated in sub-section (2) of section 211 to be appointed by the Central Government from amongst persons of ability, integrity and experience. The wide powers that this office enjoys, as is set out in various sub-sections of section 212, would denote as to how its involvement comes after the investigations are assigned to it by the Central Government. By their very nature the investigations into frauds relating to a company have to be assigned. They have to be of such magnitude and seriousness demanding involvement of experts in the fields enumerated in sub-section (2) of section 211. Therefore, while exercising the powers under sub-section (1) of section 212, the Central Government ought to be not only forming an opinion about the necessity to investigate into the affairs of the company, but further that such investigations have to be assigned to the Serious Fraud Investigation Office. We do not think that there were materials in the present case and which can be termed as enough to warrant the exercise of power by the Central Government by resorting to section 212(1) of the Act of 2013. The Central Government, in the order under challenge, did not spell out any circumstances, except outlining its power under the above sections to order investigation into the affairs of a company in public interest. None disputes that power or its existence. In paragraph 2 of the impugned order, however, a reference is made to the report of the Registrar of Companies, West Bengal, dated January 13, 2016. We have already held that the findings in this report are not enough for the Central Government to exercise the drastic power. Something more was required and to be established as circumstances or material enough for exercise of the power. That is clearly lacking in this case. This is the only basis, namely, the report of the Registrar of Companies, West Bengal, or its contents which has enabled the Central Government to exercise its powers under section 212(1)(c). It is, therefore, apparent that it has not necessarily acted in terms of its power conferred by section 212 to direct investigation into the affairs of the company in public interest. The foundation for reaching the opinion or satisfaction is the report of the Registrar. We have referred to the details in that report and we are of the firm opinion that based on that the Central Government could not have recorded a satisfaction or an opinion that investigation into the affairs of the company are necessary. There is no element of public interest which is projected, save and except some vague and general references to certain allegations in matters of bank finance and allotment of coal mines and alleged diversion of raw materials. There has been absolutely no details furnished nor referred in the report. Rather, the report proceeds on the basis that as far as these issues are concerned nothing can be done by the Ministry of Corporate Affairs or the Registrar of Companies. We fail to understand, therefore, how in the present facts and circumstances and based on allegations and counter allegations between two groups of shareholders can it be even held that it is necessary in public interest to direct an investigation into the affairs of the company. Once we reach the conclusion that there is lack of requisite material to arrive at the requisite opinion or record the necessary satisfaction, then, in exercise of our powers of judicial review, we can safely quash and set aside the impugned order. We find that the opinion recorded or the satisfaction reached is vitiated by total non-application of mind. None of the factors which are germane and relevant for forming the opinion have been referred. The opinion or satisfaction is based only on the complaint of the Member of Parliament to the CVC and with regard to which report was called for from the Registrar. Even the contents of that report have been, as held above, misread and totally misinterpreted. Based on that no opinion could have been recorded that it is necessary to investigate the affairs of the company in public interest.” (Emphasis Supplied) 72. The Coordinate Bench, while rendering the aforesaid judgment, had also extensively relied upon the judgment passed by the Bombay High Court in the case of Parmeshwar Das Aggarwal (supra). The fact that the said judgment had been upheld by the Hon’ble Supreme Court was also taken note of by the Coordinate Bench. It was thus, opined as under: – “21. Thus, it has been unequivocally laid down in the aforesaid judgment that: (i) the existence of circumstances relevant for formation of opinion for the purpose of section 212(1)(c) of the Act, 2013, “must be demonstrable”; (ii) the exercise of the power under section 212 must be in consonance with the scheme of Chapter XIV of the Act, 2013. In terms thereof, in the first instance, power is conferred under section 206 to conduct inspection/inquiry (section 206(4) of the Companies Act, 2013). Section 208 specifically contemplates that the Registrar or inspector shall, after inspection of books of account or inquiry under section 206, submit a report in writing to the Central Government, along with such documents, if any, and the report may also include or recommend further investigation into the affairs of the company, if necessary. (iii) Section 210 contemplates investigation into the affairs of a company: (a) on the receipt of a report of the Registrar or inspector under section 208; (b) on intimation of a special resolution passed by a company that the affairs of the company ought to be investigated; or (c) in public interest. (iv) Where a report under section 208 does not find any occasion to conduct a further investigation, the same has a bearing on the exercise of the power under section 212(1)(c) of the Act, 2013 (as in the facts of the case before the Bombay High Court). (v) Where recourse is sought to be taken to section 212(1)(c) of the Act, 2013, the same ought to be “based on the report of the Registrar or inspector under section 208; on intimation of a special resolution passed by a company that its affairs are required to be investigated; in the public interest or on the request from any Department of the Central Government or the State Government” (vi) It is necessary for the Central Government not only to form an opinion regarding necessity to investigate into the affairs of the company, but also to justify the assignment of such investigation/s to the Serious Fraud Investigation Office. (vii) An order under section 212 must disclose the relevant circumstance/s which warrant (i) conduct of investigation and (ii) conduct of investigation by the Serious Fraud Investigation Office. *** 22. In the present case, the impugned order (apart from other infirmities referred to hereinbelow), fails to articulate the “necessity of investigation by the Serious Fraud Investigation Office”, thereby contravening the mandatory requirement articulated in paragraph 47 of the aforesaid judgment of the Bombay High Court. *** 24. The impugned order, inasmuch as it does not satisfy the ingredients enunciated by the Bombay High Court for the purpose of an order under section 212(1)(c) of the Act, 2013, suffers from an apparent and incurable legal lacuna.” (Emphasis Supplied) 73. It was further emphasised that, the formation of opinion by the central government though is subjective, but the circumstances forming the basis of such opinion must be demonstrable from the order. The relevant observations are as follows: “42. The law is well-settled that although the formation of opinion by the Central Government is subjective, but the existence of circumstance/s forming the basis of such opinion must be “demonstrable”. The legal position in this regard has been expounded by the Supreme Court in the landmark cases of Barium Chemicals Ltd. v. Company Law Board [(1966) 36 Comp Cas 639 (SC); 1966 SCC OnLine SC 53; AIR 1967 SC 295.] , Rohtas Industries Ltd. v. S.D. Agarwal [(1969) 39 Comp Cas 781 (SC); (1969) 1 SCC 325; 1968 SCC OnLine SC 372.] and Rampur Distillery and Chemical Co. Ltd. v. Company Law Board [(1970) 40 Comp Cas 916 (SC); (1969) 2 SCC 774; 1969 SCC OnLine SC 319.] . The same has also been reiterated by the Division Bench of the Bombay High Court in Parmeshwar Das Agarwal v. Additional Director (Investigation) [(2016) 199 Comp Cas 353 (Bom); 2016 SCC OnLine Bom 9276.] . As held therein, the legal position expounded by the Supreme Court in ( Barium Chemicals Ltd. v. Company Law Board, , Rohtas Industries Ltd. v. S.D. Agarwal, and Rampur Distillery and Chemical Co. Ltd. v. Company Law Board, ) for the purpose of judicial review of the “opinion” under sections 237 and 326 of the 1956 Act, is also applicable, and relevant for the purpose of testing the “formation of opinion” under section 212 of the Act, 2013.” (Emphasis Supplied) 74. In paragraphs 70 and 71 of the Nita Puri (supra), this Court concluded the issue, by holding that an order under Section 212(1)(c) of the Act, must mandatorily be issued only after due “application of mind” and upon “examination of all relevant circumstances, which is a sine qua non for the formation of opinion under Section 212(1)(c) of the Companies Act, 2013” and existence of such relevant circumstances must be “demonstrable” before the Court and must be discernible from the language of the order itself. Relevant extract reads as under: – “70. An order under section 212(1)(c) of the Act, 2013 directing investigation by the Serious Fraud Investigation Office is not a routine administrative measure. It is in the nature of an extremely serious statutory action having grave consequences and repercussions for the subject entities and individuals. It is therefore, imperative that such an order must be issued only after due application of mind, after examining all relevant circumstances. 71. The existence of “relevant circumstances” is sine qua non as for the purpose of formation of opinion under section 212(1)(c) of the Act, 2013. As held by the Supreme Court in Barium Chemicals Ltd. v. Company Law Board [[1966] 36 Comp Cas 639 (SC); 1966 SCC OnLine SC 53; AIR 1967 SC 295.] , Rohtas Industries Ltd. v. S.D. Agarwal [(1969) 39 Comp Cas 781 (SC); (1969) 1 SCC 325; 1968 SCC OnLine SC 372.] and Rampur Distillery and Chemical Co. Ltd. v. Company Law Board [(1970) 40 Comp Cas 916 (SC); (1969) 2 SCC 774; 1969 SCC OnLine SC 319.] , and reiterated by the Bombay High Court in Parmeshwar Das Agarwal v. Additional Director (Investigation) [[2016] 199 Comp Cas 353 (Bom); 2016 SCC OnLine Bom 9276.] , the existence of the relevant circumstances has to be “demonstrable”. Exercise of power under section 212(1)(c) in a casual or perfunctory manner, seriously undermines the statutory provision itself and the safeguards implicit thereunder. The use of boilerplate language and/or extrapolations from third party documents, without consideration of all the “relevant circumstances”, reflects a disregard for procedural propriety. It can hardly be emphasised enough that the power under section 212(1)(c) must be exercised with circumspection and deliberation. In the present case, the impugned order under section 212(1)(c) appears to have been issued in a rather casual manner, unmindful of the statutory pre-requisites therefor.” (Emphasis Supplied) 75. Notably, the aforesaid judgment of this Court passed in Nita Puri (supra) was assailed by way of an SLP before the Hon’ble Supreme Court. The Hon’ble Apex Court, by a detailed order dated 27.01.2026 passed in SLP (C) Diary No(s). 4494/2026, affirmed the findings and reasoning given by the Coordinate Bench of this Hon’ble Court in Nita Puri (supra). The order dated 27.01.2026 of the Supreme Court is extracted hereinbelow: “SLP (CIVIL) Diary No(s). 4494/2026 ……… 3. The challenge in this Special Leave Petition is to the order passed by the High Court of Delhi allowing the Writ Petition preferred by the respondent no.2 assailing the order dated 05.09.2024 passed by the Ministry of Corporate Affairs, Government of (c) under Section 212(1)(c) of the Companies Act, 2013 (for short, ‘the Act’) directing the Serious Fraud Investigation Office (for short, ‘SFIO’) to conduct an investigation into the affairs of Moser Baer (c) Ltd. (MBIL) and its Subsidiaries including joint ventures and associate companies as on date. ……. 6. Thereafter, the Central Government has issued the order which has been set aside by the High Court of Delhi relying on the order of the Bombay High Court in the matter of Parmeshwar Das Agarwal & Ors. Vs. The Additional Director (Investigation) Serious Fraud Investigation Office & Ors. 2016 SCC Online BOM 9276 which in turn has already been affirmed by this Court in SLP (Civil) Diary No(s). 2680/2026. According to the High Court, in the case of Parmeshwar Das Agarwal (supra), the High Court of Bombay referred to Barium Chemicals Limited and Anr. Vs. Company Law Board and Ors., AIR 1967 SC 295 and Rohtas Industries vs. SD Aggarwal and Ors., 1969 1 SCC 325, to hold that all the relevant circumstances which is sine qua non for the purpose of formation of opinion under Section 212(1)(c) of the Act, has not been considered and therefore, exercise of power under Section 212(1)(c) of the Act in a casual and perfunctory manner, undermines the statutory provisions itself and the safeguard implicit thereunder. 7. The High Court quashed the order passed by the Central Government also on the ground that the additional reasons supplied in the affidavit could not be considered in view of the law laid down by this Court in the matter of Mohinder Singh Gill & Another v. The Chief Election Commissioner, New Delhi & Ors, Opto Circuit (c) Ltd. V. Axis Bank & Ors. And Ritesh Tiwari v. State of Uttar Pradesh. Thus, in the peculiar circumstances of the case, the High Court being bound by the law laid down in Barium Chemicals (supra) and Mohinder Singh Gill (supra), has rightly adjudged the validity of the order under Section 212(1)(c) of the Act on the basis of the contents of the Order itself, and not on the vice of fresh reasons or subsequent developments or by calling report of SFIO, and as such High Court has not committed any error. 8. Considering the submissions made by the Ld. Senior counsel for the petitioner, we are not inclined to entertain this Special Leave Petition and the same is, accordingly, dismissed.” (Emphasis Supplied) 76. I am in complete agreement with the view expressed by the Coordinate Bench that an order under Section 212 (1)(c) of the Act must clearly demonstrate the due application of mind and that the power has been exercised after examination of all relevant circumstances and material before forming the requisite opinion. The existence of the relevant circumstances / material as well as application of the mind must be demonstrable and discernible from the language of the order itself. 77. Reliance has also been placed upon the judgment passed by the Hon’ble Apex Court in the Barium Chemicals Limited and Anr. v. The Company Law Board and Others : AIR 1967 SC 295 and Rohtas Industries Limited v. S.D. Aggarwal : (1969) 1 SCC 325. The Hon’ble Apex Court in the said judgments had considered the provision of Section 237 of the Companies Act, 1956, which empowered the authorities to inquire into the affairs of the company and can be called the predecessor provision to Section 210 and 212 of the Companies Act, 2013. It is, therefore, well settled that although the formation of opinion by the authorities is subjective but the existence of circumstances forming the basis of such opinion must be demonstrable from the order itself. 78. In this backdrop, the validity of the impugned order dated 17.08.2018 has to be considered in light of the crystallized legal principles governing the prerequisites to exercise power under Section 212(1)(c) of the Act. 79. At this juncture, it would be beneficial to peruse the impugned order dated 17.08.2018, which reads as under: “ORDER Whereas by virtue of powers conferred under section 212 (1) (c) of the Companies Act, 2013, in the public interest, Central Government is empowered to order investigation into the affairs of the company through Serious Fraud Investigation Office (SFIO). 2. Now, therefore, in exercise of powers conferred under section 212 (1) (c) of the Companies Act, 2013, the Central Government hereby orders investigation into the affairs of Educomp Solution Limited through SFIO. 3. By virtue of provio to section 212 (1) of the Companies Act, 2013, the inspector (s) appointed by the Director, Serious Fraud Investigation Office (SFIO) to carry out the investigation of Educomp Solution Limited shall exercise powers conferred upon them under the Companies Act, 2013. The Director, SFIO shall complete the investigation and submit the report to the Ministry within four (4) months hereof. 4. This order is issued for and on behalf of the Central Government.” 80. A bare perusal of the impugned order, ex facie, reflects that the order entrusting the investigation to SFIO, is devoid of any reason whatsoever. Law, as noted above, mandates that the reason should be demonstrable from the order itself. It is not understandable or demonstrable that examination of which material, brought before the authorities, enabled it to form an opinion to entrust the investigation to SFIO. The order also fails to reflect any discernible application of mind by the competent authorities to the inculpatory circumstances /material, if any, placed before it and what weighed with the authorities for arriving at an independent opinion regarding the necessity of entrusting the investigation to the SFIO. 81. The impugned order, therefore, in the opinion of this Court, does not satisfy the test laid down for exercise of power under Section 212(1)(c) of the Act. The impugned order, as apparent, only reproduces the power conferred on the Central Government by the said section, and is bereft of any reason for exercising the same and directing the investigation by the SFIO. 82. Significantly, the mere reproduction of the statutory language cannot, by itself, demonstrate that the requisite opinion was formed after due consideration of relevant material. 83. It is also well settled that the powers as proclaimed under Section 212(1)(c) of the Act, cannot be equated with the registration of an FIR, for which the statutory threshold is materially different as the same only requires allegations about a cognizable offence. Powers such as under Section 212(1)(c) of the Act cannot be exercised in a routine administrative manner, particularly in view of the safeguards inherent in the statutory scheme and emphasised by the Courts from time to time. 84. At the same time, though the formation of opinion is founded on a subjective satisfaction of the authorities but the same has to be routed in objective consideration of cogent and relevant material available at the time of passing of the order. Mere presence of material is of no consequence unless the same is discernible from the order itself. Formation of opinion cannot also be whimsical or on unverified allegations or a third-party material unless the same is independently examined and authenticated through the scheme of the Act warranting investigation. 85. In this regard, this Court, therefore, agrees with the submissions made by Mr. Jethmalani, learned Senior Counsel, that material before the Central Government must prima facie indicate fraud, siphoning or diversion of funds, use of power with intent to deceive, undue will, an injury to company etc. so as to attract the consequences contemplated under the Companies Act. No order of such nature ought to be passed without assigning and supplying due reasons. 86. This also becomes important, as in the present case, the initial reports issued under the Companies Act itself, did not discover any infraction of such nature as warranting passing of the impugned order under Section 212 of the Act. 87. The absence of any discernible reasoning in the impugned order assumes further significance when considered alongside the statutory inspection report dated 31.03.2017 and the supplementary inspection report dated 31.07.2017, both of which preceded the impugned order. 88. The statutory inspection report dated 31.03.2017 into the affairs of the company discovered some compoundable infractions, which as argued have already been compounded. It is also significant to note that the supplementary investigation report dated 31.07.2017, also found the allegations of fraud and diversion of funds as unfounded. 89. Unlike the orders passed under other provisions of chapter XIV, the orders passed under Section 212(1)(c) of the Companies Act are distinct, as the same confers wide discretion upon the competent authority to entrust the investigation into the affairs of a company to a specialised agency. Passing of an order under Section 212(1)(c) of the Act by the authorities entail serious civil as well as criminal consequences and, therefore, requires strict adherence to the tests laid down by the Courts. 90. As discussed in the preceding paragraphs and in Nita Puri (supra), the compliance of the prerequisite, that is, demonstrable application of mind on the available material is a sine qua non for sustaining on order under Section 212(1)(c) of the Act. The same should be clearly reflected in the order itself which is missing in the present case. The reflection of the exercise undertaken by the authority for examining such material, and lastly the reasoning on the basis of which the authority reaches an opinion to exercise the statutory power. 91. These ingredients have to be necessarily reflected in the order itself so as to obviate any challenge on the ground of arbitrariness. In the absence of such opinion being reflected in the order, the same makes it vulnerable on the ground of arbitrariness and having been passed on irrelevant consideration and extraneous factors. 92. Having held that from a perusal of the Impugned Order no reasoning is discernable, this Court, now proceeds to examine the further submissions advanced on behalf of the respondents that the impugned order must be read in conjunction with the minutes of the Oversight Committee dated 28.06.2018. 93. This submission does not persuade the Court, because of two reasons. Firstly, that the impugned order has to stand on its own legs and no amount of additional reasons, supplied in other forms, either by way of an affidavit or otherwise can justify passing of an unreasoned order; and Secondly, on an independent examination of the Minutes of Oversight Committee dated 28.06.2018, the alleged material in the opinion of this Court also does not justify the formation of opinion for passing an order under Section 212(1)(c) of the Act for ordering the investigation by the SFIO. 94. Though the impugned order is vitiated for want of due application of mind and demonstrable circumstances and reasoning in the order, this Court has been constrained to look into the material since the arguments have been advanced that the minutes of Oversight Committee shall be read in conjunction with the impugned order. 99. The first set of documents referred to by the Oversight Committee comprises the inspection and supplementary inspection reports of the Regional Director. In these circumstances, once the statutory inquiry into the allegations were not substantiated, the complaint by IFC also could not form separate and independent material so as to justify an order under Section 212(1)(c) of the Companies Act. 106. The reliance placed by the Oversight Committee on the Internal Fraud Monitoring Report by Yes Bank is also misplaced. The report indicates that the Yes Bank was in the process of conducting a detail investigation in the matter and had also filed a complaint with EOW, New Delhi. Thus, once the investigation had already been undertaken on the alleged fraud committed by the ESL and Yes Bank by the Competent Authority being EOW, Delhi Police, the reliance on the same for the purpose of starting a parallel investigation by SFIO belies any logic, as the decision to entrust the investigation to the SFIO still required an independent and reasoned basis in accordance with the statutory framework. 107. In regard to the PMO references, this Court, upon examination of the record, finds that no such complaint was annexed by the Respondent No. 1 with the presentation made by them before the Oversight Committee. The minutes of meeting of the Oversight Committee though mentions representation forwarded by the PMO, however, they do not specify the date or identify the document in question. Thus, it is not clear as to which material came into the possession of the Oversight Committee which was being termed as PMO references and why the same was mentioned in the minutes. 108. The respondents in the affidavit filed before this Court have also not referred or acknowledged or produced any complaint referred / forwarded by the PMO which formed the basis for passing the impugned order. One complaint was mentioned during the proceedings, but the same is of a subsequent date being 16.05.2019 and, therefore, obviously cannot be the part of the material examined by the Oversight Committee or the respondents, before passing the impugned order on 17.08.2018. The said complaint was sent by the Raffles Group to the PMO on a subsequent date and, therefore, could not have any bearing on the decision-making process when no contemporaneous PMO reference has been brought on record. 109. The other material, which as noted above, which was considered by the Oversight Committee, are letters addressed by the Member of Parliament and the article published in the “the Wire”, which, in the opinion of this Court, were self-serving documents and could not be the basis for any consideration for the purpose of statutory exercise as serious as Section 212(1)(c) of the Act. The article as well as one complaint by Member of Parliament at best can form basis for some inquiry, however, could not be of any independent evidentiary or probative value so as to warrant exercise of power under Section 212(1)(c) of the Act. 110. It is clarified that this Court is refraining from commenting on the allegations that the documents were planted at the instance of the corporate rival, that is, Raffles Group, though it is vehemently argued by Mr. Jethmalani, learned Senior Counsel that the material seized during the period of investigation, particularly the chats retrieved from the Account of Mr. Saurabh Sharma shows that the article published by “the Wire” had already been shared with him on 02.04.2018, that is, one day prior to the publication by “the Wire” and, therefore, the same was planted for the purpose of initiating the serious step under Section 212(1)(c) of the Act. 111. This Court does not find it apposite to adjudicate on the said allegation, since in the opinion of this Court, the article in itself cannot constitute sufficient basis for starting of investigation without there being any corroboration. 112. It was also pointed out that the letter written by the Member of Parliament was immediately on the next day after the article was published in “the Wire”. However, once the said material is held to be of no significance for starting the investigation, this Court would refrain from commenting further on the allegations made by the petitioners. 113. The cavalier manner in which the material, as noted above, has been cited, for justifying the entrusting of the investigation to the SFIO, in the opinion of this Court, ought to be deprecated. The officers appointed under the scheme of the Act are experts in the matters of corporate governance and cannot be expected to rely upon unsubstantiated material and the reports so as to justify the serious action as contemplated under Section 212(1)(c) of the Act. Therefore, once the material clearly belies any logic to justify the investigation, the respondents cannot be allowed to take the arguments that the subjective satisfaction is based on such material. While the Court ought not substitute its own opinion for that of the competent authority, it is entitled to examine whether the statutory prerequisites for the exercise of power have been satisfied and whether the decision is vitiated by non-application of mind or reliance upon irrelevant considerations. 114. At this stage, this Court also takes support of the judgment passed by the Bombay High Court in Parmeshwar Das Aggarwal (supra) where the Hon’ble Bombay High Court, noted that if the report under Section 208 of the Act does not find any occasion to conduct further investigation, the exercise of power under Section 212(1)(c) of the Act would be unjustified. 115. Once a statutory inspection and supplementary inspection report submitted under Section 208 of the Act does not lead to further serious subsequent actions under Section 212(1)(c) of the Act, no support of other material such as the newspaper report and the letter from the Member of Parliament can form basis to justify the same. 116. Mr. Jethmalani, learned Senior Counsel has also referred to various contemporaneous letters written by the Raffles Group to SFIO, the chats recovered by the EOW and the manner in which the article was published by “the Wire” and the same being made before the respondents can justify the apprehension of the petitioners that the investigation was vitiated at the behest of the corporate rivals. However, once this Court has reached the conclusion that the material, even if taken on the face value, does not justify passing of the impugned order, no further adjudication is necessary in that regard at this stage. 117. Once a statutory report issued by the respondent authorities itself did not arrive at any finding or siphoning or defalcation of funds, the other documents being only allegations, loses significance and cannot be relevant for the purpose of exercise of power under Section 212(1)(c) of the Act. 118. As already noted in the preceding paragraphs by this Court, that the order passed for investigation of by the SFIO cannot be equated with the registration of FIR. 119. The expert bodies being tasked to perform the functions under the Companies Act are required to apply their “expert mind” to the material placed before them so as to reach a conclusion that a serious action such as envisaged under Section 212(1)(c) of the Act can be taken. The decisions, therefore, are to be tested on a touchstone of the standard expected of an expert and not of an ordinary person. 120. The Courts have repeatedly observed that such significant powers are deliberately vested upon the experts and, therefore, attract personal responsibility and duty to exercise their expertise, prudence and independent evaluation of the material. Such power cannot be used in a mechanical manner as sought to be done in the present case. 121. Even though a lot is sought to be justified by Mr. Tiwari, learned counsel for respondent by filing an affidavit, however, it is a settled law that no amount of additional reasons supplied in the form of an affidavit or subsequent reports can justify the initial action [Ref.: Mohinder Singh (supra)]. 122. It is also settled law that once the foundation is found to be defective, the superstructure raised upon it also cannot be allowed to stand. If the initial order of investigation is found to be unjustified, all subsequent actions cannot validate the same. [Ref : Ritesh Tewari v. State of U.P. :(2010) 10 SCC 677] 123. The minutes of Oversight Committee and the consequential impugned order is ex facie product of non-application of mind and it appears to be a mere formality for starting the investigation. Thus, when the decisions are taken either on a mistake or an inadvertent error ignoring the relevant material, the aggrieved party would be justified in alleging arbitrariness or that the decision has been taken for extraneous consideration at the instance of corporate rivals. 124. At this stage, it is necessary to deal with the further objection raised on behalf of the respondents that the arguments which have been raised, require adjudication of seriously disputed questions of facts concerning documents, electronic records and forensic material, which ought not to be adjudicated in the writ jurisdiction. 125. It was further contended that the learned Special Court is already seized of the complaint filed pursuant to the investigation and the petitioner would have a statutory remedy before the learned said Court, to seek discharge and this Court ought not to conduct a mini-trial and assess the sufficiency or the probative value of the evidence collected during the investigation. 126. This Court concurs with the general proposition that the triable issues and the disputed questions of fact should not ordinarily be adjudicated while exercising jurisdiction under Section 482 of the CrPC or under Article 226 of the Constitution of India. It is well-settled that the High Court, while exercising inherent extraordinary jurisdiction, should be cautious and circumspect. The Court does not contemplate enquiry or mini trial to substitute its view and where the allegations and material disclose a prima facie case, the question in regard to the truth and evidentiary value of such allegation and the defence must ordinarily be left to the learned Trial Court to adjudicate after the evidence is led by the parties. 127. However, this Court has only considered the validity of the initiation of the investigation and therefore, has not adjudicated the allegations and the defence, which necessarily would require a trial. Once the Court reaches a conclusion that the very initiation of the investigation stood vitiated due to non-application of mind and the impugned order has been passed in a mechanical manner, all other aspects/ merits would not require adjudication. Once an initial order of investigation is found to have been passed dehors the powers conferred under Section 212(1)(c) of the Act, all proceedings emanating therefrom are liable to be brought to an end. 128. Additionally, once the petition is entertained by this Court by issuing notice, the same cannot be rejected, after more than three years, on the ground that the disputes should be left to be decided by the learned Trial Court. 129. The proceedings which become susceptible to interference on their inception, do not acquire immunity merely because some water is flown before it is finally adjudicated. The progress or the evidence collected may have some evidentiary value in appropriate proceedings, but cannot cure the foundational illegality. [Ref : A.M. Mohan v. State : (2024) 12 SCC 181]. 130. Thus, in view of the foregoing, this Court holds that the impugned order dated 17.08.2018, cannot be sustained in law and are hereby quashed, along with all consequential proceedings emanating therefrom. 131. The present petitions are allowed in the above terms. Pending application(s), if any, also stand disposed of. 132. A copy of this Judgment be placed in both the matters.

Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); Khem Ka Bengal Telecom Vs ITO (ITAT Kolkata) Demonetisation Deposits Supported by Sales, VAT Returns and Buyer Details: ITAT Deletes ₹74.18 Lakh Section 69A Addition Background of the Case The Kolkata Tribunal deleted an addition of ₹74,17,500 under Section 69A relating to deposits of specified bank notes during demonetisation. The assessee had supported the deposits with extensive business records, while the Assessing Officer had neither rejected the books nor identified defects in the supporting evidence. The assessee-firm carried on distributorship of Hindustan Unilever FMCG products and distribution of mobile recharge vouchers and related products of the Airtel group. It filed its return declaring income of ₹4,16,180. During scrutiny, the Assessing Officer questioned deposits of ₹74,17,500 in specified bank notes. Although the assessee furnished explanations and records, the officer considered them insufficient and assessed the entire deposit as unexplained money. Total income was consequently determined at ₹78,33,680. The CIT(A) sustained the addition. Deposits Explained as Recorded Trading Receipts Before the Tribunal, the assessee contended that the deposits arose from cash sales already recorded in its regular books. Its total turnover for Financial Year 2016–17 was ₹27,49,40,775, including the sales underlying the disputed deposits. The assessee argued that the Revenue had accepted the sales and the profit disclosed from those sales. Treating the corresponding cash receipts again as unexplained income, without identifying defects in the records, was therefore unjustified. It also referred to a recorded cash balance of ₹74,72,342 and furnished detailed material concerning the source and deposit of cash. Extensive Evidence Furnished During Assessment The Tribunal recorded that the assessee had produced audited financial statements and the tax audit report, together with cash books, ledgers, creditors’ accounts, cash-flow statements and day-wise and month-wise sales and purchase details. The supporting material also included West Bengal VAT returns for all four quarters, denomination-wise details of specified bank notes deposited, and comparative statements of cash receipts and bank deposits for the preceding and relevant financial years. Significantly, the assessee furnished names and addresses of 670 buyers to whom cash sales were made during the demonetisation period, along with stock-related details. Distribution agreements with HUL and Airtel, municipal trade licences, bank statements and financial records for surrounding years supported the existence and continuity of the business. Third-Party Replies Provided Further Corroboration The assessment record included replies received from Airtel Payment Bank, Bank of India and Bharti Telemedia Ltd. pursuant to notices under Section 133(6). These replies formed part of the material available for examining the assessee’s explanation. The Tribunal specifically noted that the Revenue had recorded no adverse findings concerning these third-party responses. This mattered because the explanation did not rest solely on the assessee’s own statements. It was supported by business records, statutory returns and information obtained through the Department’s independent enquiries. No Defects Identified in Books or Supporting Records The Tribunal found no adverse findings on the VAT returns, the particulars of the 670 buyers, the tax audit report or the audited books. It also noted that the declared turnover included the relevant sales and that the books had neither been rejected nor found defective. Thus, the dispute was not resolved merely by observing that the assessee maintained accounts. The Tribunal considered the breadth of the evidence and the absence of a demonstrated defect undermining the claimed sales source. The Revenue’s general dissatisfaction did not displace that factual foundation. Earlier Kolkata Decisions Followed The Tribunal relied on the reasoning in ITO v. Sanhati Infocom Services Pvt. Ltd., where cash deposits were explained through regularly accounted business receipts. The discussion reproduced in the order also referred to ITO v. Senco Alankar [2022] 142 taxmann.com 578, recognising the relevance of accepted purchases and the absence of specific defects in the books when examining demonetisation deposits. Applying that approach to the present record, the Tribunal found substance in the assessee’s explanation. It deleted the entire ₹74,17,500 addition under Section 69A and allowed the appeal. Mobile Recharge Exception Was Not the Decisive Finding The assessee’s grounds also referred to the permitted use of specified bank notes for certain prepaid mobile top-ups during demonetisation. However, the Tribunal did not separately determine that contention or base its operative relief on a finding that every receipt satisfied the conditions of that exception. The decisive reasoning concerned the established trading activity, recorded sales, extensive corroboration and absence of adverse findings. Author’s Comments The ruling demonstrates that a cash-sales explanation becomes persuasive when it is supported by a complete chain of records linking business activity, stock, sales, customers, cash availability and bank deposits. The non-rejection of books was relevant, but it was accompanied here by substantial documentary and third-party corroboration. The decision should therefore not be reduced to a proposition that every deposit must be accepted merely because the books remain unrejected. Equally, deposit of specified bank notes during demonetisation does not by itself establish unexplained income. The source must be evaluated against the actual evidence, and any rejection should address identifiable discrepancies rather than general suspicion. List of Cases Discussed 1. ITO v.

The bank is prioritizing water-related projects as extreme weather threatens agricultural growth. It has allocated $13.8 billion in total funding to India since 2016, but only 7% is aimed at water projects. Advt Also read: Super El Nino: The methodology behind '15,800 excess deaths' in India estimateAlthough the AIIB has allocated $13.8 billion in total funding to India since it began operations, direct loans designated for water-related projects account for less than 7%. The institution is pivoting its focus toward water projects as extreme weather disturbances imperil growth across South Asia’s largest economy. India is currently dealing with a 15% rainfall shortfall this monsoon season. Maharashtra, India's most affluent state which includes the financial hub Mumbai, has reported drought conditions across multiple districts as reservoir levels fall. Meanwhile, Karnataka, home to tech capital Bengaluru, has implemented state-wide drought relief measures.Additionally, broader infrastructure loans from the AIIB may feature water-conservation mandates, such as automated shut-off mechanisms for solar irrigation pumps under a $1.1 billion project in Maharashtra. The bank also intends to direct capital toward industrial wastewater recycling, water utilities, and groundwater replenishment.A joint study published Friday by the AIIB and climate think-tank Council on Energy, Environment and Water highlights a $35 billion potential market in treated wastewater reuse and a $118 billion opportunity in micro-irrigation systems.Also read: Delhi winter pollution could be worse this year: 47 farm fires already recorded, Super El Nino, late Diwali and delayed harvesting likely to complicate things Advt However, attracting these investments will require crowding in private sector capital into an area where public tax revenues currently cover 85% of financing, according to the lender.Commercial private investment has remained subdued because most water developments function as public goods. Thia noted that urban water utilities present the most viable entry point for private enterprises, highlighting smart metering and residential billing as proven frameworks to secure reliable revenue streams.Even so, that alone will not fully resolve the systemic shortage. The agricultural sector is “decisive,” Thia emphasized, because it consumes as much as 80% of India’s freshwater supplies.

(Supreme Court of India) Professional College Fees Must Be Disclosed for the Entire Course Before Admission; Mid-Course Increase Permissible Only in Exceptional Circumstances Issue Before the Court Whether a self-financing professional college could increase fees after students had completed admission and commenced their course, relying on a subsequent Government notification and a prospectus clause permitting changes pursuant to Government decisions. The Supreme Court held that certainty regarding the financial commitment for the entire course is an essential part of fair fee regulation. Mid-course variation is permissible only in exceptional circumstances, such as an unforeseen increase in expenditure, and cannot substitute for timely regulatory planning. Facts of the Case Students were admitted to the 2023–2027 B.Tech. courses at Dr. B.C. Roy Engineering College in September 2023, following centralised counselling, document verification and payment of the prescribed fees. The college had published a semester-wise fee structure covering the course. Its prospectus nevertheless stated that the fee structure was subject to change from time to time according to Government decisions. On 16 October 2023, after admissions had concluded, the West Bengal Government issued a notification revising the fee ceiling for private self-financing engineering, pharmacy and architecture colleges. It permitted annual tuition fees up to ₹1.10 lakh, besides specified additional charges. Acting on that notification, the college revised its fees in December 2023, making the increase applicable from the second semester in January 2024. It also introduced a development fee. The judgment records a resultant 37.5% increase in fees payable. The students challenged application of the revised structure to their existing batch. High Court Proceedings The Single Judge upheld the increase, relying, among other things, on the prospectus clause reserving the possibility of Government-directed revision. The Division Bench reversed that conclusion. It held that the revised structure could apply to students admitted on or after 16 October 2023, but not to those whose admission process had already concluded before that date. It also directed reversal of the additional collections, including refund of enhanced fees collected from protected students. The colleges appealed to the Supreme Court. Students’ Certainty and Institutions’ Financial Sustainability Must Be Balanced The Supreme Court recognised two legitimate interests. Students and their families require reasonable fees and certainty about the amount and timing of payment. Their admission decisions involve substantial financial commitments extending over several years. Equally, self-financing institutions require adequate resources for infrastructure, competent faculty, technology, maintenance and student services. They must be permitted a reasonable return for sustenance and development after meeting legitimate expenditure. The Court therefore did not disapprove fee revision as such. It emphasised that the real question concerned the method, timing and implementation of revision. Fee Revision Must Follow an Evidence-Based Regulatory Process Reading the relevant provisions of the AICTE Regulations, 1994, the Court explained that fee revision must proceed through the prescribed committee mechanism. The assessment must consider expenditure necessary for efficient functioning, including at least the preceding two years’ audited recurring expenditure and projected requirements for the next three years. The Court also referred to the National Education Policy, 2020, which requires transparent disclosure of fees and guards against arbitrary increases during a student’s enrolment. Thus, revision must emerge from a structured assessment of legitimate costs rather than an ad hoc demand imposed after students have joined. Entire-Course Liability Must Be Known Before Admission The Court held that fee fixation committees must make recommendations sufficiently early, enabling the competent authorities to notify fees before admissions commence. A student entering a four- or five-year professional programme should ordinarily know, at the outset, the financial liability being undertaken. The assessment should account for reasonably foreseeable increases in infrastructure costs, faculty remuneration, maintenance, technology and statutory requirements. This does not require every semester to carry an identical fee. It requires advance certainty about the applicable course-wise payment structure. A narrowly defined mechanism may accommodate extraordinary economic conditions, statutory changes or other genuinely unforeseen circumstances. However, such adjustments must remain exceptional. Regulatory Delay Cannot Become the Basis for Routine Mid-Course Revision The previous fee revision had occurred in 2016–2017. The Court found that the committee and Government had failed to complete the subsequent revision in time for the 2023 admissions. It acknowledged the college’s concern about implementing a revision after seven years. Nevertheless, the dispute could have been avoided through timely notification before the academic session. The Court stressed that fee fixation committees must operate as continuing institutions with efficiency, expertise and integrity, subject to appropriate judicial review. Decision The Supreme Court affirmed the High Court’s operative protection for students admitted before 16 October 2023, including the consequential directions concerning enhanced collections. However, it rejected the High Court’s reasoning based on retrospectivity, equity and estoppel arising from the admission brochure. The appeals were therefore partly allowed, while the practical relief granted to the students remained intact. Author’s Comments The judgment makes timely regulation and advance disclosure central to fee certainty. A general reservation in the prospectus cannot be treated as unrestricted authority to revise an existing batch’s financial commitment. Equally, the ruling recognises institutions’ legitimate costs and permits exceptional adjustments. The practical requirement is to determine and disclose a realistic entire-course fee schedule before admission, supported by audited expenditure and reasonable projections, while addressing unforeseen changes through a properly defined regulatory mechanism.

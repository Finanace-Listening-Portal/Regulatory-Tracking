Summary: The e-way bill mechanism under the Goods and Services Tax framework regulates the movement of goods, but genuine transportation difficulties such as vehicle breakdowns, traffic obstruction, accidents, transshipment and delays may result in expired e-way bills, roadside detention, penalties and confiscation disputes. The principal statutory framework comprises Section 68 of the CGST Act, 2017, Rules 138 to 138D of the CGST Rules, 2017, and Sections 129 and 130 governing detention and confiscation. Taxpayers and transporters must ensure proper invoices, accurate vehicle particulars, completion of Part B, monitoring of e-way bill validity, timely extensions and preservation of documentary evidence supporting actual movement. The prescribed eight-hour extension window, procedures for vehicle changes and treatment of minor documentary errors are particularly important. CBIC Circular No. 64/38/2018-GST provides specific relief for prescribed minor discrepancies, while Section 129 establishes penalties, procedural timelines and hearing requirements for detention proceedings. The Supreme Court’s decisions in Satyam Shivam Papers and ASP Traders emphasise that expiry of an e-way bill does not automatically establish tax evasion and that payment made under protest to secure release does not eliminate the obligation to pass a reasoned order. The Gujarat High Court’s decision in Panchhi Traders addresses the independent operation of Sections 129 and 130, while Dinesh Kumar Pradeep Kumar examines confiscation proceedings involving alleged excess stock. Decisions in LGW Industries, Gargo Traders and Ecom Gill Coffee Trading highlight the significance of genuine transaction records, supplier verification and evidence of actual delivery of goods. The essential principle is that procedural mistakes, genuine transport difficulties, disputed purchases and deliberate tax evasion must be examined separately, with enforcement action based on statutory requirements, reliable evidence, procedural fairness and proportionate legal consequences. Introduction: a transport difficulty should not become a finding of tax evasion A manufacturer may correctly issue a tax invoice, generate an e-way bill, load the goods and engage a genuine transporter. Yet the journey may not proceed as planned. A vehicle may suffer engine failure. The diesel tank may develop a leak. A highway may be closed. The driver may fall ill. Goods may have to be transferred to another vehicle. By the time the difficulty is resolved, the e-way bill may have expired. These are ordinary transport risks. They nevertheless create serious compliance questions under GST. The answer cannot be that every transport difficulty excuses non-compliance. Equally, an expired document or a minor mistake cannot, without examination of the circumstances, establish a fraudulent transaction. In Assistant Commissioner (ST) & Ors. v. Satyam Shivam Papers Pvt. Ltd. & Anr., the Supreme Court rejected an inference of evasion drawn merely from expiry of the e-way bill where the delay arose from circumstances beyond the taxpayer’s control. The governing principle should be proportionality: identify the actual contravention, examine the explanation, apply the correct provision and impose only the consequence authorised by law. The difficulty faced by trade is not confined to roadside interception. Information about an allegedly non-existent supplier or retrospective cancellation of a supplier’s registration may subsequently lead to investigation, ITC proceedings or a proposal for confiscation. These situations require separate legal analysis. A disputed purchase, a defective transport document and a confessable consignment are not interchangeable concepts. This article examines the duties of the taxpayer, manufacturer, transporter and driver, and the corresponding limits on enforcement. 2. Understand the legal structure before the vehicle starts The principal framework consists of Section 68 of the CGST Act and Rules 138 to 138D of the CGST Rules. Broadly: Provision Subject Section 68 Inspection of goods in movement and production of prescribed documents Rule 138 Information before movement, generation and validity of e-way bills Rule 138A Documents and devices to be carried by the person in charge Rule 138B Verification of documents and conveyances Rule 138C Inspection and verification reports Rule 138D Reporting detention exceeding thirty minutes Section 129 Detention, seizure and release of goods and conveyances in transit Section 130 Confiscation where the prescribed statutory conditions are satisfied These provisions form a connected framework, but they perform different functions. Section 129 addresses contraventions involving goods in transit. Section 130 creates a separate confiscation power subject to its own ingredients and hearing requirement. An officer’s authority to verify a vehicle does not remove the obligation to issue the appropriate notice, consider the response and pass a reasoned order. Conversely, possession of a genuine invoice does not excuse every failure to comply with the transport rules. 3. When is an e-way bill required? Under the general rule, a registered person causing movement of goods of consignment value exceeding ₹50,000 must furnish the prescribed information before movement. The movement may be: In relation to a supply; For reasons other than supply; or Due to inward supply from an unregistered person. Accordingly, the requirement is not limited to sales. Stock movements, goods sent for processing, repair or other non-sale purposes must also be examined under the rules. The applicable exemptions and State-specific notifications must be checked for the particular movement. The ₹50,000 threshold should not be applied mechanically to taxable value alone. Rule 138 explains that consignment value includes the GST and cess charged in the document, subject to its specific treatment of exempt goods in a mixed invoice. Illustration 1: taxable value below ₹50,000 A trader issues an invoice for taxable goods valued at ₹48,000, with GST of ₹8,640. The invoice value is ₹56,640. Assuming no exemption or other special provision applies, the consignment exceeds the general threshold. The trader should not conclude that an e-way bill is unnecessary merely because the taxable value is ₹48,000. Certain movements require an e-way bill irrespective of value. An important example is goods sent by a principal in one State or Union territory to a job worker in another State or Union territory, as provided in Rule 138. 4. The invoice, e-way bill and actual goods must tell the same story An e-way bill is not a substitute for the underlying tax invoice, bill of supply or delivery challan, as applicable. The transport documents should consistently identify: The actual supplier and recipient; The invoice or challan number and date; The goods, quantity and value; The place of dispatch and delivery; The transporter; and The vehicle carrying the goods. Rule 138A governs the prescribed documents carried by the person in charge. The departmental interception procedure also recognises electronic verification of the e-way bill number; a printed e-way bill is not the only recognised means of producing that number. The taxpayer should maintain one reliable dispatch record rather than several inconsistent records generated independently by the accounts department, warehouse and transporter. Illustration 2: correct invoice, wrong dispatch information Manufacturer invoices goods from Mysuru to a buyer in Bengaluru. The goods are actually dispatched from a separate warehouse. If the e-way bill incorrectly shows dispatch from the registered office, questions may arise about the route and loading point. The correct practice is to record the actual transaction and movement details at the outset. A later oral explanation by the driver is a poor substitute for accurate documents. 5. Part A is not ordinarily enough for road movement A recurring mistake is to prepare Part A and assume that the goods may proceed on that basis. Rule 138 states that an e-way bill is not valid for movement by road unless Part B information has been furnished, except for the specific movements covered by the prescribed exceptions. There is a limited relaxation for movement up to fifty kilometres within a State or Union territory from the consignor’s place of business to the transporter’s place of business for further transportation. This is a relaxation concerning conveyance particulars in the specified circumstances. It is not a general exemption from e-way bills for every journey below fifty kilometres. Illustration 3: the fifty-kilometre misunderstanding A trader sends goods worth ₹3 lakh directly to a customer thirty kilometres away. He cannot claim a blanket e-way bill exemption merely because the journey is below fifty kilometres. The particular relaxation for delivery to a transporter must not be confused with direct delivery to the customer. Before dispatch, the person responsible should verify that the vehicle actually standing at the loading point matches the vehicle particulars entered in the e-way bill. 6. Documents concerning the vehicle and driver Two separate compliance folders should be maintained. GST and consignment folder This should contain, as applicable: Tax invoice, bill of supply or delivery challan; E-way bill number and accessible copy; Lorry receipt or goods consignment note; Dispatch and delivery addresses; Transporter details; Relevant permits or supporting documents for the particular goods; and Contact details of the consignor, consignee and person handling GST compliance. The prescribed GST carriage requirements arise principally under Rule 138A. Additional commercial records strengthen verification but should not all be described as documents compulsorily carried under that rule. Vehicle and driver folder As a practical transport-control measure, the owner should verify the applicable driving licence, vehicle registration, insurance, fitness certificate, permit and pollution certificate requirements before assigning the journey. These requirements arise under the motor-vehicle framework, not merely because GST applies. Their validity should be checked separately for the vehicle, route and cargo. A GST objection should identify the GST contravention relied upon. Equally, the transporter should not assume that correct GST documents cure defects under transport law. The driver should be instructed to state facts within his knowledge. He should not be expected to explain the supplier’s entire tax compliance or make admissions about accounting matters that he does not understand. 7. Validity: use the expiry shown on the portal For ordinary cargo, the e-way bill system applies the two-hundred-kilometre-per-day validity framework introduced from 1 January 2021. Special categories, including over-dimensional cargo, require separate examination under Rule 138(10). Old material referring generally to one hundred kilometres per day should not be used for current ordinary-cargo journeys. The practical instruction is simple: record the actual “valid up to” date and time shown on the generated e-way bill. Businesses should not leave expiry monitoring entirely to the driver. The dispatch office or transporter should maintain an expiry register with: E-way bill number; Vehicle number; Destination; Current location; Expiry date and time; Estimated arrival; and Person responsible for extension. The official system provides facilities for viewing e-way bills approaching expiry. These facilities should form part of daily transport supervision. 8. Vehicle breakdown: what should be done immediately? A breakdown should trigger a documented compliance response, not merely a telephone call to a mechanic. The following steps are advisable: Inform the consignor, consignee and transporter immediately. Record the location, date and time of breakdown. Photograph the vehicle and the defect where practicable. Preserve workshop job cards, repair bills, towing receipts and mechanic communications. Check the remaining e-way bill validity. Decide whether repair or transshipment is required. Arrange the appropriate Part B update or validity extension before further movement. These records help explain why the goods remained at a particular location and why the original journey could not be completed. Rule 138 and the official user guidance provide the mechanisms for updating transport particulars and extending validity in exceptional circumstances. Illustration 4: engine failure before expiry A lorry carrying machinery breaks down at 6 p.m. The e-way bill expires at midnight. The workshop confirms that repairs will take two days. The transporter should not wait until the repairs are finished. It should monitor the extension window, record the actual location and reason, and use the extension facility within the permissible time. Repair documentation should be retained with the consignment records. An engine failure or diesel-tank defect does not itself extend the electronic validity. Evidence of the breakdown supports the explanation, but the extension procedure must still be followed wherever available. 9. Extension of validity: the eight-hour window matters Official e-way bill guidance permits extension within the window beginning eight hours before expiry and ending eight hours after expiry. Rule 138 expressly provides for extension within eight hours from expiry. The official user manual identifies the present transporter as the person who can extend the e-way bill. The extension entry should accurately state the reason, current location and remaining journey particulars required by the portal. Two important distinctions must be understood: The post-expiry extension facility is not permission to continue transporting goods under an expired e-way bill. An email to an officer or a portal grievance does not, by itself, extend the e-way bill. If the remaining journey cannot be completed, the safest course is to address validity before resuming movement rather than rely on an explanation after interception. Illustration 5: breakdown at midnight An e-way bill expires at midnight. The vehicle remains stationary at a workshop. At 5 a.m., the responsible transporter completes the permitted extension process and obtains the updated validity. The extension record and workshop evidence should be preserved. The vehicle should proceed only after checking that the document is valid and the vehicle particulars are correct. 10. What if the extension window has already closed? This is one of the most difficult practical situations. Once the permitted extension window is missed, the taxpayer should not assume that the expired e-way bill can be revived by an ordinary letter. Nor should a fresh invoice be fabricated to conceal the original journey. The official system imposes additional validations: generation is restricted where the base document is more than 180 days old, and extension is capped at 360 days from original generation. These are portal validations and should not be confused with a general entitlement to move goods for those periods. Where extension is no longer available: Stop onward movement until the document position is assessed. Preserve the original invoice, expired e-way bill and breakdown evidence. Raise a portal grievance and retain the acknowledgment. Communicate the complete facts to the jurisdictional officer and, where appropriate, the local enforcement authority. Examine whether a fresh e-way bill can lawfully be generated on the existing genuine document under the applicable portal functionality and rules. Obtain professional advice where the portal rejects generation or the movement history creates ambiguity. A fresh e-way bill, even if the system permits it, does not retrospectively erase an earlier lapse. The original movement and reason for interruption must remain transparent. The taxpayer should not treat the generation of another number as a universal cure. Where no workable portal route exists, urgent written administrative assistance may be necessary. A grievance acknowledgment is evidence of the attempt to comply; it is not a substitute transport authorisation. 11. Transshipment: changing the vehicle does not mean changing the transaction Where goods must be transferred to another vehicle, the relevant transport particulars should be updated in accordance with Rule 138 before further movement. Merely updating the vehicle number does not restart the original validity period; validity and vehicle particulars are separate matters. Illustration 6: damaged diesel tank A truck carrying scrap develops a serious diesel-tank leak. The transporter arranges another truck. Before resuming the journey, it should: Record the reason and place of transshipment; Preserve photographs and repair evidence; Reconcile the quantity transferred; Update the new vehicle particulars; and Check whether validity also requires extension. If there is a shortage, damage or partial unloading, record it contemporaneously. A later discrepancy between the invoice quantity and the physical goods may otherwise create an avoidable dispute. In M/s ASP Traders v. State of Uttar Pradesh & Ors., the Supreme Court proceedings arose from a consignment involving transshipment, missing bags and allegations concerning the parties’ particulars. The eventual ruling concerned the obligation to pass a reasoned order; the case illustrates why the physical movement record must be maintained carefully. 12. Roadside checking: cooperation and procedure must go together The driver should cooperate with lawful verification, produce the prescribed documents and promptly contact the consignor or transporter. At the same time, the enforcement process should leave a clear written record. CBIC Circular No. 41/15/2018-GST dated 13 April 2018 prescribes the interception and inspection framework. Relevant forms include: Form Principal purpose GST MOV-01 Statement of person in charge GST MOV-02 Order for physical verification GST MOV-04 Physical verification report GST MOV-05 Release order GST MOV-06 Detention order GST MOV-07 Notice under Section 129 GST MOV-09 Order determining the demand GST MOV-10 Notice proposing confiscation GST MOV-11 Confiscation order The circular must be read with subsequent statutory amendments. Its original references to tax-and-penalty amounts and automatic progression to Section 130 should not be reproduced as though the law remained unchanged. The taxpayer should immediately record the interception time, location, officer’s details, documents produced and copies of forms served. Under Rule 138D, where interception and detention exceed thirty minutes, the transporter may upload the information in Form GST EWB-04. This creates a contemporaneous record; it does not automatically invalidate detention. 13. Minor errors: Parliament did not create confiscation for every typing mistake CBIC Circular No. 64/38/2018-GST dated 14 September 2018 addresses specified minor discrepancies where the consignment is accompanied by an invoice and e-way bill. The listed situations include: Spelling mistakes in names where GSTIN is correct; Certain PIN-code errors not increasing validity; Certain address errors where the locality is correct; Errors in one or two digits of the document number; Specified HSN errors where the first two digits and tax rate are correct; and Errors in one or two digits or characters of the vehicle number. For these specified situations, the circular directs against initiation of Section 129 proceedings and prescribes a nominal penalty of ₹500 each under the CGST and corresponding SGST Acts, or ₹1,000 under the IGST Act, as applicable. Illustration 7: one incorrect vehicle digit The invoice, supplier, recipient, goods, quantity and destination are correct. One digit in the vehicle number has been entered incorrectly. The taxpayer should identify the precise clause of the circular and seek its application. But a substantially different vehicle number, unexplained change of vehicle or other material discrepancy should not automatically be described as a one-digit clerical error. The circular’s protection is specific. It does not cover every defect that a taxpayer chooses to call “technical.” 14. Expired e-way bills: the importance and limits of Satyam Shivam Papers In Satyam Shivam Papers Pvt. Ltd. v. Assistant Commissioner (ST) & Ors., W.P. No. 9688 of 2020, decided on 2 June 2021, the Telangana High Court examined detention after expiry of the e-way bill in circumstances involving traffic obstruction. The explanation was not properly considered, and evasion was inferred merely from expiry. The Court set aside the adverse action. The Supreme Court dismissed the Department’s special leave petition in Assistant Commissioner (ST) & Ors. v. Satyam Shivam Papers Pvt. Ltd. & Anr., SLP (C) No. 21132 of 2021, on 12 January 2022, strongly disapproving the baseless inference and the conduct found in that case. The decision is valuable, but its limits must be respected. It does not abolish e-way bill validity. It does not authorise unrestricted movement under expired documents. Nor does it establish that every Section 129 case necessarily requires proof of the same mental element expressly required by particular clauses of Section 130. Its practical lesson is that the officer must examine the actual explanation and evidence. The taxpayer, in turn, should preserve that evidence instead of relying only on the expression “there was no intention to evade tax.” 15. Section 129: penalty, timelines and hearing Under the amended Section 129: Where the owner comes forward, the penalty for taxable goods is 200% of the tax payable on the goods. Where the owner does not come forward, the penalty is 50% of the goods’ value or 200% of the tax payable, whichever is higher. Separate, lower formulas apply to exempt goods. Security is also contemplated under Section 129(1)(c). Illustration 8: why the owner should respond promptly Goods have a value of ₹10 lakh and applicable tax of ₹1.8 lakh. If the owner comes forward, the statutory penalty formula is ₹3.6 lakh. If the owner does not come forward, the comparison is between ₹5 lakh and ₹3.6 lakh, resulting in ₹5 lakh. This illustration assumes that detention and penalty are otherwise legally sustainable. It does not mean that every interception permits the penalty. Section 129(3) requires notice within seven days of detention or seizure and an order within seven days from service of that notice. Section 129(4) requires an opportunity of hearing before penalty is determined. A hearing is not meaningful if the officer fixes a reply deadline but decides the matter before that deadline expires. The taxpayer should submit objections promptly, request personal hearing expressly and retain proof of submission. 16. Payment for release does not remove the duty to decide objections Goods cannot always remain detained while litigation proceeds. A business may pay to secure release and then challenge the levy. In M/s ASP Traders v. State of Uttar Pradesh & Ors., Civil Appeal No. 9764 of 2025, decided on 24 July 2025, the Supreme Court held that, where objections were filed and payment was made under protest for business reasons, the officer remained bound to pass a speaking order. Payment did not justify depriving the taxpayer of an effective appeal remedy. The practical course is to: File the factual and legal objections in writing. State clearly if payment is made under protest to secure release. Avoid signing a withdrawal of objections unless withdrawal is genuinely intended. Obtain the release order and reasoned adjudication order. Take timely steps under the applicable appeal provisions. The judgment reproduces earlier legislative wording. Its procedural principle should be distinguished from the current penalty rates, which must be taken from the amended statute. 17. Section 129 does not automatically become Section 130 A major source of confusion is the assumption that unpaid detention penalty automatically results in confiscation. The amended Section 129(6) provides for sale or disposal to recover unpaid penalty after fifteen days from receipt of the order, subject to its provisos. The earlier statutory linkage directing further proceedings under Section 130 was replaced. The current provision also contains a separate mechanism for release of the conveyance on payment by the transporter of the prescribed penalty or ₹1 lakh, whichever is less. However, it would be equally incorrect to say that Section 130 can never be invoked independently. In Panchhi Traders v. State of Gujarat, Special Civil Application No. 9250 of 2020 and connected matters, decided on 11 December 2025, the Gujarat High Court examined the post-amendment relationship between the provisions. The Court recognised their independent operation while insisting that confiscation cannot rest on mere suspicion or unsupported assertions. The legally correct questions are: Which clause of Section 130(1) is invoked? What facts satisfy that clause? Where intention to evade is required, what evidence establishes it? What goods or conveyance are proposed to be confiscated? Has the person concerned received the relied-upon material and a hearing? A notice should answer these questions rather than merely reproduce the entire section. 18. Confiscation outside roadside interception: neither a blanket prohibition nor a free power Section 129 concerns goods being transported or stored while in transit. Section 130 is not worded as a power exercisable only when a vehicle is intercepted. Its clauses include receipt or supply in contravention with intent to evade, failure to account for taxable goods, specified unregistered supplies and certain misuse of conveyances. Therefore, a defence based only on “the vehicle was not intercepted” may be insufficient against Section 130. But the absence of interception does not permit the Department to dispense with the statutory ingredients. If the real issue is disputed ITC on historical purchases, the officer must identify why confiscation is attracted, rather than use Section 130 as a substitute for the applicable tax-determination procedure. In S/S Dinesh Kumar Pradeep Kumar v. Additional Commissioner Grade 2 & Anr., Writ Tax No. 1082 of 2022, decided on 25 July 2024, the Allahabad High Court set aside the Section 130 action concerning alleged excess stock and held that the matter required proceedings under the tax-determination provisions on the facts before it. This decision should be applied to comparable facts, not converted into a universal proposition that goods at business premises can never attract confiscation. 19. Retrospective supplier cancellation and third-party information Suppose a purchaser received goods, recorded them in stock, paid through the bank and used or resold them. Months later, the Department reports that the supplier was non-existent or cancels its registration retrospectively. That information justifies examination. It does not, by itself, answer every question about the purchaser’s transactions. In LGW Industries Limited & Ors. v. Union of India & Ors., WPA No. 23512 of 2019 and connected matters, decided on 13 December 2021, the Calcutta High Court directed examination of the supporting records, transaction dates and genuineness of purchases rather than rejection solely through retrospective cancellation. Similarly, in M/s Gargo Traders v. Joint Commissioner, Commercial Taxes (State Tax) & Ors., WPA No. 1009 of 2022, decided on 12 June 2023, the Court required proper consideration of the purchaser’s documents and a reasoned decision. These decisions support transaction-specific verification. They do not establish unconditional ITC entitlement. For a Section 130 proposal based on third-party information, the recipient should request: The precise statutory clause; The adverse information proposed to be relied upon; The supplier’s relevant cancellation or verification records; Identification of the transactions and goods concerned; The alleged connection between the recipient and the wrongdoing; and A reasonable opportunity to rebut the material. A superior officer’s direction may initiate verification. It cannot replace the adjudicating officer’s own examination of the evidence and the statutory hearing mandated by Section 130(4). 20. A genuine purchaser still needs evidence of genuine movement An active GSTIN, GSTR-2B reflection and bank payment are important, but should not be the entire defence. In State of Karnataka v. M/s Ecom Gill Coffee Trading Private Limited, Civil Appeal No. 230 of 2023 and connected appeals, decided on 13 March 2023, the Supreme Court emphasised, under the Karnataka VAT law, that invoices and payment by cheque alone were insufficient where purchases were disputed. Proof of actual movement and delivery was material. It is not a direct interpretation of CGST Section 16, but its evidentiary lesson is important. For goods such as scrap, practical evidence may include: Lorry receipts and transporter invoices; Weighbridge slips; Loading and unloading records; Goods-receipt entries; Stock records; Vehicle and freight-payment details; Correspondence; and Links to subsequent sale or consumption. An e-way bill records declared movement. Supporting records help establish that the declared movement actually occurred. 21. A workable daily compliance system Every business regularly moving goods should adopt a written transport procedure. Before dispatch Verify the transaction document, GSTINs, dispatch and delivery locations, quantity, vehicle number, Part B completion and validity. Explain the journey to the driver and give him a contact person. During the journey Monitor location and expiry. Require immediate reporting of breakdown, route closure, accident, transshipment or enforcement interception. Assign a backup person who can operate the portal when the accountant is unavailable. When a problem occurs Preserve contemporaneous evidence. Update the vehicle or seek extension within the permissible process. Do not conceal the original transaction by altering dates or creating fictitious documents. After delivery Obtain delivery acknowledgment and reconcile the quantity, freight, invoice, e-way bill and stock entry. These controls do not guarantee that no dispute will arise. They materially improve both compliance and the ability to answer a notice. 22. The Department must educate as well as enforce Taxpayers should not depend on newspaper reports or informal messages for legal compliance. At the same time, a digital compliance system works better when the Department clearly communicates its rules, circulars and portal changes. A useful administrative programme would include local-language guidance for drivers, transport-association meetings, short breakdown-response instructions and clear explanations of the minor-error circular. The Department already has published instructions distinguishing minor errors from cases warranting detention. Consistent application of those instructions is as important as issuing them. Training should also explain the distinction between Section 129 and Section 130. Neither excessive leniency towards fabricated transactions nor excessive severity towards documented transport difficulties serves the revenue. 23. Conclusion: examine the transaction before imposing the consequence The e-way bill system is necessary for monitoring goods in movement. Its credibility depends on accurate compliance by trade and disciplined application by officers. A taxpayer must generate the correct documents, complete vehicle particulars, monitor validity and preserve proof of the journey. A transporter must report difficulties immediately rather than leave the consignor to discover them after detention. A driver must cooperate and give truthful information. The officer’s responsibility is equally clear: identify the actual defect, disclose the proposed action, consider the explanation and pass a reasoned order. The Supreme Court’s decisions in Satyam Shivam Papers and ASP Traders demonstrate why an expired document cannot automatically prove evasion and why commercial payment for release cannot be used to avoid adjudication of written objections. Confiscation requires a separate statutory foundation. It should not follow merely because the taxpayer disputes a penalty, because a superior officer has forwarded an allegation, or because a supplier’s registration has subsequently been cancelled. Equally, a genuine case of fabricated supply or deliberate evasion cannot be defended merely by producing an e-way bill. The sound approach is to preserve the distinction between a mistake, a procedural contravention, an unproved allegation and an established case of evasion. The law authorises different consequences because these are different situations. Fair administration begins by recognising that difference. Cases Discussed M/s ASP Traders v.

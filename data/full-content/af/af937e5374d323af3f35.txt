Vs Commissioner of Kerala SGST (GSTAT Thiruvananthapuram) Summary : In Josco Gold Corporation Private Ltd. Vs Commissioner of Kerala SGST (GSTAT Thiruvananthapuram), the Tribunal examined a demand arising from scrutiny of the jeweller’s GST returns for July 2017 to March 2018. The department alleged escaped turnover of ₹70,29,725 on account of sales returns, with short payment of CGST and SGST of ₹1,05,446 each (₹2,10,892 in total), together with interest and penalty. A notice dated 28 September 2023 proceeded substantially on the premise that no credit notes had been issued. The adjudicating authority confirmed the demand on 29 December 2023 and the Joint Commissioner (Appeals), Kottayam, rejected the assessee’s appeal on 12 June 2025. The appellant maintained that it had issued credit notes for jewellery exchanges, netted relevant B2C transactions in its returns and supplied a sales-return ledger with sample credit notes, while offering the remaining records for verification. It objected that the adjudicating authority subsequently questioned credit-note dates, invoice values and transaction sequencing without putting those allegations in the show-cause notice or providing a personal hearing. The Revenue argued that genuineness of returns remained the original issue, that samples were insufficient and that the credit notes did not meet Section 34 requirements, particularly for pre-GST invoices, larger credit-note values and alleged buy-back transactions. The first appellate authority had additionally relied on reporting delays, annual-return omissions and perceived improbability of very quick jewellery exchanges. The Tribunal found that the foundation of the notice—non-issuance of credit notes—was contradicted by the order-in-original itself, which examined actual credit notes. It held that invoking Section 34 and scrutinising the date and value of those notes introduced grounds not specified in the notice, contrary to Section 75(7) of the CGST/KGST Act. It also found no record of a personal hearing before adjudication, violating Section 75(4). The Tribunal rejected the first appellate authority’s additional reasoning based on assumptions about consumer behaviour and jewellery trade practice, and held that further inquiry under Section 107(11) could not dispense with notice and hearing. Relying particularly on the Supreme Court’s decision in Armour Security (India) Ltd., it distinguished the natural-justice authorities relied upon by the Revenue because actual prejudice was established. The Tribunal held that both orders had been made in breach of the notice’s scope and natural justice, and that examination of the substantive tax merits would therefore be unnecessary. By Final Order No. 20/TVP/KERALAM/2026 dated 5 November 2026, it allowed the appeal, set aside the order-in-appeal and granted consequential relief. It did not finally determine the substantive eligibility of each jewellery-exchange credit note under Section 34. Cases Discussed M/s Armour Security (India) Ltd. Vs Commissioner, CGST, Delhi East Commissionerate & Anr. — [2025] 177 taxmann.com 478 (SC); 2025 INSC 982; SLP (C) No. 6092 of 2025; 14/08/2025 (Supreme Court). The Tribunal relied on its discussion of Section 75(7) and the prohibition against new grounds absent from a show-cause notice. Commissioner of Customs (Import) Vs Dilip Kumar and Company & Ors. — (2018) 9 SCC 1 (Supreme Court). Cited by Revenue for the burden on a person claiming a statutory concession; the Tribunal did not decide the underlying Section 34 merits. Dharampal Satyapal Ltd. Vs Deputy Commissioner of Central Excise — (2015) 8 SCC 519 (Supreme Court). Cited by Revenue concerning prejudice from breach of natural justice; the Tribunal considered it inapplicable on the established facts. Commissioner of Central Excise, Bhubaneswar-I Vs Champdany Industries Ltd. — (2009) 9 SCC 466 (Supreme Court). Quoted through Armour Security for the restriction on building a new Revenue case beyond the notice. Commissioner of Central Excise, Nagpur Vs Ballarpur Industries Ltd. — (2007) 8 SCC 89; MANU/SC/3595/2007 (Supreme Court). Aggrieved by the said order in appeal, the appellant has filed this appeal before the Tribunal. 4.0 Grounds of Appeal / Submissions of the Appellant: a. The Show Cause Notice was confined to the allegation that the Appellant had not issued any credit notes in respect of the sales returns claimed. In response, the Appellant furnished a list of credit notes with sample copies and expressly stated that the complete set was available for verification on requisition. The Adjudicating Authority, without issuing any deficiency notice, without calling for the remaining credit notes, and without granting any further opportunity of hearing proceeded to confirm the demand on entirely new technical grounds relating to the value and sequencing of credit notes vis-à-vis the original invoices — grounds not contained in the SCN. The completion of adjudication on grounds not stated in the SCN and without affording an opportunity to respond to those grounds is a fundamental breach of the principle of audi alteram partem. The Order-in-Original is accordingly void and liable to be set aside on this ground alone. b. It is a settled proposition of law that the Adjudicating Authority is bound by the four corners of the Show Cause Notice and cannot travel beyond its specific averments. The demand confirmed in the Order-in-Original rests on grounds — namely, value differentials between credit notes and invoices, and the sequencing of credit note issuance — that do not feature in the SCN. The demand, to the extent it is based on grounds not notified to the Appellant, is without jurisdiction and void, and is liable to be set aside by this Hon’ble Tribunal. c. Section 34 of the CGST Act provides that a registered person may issue a credit note in respect of a supply already made where: (a) the tax charged exceeds the tax payable; (b) the goods are returned by the recipient; or (c) the goods or services are found to be deficient. The provision uses the discretionary expression “may issue”, indicating a conditional entitlement and not a mandatory obligation in every case of exchange. The exchange transactions effected by the Appellant do not fall squarely within Section 34 for the following reasons: (i) In each exchange, the customer does not return goods for a cash refund or reject the supply — the customer re-selects ornaments of equivalent quantity and value. The original supply, in substance, survives and is merely substituted in kind. (ii) No excess tax is collected from any customer in the exchange transactions. The credit note adjusts only the accounting entry, not the underlying tax liability. (iii) The credit notes have been disclosed in Column 13 of Form GSTR-1 for each relevant month, satisfying the reporting requirement under the CGST framework. 29. (iv) The Appellant, therefore, has not violated Section 34, and the rejection of credit notes on that basis is unsustainable. d. The First Appellate Authority dismissed the appeal on four grounds that were not raised in the Show Cause Notice and were not the basis of the Order-in-Original: non-disclosure within the time limit under Section 34; non-reflection in annual returns; simultaneous generation of credit notes and invoices; and improbability of exchanges within a few days. It is well-settled that an appellate authority, even while exercising suo motu powers, cannot confirm an adverse order on new grounds without putting the assessee to notice and affording a hearing. The failure to do so constitutes a fundamental breach of natural justice and renders the Order- in-Appeal non-est in law. e. (i) The finding that credit notes were not declared within the prescribed time is factually incorrect. The credit notes were declared in Column 13 of Form GSTR-1 for each relevant month of F.Y. 2017-18. The nonappearance in Table 5H of Form GSTR-9 is a consequence of the reporting format of the annual return and does not constitute non-disclosure of the credit notes. (ii) As to simultaneous issuance of credit notes and invoices, the finding that credit notes were generated on the same date as original invoices is factually incorrect. A reference to Annexure – I demonstrates that in each exchange transaction, the credit note and the fresh invoice are raised on a date subsequent to the original invoice date, typically one to several days later, which is entirely consistent with the exchange process described above. (iii) As to improbability of exchange within a few days, the finding that it is “unbelievable” that customers would exchange ornaments within three to four days due to loss of making charges is misconceived. In the Appellant’s case, returned ornaments are accepted as new gold, not old gold, and no making charges are imposed on the customer. This is standard practice in organized retail jewellery, and the inference of improbability drawn without any factual basis is unsustainable. (iv) Each credit note issued by the Appellant is correlated to a specific original sale invoice issued by the Appellant itself. The provenance of the returned ornaments is established by the documentary chain and is not in dispute. f. The exchange transactions in retail gold jewellery are fundamentally different from conventional sales returns that Section 34 is designed to address. The following distinguishing features are relevant: (i) No quantity is finally returned or removed from the supply chain — the customer exits the transaction with equivalent or higher quantity of new gold ornaments. (ii) No cash refund is made — the credit note value is fully absorbed against the fresh invoice. (iii) The returned ornaments are accepted as new gold and are immediately replaced with new ornaments of the selected design. No depreciation, making charge loss, or quality degradation is involved. (iv) The credit notes serve purely as an accounting mechanism to avoid duplication of turnover — the original invoice is cancelled by the credit note and the selected ornaments are billed under the fresh invoice. In these circumstances, the demand of tax by treating the exchange as an escapement of turnover mischaracterizes the nature of the transaction and is not sustainable in law. g. With regard to the specific findings of the adjudicating authority, i. The Adjudicating Authority mistook Invoice No. NMD-253 as the original invoice. In fact, the original invoice is Invoice No. NMD-7960 dated 30.06.2017 for Rs. 5,383.63/-. The customer returned the ornament purchased under Invoice NMD-7960 and re-selected an ornament of lesser value (Rs. 4,416.72/-). Credit Note No. NMD-7 dated 05.07.2017 for Rs. 5,334.52/- was accordingly issued against Invoice NMD-7960, and fresh Invoice No. NMD-253 was raised for Rs. 4,416.72/-. The credit note value is less than the original invoice value — there is no excess credit note. ii. Where the credit note is less than the original invoice value, this arises from cases where the customer exchanged only a portion of the ornaments originally purchased, retaining the balance. For example, under Invoice No. NMD-7176 dated 09.09.2017 (total value Rs. 22,515.54/-), only ornaments worth Rs. 12,835.66/- were returned. Credit Note No. NMD-224 dated 11.09.2017 was issued for the returned quantity, and fresh Invoice No. NMD-7285 dated 11.09.2017 was raised for Rs. 12,515.28/-. The undisturbed balance of the original invoice remains billed and taxed. There is no irregularity. Champdany Industries Ltd – para 50 )] b. if there is no invocation of the concerned rules in the show-cause notice, it would not be open to the Commissioner to invoke the said Rule.” [Commissioner of Central Excise, Nagpur v.Ballarpur Industries Ltd. MANU/SC/3595/2007 :] h. The Court went on to state that: From the above exposition of law, we can safely conclude that a show cause notice delineates the scope of the proceedings in the expression of subject matter with which the authority would be dealing. It would be impermissible for an authority to invoke such rules, claims or grounds at a later stage which do not figure in the show cause notice. That is to say, any ground, reasoning or claim which does not figure out in the show cause notice cannot be permitted to adversely affect the noticee. Such recognition has even been made statutorily, as per sub-section (7) of Section 75 of the Act, which reads ……..as thus: f. On the issue of the OIO / OIA relying on grounds extraneous to the SCN we find that the learned Revenue has contended as follows: (i) The comments on values and dates are reasons drawn from the Appellant’s own sample credit notes. (ii) Weighing a reply is not a new ground. (iii) The Appellant had a personal hearing date (18.10.2023), filed three replies, and got two reminders. (iv) The OIO records that the transaction-wise statement called for was not produced. (v) The burden was on the Appellant to produce all its credit notes. It cannot file samples, keep the rest back, and then blame the officer for not asking again. (vi) The first appellate authority examined this plea and found no deviation from the SCN (OIA para 29). No prejudice is shown. The Appellant has answered every point in two appeals. (vii) Natural justice is not a ritual, and a breach must cause real prejudice (State Bank of Patiala v. S.K. Sharma, (1996) 3 SCC 364; Dharampal Satyapal Ltd. v. Deputy Commissioner of Central Excise, (2015) 8 SCC 519). (viii) Under Section 107(11), the appellate authority may confirm an order after such further inquiry as needed. It must give its own reasons. (ix) The reasons in OIA para 33 answer the Appellant’s own pleas and the statement of 263 credit notes it filed in that appeal. The Appellant was heard on 04.02.2025 through its Chartered Accountant. (x) Same day credit notes were a finding of the OIO itself. (xi) The improbability of quick exchanges is an assessment of the Appellant’s own evidence, not a new charge. (xii) The core finding, that genuine sales returns were not proved, is the same in the SCN, the OIO and the OIA. The demand stands on that finding, whatever view is taken of the OIA’s remarks on reporting time limits. (xiii) In any case, this Tribunal now hears the matter on facts and law. The Appellant has full opportunity here. g. We provide underneath our analysis / comments on each of the above contentions: (i) The fact that values and dates are reasons drawn from the Appellant’s own sample credit notes, does not give any license to the adjudicating authority to make these as fresh grounds against the appellant. Even if they wanted to use these contentions against the appellant, the minimum that is expected of the adjudicating authority is to put the tax payer on notice with respect to these new grounds ( whatever be their provenance), at least during the personal hearing. Unfortunately, we find that in this case no personal hearing was conducted wherein the tax payer could have been confronted with these new grounds. We find that a personal hearing is an opportunity not only for the tax payer to present his oral defense, but it also provides an opportunity for a fair and unbiased adjudicating authority to clarify his doubts regarding the case. By not conducting a personal hearing, not only was this valuable opportunity lost, there was also a clear violation of Section 75(4) of the CGST Act / SGST Act which reads as follows: An opportunity of hearing shall be granted where a request is received in writing from the person chargeable with tax or penalty, or where any adverse decision is contemplated against such person. (ii) We also find that the order in original went about sustaining a case against the appellant on grounds which were not in the SCN, thus clearly violating section 75(7) of the CGST Act which clearly and unambiguously states that no demand shall be confirmed on the grounds other than the grounds specified in the notice.” (iii) We observe that the instant case the appellant came to know of the fresh grounds (which were formulated against him behind his back, without putting him on any kind of notice) only after issue of the adjudication order. h. We therefore find that the order in original is liable to be held null and void for the following reasons: It was passed in total violation of all canons of natural justice It was passed in violation of the specific provisions of section 75(4) and 75(7) of the KGST / CGST Act. (i) We also find that the Revenue has contended that the OIO records that the transaction-wise statement called for was not produced. the burden was on the Appellant to produce all its credit notes. It cannot file samples, keep the rest back, and then blame the officer for not asking again. (j) We find that OIO itself records the undertaking of the tax payer that they are ready to provide the details of credit notes if any selected for verification. This would imply that if the adjudication officer had called for all the credit notes, the appellant was bound to produce the same. There is no record of the adjudicating officer having called for production of all the credit notes and the appellant refusing to produce the same. For the Revenue to contend that the appellant cannot file samples , keep the rest back and then blame the officer for not asking again smacks of a peevish and churlish behavior, which is certainly not expected in this day and age when tax payers services are a critical part of the revenue’s charter of duties. (k) Revenue has also contended that the first appellate authority found no deviation from the SCN (OIA para 29). No prejudice is shown. The Appellant has answered every point in two appeals. (l) On examination of the SCN and OIO we find that one of the important findings of the OIO is as follows: While verifying actual sales invoices with corresponding credit notes , all of the credit notes are issued on the same day of the actual sales invoices and sales return amount claimed by the taxpayer are also adjusted in the same invoices on the same date. It means that before issuing sales invoices , credit notes are generated and that amount adjusted in the actual sales invoices . We find that these allegations never put to the appellant at any stage and were made known them only after issuance of the OIO. We therefore find that notwithstanding the clean chit given by the OIA, the OIO has been vitiated by extraneous grounds not put to the appellant at any stage. This not only prejudiced their defense, but also was a clear violation of section 75(7) of the CGST Act as discussed above. (m). In the light of our findings that the appellant was severely prejudiced on account of confirmation of the demand on grounds which were made known to them only after the issue of the OIO, we find that the case laws cited by the Revenue (State Bank of Patiala v. S.K. Sharma, Dharampal Satyapal Ltd., et al) have no application to the issue under consideration. We find that the ratio adopted by the honorable Apex Court in the case of Armour Security supra, which specifically cites section 75(7) of the CGST Act is more appropriate to the current case and is squarely in favor of the appellant . (n) Revenue has also contended that ‘Under Section 107(11), the appellate authority may confirm an order after such further inquiry as needed. It must give its own reasons. “ (o) We find that the section reads as follows: The Appellate Authority shall, after making such further inquiry as may be necessary, pass such order, as it thinks just and proper, confirming, modifying or annulling the decision or order appealed against but shall not refer the case back to the adjudicating authority that passed the said decision or order: (p) While we wholeheartedly agree with the contention that under Section 107(11) the Appellate may confirm the order after such further enquiry as it thinks and just and proper in our opinion, any further enquiry conducted by the Appellate Authority has also to be done strictly within the framework of natural justice and the appellate authority is not permitted to enter any finding without first putting it to the appellant and recording their contentions thereof and pass a speaking order incorporating findings arrived at based on the discussions / analysis of the above. (q) We find that the Appellate Authority has made the following some findings which are dehors the grounds set out in the SCN : non-disclosure within the time limit under Section 34; non-reflection in annual returns; simultaneous generation of credit notes and invoices; and improbability of exchanges within a few days. ( r ) If it is the contention of the Revenue that these findings are based on further inquiry in terms of section 107(11) of the Act, we are unable to accept such a contention as we find no evidence to the effect that the Appellate Authority has arrived at these conclusions based on any inquiry in terms of section 107(11). As already stated by us, any such enquiry will have to be conducted in keeping with the principles of natural justice, after due notice to the appellant and recording their submissions with respect to the above. In the absence of any such discussion in the order in appeal, we find that section 107(11) has no application in the instant case. (s ) We find that the Revenue has further contended that same day credit notes were a finding of the OIO itself. We find that the order in appeal has to confine itself to the grounds made in the SCN and just because a finding on a particular ground (not contained in the SCN) figures in the OIO, it does not attain any sanctity. In fact it is the bounden duty of the appellate authority to rectify / remedy such errors found in the OIO during the appeal proceedings. In this case, the order in appeal has not rectified / remedied the errors in the OIO, but regrettably compounded the same by adding its own share of findings on grounds dehors the SCN. (t) Revenue further states that the improbability of quick exchanges is an assessment of the Appellant’s own evidence, not a new charge. We find that no basis whatsoever as to how this so called ‘assessment “ was arrived at. (r) We also find that the Revenue has contended that core finding, that genuine sales returns were not proved, is the same in the SCN, the OIO and the OIA. The demand stands on that finding, whatever view is taken of the OIA’s remarks on reporting time limits. We reproduce the paragraph in question form the OIA as follows: 33. But in the instant case, the appellant has not declared the details of the credit notes within the time limit prescribed in Section 34. The credit notes were claimed as issued throughout the year. The appellant has not 7 declared the details of credit notes in the returns of the said months or before the 30th September 2018. The appellant has not declared the credit note details even in the annual return or in Form GSTR 9C filed for the year 2017-18 filed on 26.12.2019, i.e. after 30th September 2018. Further the respondent in the impugned order stated that, while verifying actual sale invoices with corresponding credit notes produced, all the credit notes are issued on the same day of the actual sale invoices and sales return amount claimed by the taxpayer are also adjusted in the same invoices on the same date which means that before issuing sale invoices, credit notes are generated and that amount adjusted in the actual sales turnover. (s) On a perusal of the above, it is seen that the above is not a stray / harmless remark, but one with which the OIA seeks to discredit the credit notes submitted by the appellant, also relying on similar findings made in the OIO [ on grounds not found in the SCN, which were found to have been made behind the back of the appellant , without providing the statutory personal hearing under section 75(4)]. We therefore find that for the above reasons, the contention of Revenue that “the demand stands, whatever view is taken of the OIA’s remarks on reporting time limits” is completely untenable and misconceived. (t) In response to the appellants contentions regarding not being provided a proper opportunity to present their case, Revenue has contended that “In any case, this Tribunal now hears the matter on facts and law. The Appellant has full opportunity here.” (u) We are amazed by these contentions which seem to imply that the fact that the appellant is provided full opportunity at the Tribunal somehow compensates for the lack of such opportunities at the lower fora. In our view, it is the vested legal right of the appellant to be provided full opportunity to present his case at every stage of the adjudication proceedings and any contention to the effect that any lack of opportunity at the lower level can be condoned on the ground that “in any case Tribunal hears the matter on facts and law” is foreign to all known principles of jurisprudence and cannot be countenanced even to the slightest degree. We are disappointed that the Revenue even thought it fit to raise such a contention in the first place and reject the same with the disdain it deserves. (v) To sum up, we find that in the instant case, a. The order in original and order in appeal have been passed in a totally egregious manner, dehors the contents of the SCN, in total violation of the principles of natural justice as well as the specific provisions of section 75(7) of the CGST / KGST Act b. The order in original also has been passed in clear violation of the provisions of section 75(4) of the CGST / KGST Act We find that as the orders in question have not passed the minimum threshold of being in conformity with the principles of natural justice and as they have also been passed in clear violation of statutory provisions, they are liable to be quashed and declared null and void on these grounds alone and any further examination on the merits of the issue would be otiose and an exercise in futility. 7. In view of the above findings, the following order is passed. The appeal is allowed and the order in appeal is set aside, with consequential relief.

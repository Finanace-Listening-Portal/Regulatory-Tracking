The scale of the mobilisation is unprecedented compared with the roughly $26 billion raised through the 2013 FCNR(B) scheme. The window was originally scheduled to remain open until September 30 but was closed on August 31 after inflows exceeded expectations.The inflows have strengthened India's external liquidity position at a time when the rupee has faced pressure from foreign portfolio outflows, geopolitical tensions and elevated crude prices. As the FCNR(B) deposits represent liabilities that mature in three to five years, the reserve accretion also creates future dollar repayment obligations. The risks extend from external debt and the current account to the RBI's balance sheet, domestic liquidity and future foreign-exchange management. Advt Large future dollar repayment obligationsThe biggest risk is that the FCNR(B) inflows are not permanent additions to India's external resources. The deposits mobilised under the special window have maturities of three to five years and will ultimately have to be repaid unless they are rolled over or replaced by other foreign currency inflows.The analysis underlying the scheme estimates that existing FCNR(B) deposits of $33.756 billion, combined with the new mobilisation, could take the stock of FCNR(B) liabilities substantially higher. However, the $132.98 billion figure represents mobilisation under the special scheme and should not be treated as the current outstanding stock of all FCNR(B) deposits.Record forward-dollar liabilitiesThe FCNR(B) scheme transfers the exchange-rate risk on the principal to the RBI through the swap arrangement. The central bank receives dollars from banks initially and commits to provide the dollars back at the agreed exchange rate when the deposits mature.The RBI's net forward dollar liabilities reached a record $200 billion in August, according to current reporting. The forward position reflects the broader foreign currency inflows under the special arrangements, including FCNR(B), overseas foreign currency borrowings and external commercial borrowings, rather than FCNR(B) alone. Advt Interest payments could add pressure to the current accountThe special FCNR(B) deposits were made attractive through interest rates substantially above historical levels. The analysis estimates annual interest payments of around $8.5 billion on the deposits, against roughly $5 billion of potential income if the corresponding reserves earn around 4%.On those assumptions, the interest differential could create a net external cost of around $3.5 billion a year. Over the life of the deposits, this could add around $15 billion to the external-sector burden.These are analytical estimates rather than official RBI projections, but they highlight a potential cost of raising foreign currency through relatively expensive deposits. Advt Sterilisation could impose a cost on the RBIThe swaps generated more than Rs11 lakh crore of rupee liquidity for the banking system. The RBI has had to absorb a substantial part of this liquidity through instruments including variable-rate reverse repos and the Standing Deposit Facility. The surplus has subsequently declined as the RBI has used foreign-exchange operations, government securities sales and liquidity-management operations. Nevertheless, interest paid on liquidity-absorption facilities represents a cost to the central bank and can ultimately reduce the surplus available for transfer to the government. Advt Excess liquidity could put pressure on bank marginsThe large inflow of rupee liquidity gives banks additional funds that need to be deployed. If loan demand does not absorb the surplus quickly, banks may park funds in short-term government securities and other liquid instruments. At the same time, competition for borrowers could increase if banks seek to deploy the excess liquidity. This could put pressure on lending rates and margins, even though the deposits provide banks with stable funding over three to five years.The rise in reserves comes with a corresponding increase in foreign currency liabilitiesThe reserve increase needs to be viewed alongside the liabilities created by the deposits and other foreign currency borrowing. India's external debt already stood at $762.8 billion, or 20.8% of GDP, according to the data cited in the analysis.The additional foreign currency liabilities under the special arrangements would increase the country's overall external debt burden. The reserve increase therefore improves the immediate liquidity position but does not eliminate the underlying obligation to repay the borrowed foreign currency.Maturity concentration could create pressure in 2029-31The three-to-five-year maturity structure means the external-sector burden will become more significant as these deposits approach maturity. Around $53 billion could fall due in 2029-30 and another $62 billion around mid-2031. These figures are estimates from the analysis, rather than an official RBI maturity schedule As the deposits move into their final year, they would also increase the share of short-term external debt measured on a residual-maturity basis, potentially increasing the demand for foreign exchange during the repayment period.The RBI may need extensive future FX managementThe scale of the current mobilisation is far larger than the 2013 FCNR(B) scheme. During the earlier episode, the RBI spent around two years building a forward position ahead of the 2016 repayment of about $26 billion of deposits. The present mobilisation is several times larger. Managing the eventual repayment could therefore require substantial forward-market operations, reserve management and coordination between spot and forward foreign-exchange operations. The RBI's current record forward position demonstrates that this management process is already underway.The balance-of-payments benefit could reverse when the deposits matureThe FCNR(B) scheme provides an immediate balance-of-payments benefit by bringing foreign currency into the country. It also strengthens the RBI's ability to manage episodes of pressure on the rupee. However, the benefit is partly temporary because the deposits are liabilities. When principal and interest are eventually repaid, foreign currency will flow out unless the obligations are rolled over or offset by exports, remittances, capital inflows or other foreign-exchange earnings. The scheme therefore shifts part of the external financing requirement from the present to the future.More complex trade-offs for reserve and rupee managementThe record inflows have given the RBI additional foreign-exchange resources, but they have simultaneously increased the complexity of managing reserves, forward positions, domestic liquidity and future repayment obligations. The RBI has already had to balance the impact of the inflows against excess rupee liquidity in the banking system and its enlarged forward dollar position. As the deposits approach maturity, the central bank will also have to manage the potential demand for dollars without allowing the repayment cycle to generate unnecessary volatility in the rupee or the domestic financial system.The FCNR(B) mobilisation therefore provides significant near-term external-sector support, but its longer-term impact will depend on how effectively the RBI manages the associated liquidity, interest costs, forward positions and maturity-related foreign currency obligations. The key measure of its benefit is not the increase in gross reserves alone, but the net external position after accounting for the liabilities created by the inflows.

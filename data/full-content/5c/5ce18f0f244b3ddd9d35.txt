(Supreme Court of India) Summary: The Supreme Court of India upheld the CESTAT’s decision setting aside a customs duty demand of ₹1,16,09,181 against Ganpati Overseas, arising from alleged undervaluation of imported electronic tuners and saw filters. The Court held that the Customs Department could not enhance the declared transaction value merely on the strength of unattested photocopies of export declarations filed before the Hong Kong customs authorities. The Department was required to establish undervaluation through reliable evidence, particularly when the foreign supplier had subsequently corrected the declarations and the Hong Kong authorities had accepted those corrections. The dispute originated from imports made by M/s Ganpati Overseas, a proprietary concern of Yashpal Sharma, from M/s Arise Enterprises, Hong Kong, during 1997–98 and 1998–99. Following an investigation by the Directorate of Revenue Intelligence (DRI), the Department alleged that twenty consignments of tuners and saw filters had been imported at substantially understated prices. It relied on statements recorded under Section 108 of the Customs Act, 1962, and higher values appearing in the original Hong Kong export declarations. The adjudicating authority consequently confirmed differential customs duty of ₹1,16,09,181 with interest, imposed an equivalent penalty under Section 114A, and levied an additional penalty of ₹5 lakh on Yashpal Sharma under Section 112(a). The CESTAT, Mumbai, set aside the valuation enhancement and penalties on 27 June 2008. It observed that the original export declarations were unattested photocopies and that the foreign supplier had subsequently filed corrected declarations reflecting the prices shown in the Indian import invoices. These corrected declarations were accepted by the Hong Kong customs authorities after imposing a penalty on the supplier. The Tribunal also found that the Department had not produced reliable evidence of higher-priced contemporaneous imports or other corroborative material establishing undervaluation. Conversely, the importer had produced invoices relating to contemporaneous imports by Bharat Electronics and K.S. International showing comparable prices. Examining the evidentiary value of the export declarations, the Supreme Court agreed with the Tribunal that unattested photocopies could not establish undervaluation without proper proof of their authenticity and contents. The Court emphasised that the original declarations had lost their evidentiary foundation after the competent Hong Kong authorities accepted the corrected declarations. The Department had also failed to conduct an investigation with those authorities to establish anything contrary to the importer’s explanation. Consequently, the original export declarations could not constitute a reliable basis for rejecting the declared import value and determining a higher assessable value under the Customs Valuation (Determination of Price of Imported Goods) Rules, 1988. Regarding the statements recorded under Section 108, the Supreme Court explained that statements made before customs officers are admissible in evidence because customs officers are not police officers for the purposes of the relevant provisions of the Evidence Act. However, admissibility does not automatically establish reliability. The Court considered the principles laid down in K.I. Pavunny v. Assistant Collector, (1997) 3 SCC 721, concerning retracted confessions and the importance of corroboration. In the present case, the statements of Yashpal Sharma and Suresh Chandra Sharma had been retracted, allegations of coercion had been raised, and independent evidence supporting the alleged undervaluation was lacking. The Court therefore found no fault with the Tribunal’s refusal to rely upon those statements. The Supreme Court ultimately concluded that the Revenue had failed to discharge its burden of proving undervaluation through legally acceptable and reliable evidence. The Tribunal’s decision was consistent with the principles governing customs valuation, evidentiary requirements and the assessment of retracted statements. Accordingly, the Court upheld the CESTAT order dated 27 June 2008, sustaining the deletion of the enhanced customs valuation, differential duty demand and associated penalties. Both departmental civil appeals were dismissed, with no order as to costs. Cases Discussed State of Punjab Vs.

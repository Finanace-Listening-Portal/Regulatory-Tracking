Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); EMC Limited Vs State Tax Officer (Madras High Court) Summary: The Madras High Court, Madurai Bench, considered EMC Limited’s challenge to a GST demand for the tax period 2021–2022 following its acquisition as a going concern in liquidation by Salasar Techno Engineering Limited. The order dated 30.12.2025, issued under Section 73 of the respective GST enactments pursuant to a DRC-01 notice dated 03.09.2025, recorded an aggregate demand of ₹87,68,173, comprising tax of ₹49,59,993, interest up to 30.12.2025 of ₹33,02,132, interest on late reporting of invoices of ₹9,149, late fee of ₹900 and penalty of ₹4,95,999. EMC sought quashing of the demand and a declaration that the proceedings stood extinguished by the NCLT’s order approving the going-concern sale. CIRP had been admitted against EMC on 12.11.2018 in C.P.(IB)No.1237/KB/2018. No resolution plan was approved, and the NCLT ordered liquidation on 21.11.2023. The subsequent going-concern sale received NCLT approval on 22.10.2024. EMC relied on Ghanashyam Mishra and Sons Private Limited Vs Edelweiss Asset Reconstruction Company Limited and other decisions to claim a clean slate. It submitted that the Department’s earlier application before the NCLT had been disposed of as infructuous with liberty to approach the liquidator, and that the liquidator’s letter dated 09.12.2023 had specifically invited its claim by 21.12.2023. According to EMC, the Department’s failure to submit a claim, read with Section 238 of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016, extinguished the liability. EMC also alleged that the demand invoked Section 88 without its inclusion in the show cause notice, contrary to Section 75(7), and that its submissions had not been considered. The Revenue disputed the application of the clean-slate principle to GST dues arising after commencement of CIRP. It referred to Sections 14 and 17(2)(e) of the Code, the obligation to file annual returns under Section 44 of the GST enactments, and the consequences of non-compliance for registration under Section 29. It also argued that the insolvency claim-submission timeline could not practically accommodate GST liabilities arising during CIRP when adjudication under Sections 73 and 74 could occur within their respective statutory periods. The Revenue relied on Aqua Terra Coke and Energy Limited and Transafe Services Ltd. The Court examined the statutory framework governing moratorium, appointment and powers of insolvency professionals and liquidators, liquidation estates, and access to information concerning assets and liabilities. It held that current tax liability incurred after appointment of the interim resolution professional, including during the moratorium, could not be written off under the Code. It distinguished an approved resolution plan under Sections 30 and 31 from a going-concern sale during liquidation under Section 33. For the latter, it read Regulations 32(e) and (f) and 32A of the Liquidation Process Regulations together with Regulation 39C of the CIRP Regulations, as applicable to the 2024 sale. The Court emphasised identification and grouping of assets and liabilities by the Committee of Creditors or, where necessary, by the liquidator in consultation with the relevant committee. Although the NCLT order did not make clear which route had led to the sale, the Court held that EMC had been transferred as a going concern with its liabilities, rather than through a sale of assets alone. In its reasoning, the purchaser took a calibrated commercial risk and acquired the assets and liabilities as a package; the corporate debtor’s identity continued under the new management. The Court contrasted this with sales under Regulations 32(a) to (d), followed by dissolution and distribution under Section 53. It held that Section 53 did not apply to the going-concern sale before it and that Ghanashyam Mishra did not decide this regulatory situation. These were findings in the present order, whose reasoning expressly differed from decisions extending clean-slate protection to such sales. The Court declined to accept the reasoning in Kashvi Power & Steel Private Limited and Rabirun Vinimay Private Limited, and regarded the tribunal decisions relied on in the NCLT order as per incuriam and non-binding. It distinguished Agniti Industrial Parks on the ground that the claims there related to the period before commencement of liquidation. It also referred to Aqua Terra Coke and Energy Limited, P DOT G Constructions and Transafe Services concerning liabilities arising during CIRP. Accordingly, it found no merit in EMC’s substantive challenge seeking extinguishment of the demand. Nevertheless, the Court granted a conditional opportunity for fresh adjudication because EMC had not filed an effective reply and might have a case under Section 85 of the GST enactments. It discussed the joint and several liability of the transferor and transferee for dues up to transfer and the transferee’s liability for subsequent transactions. EMC was directed to deposit 25% of the disputed tax liability within 30 days of receiving the order and, within the same period, submit a detailed reply treating the impugned order as an addendum to the show cause notice. The authority was directed to decide the matter on merits within three months thereafter. Thus, the writ petition was disposed of with a conditional remand, without costs; the Court did not accept the plea that the GST liability automatically stood extinguished. As stated elsewhere, the Petitioner company was initially exposed to a Corporate Insolvency Resolution Process (CIRP) in C.P.(IB)No.1237/KB/2018, the National Company Law Tribunal, Kolkata admitted the said CIRP vide its Order dated 12.11.2018. 22. Though the actual date of Moratorium under Section 14 of the Code is not available from the records, it is evident and discernible that all the proceedings would have preceded a moratorium in 2018 under Section 14 of the Code. 24. As per Section 16 of the Code, the National Company Law Tribunal (NCLT) as the Adjudicating Authority as defined in Section 5(1) of the Code would have been appointed an Interim Resolution Professional (IRP) within 14 days of Insolvency Commencement Date (ICD) as defined in Section 5(12) of the Code i.e., the date of admission of application for CIRP before the National Company Law Tribunal (NCLT) under Section 7, 9 & 19, as the case may be. Same Resolution Professional (RP) would have been appointed as a Liquidator under Section 33 of the Code with the same powers. 25. During the Moratorium, the following are prohibited:- (a) the institution of suits or continuation of pending suits or proceedings against the corporate debtor including execution of any judgment, decree or order in any court of law, tribunal, arbitration panel or other authority; (b) transferring, encumbering, alienating or disposing of by the corporate debtor any of its assets or any legal right or beneficial interest therein; (c) any action to foreclose, recover or enforce any security interest created by the corporate debtor in respect of its property including any action under the Securitisation and Reconstruction of Financial Assets and Enforcement of Security Interest Act, 2002; (d) the recovery of any property by an owner or lessor where such property is occupied by or in the possession of the corporate debtor. 26. Section 14(1) is however subject to Section 14(2) & 14(3) of the Code. As per Proviso to Section 14, the Moratorium shall cease to have effect, if at any time during the CIRP, where the Adjudicating Authority i.e., the National Company Law Tribunal (NCLT), either approves the Resolution Plan (RP) under sub-section (1) of Section 31 or passes an order for liquidation of Corporate Debtor under Section 33 of the Code. 27. However, from the date of the appointment of the Interim Resolution Professional (IRP), the Petitioner Corporate Debtor against whom the proceedings have been initiated under the Code would have been under the control of the said Interim Resolution Professional (IRP). During this period, the Interim Resolution Professional (IRP) was appointed for complying with the statutory obligations under the applicable laws / statute. 28. In this case, admittedly, the proceeding under Section 10 of the Code was initiated against the Petitioner Corporate Debtor, in the year 2018. The liability incurred by the Petitioner Corporate Debtor after the Interim Resolution Professional (IRP) was apppointed i.e., when the period of Moratorium under Section 14 was in operation, cannot be written off under the scheme of the Code. The current tax liability of a Corporate Debtor cannot be isolated under the provisions of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (IBC), 2016. I shall explain the position in due course. 29. Liquidation of a Corporate Debtor under Chapter III of the Code, which is titled as ‘Liquidation Process’. There are about 23 provisions in Chapter-III of the Code. Sections 38 to 42 which deal with the claims submitted before the Liquidator has been omitted vide Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (Amendment) Act, 2026 (Act No.6 of 2026). During the period in dispute when the order was passed by the National Company Law Tribunal (NCLT), these provisions were there in the Code. 30. According to Section 33, where the Adjudicating Authority i.e., the National Company Law Tribunal (NCLT) either fails to receive a Resolution Plan or rejects a Resolution Plan received by it, it shall pass an order requiring the corporate debtor to be liquidated in the manner as laid down in the said Chapter. Section 33 of the Code is extracted below:- 33. (1) Where the Adjudicating Authority, — (a) before the expiry of the insolvency resolution process period or the maximum period permitted for completion of the corporate insolvency resolution process under section 12 or the fast track corporate insolvency resolution process under section 56, as the case may be, does not receive a resolution plan under sub-section (6) of section 30; or (b) rejects the resolution plan under section 31 for the non-compliance of the requirements specified therein, it shall— (i) pass an order requiring the corporate debtor to be liquidated in the manner as laid down in this Chapter; (ii) issue a public announcement stating that the corporate debtor is in liquidation; and (iii) require such order to be sent to the authority with which the corporate debtor is registered. (2) Where the resolution professional, at any time during the corporate insolvency resolution process but before confirmation of resolution plan, intimates the Adjudicating Authority of the decision of the committee of creditors to liquidate the corporate debtor, the Adjudicating Authority shall pass a liquidation order as referred to in sub-clauses (i), (ii) and (iii) of clause (b) of sub-section (1). (3) Where the resolution plan approved by the Adjudicating Authority is contravened by the concerned corporate debtor, any person other than the corporate debtor, whose interests are prejudicially affected by such contravention, may make an application to the Adjudicating Authority for a liquidation order as referred to in sub-clauses (i), (ii) and (iii) of clause (b) of sub-section (1). (4) On receipt of an application under sub-section (3), if the Adjudicating Authority determines that the corporate debtor has contravened the provisions of the resolution plan, it shall pass a liquidation order as referred to in sub-clauses (i), (ii) and (iii) of clause (b) of sub-section (1). (5) Subject to section 52, when a liquidation order has been passed, no suit or other legal proceeding shall be instituted by or against the corporate debtor: Provided that a suit or other legal proceeding may be instituted by the liquidator, on behalf of the corporate debtor, with the prior approval of the Adjudicating Authority. (6) The provisions of sub-section (5) shall not apply to legal proceedings in relation to such transactions as may be notified by the Central Government in consultation with any financial sector regulator. (7) The order for liquidation under this section shall be deemed to be a notice of discharge to the officers, employees and workmen of the corporate debtor, except when the business of the corporate debtor is continued during the liquidation process by the liquidator. 31. Once a decision has been taken under Section 33 of the Code, to liquidate the Corporate Debtor in the manner laid down in Chapter-III of the Code, the Resolution Professional (RP) who was till then managing the affairs of the Corporate Debtor is to be appointed as a Liquidator subject to the written consent by such Resolution Professional to the Adjudicating Authority in the specified form. This is provided in Section 34(1) of the Code, which deals with appointment of Liquidator and the fee to be paid to the Liquidator. 32. Once a payment is made under Section 34(1) of the Code, the Liquidator has all the powers of the Board of Directors, Key Managerial Personnel and the partners of the Corporate Debtor, as the case may be, shall cease to have effect and shall be vested in the Liquidator. 33. An elaborate procedure has been prescribed for the Personnel of the Corporate Debtor of the Liquidator in the process of liquidation, and in this connection, the provisions of Section 19 of the Code shall apply in relation to voluntary liquidation as they apply in relation to liquidation process (Corporate Insolvency Resolution Process) with the substitution of references to the Liquidator for references to the Interim Resolution Professional (IRP). In other words, the procedure contemplated under Section 19 of the Code qua the Interim Resolution Professional is to apply to the Liquidator appointed under Section 33 of the Code. 34. Once the Liquidator is appointed, the Liquidator is vested with wide powers under Section 35 of the Code. The Liquidator is required to form an Estate of the Assets as is contemplated in Section 36(3) of the Code called “Liquidation Assets” in relation to the Corporate Debtor. Under Sub-Section (3) to Section 36 of the Code, the Liquidator holds the “Liquidating Estate Assets” as a fiduciary for the benefit of creditors which would also include an Operational Creditor like the Tax Department within the meaning of Section 3(10) of the Code. 35. The Liquidation Estate shall compromise all Liquidation Estate Assets as detailed in Sub-Section (3) to Section 36 of the Code. Similarly, certain categories of Liquidation Estates Assets shall not be used for effecting recovery pursuant to liquidation. For the sake of clarity, Section 36 of the Code is reproduced below:- 36. Liquidation Estate:- (1) For the purposes of liquidation, the liquidator shall form an estate of the assets mentioned in sub-section (3), which will be called the liquidation estate in relation to the corporate debtor. (2) The liquidator shall hold the liquidation estate as a fiduciary for the benefit of all the creditors. (3) Subject to sub-section (4), the liquidation estate shall comprise all liquidation estate assets which shall include the following:— (a) any assets over which the corporate debtor has ownership rights, including all rights and interests therein as evidenced in the balance sheet of the corporate debtor or an information utility or records in the registry or any depository recording securities of the corporate debtor or by any other means as may be specified by the Board, including shares held in any subsidiary of the corporate debtor; (b) assets that may or may not be in possession of the corporate debtor including but not limited to encumbered assets; (c) tangible assets, whether movable or immovable; (d) intangible assets including but not limited to intellectual property, securities (including shares held in a subsidiary of the corporate debtor) and financial instruments, insurance policies, contractual rights; (e) assets subject to the determination of ownership by the court or authority; (f) any assets or their value recovered through proceedings for avoidance of transactions in accordance with this Chapter; (g) any asset of the corporate debtor in respect of which a secured creditor has relinquished security interest; (h) any other property belonging to or vested in the corporate debtor at the insolvency commencement date; and (i) all proceeds of liquidation as and when they are realised. (4) The following shall not be included in the liquidation estate assets and shall not be used for recovery in the liquidation:— (a) assets owned by a third party which are in possession of the corporate debtor, including— (i) assets held in trust for any third party; (ii) bailment contracts; (iii) all sums due to any workman or employee from the provident fund, the pension fund and the gratuity fund; (iv) other contractual arrangements which do not stipulate transfer of title but only use of the assets; and (v) such other assets as may be notified by the Central Government in consultation with any financial sector regulator; (b) assets in security collateral held by financial services providers and are subject to netting and set-off in multi-lateral trading or clearing transactions; (c) personal assets of any shareholder or partner of a corporate debtor as the case may be provided such assets are not held on account of avoidance transactions that may be avoided under this Chapter; (d) assets of any Indian or foreign subsidiary of the corporate debtor; or (e) any other assets as may be specified by the Board, including assets which could be subject to set-off on account of mutual dealings between the corporate debtor and any creditor. 36. That apart, Section 37(1) of the Code also gives the powers to the Liquidator to access any information systems for the purpose of admission and proof of claims and identification of the Liquidation Estates Assets relating to the Corporate Debtor from the following sources, namely :- (a) an information utility; (b) credit information systems regulated under any law for the time being in force; (c) any agency of the Central, State or Local Government including any registration authorities; (d) information systems for financial and non-financial liabilities regulated under any law for the time being in force; (e) information systems for securities and assets posted as security interest regulated under any law for the time being in force; (f) any database maintained by the Board; and (g) any other source as may be specified by the Board. 37. In fact, once the Moratorium under Section 14 of the Code gets triggered, the Interim Resolution Professional (IRP), Resolution Professional (RP) or / as the case may be, the Liquidator would have all the informations particularly in relation to the Assets and Liabilities of the Petitioner Corporate Debtor as it was under the control of such person appointed by the National Company Law Tribunal (NCLT). 38. In this regard, Regulation 32 and Regulation 32A of the Insolvency & Bankruptcy Board of India (Liquidation Process) Regulations, 2016 are relevant. Regulation 32 and Regulation 32A of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Board of India (Liquidation Process) Regulations, 2016 has to be read in conjunction with Regulation 39C of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Board of India (Insolvency Resolution Process for Corporate Persons) Regulations, 2016. 39. For the sake of convenience, Regulation 39C of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Board of India (Insolvency Resolution Process for Corporate Persons) Regulations, 2016 is extracted below:- “39C. : Assessment of sale as a going concern. (1) While approving a resolution plan under section 30 or deciding to liquidate the corporate debtor under section 33, the committee may recommend that the liquidator may first explore sale of the corporate debtor as a going concern under clause (e) of regulation 32 of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Board of India (Liquidation Process) Regulations, 2016 or sale of the business of the corporate debtor as a going concern under clause (f) thereof, if an order for liquidation is passed under section 33. (2) Where the committee recommends sale as a going concern, it shall identify and group the assets and liabilities, which according to its commercial considerations, ought to be sold as a going concern under clause (e) or clause (f) of regulation 32 of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Board of India (Liquidation Process) Regulations, 2016. (3) The resolution professional shall submit the recommendation of the committee under sub-regulations (1) and (2) to the Adjudicating Authority while filing the approval or decision of the committee under section 30 or 33, as the case may be.” 40. Ordinarily, during the course of CIRP under Section 30 & 31 of the Code, the re-structuring of the Corporate Debtor takes place, where the Committee of Creditors play a pivotal role in deciding how the debt [whether Operational and/or Financial debt(s) or both] are to be dealt with. The Resolution plan recommended by the Committee of Creditors has to be further approved by the Adjudicating Authority viz, the National Company Law Tribunal (NCLT) under Section 31 of the Code. 46. Where, however, it is decided to liquidate the Corporate Debtor under Section 33 of the Code, a different set of provisions viz. Regulations 32 & 32A the Insolvency & Bankruptcy Board of India (Liquidation Process) Regulations, 2016 come into play. 47. During the course of Liquidation under Section 33 of the Code, either the Resolution Professional is either re-designated as the Liquidator or is appointed afresh. 48. Regulation 32 of the Insolvency & Bankruptcy Board of India (Liquidation Process) Regulations, 2016 deals with Sale of Assets etc. of a Corporate Debtor in general. 49. Clauses (e) & (f) of Regulation 32 of the Insolvency & Bankruptcy Board of India (Liquidation Process) Regulations, 2016, deal with the Sale of :- i. ‘Corporate Debtor as a Going Concern’; and ii. ‘Business(es) of the Corporate Debtor as a Going Concern’. Respectively. 50. In this Case, the Petitioner Corporate Debtor was sold as a Going Concern under Regulation 32 (e) read with Regulation 32A of Regulation 32A of the Insolvency & Bankruptcy Board of India (Liquidation Process) Regulations, 2016. Regulation 32A specifically deals with “Sale of a Going Concern”. 51. As per Regulation 32A(1) of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Board of India (Liquidation Process) Regulations, 2016, where, either 1. the Committee of Creditors, has recommended sale under clause (e) or (f) of Regulation 32 of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Board of India (Liquidation Process) Regulations, 2016 during CIRP; or 2. where the liquidator is of the opinion that sale under clause (e) or (f) of the Regulation 32 of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Board of India (Liquidation Process) Regulations, 2016 will maximise the value of the corporate debtor, the liquidator shall endeavour to first sell under the said clauses of the said Regulation. 52. Regulation 32A(2) has to be read along with Regulation 39C(2) of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Board of India (Insolvency Resolution Process for Corporate Persons) Regulations, 2016. 53. As per Regulation 39C(1) of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Board of India (Insolvency Resolution Process for Corporate Persons) Regulations, 2016, while:- 1. Approving a Resolution Plan under section 30; or 2. Deciding to Liquidate the Corporate Debtor under section 33, the Committee may recommend that the liquidator may,- 1. first explore Sale of The Corporate Debtor as a Going Concern under regulation 32(e) of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Board of India (Liquidation Process) Regulations, 2016 ; and if same is not possible, thereafter, 2. sale of the Business of The Corporate Debtor as a Going Concern under Regulation 32(e) of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Board of India (Liquidation Process) Regulations, 2016, if an order for liquidation is passed under Section 33 of the Code. 54. Under Regulation 39C(3) of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Board of India (Insolvency Resolution Process for Corporate Persons) Regulations, 2016, during CIRP, the Resolution Professional would have submitted the recommendation of the Committee before the Adjudicating Authority for approval under Section 30 & 33. 55. For the purpose of sale under Regulation 32 (e) & (f) of Insolvency & Bankruptcy Board of India (Liquidation Process) Regulations, 2016 during the course of the Liquidation under Section 33 of the Code, the Assets & Liabilities identified during the course of CIRP by the Committee of Creditors under Regulation 39C(2) of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Board of India (Insolvency Resolution Process for Corporate Persons) Regulations, 2016 shall be sold as a Going Concern under Regulation 32A(2) of the Insolvency & Bankruptcy Board of India (Liquidation Process) Regulations, 2016. 56. Where, however, no identification of Assets & Liabilities was made by the Committee of Creditors under Regulation 39C(2) of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Board of India (Insolvency Resolution Process for Corporate Persons) Regulations, 2016 during CIRP under Section 31 of the Code, the Liquidator appointed under Section 34 of the Code during liquidation under Section 33 of the Code has to identify the group of Assets & Liabilities of a Corporate Debtor to be sold as a Going Concern in consultation with the Committee of Creditors. This has been provided under Regulation 32A(3) of the Insolvency & Bankruptcy Board of India (Liquidation Process) Regulations, 2016. 57. Thus, to implement Regulation 32 (e) & (f) of the Insolvency & Bankruptcy Board of India (Liquidation Process) Regulations, 2016, Regulation 32A has been provided. Regulation 32A in turn relies on the Group of Assets & Liabilities identified under Regulation 39C(2) of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Board of India (Insolvency Resolution Process for Corporate Persons) Regulations, 2016 by the Committee of Creditors during the course of CIRP. 58. It has to be also emphasized that under Regulation 32A(4) of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Board of India (Liquidation Process) Regulations, 2016, the Liquidator has to sell the Corporate Debtor as a Going Concern exclusively at the First Auction.viz., as is contemplated in Clause (e) of Regulation 32 of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Board of India (Liquidation Process) Regulations, 2016. 59. In terms of Regulation 32A(5) of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Board of India (Liquidation Process) Regulations, 2016, where the Liquidator is of the opinion that it is viable to run the Corporate Debtor as a Going Concern, the Liquidator after consulting the Committee can run the affairs of the Corporate Debtor on the advice of the Consultation Committee alone. 60. In this case, admittedly, the CIRP failed and therefore, the National Company Law Tribunal, decided to liquidate the petitioner Corporate Debtor under Section 33 of the Code. 61. From, a reading of Order dated 22.10.2024 of the NCLT in I.A.(IB)No.1140/KB/2024 in Company Petition(IB)No.1237/KB/2018 passed under Section 33 of the Code read with Regulation 32(e) and Regulation 32A of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Board of India (Liquidation Process) Regulations, 2016 and Regulation 39C of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Board of India (Insolvency Resolution Process for Corporate Persons) Regulations, 2016, which approved the Sale of the petitioner as a going concern to M/s.Salasar Techno Engineering Limited, it is not clear, as to whether the sale emanated from the recommendation of the Committee of Creditors under Regulation 32A(2) or was on account of the opinion of the Liquidator for the sale of the petitioner Corporate Debtor, as a Going Concern under Regulation 32A(3) to the said company. 62. However, after the Petitioner Corporate Debtor was sold as a “Going Concern” to M/s.Salasar Techno Engineering Limited by the Liquidator, presumably following one of the above procedure with the approval of the National Company Law Tribunal under the above provisions, the Petitioner became the subsidiary of the said M/s.Salasar Techno Engineering Limited. 63. While approving the sale of the petitioner Corporate Debtor under the aforesaid Regulations, the National Company Law Tribunal in its aforesaid order dated 22.10.2024, has not approved the sale of ‘Assets” simpliciter, sans the liability, to the said M/s.Salasar Techno Engineering Limited. The sale was indeed with the liabilities of the Petitioner Corporate Debtor as a Going Concern. 64. In fact, NCLT could not have approved the sale of Assets of the Petitioner Corporate Debtor simpliciter, sans the liability under the scheme of the above Regulations. 65. All that the National Company Law Tribunal has stated in its aforesaid order dated 22.10.2024 is that the petitioner will be entitled for relief as per the decision of the Hon’ble Supreme Court in Ghanashyam Das case. However, there has been no adjudication or application of mind on this aspect by the National Company Law Tribunal. 66. Instead of winding up the affairs of the Corporate Debtor and liquidating its assets and distributing the proceeds thereof under Section 53 of the Code, the Liquidator under Regulation 32(e)& (f) and Regulation 32A of the Insolvency & Bankruptcy Board of India (Liquidation Process) Regulations, 2016 read with Regulation 39C(2) of the Insolvency & Bankruptcy Board of India (Insolvency Resolution Process for Corporate Persons) Regulations, 2016, merely sells the identified assets and liabilities of the Corporate Debtor as a “Going Concern”, lock, stock and barrel. 67. When a Corporate Debtor is sold as a Going Concern, the buyer not only purchases the assets but also the liabilities of the Corporate Debtor as a package and not merely the assets sans the liabilities of the said Corporate Debtor. 68. The buyer takes a calibrated risk while buying the assets and liabilities of the Corporate Debtor when the Corporate Debtor is sold as a “Going Concern” under Regulation 32(e) read with Regulation 32A of the Insolvency & Bankruptcy Board of India (Liquidation Process) Regulations, 2016 read with Regulation 39 C(2) of the Insolvency & Bankruptcy Board of India (Insolvency Resolution Process for Corporate Persons) Regulations, 2016. 69. Therefore, after buying the Corporate Debtor as a “Going Concern”, the buyer or the new management of the Corporate Debtor cannot avoid either the past and/or the current liability incurred or liability incurred during the Moratorium under Section 14 of the Code and during the CIRP / Liquidation, as the case may be. 70. It is for this purpose, the assets and liabilities identified under Regulation 39C(2) of the Insolvency & Bankruptcy Board of India (Insolvency Resolution Process for Corporate Persons) Regulations, 2016 by the Committee of Creditors or by the Liquidator under Regulation 32A(3) of the Insolvency & Bankruptcy Board of India (Liquidation Process) Regulations, 2016, before a sale is effected by the Liquidator. 71. The Purchase Value of the Corporate Debtor is infused into the capital of the Corporate Debtor and increases the net worth of the Corporate Debtor. This is similar to the treatment under the erstwhile Sick Industrial Companies (Special Provisions) Act, 1985, without any reduction of the liability. 72. In a sense, Regulations 32 & 32A of the Insolvency & Bankruptcy Board of India (Liquidation Process) Regulations, 2016 and Regulation 39C of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Board of India (Insolvency Resolution Process for Corporate Persons) Regulations, 2016 form a Triangle in the context of sale of assets of the Corporate Debtor as per the Code and the above Regulations made thereunder. 73. It would have been different in the case of sale under Clause (a) to (d) of Regulation 32 of the Insolvency & Bankruptcy Board of India (Liquidation Process) Regulations, 2016, where: i. merely an asset of the Corporate Debtor is sold on a standalone basis; or ii. the assets of the Corporate Debtor is sold in a slump sale; or iii. a set of assets of the of the Corporate Debtor is collectively sold; or iv. the assets of the of the Corporate Debtor in parcels 74. There the Liquidation would have culminated in the dissolution of the Corporate Entity itself under Section 54 of the Code. There, possibly the “Clean Slate Rule” as enunciated in Ghanashyam Case may apply as the Corporate Debtor is liquidated. 75. The amount recovered from such sale under Regulation 32(a) to (d) of the Insolvency & Bankruptcy Board of India (Liquidation Process) Regulations, 2016 has to be distributed only in such manner as is contemplated under Section 53 of the Code and in such order as has been recognized by the Hon’ble Supreme Court in Ghanashyam’s case as in the case of successful CIRP. 76. The distribution of the Liquidated assets of the Corporate Debtor after its sale, has to be carried out in accordance with the order of priority under Section 53 of the Code. Whereas, in the case of Sale of the Corporate Debtor as a Going Concern or the Sale of Business of the Corporate Debtor, as a Going Concern, there is no disintegration of the Corporate Identity of the Corporate Debtor as it survives with new funds being infused for carrying on the same business. 77. However, where the sale of the Corporate Debtor is under Regulation 32(e) & (f) read with Regulation 32A of the Insolvency & Bankruptcy Board of India (Liquidation Process) Regulations, 2016, a different yardstick applies. 78. When the Corporate Debtor is sold as a “Going Concern” under Regulation 32(e) read with Regulation 32A of the Insolvency & Bankruptcy Board of India (Liquidation Process) Regulations, 2016 read with Regulation 39 C of the Insolvency & Bankruptcy Board of India (Insolvency Resolution Process for Corporate Persons) Regulations, 2016, the buyer understands bargain in the purchase and risk associated with it. The buyer not only buys the existing assets at the value determined but also the liability of the Corporate Debtor. 79. The reliance placed on the decisions of the Tribunals at Paragraph Nos.19 & 22 of the Order date 22.10.2024 by the National Company Law Tribunal, Kolkata are per incuriam and are in any event not binding on this Court. 80. The decision of the Calcutta High Court in Kashvi Power & Steel Private Limited & Another Vs. West Bengal State Electricity Distribution Company Limited & Others, in W.P.A.No.6327 of 2022 dated 12.08.2022, has taken a somewhat diametrically contrary view to the above Scheme, that the Petitioner therein was entitled to the relief sought for it by extending the benefit of the “Clean Slate Rule”. There, the Calcutta High Court dealt with pre-CIRP liability of the Corporate Debtor. 81. In Kashvi Power (supra), the Respondent No.3, a Corporate Debtor was acquired as a going concern through liquidation sale under the powers of the Code. When the Respondent No.1-State Electricity Distribution Company denied its services for the past dues of the Corporate Debtor i.e., pre-CIRP liabilities incurred by the Corporate Debtor, the Calcutta High Court held that the dues that were pending prior to liquidation sale were extinguished with the following observation while interpreting the very same provisions:- 58. Thus, the operational creditors, who fall within category (f), that is, “any remaining debts and dues”, cannot claim any priority over the preceding categories in having their debts paid off. 59. However, that is precisely what will happen if the argument of the WBSEDCL is accepted, that is, if the expression “sale of going concern”, as used in the IBC and connected Regulations in respect of liquidation, is construed to include transfer of pre-CIRP liabilities of the corporate debtor. 60. Regulation 32-A of the Liquidation Process Regulations of 2016 clearly specifies that “liabilities” for the purpose of going concern sales in liquidation are only those liabilities which have been identified and earmarked for the going concern sale by the committee of creditors, if not, by the liquidator. 61. The said provision, read with Regulation 39-C of the Corporate Persons Regulations, 2016, is unambiguous and leaves no scope of construing that all the liabilities, including pre-CIRP liabilities, are transferred to the successful Resolution Applicant in a going concern sale. 62. It is well-settled that the debts of the power distribution licensee do not operate as charge on the assets of the corporate debtor. 63. Hence, both sale of the corporate debtor and the business(es) of the corporate debtor as a going concern, as envisaged in Regulation 32, Clauses (e) and (f) respectively, do not contemplate automatic transfer of all pre-CIRP liabilities of the corporate debtor to the auction purchaser. 64. In any event, the Regulations framed under the authority conferred by the IBC cannot be construed to override the provisions of the Code itself. Hence, no interpretation contrary to Section 53 of the IBC which, again, is preceded by a non obstante clause, can be attributed to the expression “going concern sale”, as contemplated in Rule 32 of the Liquidation Process Regulations, 2016. The case dealt with pre-CIRP dues of the said Company, namely, Kashvi Power & Steel Private Limited & Another. 82. The principle in Kashvi Power (supra) was blindly followed by the Calcutta High Court in the case of M/s.Rabirun Vinimay Pvt. Ltd. & another Vs. Union of India, W.P.A.27722 of 2024 dated 12.11.2025, wherein the facts are identical to the facts of the present case. 83. I am afraid such an erroneous interpretation of the Regulation with the view to defeat the very object of Code & the above Regulation are unacceptable in view of the discussion above. 84. The decisions of the Calcutta High Court cited above, as also, the decisions of the Calcutta Bench of the National Company Law Tribunal are not correct, in view of the Scheme under Regulation 32 (e) & (f) and Regulation 32A of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Board of India (Liquidation Process) Regulations, 2016 read with Regulation 39C of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Board of India (Insolvency Resolution Process for Corporate Persons) Regulations, 2016. 85. That apart, in the present case, the Order passed by the National Company Law Tribunal on 22.10.2024 in C.P. (IB) No. 1237/KB/2018 in granting the relief of approval of sale of the Petitioner company, as a “Going Concern” to M/s. Salasar Techno Engineering Limited, with effect from 16.05.2024 has to be read in the context of Chapter – III of the Code encompassing Sections 33 to 54, as they stood along with Regulations 32 & 32A of the Insolvency & Bankruptcy Board of India (Liquidation Process) Regulations, 2016 and Regulation 39C(2) of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Board of India (Insolvency Resolution Process for Corporate Persons) Regulations, 2016 as they form a Code by themselves in so far as sale of the Corporate Debtor as a “Going Concern”. 86. Section 53 of the Code has no application in the context of Sale of the Corporate Debtor as a Going Concern under Regulation 32(e) read with Regulation 32A of the Insolvency & Bankruptcy Board of India (Liquidation Process) Regulations, 2016 read with Regulation 39 C of the Insolvency & Bankruptcy Board of India (Insolvency Resolution Process for Corporate Persons) Regulations, 2016. 87. The observation of the National Company Law Tribunal in its Order dated 22.10.2024 in I.A.(IB)No.1140/KB/2024 in C.P.(IB)No.1237/KB/2018 that the reliefs granted are strictly in accordance with Ghanashyam Mishra (supra) is superfluous. The said decision did not deal with a situation on sale of asset of Corporate Debtor as a Going Concern under Regulation 32 (e) or (f) read with Regulation 32A of the Insolvency & Bankruptcy Board of India (Liquidation Process) Regulations, 2016, and Regulation 39C of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Board of India (Insolvency Resolution Process for Corporate Persons) Regulations, 2016, as inserted in the year 2019. 88. The reliance placed by the Petitioner on the decision of a learned Single Judge of this Court in Agniti Industrial Parks Pvt. Ltd. Vs. The Superintendent of CGST & Central, in W.P.No.11097 of 2021 dated 11.12.2023, is not applicable to the facts of the present case as the the claims therein relate to the period prior to the commencement of liquidation. 89. Subsequent to the above decision, the Learned Single Judge had also passed another judgement in a batch of Writ Petitions in Aqua Terra Coke and Energy Limited Vs. Income Tax Officer & Ors, W.P.Nos.22505 of 2024 etc., batch where the Learned Judge had extensively examined the tax legislations, more particularly the Tamil Nadu Goods and Services Tax Act, 2017, vis-a-vis the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016, and had stressed the sui generis nature of the respective GST Enactments, in the context of dues arising during CIRP. 90. Similar views were also taken by this Court in P DOT G Constructions Private Limited Vs. Assistant Commissioner of Income Tax in W.P.No.27692 of 2023, and in M/s. Transafe Services Ltd Vs. The Superintendent of Central GST and Central Excise in W.P.No.14266 of 2026. 91. Thus, there is no merit in the challenge to the Impugned Order in this Writ Petition, and therefore, this Writ Petition does not warrant any interference and is liable to be dismissed. 92. Further, under Section 85(1) of respective GST Enactments, where a taxable person, liable to pay GST, transfers his business in any manner whatsoever, the taxable person and the person to whom the business is so transferred shall, jointly and severally, be liable to the extent of such transfer. 93. Likewise, under Section 85(2), the transferee of a business is liable to pay GST on transactions effected after the date of such transfer and shall apply for amending his registration. Section 85 of the respective GST Enactments is extracted below:- Section 85. Liability in case of transfer of business.- (1) Where a taxable person, liable to pay tax under this Act, transfers his business in whole or in part, by sale, gift, lease, leave and license, hire or in any other manner whatsoever, the taxable person and the person to whom the business is so transferred shall, jointly and severally, be liable wholly or to the extent of such transfer, to pay the tax, interest or any penalty due from the taxable person upto the time of such transfer, whether such tax, interest or penalty has been determined before such transfer, but has remained unpaid or is determined thereafter. (2) Where the transferee of a business referred to in sub-section (1) carries on such business either in his own name or in some other name, he shall be liable to pay tax on the supply of goods or services or both effected by him with effect from the date of such transfer and shall, if he is a registered person under this Act, apply within the prescribed time for amendment of his certificate of registration. 94.

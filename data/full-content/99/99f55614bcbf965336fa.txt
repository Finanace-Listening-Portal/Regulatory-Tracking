(Supreme Court of India) The Supreme Court of India, in Baharul Islam & Ors. Vs Indian Medical Association & Ors., 2023 INSC 81, examined the constitutional validity of the Assam Rural Health Regulatory Authority Act, 2004, which established a framework for introducing a three-year Diploma in Medicine and Rural Health Care (DMRHC) and registering diploma holders as Rural Health Practitioners in Assam. The legislation was enacted to address the shortage of qualified medical practitioners in rural areas by permitting diploma holders to provide limited medical services. The Indian Medical Association challenged the legislation before the Gauhati High Court, arguing that the State Legislature lacked the constitutional authority to establish such a medical education and registration system. On 30 October 2014, the High Court declared the 2004 Act unconstitutional, prompting affected diploma holders to approach the Supreme Court. The appellants contended that Entry 25 of List III of the Seventh Schedule to the Constitution empowered the State Legislature to regulate medical education and rural healthcare. They argued that the diploma course did not confer a recognised medical qualification under the Indian Medical Council Act, 1956, and therefore did not require prior permission from the Central Government under Section 10A of that Act. According to the appellants, the legislation was intended to create a separate category of rural healthcare practitioners authorised to treat common diseases and prescribe specified medicines within limited geographical and professional boundaries. They further submitted that the legislation served an important public health objective by improving access to basic medical services in underserved rural communities. The Indian Medical Association and the Medical Council of India opposed the appeals, maintaining that the Indian Medical Council Act, 1956 established nationally applicable standards governing medical education, qualifications and professional practice. They argued that the Assam legislation attempted to create an alternative medical qualification and authorise medical practice without complying with the statutory requirements prescribed by Parliament. The respondents emphasised that Section 10A regulated the establishment of medical colleges and the commencement of new courses, while the Central legislation prescribed the qualifications necessary for registration and practice. They also raised concerns about patient safety and the quality of medical treatment provided by persons who had not completed recognised medical education. The Supreme Court examined the constitutional distribution of legislative powers under Article 246, Entry 66 of List I and Entry 25 of List III of the Seventh Schedule. It explained that although education, including medical education, falls within the Concurrent List, the legislative power of States is expressly subject to Parliament’s exclusive authority concerning the coordination and determination of standards in institutions of higher education. The Court discussed the principles governing legislative competence, including the doctrines of pith and substance, harmonious construction and incidental encroachment. It observed that legislative entries must be interpreted broadly while respecting the constitutional division of powers between Parliament and State Legislatures. Examining the Indian Medical Council Act, 1956 alongside the Assam Rural Health Regulatory Authority Act, 2004, the Court considered whether the State legislation could validly establish a separate system of medical education and registration. It held that the Assam Legislature could not enact provisions that intruded upon the Union’s exclusive legislative field concerning standards of medical education. The Court distinguished between the State’s authority over public health and its constitutionally limited authority to prescribe standards in medical education. It concluded that the 2004 Act exceeded the legislative competence of the Assam Legislature because its provisions operated within a field constitutionally reserved for Parliament. The Supreme Court also clarified the distinction between lack of legislative competence under Article 246 and repugnancy under Article 254. The Gauhati High Court had struck down the Assam legislation principally on the ground of repugnancy with the Indian Medical Council Act, 1956. The Supreme Court explained that the controversy was more appropriately resolved by examining the constitutional allocation of legislative powers. Where a State Legislature lacks competence to enact legislation affecting a subject reserved for Parliament, the validity of that legislation must be determined with reference to Article 246 and the relevant legislative entries. Accordingly, the Court upheld the High Court’s ultimate conclusion invalidating the 2004 Act, although on a different constitutional basis. The Court separately considered the Assam Community Professionals (Registration and Competency) Act, 2015, enacted following the High Court’s decision. The subsequent legislation sought to provide a statutory framework for persons who had completed or undertaken the diploma course by recognising them as Community Health Professionals. The diploma holders challenged this legislation because it altered their earlier professional status and restricted the functions they could perform. The Supreme Court examined the nature and scope of the 2015 Act and concluded that it did not suffer from the constitutional defect affecting the 2004 Act. The Court upheld the legislative competence of Assam to enact the 2015 law, recognising the distinction between establishing an independent system of medical practice and providing a framework for community healthcare services. Consequently, the Supreme Court upheld the invalidation of the Assam Rural Health Regulatory Authority Act, 2004, while sustaining the constitutional validity of the Assam Community Professionals (Registration and Competency) Act, 2015. The civil appeals challenging the Gauhati High Court’s decision and the transferred cases concerning the subsequent legislation were dismissed. The judgment reaffirmed that States may undertake legislative measures to improve rural healthcare, but such measures must remain within their constitutional competence and cannot interfere with standards of medical education reserved for Parliament. The decision also clarified the distinction between legislative incompetence and statutory repugnancy when examining the constitutional validity of State legislation.

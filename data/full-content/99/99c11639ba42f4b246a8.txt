Summary: White collar crimes increasingly involve complex corporate structures, financial transactions and professional intermediaries that can obscure the identity of those who ultimately benefit from unlawful activities. This article examines the concept of the “invisible beneficiary” and the extent to which Indian criminal law can impose liability on persons who knowingly assist, facilitate or participate in economic offences without being the principal offenders. It analyses abetment under Section 45 and criminal conspiracy under Section 61 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023; money laundering under Sections 2(1)(u), 3 and 4 of the Prevention of Money-laundering Act, 2002; bribery-related offences under the Prevention of Corruption Act, 1988; and corporate fraud under Section 447 of the Companies Act, 2013. Drawing on the Supreme Court’s decisions in Vijay Madanlal Choudhary v. Union of India, Sunil Bharti Mittal v. Central Bureau of Investigation and Iridium India Telecom Ltd. v. Motorola Inc., the article distinguishes corporate criminal responsibility from the individual liability of directors, employees and professional advisers. It emphasises that knowledge, intention, agreement, statutory participation and the connection between property and a scheduled offence must be established wherever required by the applicable provision. The analysis also considers financial records, beneficial ownership information, transaction trails and communications as potential evidence of deliberate facilitation. It concludes that effective enforcement should identify the persons who control or benefit from unlawful proceeds while protecting innocent or negligent service providers from criminal prosecution based solely on their occupation, association or involvement in legitimate commercial transactions. Introduction White collar crime presents a distinctive challenge to criminal justice because it is frequently committed through positions of trust, complex transactions and apparently lawful institutional arrangements. The immediate wrongdoer may be visible, but the execution and concealment of the wrongdoing can depend on a wider network: directors, employees, agents, accountants, consultants, brokers, financial institutions and other service providers. Some members of that network may knowingly assist the offence others may perform ordinary professional functions without knowledge of the underlying illegality. The legal problem is to distinguish these situations without either granting immunity to deliberate facilitators or treating every professional connection as criminal participation. The expression ‘white collar crime’ is associated with Edwin H Sutherland’s account of crime committed by persons of respectability and high social status in the course of their occupation. The concept remains useful because it draws attention to the misuse of occupational access, commercial power and institutional confidence. Contemporary financial wrongdoing, however, often involves more than one occupational actor. It may be organised through companies, nominees, contractual layers and electronic transfers that separate the person who plans the offence from the person who receives its proceeds.[1] This article uses the expression ‘invisible beneficiary’ to describe a person who obtains, controls or helps secure an unlawful benefit while remaining concealed behind intermediaries, entities or transactions. The expression is an analytical description, not a separate offence or a presumption of guilt. The central research question is: how far does Indian law permit criminal liability to extend to intermediaries and facilitators who knowingly assist white collar crime, and what safeguards prevent liability from becoming guilt by association? The article adopts a doctrinal methodology. It examines the relevant statutory provisions and leading Supreme Court decisions to identify the legal tests for abetment, conspiracy, money laundering and corporate fraud. It does not claim to present empirical findings about the frequency of intermediary involvement. Its contribution is a structured distinction between (i) a principal offender, (ii) a knowing facilitator, (iii) an innocent or negligent service provider, and (iv) a person who ultimately benefits from the proceeds. These roles may overlap, but they should not be treated as interchangeable. The Invisible Beneficiary and the Facilitation Network A network rather than a single actor A financial offence may be carried out in stages. One person may devise a fraudulent representation another may prepare supporting records a third may arrange transfers and a fourth may receive property through an entity that appears commercially independent. The use of multiple actors can create distance between the unlawful source of funds and the person who ultimately enjoys them. It can also make it harder to establish who knew what, when that knowledge arose, and whether a particular act was intended to advance the unlawful scheme. Intermediaries are not inherently suspicious. Their services are necessary to legitimate commerce. A bank employee may process a payment in the ordinary course an accountant may prepare accounts from information supplied by a client a lawyer may advise on a lawful transaction and a company secretary may file documents required by statute. The fact that a client or company is later investigated does not retrospectively convert these services into offences. Liability depends on the conduct and mental element specified by the law invoked. Categories of participation For analytical purposes, facilitators can be divided into three broad categories. First, direct facilitators knowingly perform acts that assist the commission of a predicate offence—for example, preparing false records or arranging fictitious transactions. Secondly, concealment facilitators knowingly participate in dealing with property derived from crime, including by concealing its origin, possession, location or ownership. Thirdly, professional intermediaries provide services in circumstances where the prosecution alleges that they knew of, or deliberately participated in, the unlawful purpose. These categories are descriptive the applicable offence must still be established on its statutory elements. A separate question concerns the ultimate beneficiary. A person who receives or controls proceeds may be a participant in laundering, a conspirator, a principal offender, or simply a recipient whose knowledge and role require investigation. The label ‘beneficiary’ does not itself establish criminal liability. Investigators must identify the underlying scheduled offence where the PMLA is invoked, trace the relevant property, and establish the accused person’s connection to the process or activity alleged. The Indian Legal Framework Abetment under the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 Section 45 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 (BNS) sets out the modes of abetment: instigating a person to do a thing engaging in a conspiracy for its doing where an act or illegal omission takes place in pursuance of that conspiracy and in order to its doing or intentionally aiding the doing of the thing by an act or illegal omission. Explanation 2 is especially relevant to facilitation: a person who, before or at the time of an act, does something to facilitate its commission and thereby facilitates it is said to aid that act.[2] The provision can apply to an intermediary who intentionally assists the commission of an underlying offence. For example, a person who knowingly creates false documents to enable a fraudulent claim may potentially be an abettor, depending on the offence and evidence. The critical requirement is intentional assistance, not mere proximity to the transaction. The prosecution must also identify the principal act or offence and the applicable punishment provision section 45 is not a free standing label for any conduct that appears commercially irregular. Criminal conspiracy Section 61 of the BNS addresses criminal conspiracy. Its central requirement is an agreement between two or more persons to do an illegal act or a legal act by illegal means. Where the agreement is to commit an offence, the agreement itself is the relevant core in other cases, the statutory requirement concerning an act besides the agreement applies. In complex financial cases, conspiracy may explain coordinated conduct that would otherwise appear as separate transactions. Nevertheless, parallel conduct, professional association or ordinary commercial dealings do not by themselves prove an agreement to commit an illegal act.[3] The distinction between conspiracy and abetment matters. Abetment by intentional aid focuses on assistance to the doing of a thing conspiracy focuses on the agreement and the statutory conditions attached to it. A person may be involved in one without necessarily satisfying the other. Charging decisions should therefore identify the particular form of participation alleged rather than treating all persons in a transaction chain as members of a single criminal conspiracy. Money laundering under the Prevention of Money-laundering Act, 2002 The Prevention of Money-laundering Act, 2002 (PMLA) is particularly important where the alleged facilitation concerns proceeds of crime. Section 2(1)(u) defines ‘proceeds of crime’ by reference to property derived or obtained, directly or indirectly, by any person as a result of criminal activity relating to a scheduled offence, or the value of such property. Section 3 criminalises involvement in processes or activities connected with proceeds of crime, including concealment, possession, acquisition, use, and projecting or claiming the property as untainted, as set out in the section and its Explanation. Section 4 prescribes punishment.[4] The statutory language expressly includes a person who directly or indirectly attempts to indulge, knowingly assists, knowingly becomes a party to, or is actually involved in a process or activity connected with proceeds of crime. This makes the provision relevant to intermediaries. But the statutory gateway remains important: there must be property qualifying as proceeds of crime, which requires a connection to criminal activity relating to a scheduled offence. Suspicion about unexplained wealth, a complicated transaction or a person’s professional role cannot by itself replace that requirement.[5] In Vijay Madanlal Choudhary v Union of India, the Supreme Court explained the breadth of section 3 and the relationship between the offence of money laundering and proceeds of crime. The Court held, among other things, that section 3 reaches processes and activities, direct or indirect, in dealing with proceeds of crime and is not confined to the final act of projecting the property as untainted. At the same time, the judgment emphasised the statutory significance of proceeds of crime and the scheduled offence. The decision should therefore be read as recognising the breadth of covered processes, not as authorising prosecution on mere suspicion or without the required predicate crime connection.[6] Bribery and commercial organisations The Prevention of Corruption Act, 1988 addresses bribery involving public servants and commercial organisations. Section 8 concerns the offence relating to a person who gives or promises to give an undue advantage to a public servant, subject to the statutory terms and exceptions. Section 9 addresses bribing a public servant by a commercial organisation, and section 10 concerns persons in charge of a commercial organisation in the circumstances specified by the Act. Section 12 addresses abetment of offences punishable under the Act. These provisions recognise that corruption may involve private actors and organisational decision making, not only the public servant who receives an advantage.[7] The Act does not make every employee or intermediary automatically liable because an organisation is implicated. Section 10 contains its own requirements concerning the person’s position and responsibility, together with the statutory defences. The prosecution must apply the relevant provision as written and establish its conditions. The distinction is important where a transaction is routed through an agent or consultant: the fact that the person facilitated a payment must be considered alongside the statutory definition, knowledge, consent, connivance or other relevant requirements. Corporate fraud under the Companies Act, 2013 Section 447 of the Companies Act, 2013 provides punishment for fraud. Its Explanation defines fraud, in relation to the affairs of a company or body corporate, to include an act, omission, concealment of fact or abuse of position committed by a person, or by another person with that person’s connivance, with intent to deceive, gain undue advantage or injure the interests of the company, its shareholders, creditors or any other person. The provision can therefore be relevant to persons beyond the formal directors of a company, where its elements are established.[8] The wording of section 447 makes intention and the nature of the conduct central. A clerical error, a commercial failure or a professional disagreement is not automatically fraud. Conversely, a person cannot avoid scrutiny merely by acting through a corporate intermediary if the evidence establishes the required intentional conduct or connivance. The provision must be read with other applicable provisions of the Companies Act and any relevant sectoral law. Judicial Principles: Corporate Liability and Individual Responsibility Sunil Bharti Mittal v Central Bureau of Investigation In Sunil Bharti Mittal v Central Bureau of Investigation, the Supreme Court considered whether senior corporate officers could be summoned in connection with alleged offences involving a company. The Court rejected the proposition that a director or officer becomes criminally liable merely by virtue of office. In the absence of a statutory provision creating vicarious liability, an individual may be proceeded against where there is sufficient material showing active involvement coupled with the requisite criminal intent, or where the law otherwise permits attribution.[9] The decision is a significant safeguard in cases involving intermediaries. A director, accountant, adviser or officer cannot be prosecuted simply because the person held a senior position or signed a document in the ordinary course. The investigation must connect the individual to the alleged offence through legally relevant conduct and mental state. At the same time, the judgment does not immunise an officer whose own actions satisfy the ingredients of an offence. It requires an individualised assessment. Iridium India Telecom Ltd v Motorola Inc In Iridium India Telecom Ltd v Motorola Inc, the Supreme Court recognised that a company may possess the mens rea required for a criminal offence. The Court rejected the broad argument that a corporation is incapable of committing an offence requiring a guilty mind. The decision is important because corporate form should not, by itself, shield an entity from prosecution where the law permits corporate criminal liability.[10] Iridium must be distinguished from the question of when an individual employee or intermediary is personally liable. Corporate mens rea and personal criminal responsibility are related but separate inquiries. The fact that a company may be prosecuted does not automatically establish the guilt of each director, employee or external professional. The prosecution must still satisfy the relevant rules of attribution and the elements of the offence charged against each person. Reading the cases together Read together, Sunil Bharti Mittal and Iridium support a balanced approach. Corporate entities may be criminally responsible where the offence and attribution rules permit individuals may also be prosecuted for their own participation. But individual liability is not inferred solely from designation, employment or association. The invisible beneficiary analysis should therefore guide investigation towards the real actors and flows of value, while the legal conclusion must remain anchored in the elements of each offence. Proving Knowledge, Intention and Participation The central evidentiary difficulty in prosecuting a facilitator is often proving the person’s state of mind. A transaction may be suspicious in retrospect but may have appeared legitimate when processed. A service provider may have been misled by a client, may have failed to exercise appropriate care, or may have knowingly helped to disguise criminal proceeds. Those possibilities are legally different. The prosecution must prove the mental element required by the particular statute the court must evaluate the evidence as a whole and apply the relevant standard of proof. Potentially relevant evidence includes transaction records, beneficial ownership filings, board minutes, instructions, invoices, contracts, communications, audit trails, source of funds material and the chronology of transfers. Investigators may examine whether documents were fabricated, whether commercial explanations are inconsistent with the transaction pattern, whether the intermediary had actual access to information about the source of funds, and whether steps were taken to conceal the true owner or purpose. These matters are evidentiary indicators, not automatic presumptions of guilt. The distinction between actual knowledge and negligence deserves particular care. A professional may have breached a regulatory or contractual duty without intentionally assisting a crime. Depending on the statute, negligence may attract civil, disciplinary or regulatory consequences, but it does not necessarily satisfy the mental element of abetment, conspiracy, money laundering or fraud. Conversely, deliberate avoidance of obvious facts may be relevant evidence, but the legal consequences must be assessed under the precise statutory language and applicable judicial principles. A sound investigation should also avoid confusing the receipt of a fee with receipt of criminal proceeds. Professionals are ordinarily paid for services. A fee becomes relevant to criminal liability only when the evidence connects the payment or service to the ingredients of an offence for example, where the fee is itself part of a laundering arrangement or is paid in return for intentional assistance. The inquiry must be contextual and individualised. Accountability Without Guilt by Association The policy case for pursuing facilitators is strong. If enforcement stops with the person who made the first false representation, those who knowingly designed the concealment structure or moved the proceeds may retain the practical benefit of the crime. Tracing the full chain can support asset recovery, deter professional misuse and expose the actual control of corporate entities. The concept of the invisible beneficiary encourages investigators to ask who controlled the transaction, who benefited, and who knowingly made the arrangement possible. There is, however, an equally important rule of law concern. Financial and professional services depend on trust, confidentiality and the ability to act for clients without being treated as responsible for every later discovered wrong. A rule that criminalises mere association would chill legitimate services and undermine the presumption of innocence. In particular, the provision of legal advice, accounting services or routine banking facilities should not be equated with participation in an offence unless the evidence satisfies the applicable legal test. The correct approach is neither immunity nor indiscriminate prosecution. It is evidence based accountability. Investigators should identify the specific conduct attributed to each person, the offence said to have been facilitated, the knowledge or intention required, and the evidence supporting each element. Prosecutors should avoid relying on job titles or the mere existence of a professional relationship. Courts should scrutinise whether the alleged conduct crosses the line from legitimate service into intentional participation. Conclusion White collar crime may be planned by one person, executed through several intermediaries and ultimately enjoyed by a beneficiary who is separated from the original transaction by layers of corporate and financial arrangements. The invisible beneficiary is therefore a useful way of describing the person or network that remains behind the visible offender. It is not, however, a new category of criminal liability. The law must continue to operate through recognised offences and their elements. Indian law contains several routes for addressing intentional facilitation: abetment and conspiracy under the BNS, money laundering under the PMLA, bribery related offences under the Prevention of Corruption Act and fraud under the Companies Act. The Supreme Court’s decisions on corporate criminal liability and the responsibility of directors demonstrate the need to distinguish corporate accountability from automatic personal liability. A person may be prosecuted for their own participation, but not merely because they occupy a position or provide a service. The central challenge is to look beyond the immediate offender without abandoning individual criminal responsibility. Effective enforcement must trace the movement and control of unlawful proceeds, identify those who knowingly assisted, and establish the statutory elements with reliable evidence. Equally, it must protect professionals and intermediaries who act legitimately and in good faith. A criminal justice system that combines transparency, specialised investigation and procedural fairness is better placed to expose the invisible beneficiary while preserving the rule of law.

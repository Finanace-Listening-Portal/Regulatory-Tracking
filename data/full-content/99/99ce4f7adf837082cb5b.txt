(Supreme Court of India) Highest Bid Cannot Be Rejected Merely Because It Is Lower Than the Bidder’s Earlier Offer: Supreme Court Summary: The Supreme Court, in the connected appeals of M/s Dhanlaxmi Merchandise Pvt. Ltd. and Sahakar Global Ltd. against the State of Madhya Pradesh and others, examined the cancellation of a competitive e-tender-cum-auction for the Katni and Shahdol sand mining groups. The appellants had surrendered earlier three-year mining contracts under Rule 16 of the Madhya Pradesh Sand (Mining, Transportation, Storage and Trading) Rules, 2019. They subsequently participated in a fresh auction conducted under the then-prevailing Rules and emerged as the highest bidders. Although both offers exceeded their respective reserve prices and the auction attracted multiple technically qualified bidders, the Madhya Pradesh State Mining Corporation Limited cancelled the auction principally because the fresh bids were substantially lower than the same contractors’ previous annual commitments. Sahakar had earlier obtained the Shahdol group, comprising 34 mines, at an annual bid of approximately ₹68.84 crore. It subsequently surrendered that agreement after disputes concerning the availability of mines with requisite statutory permissions. Dhanlaxmi had earlier obtained the Katni group, comprising 49 mines, at approximately ₹64.77 crore annually and surrendered the contract following commercial difficulties. The Court distinguished these factual circumstances without deciding the parties’ liabilities under their surrendered agreements. Under the Rule 16 regime prevailing during September and October 2025, neither contractor was prohibited from participating in a subsequent tender for the same group, nor was either obliged to equal its previous bid. Background of the Case Sahakar Global Ltd. had operated the Shahdol sand group, comprising 34 mines, under a three-year agreement at an annual bid of approximately ₹68.84 crore. Dhanlaxmi Merchandise Pvt. Ltd. operated the Katni group, comprising 49 mines, at approximately ₹64.77 crore annually. Both surrendered their contracts under Rule 16 of the Madhya Pradesh Sand Rules, 2019, which then permitted surrender on three months’ notice, subject to the prescribed conditions. Sahakar had already raised a dispute regarding availability of mines with statutory permissions. Dhanlaxmi subsequently explained its surrender by reference to commercial difficulties. The Court recognised these factual differences but did not adjudicate their liabilities under the earlier contracts. Crucially, the Rules then neither prohibited surrendering contractors from participating again nor required them to match their previous bids. Fresh Competitive Bids Rejected by Comparing Earlier Offers The Corporation consciously decided to retender under the existing Rules, despite proposed amendments concerning surrendering contractors. In the October 2025 auction, seven technically qualified participants competed for Shahdol and five for Katni. Sahakar emerged highest for Shahdol at ₹48.20 crore, against a reserve price of ₹44 crore. Dhanlaxmi emerged highest for Katni at ₹54.07 crore, against a reserve price of ₹50 crore. The Corporation nevertheless cancelled the process, principally because these offers were approximately 29.98% and 16.52% lower, respectively, than the appellants’ earlier annual bids. The High Court upheld the cancellation. Power to Cancel Does Not Exclude Judicial Scrutiny The Supreme Court accepted that the tender conditions reserved power to reject the highest bid and cancel the process. Neither emergence as H1 nor an offer exceeding the reserve price compelled acceptance. However, the existence of discretion did not conclude the inquiry. The Court had to examine whether the reasons for exercising that discretion were sufficient in the circumstances. A reliable valuation showing undervaluation, evidence of collusion, artificially depressed bidding, ineffective competition or a genuine supervening policy decision could justify rejection. Invocation of public revenue, without supporting material, was insufficient. The principles of Golden Food Products India v. State of Uttar Pradesh were particularly relevant because an otherwise lawful auction should not be cancelled merely upon the unsupported expectation of obtaining a better price. Earlier Bid Is Relevant, but Does Not Automatically Establish Present Market Value The Court acknowledged that the reductions were substantial and that the earlier offers were relevant. However, the Corporation had not established why those historical figures continued to represent the revenue reasonably obtainable in October 2025. There was a material distinction between showing that the current bids were lower than earlier bids and establishing that the current bids were below the value reasonably obtainable from the market. The Corporation had established the former, but not the latter. No defect in the actual competition was identified. Consequently, the difference between the two rounds could not automatically be treated as a corresponding loss to the exchequer. Subsequent Disqualification Cannot Be Applied Retrospectively An amendment dated 30 January 2026 introduced a restriction preventing surrendering contractors and their proprietors, directors or partners from participating in the same group for the prescribed three-year period. It also introduced provisions concerning recovery of loss upon retendering. The Court did not question the amendment’s validity or policy. However, it could not retrospectively disqualify bidders who were eligible when the September 2025 tender was conducted. Similarly, the later recovery mechanism could not automatically convert every difference between historical and subsequent bids into an established recoverable loss under the earlier regime. Later Higher Offers Cannot Retrospectively Validate Cancellation The subsequent Shahdol process attracted higher initial offers. Nevertheless, it arose under an amended regulatory regime, an enhanced reserve price and different timing, and had not culminated in final allotment. The legality of the earlier cancellation had to be assessed primarily against the reasons and material available when that decision was made. Later developments could inform relief but could not retrospectively supply justification. Supreme Court’s Decision The Supreme Court allowed both appeals, set aside the High Court judgment and quashed cancellation concerning Shahdol and Katni. It restored the 2025 tender process, requiring the appellants to confirm their unconditional willingness to honour their bids and complete the outstanding formalities. Upon compliance, the Corporation was directed to complete the consequential approval and allotment process. The subsequent tender was stopped for these groups. Earlier contractual dues, recoveries and statutory mining permissions remained separately enforceable. Author’s Comments The judgment draws a clear distinction between commercial discretion and an unsupported assumption of revenue loss. A previous successful bid may justify closer examination, but it cannot automatically become an undisclosed minimum price for a fresh auction. Equally, the ruling does not guarantee allotment to every highest bidder. Its protection lies in requiring the State to connect rejection with contemporaneous evidence, the notified conditions and a rational assessment of the auction outcome.

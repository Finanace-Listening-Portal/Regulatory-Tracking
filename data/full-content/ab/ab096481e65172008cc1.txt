Summary: The taxability of principal and interest loan waivers under the Income-tax Act, 1961, depends upon the nature of borrowing, its utilisation, earlier deductions claimed and the applicability of Sections 28(iv), 41(1), 43(1), 43B and 56(2)(x). While waiver of interest previously allowed as a deduction may attract Section 41(1), waiver of interest never claimed or disallowed under Section 43B ordinarily falls outside its scope. In CIT v. Mahindra & Mahindra Ltd. (2018) 404 ITR 1 (SC), the Supreme Court held that waiver of a capital loan was not taxable under the unamended Section 28(iv) or Section 41(1), where no corresponding deduction had been allowed. The Finance Act, 2023 subsequently expanded Section 28(iv), effective from AY 2024-25, to include monetary benefits, making the character and timing of loan waivers particularly significant. In Jindal India Power Limited v. DCIT (ITAT Delhi), ITA No. 4966/DEL/2026, dated 09.10.2026, as described in the supplied account, the assessee settled outstanding liabilities of approximately ₹9,431.49 crore for ₹2,450 crore under a Master Resolution Agreement, resulting in a total waiver of approximately ₹6,981.49 crore, comprising principal waiver of ₹2,767.85 crore and unpaid interest waiver of ₹4,213.63 crore. The Assessing Officer treated the principal waiver as money received without consideration under Section 56(2)(x), and the CIT(A) upheld the addition. According to the supplied account, the Delhi ITAT deleted the entire addition of ₹2,767.85 crore, holding that extinguishment of an existing loan liability through a negotiated commercial settlement did not constitute a fresh receipt of money without consideration. The Tribunal reportedly recognised that the borrowing financed construction of a power plant and that the settlement involved substantial commercial consideration. The ruling highlights the distinction between capital and trading liabilities, actual receipt of money and remission of debt, and the significance of earlier tax deductions. However, the cited ITAT order remains independently unverified, and the taxability of loan waivers under amended Section 28(iv) requires examination of the applicable facts and law. This legal landscape has been further clarified by the recent landmark judgment of the Delhi ITAT in Jindal India Power Limited v. DCIT (Order dated 09.10.2026), which quashed a massive tax addition of ₹2,768 Crore levied on a principal loan waiver under a One-Time Settlement (OTS). Waiver of Interest Claimed as Deduction in Earlier Years (Taxable u/s 41(1)) Statutory Requirement: Section 41(1) operates on the principle of recoupment of loss or expenditure. It applies strictly when an allowance or deduction has been previously claimed by the assessee and allowed in a prior assessment year. Taxability: Where an assessee following the mercantile system debits accrued loan interest to the Profit & Loss Account and obtains a tax deduction u/s 36(1)(iii) in earlier years, a subsequent waiver of that unpaid interest under an OTS scheme represents a cessation of an allowed trading liability. Consequently, the entire waived interest is brought to tax as business income under Section 41(1) in the year of waiver. Waiver of Unpaid Interest Disallowed u/s 43B or Unclaimed (Not Taxable u/s 41(1)) Absence of Prior Tax Benefit: Under Section 43B, interest payable to banks, financial institutions, or NBFCs is allowable as a deduction only upon actual payment. If the interest was never paid, it was disallowed under Section 43B or never debited to P&L in the first place. No Tax Impact: Because no prior tax benefit or deduction was ever granted in previous assessment years, the foundational prerequisite of Section 41(1) is absent. Therefore, the subsequent waiver of such unpaid/disallowed interest carries no tax liability and cannot be added back to taxable income. Pre-Amendment Position on Waiver of Principal Loan Amount (Un-amended Sec. 28(iv) & Sec. 41(1)) The Rule in CIT v. Mahindra & Mahindra Ltd. (2018) 404 ITR 1 (SC): Prior to the Finance Act, 2023 amendment, the Supreme Court settled the non-taxability of principal loan waivers on two fronts: 1. Section 41(1) Inapplicable: A principal term loan is a capital liability, not a trading liability or expense claimed in the P&L Account. Its waiver does not amount to a cessation of trading liability. 2. Section 28(iv) Inapplicable: The Apex Court held that unamended Section 28(iv) applied strictly to benefits or perquisites received in kind other than in the shape of money/cash. Since a loan waiver represents a monetary/cash benefit, it was completely excluded from Section 28(iv). Post-Amendment Position (Finance Act, 2023) — Loans for Capital Assets / Plant & Machinery Statutory Amendment: The Finance Act, 2023 amended Section 28(iv) w.e.f. 04.2023 (AY 2024-25 onwards) to include benefits/perquisites received “in cash or in kind”. Capital Receipt Protection: Despite the inclusion of cash benefits, where a loan was specifically obtained for setting up, expanding, or acquiring capital assets (plant, machinery, power plant, real estate), the principal loan waiver retains its character as a capital account transaction / capital receipt. Adjustment to Asset Cost u/s 43(1): Section 28(iv) taxes revenue or operational business benefits. A capital asset loan waiver is not business income under Section 28(iv). Instead, it reduces the “actual cost” of the capital asset under Explanation 10 to Section 43(1), which proportionately lowers Written Down Value (WDV) and future depreciation claims. Post-Amendment Position (Finance Act, 2023) — Working Capital / Trading / Revenue Account Loans Operational / Trading Character: Working capital or circulating capital loans are obtained for day-to-day trade operations (purchasing raw material, inventory, meeting operational costs). Taxability under Amended Section 28(iv): Following the Finance Act, 2023 amendment, monetary benefits arising from business are explicitly brought into the tax net. Because working capital liabilities directly relate to trading operations and revenue account expenses, the waiver of a working capital loan provides a monetary benefit in circulating capital, making it taxable as business income under Section 28(iv) (or as a trade surplus under the doctrine of CIT v. T.V. Sundaram Iyengar & Sons Ltd.). Landmark Judgment: Delhi ITAT Deletes ₹2,767.85 Crore Demand (Jindal India Power Limited) In a pivotal ruling, the ITAT Delhi Bench ‘A’ (Jindal India Power Limited v. DCIT, ITA Nos. 4966/DEL/2026, Order dated 09.10.2026) addressed the taxability of a massive loan waiver granted under a Master Resolution Agreement (MRA). Fact-Matrix of the Case: Total Outstanding Liability: ₹9,431.49 Crore (Principal: ₹5,217.85 Crore; Unpaid Accrued Interest: ₹4,213.63 Crore). MRA / OTS Settlement: The debt was settled for a lump-sum payment of ₹2,450 Crore. Waiver Granted by Lenders: Total waiver of ₹6,981.49 Crore, comprising: Principal Loan Waiver: ₹2,767.85 Crore Unpaid Interest Waiver: ₹4,213.63 Crore Accounting & Tax Return: The company credited ₹6,981.49 Crore as an “Exceptional Item” in its Profit & Loss Account. In its computation of income, it excluded the entire ₹6,981.49 Crore (treating the principal waiver as a non-taxable capital receipt and the interest waiver as non-taxable since it was never claimed as a deduction). The Revenue’s Novel Attempt u/s 56(2)(x): Rejection of Sec 28(iv) & 41(1): The Assessing Officer (AO) admitted that under Mahindra & Mahindra (404 ITR 1 SC), Section 28(iv) and Section 41(1) could not be applied to the principal waiver. Addition u/s 56(2)(x): However, the AO illegally invoked Section 56(2)(x) (income from other sources), treating the principal loan waiver of ₹2,767.85 Crore as a “receipt of money/cash without consideration”. The CIT(A) upheld this addition. Key Findings & Final Ruling of the Delhi ITAT: 1. Capital Account Character: The ITAT observed that the principal loan was availed for acquiring capital assets (constructing a power plant). The waiver was strictly on capital account and constituted a non-taxable capital receipt. 2. Section 56(2)(x) Cannot Apply to Loan Waivers: Section 56(2)(x) requires an actual, fresh receipt of a sum of money without consideration. A loan waiver under an OTS/MRA merely discharges a liability and is not “without consideration”, as it is executed pursuant to a binding commercial compromise where the borrower pays a negotiated settlement amount (₹2,450 Crore in this case). 3. Harmonious Construction: The ITAT held that the Legislature never intended to bring capital loan waivers to tax under residual provision Section 56(2)(x), nor could the Revenue bypass Section 41(1) and Section 28(iv) by misapplying gift provisions. 4. Complete Deletion of Demand: The Delhi ITAT deleted the entire addition of ₹2,768 Crore, reaffirming that loan waivers for capital asset acquisition remain completely immune from tax additions.

Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); State Bank of India Vs Deputy Director (Appellate Tribunal under SAFEMA, New Delhi) PMLA Attachment of SBI’s Mortgaged Properties Set Aside for Failure to Meet Section 5(1) Conditions The Appellate Tribunal under SAFEMA at New Delhi set aside an order confirming attachment under the Prevention of Money Laundering Act, 2002 (PMLA), concerning properties mortgaged to a consortium led by State Bank of India. The Tribunal held that the twin conditions required under Section 5(1) could not coexist on the facts, given the mortgage and existing Debt Recovery Tribunal proceedings. The ruling was delivered by Member Shri V. Anandarajan on October 13, 2026, in State Bank of India v. Deputy Director, Directorate of Enforcement, Bangalore, Appeal No. FPA-PMLA-2738/BNG/2018. Background of the dispute The proceedings arose from allegations concerning merchandise trade transactions involving M/s. Future Metal Private Limited, M/s. Future Exim India Private Limited and overseas entities. A complaint alleged conspiracy and siphoning of approximately USD 250 million overseas through transactions facilitated by State Trading Corporation Limited (STCL). Following registration of the police case and subsequent investigation by the CBI, the Enforcement Directorate (ED) registered two Enforcement Case Information Reports in October 2009. According to the investigation described in the order, STCL entered into arrangements for trading copper and nickel through overseas sellers and buyers. A consortium of eight banks, led by SBI, facilitated the transactions through letters of credit. The overseas buyers failed to make the expected payments, while the letters of credit devolved and payments were made to overseas sellers. ED alleged that properties had been acquired using proceeds of crime and projected as untainted assets. A provisional attachment order dated March 31, 2018, covered various properties. The Adjudicating Authority confirmed the attachment on September 12, 2018. SBI challenged that confirmation insofar as it concerned the mortgaged properties forming the subject matter of its appeal. SBI relies on mortgage and recovery proceedings SBI submitted that title deeds relating to approximately 154.67 acres in Patiala, Punjab, had been deposited with the consortium as collateral security. The bank claimed a mortgage by deposit of title deeds within Section 58(f) of the Transfer of Property Act, 1882. It maintained that the original documents remained in its possession. SBI also relied on recovery proceedings initiated before the Debt Recovery Tribunal (DRT) in 2011 and an injunction restraining alienation of the properties. Proceedings under the SARFAESI Act had likewise commenced, with symbolic possession taken in 2011. The bank argued that these measures preceded ED’s attachment by several years. It further submitted that the properties had been acquired before the relevant criminal transactions and disputed their characterisation as direct proceeds of crime. ED defends attachment ED argued that the PMLA empowered it to attach proceeds of crime and that its provisions had overriding effect under Section 71. It contended that the SARFAESI Act, the recovery legislation and the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code did not prevail over the PMLA. ED also submitted that SBI should seek release of the properties before the Special Court under Sections 8(7) and 8(8). The Tribunal, however, examined whether the attachment satisfied the requirements of the PMLA itself. Twin conditions under Section 5(1) The Tribunal explained that Section 5(1) required recorded reasons to believe, based on material in the authority’s possession, that a person possessed proceeds of crime. Additionally, there had to be reasons to believe that those proceeds were likely to be concealed, transferred or otherwise dealt with in a manner that could frustrate confiscation proceedings. Both conditions had to coexist for a valid provisional attachment. The Tribunal found that the mortgage, possession of title deeds and prior recovery measures were not contested by ED. Considering the borrower as the person possessing the property, the Tribunal held that the second condition was not satisfied. The borrower could not conceal, transfer or deal with property already mortgaged to the bank and covered by a DRT order. This remained so even if physical possession continued with the borrower. Alternatively, treating the bank as the relevant person, the Tribunal held that the first condition was not met. In the bank’s hands, the properties constituted collateral for financial facilities validly advanced in the ordinary course of business. Overriding effect did not cure the defect The Tribunal held that ED’s reliance on the overriding effect of the PMLA did not establish the validity of this attachment. Even accepting that the other enactments did not prevail over the PMLA, the attachment still had to satisfy Section 5(1). Those statutory conditions could not coexist in the circumstances. The Tribunal therefore allowed the appeal on this ground without deciding the other principal issues. It also observed that SBI’s objection to being relegated to the Special Court was supported by the Supreme Court decision in Nav Nirman Builders relied upon by the bank. Outcome The impugned order was set aside only insofar as it concerned the properties mortgaged to the bank and forming the subject matter of the appeal. No costs were awarded. The ruling rests on failure to satisfy Section 5(1), rather than a determination that secured-creditor legislation overrides the PMLA. Analysis & Findings, Order 24. I have given careful consideration to the facts on record and the rival contentions of the parties. At the outset, I find that the factual contentions of the Appellant Bank have not been seriously contested by the Respondent Directorate. 25. The main arguments advanced on behalf of the appellant are as follows: (i) That at the time of their provisional attachment the properties already stood mortgaged to the consortium of banks led by the appellant bank, the title deeds of the properties were in the possession of the Bank, and recovery action had also been initiated by the bank under the relevant laws; (ii) That properties were acquired prior to the period of scheduled offence and therefore, the same could not be held to be the direct proceeds of crime; (iii) That appellant cannot be relegated to the remedy of filing an application before the Special Court under Section 8(7) and 8(8) of the PMLA for release of the attached properties. 26. Coming to the first argument of the appellant, it is contention of the appellant that the properties in question, which are the subject matter of attachment in the present case, were under deemed mortgage by deposit of title deeds within the meaning of Section 58(f) of the Transfer of Property Act, 1882 and the same were still in the possession of the appellant bank as collateral security for debt granted to the State Trading Cooperation Ltd. It is also submitted that the appellant bank had informed ED on 11.06.2018 that the proceedings under the Recovery of Debts and Bankruptcy Act, 1993 (RDB) Act stand initiated and the Debts Recovery Tribunal has passed an injunction order on 18.07.2011, restraining the alienation of the properties. Notice under Section 13(2) of the SARFAESI Act was issued on 23.08.2011, and symbolic possession of properties was taken on 28.10.2011 and 16.11.2011, respectively. The aforesaid contentions of the appellant have not been contested by ED. It is also not in doubt that the provisional attachment order, which has been confirmed by the Ld. AA through the impugned order, was passed on 31.03.2018, which was many years after the aforesaid recovery action and orders thereon by the Debts Recovery Tribunal. Section of 5(1) of the PMLA reads as below: “[(1) Where the Director or any other officer not below the rank of Deputy Director authorised by the Director for the purposes of this section, has reason to believe (the reason for such belief to be recorded in writing), on the basis of material in his possession, that- (a) any person is in possession of any proceeds of crime; and (b) such proceeds of crime are likely to be concealed, transferred or dealt with in any manner which may result in frustrating any proceedings relating to confiscation of such proceeds of crime under this Chapter, he may, by order in writing, provisionally attach such property for a period not exceeding one hundred and eighty days from the date of the order, in such manner as may be prescribed: Provided that no such order of attachment shall be made unless, in relation to the scheduled offence, a report has been forwarded to a Magistrate under section 173 of the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973 (2 of 1974), or a complaint has been filed by a person authorised to investigate the offence mentioned in that Schedule, before a Magistrate or court for taking cognizance of the scheduled offence, as the case may be, or a similar report or complaint has been made or filed under the corresponding law of any other country : Provided further that, notwithstanding anything contained in [first proviso], any property of any person may be attached under this section if the Director or any other officer not below the rank of Deputy Director authorised by him for the purposes of this section has reason to believe (the reasons for such belief to be recorded in writing), on the basis of material in his possession, that if such property involved in money laundering is not attached immediately under this Chapter, the non-attachment of the property is likely to frustrate any proceeding under this Act.]. [Provided also that for the purposes of computing the period of one hundred and eighty days, the period during which the proceedings under this section is stayed by the High Court, shall be excluded and a further period not exceeding thirty days from the date of order of vacation of such stay order shall be counted.]” 27. Upon a plain reading of the aforesaid provision, the essential pre-conditions for making a valid provisional attachment are, the existence of reason to believe, which is to be recorded in writing that, firstly, any person is in possession of proceeds of crime, and, secondly, that “such” proceeds of crime (i.e., proceeds of crime which is in possession of “any person”) are likely to be concealed, transferred or dealt with in a manner which may result in frustrating the proceedings relating to confiscation of “such” proceeds of crime. The co-existence of both the conditions is necessary in order for the authority acting under Section 5(1) to make a valid attachment. 28. In the present case, if the borrower who is accused of the offence of money laundering is considered to be the “any person” referred to in Section 5(1)(a), then the second condition would not be met as the said borrower was in no position to conceal, transfer or deal with the property which was under mortgage to the appellant Bank and in respect of which an order of the DRT was already in place This would be so even if he continued to be in physical possession of the property. Conversely, if the phrase “any person” under Section 5(1)(a) were to be considered to be a reference to the appellant Bank, the first pre-condition, i.e., that it (the Bank) was in possession of “any proceeds of crime” would not be met as, in the hands of the Bank, the property did not constitute “proceeds of crime” but rather collateral security for financial facility validly advanced in the usual course of business. 29. The respondent has contended that the provisions of the SARFAESI Act, the RDB Act, and Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (IBC), do not prevail over the PMLA. They seek to rely upon the judgment of Hon’ble Delhi High Court in the case of the Axis Bank in this regard. Having considered the argument, I find that the same argument does not travel very far in supporting the action of the ED in attaching the properties which already stood mortgaged to the banks, and in respect of which the orders had already been passed by the Ld. DRT. Even if, the position that the provisions of other Acts, i.e., RDB SARFAESI Acts do not prevail over the PMLA were to be accepted, it is the requirement of PMLA itself that the authority attaching property under Section 5(1) must have the twin reasons to believe as discussed above, on the basis of material in its possession which could not have co-existed in the facts of the present case. 30. In the light of the above discussion, the appeal filed by the Bank succeeds on this ground alone, and the other issues mentioned in para-25 (supra) need not be gone into. As regards the judgment of the Nagpur Bench of the Hon’ble Bombay High Court, in Joint Director of Enforcement v. HDFC Bank, the same is on the issue as to whether the provision of the RDB Act and SARFAESI Act would override the provisions of PMLA so as to render the attachment illegal. In the present case, the order of provisional attachment under Section 5 of the PMLA has been found to be illegal not because of any conflict with the RDB Act or SARFAESI Act, but because it was not in accordance with the provisions of Section 5(1) of the PMLA itself, for the reasons discussed in para 27 and 28 (supra). However, before parting with this order, it may also be mentioned that the contention of the appellant that the appellant cannot be relegated to filing an application before the Special Court under Section 8(7) and 8(8) of PMLA is also well supported by the judgment of the Hon’ble Supreme Court in Nav Nirman Builders on which reliance has been placed by the appellant. 31. Accordingly, in light of the above discussion, the present appeal is hereby allowed and the impugned order is set aside qua the properties which were under mortgage to the Bank and constitute the subject matter of this appeal. 32. Pending applications, if any shall disposed of. 33. No order as to costs.

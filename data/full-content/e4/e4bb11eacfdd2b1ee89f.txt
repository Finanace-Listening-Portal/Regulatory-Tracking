If delivered, it would be the first rate hike since February 2023, after the central bank cut the repo rate by a cumulative 125 basis points in 2025 to 5.25 per cent.The potential shift comes as inflation has risen and the transmission of earlier policy changes through bank lending and deposit rates remains uneven. Consumer price inflation rose to 4.82 per cent in August, while the weighted average rate on fresh rupee loans increased to 8.61 per cent in August from 8.52 per cent in July. Advt At the same time, banks have not yet faced an equivalent increase in the cost of new deposits. The weighted average rate on fresh rupee term deposits fell to 5.67 per cent in August from 5.85 per cent in July, widening the gap between fresh lending and deposit rates by 27 basis points in a month. Rates on outstanding loans and deposits also moved only marginally during the month.The transmission of any rate hike to borrowers could nevertheless be relatively quick for a large portion of floating rate loans. RBI data show that external benchmark linked loans accounted for 68.2 per cent of outstanding floating rate rupee loans of scheduled commercial banks at the end of June 2026, up from 67.6 per cent at the end of March. MCLR linked loans accounted for another 29.6 per cent.This means a rate increase would raise borrowing costs more quickly for loans linked to external benchmarks, while loans linked to MCLR would depend on the timing of individual reset periods. The impact on borrowers would therefore vary across products and lenders rather than moving uniformly across the banking system.For banks, the immediate effect on margins would depend on how quickly lending rates reprice relative to deposit costs. A sustained tightening cycle could increase competition for deposits and eventually push up funding costs, although the latest data show that fresh deposit rates were still declining in August. Advt Pressure on NBFCsNBFCs could face greater funding pressure because a significant portion of their funding comes through bank borrowings and market instruments. Higher bond yields can therefore feed into their cost of funds even before the full impact of a policy rate increase is reflected in bank deposit rates.The funding environment has already become more important as the RBI has been absorbing surplus liquidity. The central bank conducted VRRR operations on October 5 and announced another overnight VRRR auction of Rs2.5 lakh crore for October 6, with reversal scheduled for October 7.The liquidity adjustment also comes against the backdrop of the large FCNR(B) mobilisation under the RBI's special forex swap facility. FCNR(B) deposits mobilised under the facility stood at $132.98 billion, forming the bulk of the total $143.596 billion inflows under the facility. Advt The FCNR(B) inflows have also created a new lending dynamic. Loans against fixed deposits rose 43.2 per cent year on year to Rs2.04 lakh crore in August, although RBI data do not establish how much of this increase was directly attributable to FCNR(B) deposits. The linkage is therefore significant but should not be treated as a one to one conversion of FCNR(B) deposits into loans.For depositors, a sustained rate hiking cycle could eventually improve returns on fresh fixed deposits, but banks may not increase deposit rates immediately or uniformly. Existing fixed deposits would also generally retain their contracted rates until maturity or renewal. Advt The bigger issue for banks and NBFCs is therefore not the impact of a single 25 basis point move, but whether the RBI begins a prolonged tightening cycle. A sustained increase would put greater pressure on lenders to balance credit growth with funding costs, while borrowers, depositors and lenders would face different degrees of rate transmission.The October policy could consequently mark a shift from the largely easing driven banking environment of 2025 towards a phase in which the cost and availability of funding become increasingly important determinants of loan growth and profitability.

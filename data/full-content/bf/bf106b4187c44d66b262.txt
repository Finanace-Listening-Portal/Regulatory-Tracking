Muthusamy Vs Additional Director General (Adjudication) (CESTAT Mumbai) Summary: The Customs, Excise and Service Tax Appellate Tribunal (CESTAT), Mumbai, in S. Muthusamy v. Additional Director General (Adjudication), Directorate of Revenue Intelligence, Mumbai, along with the connected appeals of N.R. Jayant, D.N. Mathur and Dhananjay Datar, examined the legality of penalties imposed under Sections 112 and 114AA of the Customs Act, 1962 in connection with the alleged fraudulent import of engineering drawings by ABG Shipyard Ltd. The dispute arose from investigations conducted by the Directorate of Revenue Intelligence (DRI), which alleged that the shipyard had imported drawings and designs for vessels, jack-up rigs, ship-lift systems and shipyard modernisation projects at highly inflated values, even though those drawings allegedly had little or no commercial utility for its ongoing operations. The imports were declared under Customs Tariff Heading 49119990 and were covered by the duty exemption available under Notification No. 12/2012-Cus. dated 17 March 2012 and its predecessor Notification No. 15/2005-Cus. dated 1 March 2005. The goods were purportedly supplied by Norcrane & Winch Holdings Pte Ltd., Singapore, and their declared aggregate value was approximately US$104.62 million, equivalent to ₹582.18 crore. The Department alleged that drawings already available with the importer had been modified, sent abroad and subsequently re-imported through a purported supplier to facilitate outward remittances of foreign exchange under the guise of legitimate commercial transactions. By Order-in-Original dated 27 February 2017, the adjudicating authority reduced the declared value of the imported drawings to nil under the Customs Valuation (Determination of Value of Imported Goods) Rules, 2007, ordered confiscation of goods in 20 uncleared consignments under Section 111(m), imposed a redemption fine of ₹1 lakh and levied individual penalties of ₹10 lakh under Section 112(a) and ₹5 lakh under Section 114AA on each of the four appellants. The employees challenged these penalties, raising fundamental questions concerning the jurisdiction of Customs authorities, the legal requirements for rejecting declared transaction values, the evidentiary basis for allegations of overvaluation and the applicability of penal provisions where the imported goods were otherwise exempt from customs duty. The appellants contended that the entire proceedings rested on an erroneous application of the statutory framework governing customs valuation under Section 14 of the Customs Act. They argued that valuation provisions primarily existed to determine the assessable value of imported goods for levying customs duty and could not automatically be employed as an independent mechanism for investigating suspected foreign exchange irregularities unrelated to the collection of customs revenue. Since the drawings were exempt from duty, the allegation of excessive invoice values did not, by itself, establish a customs duty evasion or justify confiscation. The appellants relied particularly on Knowledge Infrastructure Systems Private Limited v. Additional Director General, DRI, Mumbai, Final Order Nos. A/86617-86619/2018 dated 31 May 2018, which examined the limits of customs jurisdiction in cases involving alleged overvaluation and outward remittances. They also relied on Lalitpur Power Generation Co. Ltd. v. Commissioner of Customs, New Delhi, 2017 (356) ELT 82 (Tri.-Del.), and Sahil Diamonds Pvt. Ltd. v. Commissioner of Customs, 2010 (250) ELT 310 (Tri.-Ahmd.), the latter having been affirmed by the Supreme Court, to contend that confiscation and penalties could not be sustained in the circumstances of duty-exempt imports considered in those decisions. It was further submitted that the Department had not established any direct involvement of the individual appellants in falsely declaring values in bills of entry, manufacturing the allegedly fabricated drawings or deliberately submitting false documents for customs purposes. The appellants questioned the reliance placed on untested statements and referred to Basudev Garg v. Commissioner of Customs, 2013 (294) ELT 353 (Del.), concerning the evidentiary safeguards applicable to statements relied upon by Customs authorities. They maintained that the mere existence of inflated invoices, administrative irregularities or internal purchase orders could not establish liability under Section 114AA without evidence of knowing or intentional use of materially false documents in a transaction governed by the Customs Act. The Revenue opposed these arguments, contending that the imports were sham transactions arranged to facilitate illegal foreign exchange remittances and that the imported goods had been correctly confiscated for misdeclaration. It also argued that the Tribunal should not reopen the confiscation findings because the importer had not pursued the same appellate remedy and that new grounds raised by the employees should not be entertained at the appellate stage. After examining the rival contentions and the investigation record, the Tribunal, comprising C.J. Mathew, Member (Technical), and Dr. Suvendu Kumar Pati, Member (Judicial), held that the Customs authorities had incorrectly treated the alleged commercial uselessness of the drawings as sufficient justification for determining their customs value at nil. The Tribunal explained that Section 14 of the Customs Act, 1962 contemplated valuation through recognised commercial principles and prescribed statutory methods, rather than a subjective assessment of whether particular goods were useful to an individual importer. Goods could possess intrinsic commercial value even when an importer had no immediate operational requirement for them. Consequently, the existence of alternative drawings, the advanced stage of ship construction or doubts concerning the necessity of fresh designs could not independently justify substituting the declared transaction value with nil. The Tribunal observed that the adjudicating authority had adopted inconsistent positions by assessing the imported goods at nil value while simultaneously allowing redemption on the basis of their alleged scrap value. If the goods were capable of redemption as scrap, they necessarily possessed an ascertainable value, and the applicable classification and duty consequences had to be examined under the statutory scheme. The Tribunal further considered the operation of Rules 3 to 12 of the Customs Valuation Rules, 2007, emphasising that rejection of declared value and its subsequent redetermination required compliance with the prescribed valuation framework. It distinguished the authority to question the accuracy of a transaction value for assessment purposes from the separate statutory requirements necessary to establish deliberate misdeclaration warranting confiscation. The Tribunal explained that a difference between declared value and subsequently determined assessable value did not automatically establish an offence under Section 111(m). The Department was required to establish, through sufficiently reliable evidence, circumstances showing that the declared transaction concealed the actual monetary consideration or involved an impermissible arrangement. Although the Customs Act recognised overvaluation as a possible ground for confiscation where the statutory ingredients were established, the Tribunal held that a valuation adjustment derived merely from assumptions and procedural mechanisms could not substitute for proof of the alleged offence. The Tribunal also observed that the supposed relationship between the importer and overseas supplier had not been satisfactorily tested against the criteria governing related persons under Rule 2(2) of the Valuation Rules. The Tribunal placed substantial reliance on its earlier decision in Knowledge Infrastructure Systems Private Limited, which had examined the jurisdictional boundaries of Customs authorities in matters involving overvaluation and alleged financial transactions. It reiterated that the powers conferred by the Customs Act must be exercised for purposes contemplated by that enactment, particularly the levy and collection of customs duty and enforcement of import prohibitions, and could not be expanded indefinitely to address every suspected irregularity connected with an international commercial transaction. Referring to Rib Tapes (India) Pvt. Ltd. v. Union of India, AIR 1986 SC 2014, the Tribunal recognised the historical amendment of Section 111(m) to bring misdeclaration of value within the scope of confiscation, but clarified that the amendment did not confer unrestricted authority to reopen completed assessments merely because the Department suspected over-invoicing. In the present case, the goods were exempt from customs duty, and the Department had not demonstrated a customs duty implication justifying reopening the cleared imports through the valuation provisions. The Tribunal further distinguished the decision in Parkash Sancheti v. Commissioner of Customs, 2013 (292) ELT 273 (Tri.-Ahmd.), relied upon by the Revenue, observing that the dispute concerning diamonds and Kimberley Process Certificates did not determine the broader jurisdictional issues involved in the present appeals. It also rejected the contention that the earlier decision in Knowledge Infrastructure Systems should be disregarded merely because another coordinate Bench had reached a different conclusion in a distinguishable factual setting. Regarding the individual penalties, the Tribunal examined the distinct statutory requirements of Sections 112 and 114AA. It noted that Section 112 penalties were connected with liability to confiscation, while Section 114AA required the knowing or intentional making, signing or use of materially false or incorrect declarations, statements or documents in customs-related business. The alleged fabrication of purchase orders did not establish the necessary statutory nexus because those purchase orders had not been furnished, or required to be furnished, along with the relevant bills of entry and had been called for only during the investigation. Further, the Department had not established that the individual appellants were responsible for any legally relevant false declaration concerning the uncleared consignments. The Tribunal also observed that the assessment value adopted as nil was inconsistent with the different value used for redemption and penalty purposes, making the quantification of the penalties legally unsustainable. The Tribunal further examined the Department’s allegation that the drawings had originally been prepared in India, exported to Singapore and subsequently re-imported to facilitate foreign exchange remittances. It observed that Section 20 of the Customs Act, 1962 specifically contemplated the treatment of goods manufactured or produced in India and subsequently imported. The mere re-importation of goods previously exported did not, by itself, constitute an offence, and an importer could claim applicable exemptions subject to statutory conditions. In the present proceedings, however, the Department had not established the underlying export transactions through reliable records, and its conclusion that the drawings were re-imported Indian-origin goods remained based substantially on inference. The Tribunal held that the absence of evidence establishing the alleged exports further weakened the basis for attributing wrongdoing to the individual appellants. It also noted that the importer had not claimed the special treatment potentially available for re-imported goods and that the Department had not undertaken the valuation exercise required if such treatment was to be applied. Whether the drawings were considered under the prescribed customs valuation framework or in the context of the applicable exemption notifications, the proceedings did not establish the necessary duty implications or statutory conditions for sustaining penalties under Section 112. The Tribunal concluded that the adjudicating authority had relied upon legally unsustainable valuation assumptions, inconsistent treatment of the imported goods and insufficient evidence connecting the individual employees with customs offences. It therefore set aside the impugned order, insofar as challenged in the four appeals, and allowed the appeals of S. Muthusamy, N.R. Jayant, D.N. Mathur and Dhananjay Datar. The decision clarifies that suspected over-invoicing or outward foreign exchange remittances cannot, without satisfaction of the relevant statutory requirements, justify the imposition of customs penalties merely through reassessment of declared import values. It also reinforces the distinction between customs valuation for assessment purposes and the independent evidentiary and jurisdictional requirements for confiscation and penalties under Sections 111, 112 and 114AA of the Customs Act, 1962. Cases Discussed 1. Knowledge Infrastructure Systems Private Limited & Ors. v. Additional Director General, Directorate of Revenue Intelligence, Mumbai, Final Order Nos. A/86617-86619/2018 dated 31.05.2018, Customs Appeal Nos. C/85234-85236/2017 (CESTAT Mumbai) — Extensively followed as the principal precedent governing the jurisdictional limitations on customs valuation, confiscation and penalties. The Tribunal reiterated that the Customs Act cannot be invoked as an unrestricted mechanism for investigating alleged over-invoicing or outward remittances without satisfying the statutory requirements concerning duty liability, prohibition and misdeclaration. The decision also explained the restrictions on reopening completed assessments merely to redetermine import values. See the related principles concerning customs valuation and duty-exempt imports. 2. Lalitpur Power Generation Co. Ltd. v. Commissioner of Customs, New Delhi, 2017 (356) ELT 82 (Tri.-Del.) (CESTAT Delhi) — Relied upon by the appellants concerning the importation of engineering drawings and designs exempt from customs duty under Notification No. 12/2012-Cus. The decision supported the contention that customs valuation, confiscation and penalties could not be sustained merely on allegations of inflated values in the circumstances examined. It also considered the effect of service tax already discharged on the related services. 3. Basudev Garg v. Commissioner of Customs, 2013 (294) ELT 353 (Delhi High Court) — Relied upon by the appellants regarding the evidentiary safeguards under Section 138B of the Customs Act, 1962. Statements recorded during investigation could not ordinarily be relied upon against a noticee without satisfying the applicable statutory requirements and affording the opportunity of cross-examination. 4. Prakash Sancheti v. Commissioner of Customs, Ahmedabad, 2013 (292) ELT 273 (Tri.-Ahmd.) (CESTAT Ahmedabad) — Cited by the Revenue to support confiscation and penalties in cases involving alleged overvaluation of imported diamonds. The Tribunal distinguished the decision because it concerned the requirements relating to Kimberley Process Certificates and did not determine the jurisdictional limitations examined in Knowledge Infrastructure Systems Private Limited. The decision is also discussed in related TaxGuru coverage of customs valuation of rough diamonds. 5. Sahil Diamonds Pvt. Ltd. v. Commissioner of Customs, Ahmedabad, 2010 (250) ELT 310 (Tri.-Ahmd.), affirmed in Commissioner of Customs v. Sahil Diamonds Pvt. Ltd., 2010 (257) ELT A22 (Supreme Court) — Relied upon through Lalitpur Power Generation Co. Ltd. for the treatment of duty-exempt imports and the circumstances in which penalties under Section 112 could not be sustained. The decision also examined the evidentiary requirements for rejecting declared transaction values. 6. Suraj Diamonds (India) Ltd. v. Commissioner of Customs (Airport), Mumbai, 2008 (227) ELT 471 (Tribunal), 2008 (86) RLT 400 — Referred to in the quoted reasoning of Sahil Diamonds Pvt. Ltd. concerning the applicability of penalties under Section 112 in cases involving duty-exempt imports of rough diamonds. 7. Nalakath Spices Trading Co. v. Commissioner of Customs, 2007 (213) ELT 283 (Tribunal), 2007 (80) RLT 797 (CESTAT Bangalore) — Cited through Sahil Diamonds Pvt. Ltd. as an earlier precedent relevant to the interpretation of penalty provisions under the Customs Act. 8. Jay AR Enterprises, 2007 (210) ELT 459 (Tribunal), 2007 (79) RLT 291 (CESTAT Chennai) — Referred to through Sahil Diamonds Pvt. Ltd. as part of the line of authorities considered while examining the legality of penalties in relation to duty-exempt imports. 9. Shree Subhadra Industries v. Commissioner of Central Excise, Chennai, 2001 (137) ELT 1405 (Tri.-Chennai) — Cited in the extract from Sahil Diamonds Pvt. Ltd. as an earlier authority relied upon in considering the limits of statutory penalty provisions. 10. Commissioner of Customs & Central Excise, Goa v. Dempo Engineering Works Ltd., 2015 (319) ELT 359 (Supreme Court) — Relied upon by the Revenue to oppose the admission of additional grounds during the appellate proceedings. The Tribunal distinguished the decision, holding that fresh questions of law arising from subsequent judicial developments stood on a different footing from fresh factual grounds requiring verification by the original authority. 11. Mirah Exports Pvt. Ltd. v. Collector of Customs, (1998) 3 SCC 292, 1998 (98) ELT 3 (Supreme Court) — Referred to concerning the burden placed upon Customs authorities to establish that the declared transaction value did not represent the actual consideration. The Tribunal explained that rejection of the declared value must be supported by legally sufficient material and the prescribed valuation procedure. 12. Rib Tapes (India) Pvt. Ltd. v. Union of India & Ors., AIR 1986 SC 2014 (Supreme Court) — Relied upon in examining the legislative history and interpretation of Section 111(m) of the Customs Act, 1962. The Supreme Court’s interpretation of the provision before its amendment was considered while explaining the statutory treatment of misdeclaration of value and the limitations on confiscation. 13. The Tata Iron & Steel Co. Ltd. v. State of Bihar, AIR 1958 SC 452 (Supreme Court) — Discussed through Knowledge Infrastructure Systems Private Limited with reference to the constitutional concept of nexus and the permissible boundaries of taxation jurisdiction. The Tribunal drew upon the reasoning concerning the relationship between the legislative power to tax and the transactions sought to be regulated.

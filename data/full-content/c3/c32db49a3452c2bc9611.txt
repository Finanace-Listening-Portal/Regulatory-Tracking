Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); ValueLabs Services Private Limited Vs Dasari Balaiah (GSTAT Hyderabad) Summary : The GST Appellate Tribunal (GSTAT), Hyderabad, in ValueLabs Services Private Limited v. Dasari Balaiah, Additional Commissioner & Ors., Appeal No. APL/88/HYD/2026, Final Order No. 23/COURT-II/HYD/2026, dated 11 November 2026, partly allowed the taxpayer’s appeal concerning the denial of input tax credit (ITC) of ₹41,10,666 on account of discrepancies between Form GSTR-3B and Form GSTR-2A for July 2017 to March 2018. The disputed demand comprised CGST of ₹20,24,184, SGST of ₹20,24,184 and IGST of ₹62,299, together with interest under Section 50 and an equivalent penalty under Section 74 of the CGST Act, 2017. The Department alleged that the taxpayer had claimed ITC of ₹53,53,143 in GSTR-3B against a net GSTR-2A figure of ₹12,42,477 after adjustment of credit notes. The taxpayer maintained that the discrepancy arose from supplier-side reporting errors, subsequent amendments to GSTR-1 and invoices reported against its earlier GST registration. The central issue was whether such mismatches justified reversal of credit despite the availability of supplier certificates and supporting documentary evidence. The taxpayer contended that Rule 36(4) of the CGST Rules, 2017, restricting ITC availment by reference to GSTR-2A, was introduced only with effect from 9 October 2019 through Notification No. 49/2019-Central Tax and could not govern FY 2017-18. It relied on Section 16(2), amended B2B entries, supplier certificates, invoices and vendor ledgers to establish eligibility. An amended GSTR-2A entry relating to E-Support Solutions Private Limited reflected an invoice dated 5 August 2017 involving CGST and SGST of ₹12,72,387 each, aggregating ₹25,44,774. The taxpayer also furnished certificates covering Tata Communications (₹8,08,650), STT Global Data Centres (₹4,46,416), Vodafone Mobile Services (₹3,51,000) and Crayon Software (IGST ₹41,869). It argued that the authorities had rejected these documents without identifying defects or examining whether the suppliers had actually discharged their tax liabilities. The Department, however, maintained that ITC was a conditional statutory entitlement and that supplier certificates and amended returns could not automatically establish compliance with Section 16. The Tribunal examined CBIC Circular No. 183/15/2022-GST dated 27 December 2022, issued under Section 168(1), which prescribes a mechanism for resolving GSTR-3B and GSTR-2A mismatches for FY 2017-18 and FY 2018-19. The Circular expressly covers cases where suppliers reported supplies against an incorrect recipient GSTIN and applies to pending adjudication and appellate proceedings. It requires a Chartered Accountant or Cost Accountant certificate bearing a UDIN where the supplier-wise difference exceeds ₹5 lakh and permits a supplier certificate where the difference does not exceed ₹5 lakh. Relying on Ranadey Micronutrients v. Collector of Central Excise, the Tribunal held that the Department was bound to follow the Board’s Circular. Although the Circular did not confer automatic entitlement to ITC, the prescribed certificates constituted a recognised verification mechanism. The Department could examine their authenticity and supporting returns but could not reject them merely because they were certificates or insist upon requirements not contained in the Circular. Examining the documentary evidence, the Tribunal found that the amended B2B entry of E-Support Solutions Private Limited appeared on the Government’s GST portal and disclosed the relevant invoice particulars, taxable value and tax amounts. The appellate authority had rejected this entry because the taxpayer had not produced a separate copy of the invoice, without identifying any evidence that the transaction was fictitious or that the services had not been received. Similarly, the Chartered Accountant certificate for Tata Communications contained the prescribed UDIN, while certificates furnished by STT Global Data Centres, Vodafone Mobile Services and Crayon Software satisfied the applicable supplier-wise monetary requirements. The Department had not demonstrated that it verified the certificates, examined the suppliers’ GSTR-3B returns or identified any specific invoice for which payment was deficient. The Tribunal held that once the taxpayer produced relevant documentary evidence, a general allegation that the material remained unverified could not justify rejection without reasons or contrary evidence. It distinguished State of Karnataka v. Ecom Gill Coffee Trading Private Limited, (2023) 9 SCC 573, because the genuineness of the underlying transactions was not disputed in the present case. The Tribunal further held that the adjudication and appellate orders suffered from serious deficiencies in reasoning. The first appellate authority had described the taxpayer’s explanation as unclear and the supplier certificates as unacceptable without identifying the specific deficiencies or explaining why the prescribed verification mechanism was insufficient. Referring to Kranti Associates Pvt. Ltd. v. Masood Ahmed Khan, (2010) 9 SCC 496, the Tribunal reiterated that quasi-judicial authorities must provide reasons demonstrating consideration of the evidence and submissions. It also accepted the taxpayer’s objection that the Department could not introduce fresh grounds during appellate proceedings. The show-cause notice alleged non-compliance with Section 16(2)(c) on account of alleged non-payment of tax by suppliers, whereas the Department subsequently questioned possession of invoices, receipt of services and general documentary sufficiency. Applying Section 75(7) and the reasoning in Santhome Latex Enterprises v. Commissioner of CGST, the Tribunal held that a demand could not be sustained on grounds beyond those specified in the original notice. On quantification, the Tribunal found that the amended B2B entry and supplier certificates established CGST and SGST credits of ₹20,75,420 each, exceeding the disputed CGST and SGST amounts of ₹20,24,184 each. Consequently, the entire CGST and SGST demands were set aside. The Crayon Software certificate also established entitlement to IGST credit of ₹41,869 out of the disputed IGST of ₹62,299. However, the taxpayer could not produce a certificate or other cogent evidence establishing payment of tax by the supplier in respect of the remaining IGST of ₹20,430. The Tribunal emphasised that Section 155 places the burden of proving ITC eligibility upon the claimant and that Section 16(2)(c) requires actual payment of the corresponding tax to the Government. Having relied on the Circular’s certificate-based mechanism for other invoices, the taxpayer could not disregard the evidentiary requirements for the remaining invoices. Accordingly, ITC of ₹20,430 was held inadmissible, while the balance demand of ₹40,90,236 was set aside. The Tribunal separately considered whether proceedings under Section 74 of the CGST Act were sustainable. It observed that Section 74 requires foundational facts establishing fraud, wilful misstatement or suppression of facts with intent to evade tax. The show-cause notice merely asserted that the discrepancy would have remained undetected without departmental verification and that the taxpayer appeared to have suppressed material facts. Relying on G.R. Infra Projects Limited v. State of Madhya Pradesh, Tata Steel Limited v. Union of India and Continental Foundation Joint Venture Holding v. Commissioner of Central Excise, the Tribunal held that suspicion, detection of a mismatch or mechanical reproduction of statutory expressions could not establish fraudulent intent. The taxpayer had disclosed the disputed ITC in GSTR-3B and GSTR-9, responded to departmental communications and subsequently produced supporting certificates. There was no allegation of fictitious invoices, non-receipt of services or collusion with suppliers. The Tribunal therefore concluded that the essential ingredients of Section 74 had not been established and that the invocation of the extended demand provisions on the ground of fraud or suppression was unsustainable. Applying Section 75(2), the Tribunal directed that the proceedings be treated as arising under Section 73 rather than Section 74, with the surviving liability confined to the inadmissible IGST credit of ₹20,430. The Tribunal accordingly modified the impugned order and partly allowed the appeal, substantially relieving the taxpayer of the original demand of ₹41,10,666. The consequential interest and penalty were to be determined in accordance with the applicable statutory provisions and the modified tax liability, including the requirement to consider actual utilisation for interest purposes. The decision establishes that, for the initial GST implementation years, GSTR-2A mismatches must be examined through the applicable CBIC verification procedure rather than treated as conclusive evidence of ineligible credit. It also reinforces that documentary evidence must receive reasoned consideration, appellate authorities cannot enlarge the grounds of a show-cause notice, and proceedings alleging fraud or suppression require specific supporting facts rather than mere discrepancies in statutory returns. The question is whether that burden has been discharged on the record before us and, if so, on whom the onus lies thereafter. 14.0 The Board issued Circular No. 183/15/2022-GST dated 27.12.2022, in exercise of its powers under section 168(1) of the Act, to deal with the difference between the ITC availed in Form GSTR-3B and that detailed in Form GSTR-2A for FY 2017-18 and 2018-19. Paragraph 2 of the Circular records: “It is mentioned that GSTR-2A could not be made available to the taxpayers on the common portal during the initial stages of implementation of GST. In addition, the proper officer of the actual recipient shall intimate the concerned jurisdictional tax authority of the registered person, whose GSTIN has been mentioned wrongly, that ITC on those transactions is required to be disallowed, if claimed by such recipients in their GSTR-3B. However, allowance of ITC to the actual recipient shall not depend on the completion of the action by the tax authority of such registered person, whose GSTIN has been mentioned wrongly, and such action will be pursued as an independent action.” 14.2 Paragraph 4 of the Circular prescribes the procedure: “4. The proper officer shall first seek the details from the registered person regarding all the invoices on which ITC has been availed by the registered person in his GSTR 3B but which are not reflecting in his GSTR 2A. He shall then ascertain fulfillment of the following conditions of Section 16 of CGST Act in respect of the input tax credit availed on such invoices by the said registered person: i. that he is in possession of a tax invoice or debit note issued by the supplier or such other tax paying documents; ii. that he has received the goods or services or both; iii. that he has made payment for the amount towards the value of supply, along with tax payable thereon, to the supplier. …” 14.3 Paragraphs 4.1.1 and 4.1.2 of the Circular provide for the verification of the condition in section 16(2)(c): “4.1 In order to verify the condition of Section 16(2)(c) of CGST Act that tax on the said supply has been paid by the supplier, the following action may be taken by the proper officer: 4.1.1 In case, where difference between the ITC claimed in GSTR-3B and that available in GSTR 2A of the registered person in respect of a supplier for the said financial year exceeds Rs 5 lakh, the proper officer shall ask the registered person to produce a certificate for the concerned supplier from the Chartered Accountant (CA) or the Cost Accountant (CMA), certifying that supplies in respect of the said invoices of supplier have actually been made by the supplier to the said registered person and the tax on such supplies has been paid by the said supplier in his return in GSTR 3B. Certificate issued by CA or CMA shall contain UDIN. … 4.1.2 In cases, where difference between the ITC claimed in GSTR-3B and that available in GSTR 2A of the registered person in respect of a supplier for the said financial year is upto Rs 5 lakh, the proper officer shall ask the claimant to produce a certificate from the concerned supplier to the effect that said supplies have actually been made by him to the said registered person and the tax on said supplies has been paid by the said supplier in his return in GSTR 3B.” 14.4 Paragraphs 5 and 6 of the Circular provide: “5. It may also be noted that the clarifications given hereunder are case specific and are applicable to the bonafide errors committed in reporting during FY 2017-18 and 2018-19. Further, these guidelines are clarificatory in nature and may be applied as per the actual facts and circumstances of each case and shall not be used in the interpretation of the provisions of law. 6. These instructions will apply only to the ongoing proceedings in scrutiny/audit/investigation, etc. for FY 2017-18 and 2018-19 and not to the completed proceedings. However, these instructions will apply in those cases for FY 2017-18 and 2018-19 where any adjudication or appeal proceedings are still pending.” 15.0 Section 74(1) of the Act, which the Respondent Infra Projects, Tata Steel, Santhome Latex, Uniworth Textiles, Continental Foundation, Aathi Hotel and HCL Infotech. It reiterates that interest is consequential and depends on actual utilisation, and that a penalty under section 122(2)(b) cannot subsist in the absence of a finding of fraud, wilful misstatement or suppression. 17.0 Per Contra the Respondent contends that, under the self-assessment regime, the Appellant failed to satisfy the conditions of section 16, and that ITC is a conditional statutory benefit. It submits that the amended Form GSTR-2A/B2B entries, the supplier certificates and the Circular cannot, without independent verification, establish entitlement. 17.1 The Respondent’s specific assailment to the grounds of the Appellant are that: a. the Appellant had opportunities to produce invoices, books, payment particulars and supplier confirmations, natural justice does not oblige acceptance of every document, and the Appellant has not demonstrated eligibility by complete and verifiable evidence; b. Form GSTR-2A is a facilitative statement, an amendment therein does not create an indefeasible right, and the Appellant has not produced documentary evidence of payment to the suppliers; c. the Circular outlines a verification mechanism and does not confer an automatic entitlement, the certificates cannot be accepted mechanically, and the Crayon Software certificate is deficient in particulars relating to the wrong GSTIN and the supplier-side reporting; d. the Appellant’s reconciliation is a self-computation, the residual amount of ₹20,430/- remains unestablished, and the certificates require corroboration of payment; e. the matter is not one of denial solely for non-reflection in Form GSTR-2A, the onus lies on the claimant as decided in the case of State of Karnataka v. Ecom Gill Coffee Trading, and Suncraft Energy does not create an automatic entitlement; f. interest under section 50 read with Rule 88B is compensatory, and its quantum must relate to the actual dates and extent of utilisation; and g. the Appellant failed to establish entitlement despite calls for evidence, and, if the proceedings under section 74 are sustainable, a penalty under section 122(2)(b) would follow. 17.2 The Respondent seeks the dismissal of the appeal, the confirmation of the Order-in-Appeal and the restoration of the demand of ₹41,10,666/- with interest and penalty. 17.3 The Respondent’s counter does not expressly invoke section 16(2)(c) by name, nor does it address the specific allegation recorded in paragraph 13 of the SCN. The counter likewise omits any reference to sections 73 or 75(2), and makes no computation of limitation. It does not articulate any particular foundational fact of suppression beyond asserting that the Appellant failed to establish eligibility. The only remark on interest concerns its quantification following actual utilisation. 18.0 Heard both sides. The short matters to be decided in the instant appeal instituted by the Appellant are: a. Whether, having regard to the amended B2B entry of ₹25,44,774/-, the supplier certificates produced by the Appellant and the guidance in Circular No. 183/15/2022-GST, the order impugned directing reversal of ITC amounting to ₹41,10,666/-can be sustained, where those documents were rejected by the FAA without adequate or reasoned findings? b. Whether the remaining IGST shortfall of ₹20,430/- is properly chargeable to the Appellant on the material on record, or whether that residual amount has not been established with the requisite particularity and evidence? (c) Whether the invocation of section 74, and the consequential levy of interest and penalty, can be sustained? 19.0 The Appellant has taken a preliminary objection that the Respondent cannot, in the counter, justify the demand on grounds that do not figure in the SCN. We find merit in that objection. The SCN, as recorded in paragraph 6.1 above, alleged a breach of section 16(2)(c) only, on the footing that the suppliers had neither reported the supplies nor paid the tax. 19.1 Section 75(7) of the Act, reproduced in paragraph 16.3 above, forbids the confirmation of a demand “on the grounds other than the grounds specified in the notice”. The Respondent has not pointed to any allegation in the SCN, the Order-in-Original or the Order-in-Appeal that the Appellant was not in possession of the tax invoices, or that the services were not received. The submissions in the counter that the Appellant has not shown “complete and verifiable” eligibility on the totality of the evidence travel beyond the notice. 19.2 The Tribunal of Thiruvananthapuram, in Santhome Latex Enterprises v. Commissioner of CGST, Thiruvananthapuram, Appeal No. APL/21/TVP/2026, Final Order No. 04/TVP/KERALA/2026 dated 21.08.2026, (2026) 45 Centax 325 (Tri-GST-Kerala), held that the raising of additional grounds at the appellate stage which are not found in the show cause notice is against the fundamental principles of natural justice. We respectfully agree. We therefore confine our enquiry to the case actually made in the SCN, namely, the condition in section 16(2)(c). Issue (a): the amended B2B entry, the certificates and the Circular 20.0 We shall first dispose of the submission that the Circular does not bind the Respondent. The Circular was issued by the Board under section 168(1) of the Act. In the case of Ranadey Micronutrients v. Collector of Central Excise, 2002-TIOL-184-SC-CX, the Supreme Court held that it does not lie in the mouth of the Revenue to repudiate a circular issued by the Board on the basis that it is inconsistent with a statutory provision, consistency and discipline being of far greater importance than the winning or losing of court proceedings. 20.1 Paragraph 6 of the Circular, reproduced in paragraph 15.4 above, expressly extends its application to cases for FY 2017-18 and 2018-19 in which adjudication or appeal proceedings are still pending. The period in dispute is July 2017 to March 2018, and the present appeal is pending. The Circular therefore applies to the Respondent. 21.0 The Respondent submits that the Circular does not confer an automatic entitlement. We accept that proposition, and the Appellant does not contend otherwise. The Circular is, however, an instruction to the proper officer of the manner in which the condition in section 16(2)(c) is to be verified. Paragraph 4 uses the words “shall first seek the details” and “shall then ascertain”. 21.1 The Respondent has not demonstrated that the AA, before confirming the demand, or the FAA, before affirming it, undertook the exercise prescribed in paragraph 4 of the Circular in respect of the invoices on which the credit was availed. The Order-in-Original proceeds on the footing that no material was placed on record. The Order-in-Appeal rejects the material that was placed on record without reasons. 21.2 Paragraph 5 of the Circular confines its operation to bona fide errors of reporting during FY 2017-18 and 2018-19. The discrepancy in the present case arose, on the Appellant’s case, from an amendment in the supplier’s Form GSTR-1 reflected in the B2B table of the Appellant’s Form GSTR-2A in June 2018 and from the reporting of the supplies by certain suppliers against the erstwhile GSTIN of the Appellant. Both are errors or corrections on the supplier side. The Respondent has not alleged that they were contrived by the Appellant. 22.0 We shall now examine the amended B2B entry of E-Support Solutions Private Limited. The entry itself discloses the invoice number, the invoice date of 05.08.2017, the taxable value of ₹1,41,37,634/-, and the CGST and SGST of ₹12,72,387/- each. It forms part of the Appellant’s Form GSTR-2A, which is generated on the common portal from the supplier’s own declaration. The Respondent does not dispute that the entry appears there. 22.1 The FAA rejected the entry on the ground that a copy of the August 2017 invoice was not produced, and observed that the Appellant’s case was “not very clear”. That observation is not a finding. It does not state what part of the case was unclear, and it does not state that the invoice was absent from the Appellant’s books or that the supply was not received. 22.2 The SCN does not allege either of the latter facts. An entry declared by the supplier on the Government’s own portal, which discloses the very particulars of the invoice, is material of probative value on the question whether the supply was made and reported. In the absence of any contrary material, the want of a copy of the invoice cannot sustain the reversal of ₹25,44,774/-. 23.0 We now turn to the supplier certificates. The ITC covered by the certificate of Tata Communications is ₹8,08,650/- (CGST ₹4,04,325/- and SGST ₹4,04,325/-), which exceeds ₹5 lakh. Paragraph 4.1.1 of the Circular accordingly required a certificate of a Chartered Accountant or a Cost Accountant bearing a UDIN. The Appellant produced a certificate of a Chartered Accountant bearing UDIN 24116506BKDFWG8236, dated 10.05.2024. 23.1 The ITC covered by the certificates of STT Global Data Centres (₹4,46,416/-), Vodafone Mobile Services (₹3,51,000/-) and Crayon Software (₹41,869/-) does not exceed ₹5 lakh in the case of any of them. Paragraph 4.1.2 of the Circular accordingly required a certificate from the supplier concerned that the supplies were made to the Appellant and that the tax was paid in Form GSTR-3B. The Appellant produced a certificate from each of those suppliers. 23.2 The FAA described all the certificates as “not acceptable”, without stating which requirement of paragraph 4.1.1 or 4.1.2 was not met, and without recording any defect in any of them. The Respondent has pointed in its counter to two matters: that the certificates require corroboration of the payment, and that the Crayon Software certificate does not particularise the wrong GSTIN and the supplier-side reporting. 23.3 As to the first, the certificate prescribed by the Circular is itself the means by which the proper officer is to verify the payment of tax by the supplier under section 16(2)(c). A certificate that is to be asked for from the supplier or from a Chartered Accountant cannot be rejected merely because it is a certificate. If the Respondent doubted the certificate, the Circular enabled the Respondent to verify the UDIN and the supplier’s Form GSTR-3B. The Respondent does not say that it did so. 23.4 As to the second, paragraphs 4.1.1 and 4.1.2 prescribe the content of the certificate. It is to certify that the supplies were actually made by the supplier to the registered person and that the tax thereon was paid by the supplier in Form GSTR-3B. The Circular does not require the certificate to state the wrong GSTIN. Paragraph 3(d) of the Circular, moreover, expressly provides that the allowance of the credit to the actual recipient shall not depend on the completion of the action against the holder of the GSTIN wrongly mentioned. The objection reads into the Circular a requirement that it does not contain. 23.5 The Vodafone Mobile Services certificate dated 20.03.2024 misdescribes the date of the Circular. That is a clerical slip which does not touch the substance of what the certificate certifies, and the FAA did not rest his rejection on it. 24.0 The Respondent further submits that no proof of payment to the suppliers was furnished. The Appellant points to the vendor ledgers with payment entries at pages 82, 135, 202 and 226 of the Paper Book. The counter asserts the want of proof of payment in general terms. It does not controvert the entries in the ledger extracts to which the Appellant has drawn attention, and it does not identify any invoice in respect of which the payment is said to be wanting. 24.1 Section 155, reproduced in paragraph 14.2 above, places the burden of proving the claim on the Appellant. We accept that. We find, however, that the Appellant has placed on record the invoice particulars through the amended B2B entry, the certificates in the form prescribed by the Circular, and the ledger extracts. Once that material is on record, a bare denial that it is “unverified”, without any attempt at verification and without any contrary material, does not displace it. 25.0 The Respondent relies on State of Karnataka v. Ecom Gill Coffee Trading Private Limited, (2023) 9 SCC 573. The Bengaluru Bench of this Tribunal, in its order in M/s. Peekay Industries v. Commissioner of Commercial Taxes, Karnataka, APL/34/BUR/2026 dated 03.09.2026, noticed that the decision emphasises that the determination of ITC eligibility may require an examination of the underlying transactions and supporting material, and that it arose under the Karnataka Value Added Tax Act, on facts where the genuineness of the transactions themselves was in question. 25.1 In the present case, the genuineness of the transactions and the existence of the suppliers are not in dispute. The decision does not assist the Respondent. We are in agreement the observation of the Bengaluru Bench that a difference between Form GSTR-2A and Form GSTR-3B may justify verification, but cannot, without more, be treated as conclusive proof that the ITC has been wrongly availed. 26.0 We have also considered the order of the Bengaluru Bench in M/s. Vintek Control Systems v. Commissioner of Central Tax, Bengaluru, APL/31/BUR/2026 dated 06.10.2026, in which the appeal was dismissed. In that case, the order records that no supplier certificate as per the Circular, no e-way bill and no transport or stock record was produced at any stage. The present case is distinguishable, because the Appellant has produced the certificates prescribed by the Circular, the invoices and the ledger extracts, and the Respondent does not dispute the existence of the suppliers. 27.0 The FAA’s order impugned is, in addition, a non-speaking order. Section 75(6) requires the proper officer to “set out the relevant facts and the basis of his decision”. The Order-in-Appeal does not discuss a single certificate, does not state why the Appellant’s reconciliation was not accepted, and does not deal with the Appellant’s reliance on the Circular and the case law. It also records the Tata Communications amount incorrectly. 27.1 The Supreme Court, in Kranti Associates Pvt. Ltd. v. Masood Ahmed Khan, (2010) 9 SCC 496, as noticed by the Bengaluru Bench in Peekay Industries (supra), held that the reason for a quasi-judicial decision must be apparent from the order itself. An order that rejects the evidence of the party without recording any reason offends the maxim audi alteram partem, for a hearing that is not reflected in a reasoned consideration of what was said is no hearing at all. 28.0 Upon the foregoing analysis, we find that the Appellant has established the fulfilment of the conditions in section 16(2) in respect of the amended B2B entry of E-Support Solutions Private Limited (CGST ₹12,72,387/- and SGST ₹12,72,387/-), and the certificates of Tata Communications, STT Global Data Centres and Vodafone Mobile Services (CGST and SGST of ₹8,03,033/- each), and in respect of Crayon Software (IGST ₹41,869/-). 28.1 The CGST and SGST so covered amount to ₹20,75,420/- each, as against the CGST and SGST of ₹20,24,184/- each confirmed. The CGST and SGST demand of ₹20,24,184/- each is therefore not sustainable. The order impugned directing the reversal of the ITC to that extent, and to the extent of IGST ₹41,869/-, cannot be sustained. Issue (b): the residual IGST of ₹20,430 29.0 The IGST confirmed is ₹62,299/-, being the difference between the IGST of ₹98,299/- availed in Form GSTR-3B and the IGST of ₹36,000/- reflected in Form GSTR-2A. The Appellant has established ₹41,869/- by the Crayon Software certificate. The balance of ₹20,430/- is the only amount for which the Appellant acknowledges that no certificate was filed. 29.1 The Respondent submits that the residual amount remains unestablished. The question, however, is not whether the Appellant has filed a certificate, but whether the Respondent has made out the case set out in the SCN, namely, that the tax charged on the relevant supplies was not paid to the Government. 30.0 The Appellant has not produced any certificate for the balance IGST of ₹20,430/-. Nor has he established, on the standard the statute requires, that the suppliers remitted the tax collected on the relevant supplies to the Government. On its own showing, the Appellant has produced only those certificates that he was able to obtain, and he seeks the relief of ITC on that footing. For the balance amount he admits that no certificate is available. He has also not pressed the claim by establishing the genuineness of the underlying transactions through any other cogent material. Under section 155 of the CGST Act, the burden of proving eligibility lies on the person claiming the credit. Under section 16(2)(c), that eligibility depends on the tax having actually been paid to the Government. 30.1 The Appellant elected to avail himself of the mechanism in the Board’s Circular. By filing certificates in respect of certain invoices, he invoked the Circular as the means of establishing the eligibility of the disputed credit. Having taken recourse to the Circular, it is not open to him to rely on its prescribed mode of proof for one set of invoices and to disclaim it for another. Such a course would amount to adopting two different standards for the same dispute. It would also amount to approbating and reprobating i.e., to accept and reject the same stand, which cannot be permitted. A person who claims the benefit of a beneficial instrument must accept the conditions on which it is granted, and those conditions apply uniformly to every invoice for which the credit is claimed. 30.2 We are therefore of the considered opinion that, having taken recourse to the conditions stipulated in the Circular, the Appellant is bound to comply with them for the disputed ITC of ₹20,430/- as well. He has furnished neither the certificate nor any other evidence of payment of tax by the supplier for this amount. The burden under section 155 is therefore not discharged, and the condition in section 16(2)(c) is not shown to be satisfied. The ITC of ₹20,430/- is accordingly not admissible. Issue (c): section 74, interest and penalty 31.0 The SCN was issued under section 74. That section applies only where the ITC has been wrongly availed “by reason of fraud, or any wilful-misstatement or suppression of facts to evade tax”. The satisfaction of the proper officer on those ingredients is a jurisdictional requirement. 31.1 The Supreme Court, in G.R. Infra Projects Limited, Ratlam v. State of Madhya Pradesh, Civil Appeal No. 11277 of 2026 (arising out of SLP (C) No. 33594 of 2025), decided on 19.08.2026, observed: “A bare reading of the notice would indicate that but for a bland statement of ‘fraud or concealment of facts’ nothing is stated as to how fraud was inferred or concealment of facts were detected. … What is required for the extended time to be applied are the allegations, which lead to the inference of a fraud or the concealment as attempted by the assessee resulting in suppression of facts, should emanate from the notice itself. It cannot be a mechanical use of the words ‘fraud, willful misstatement or suppression of facts’ without listing out the aspects which persuades the assessing officer to conclude that there has been employed either of these surreptitious devices by the assessee.” 31.2 In Tata Steel Limited v. Union of India, (2026) 45 Centax 361 (SC), as extracted in an order of the Bengaluru Bench of this Tribunal, the Supreme Court held: “Insofar as a notice under Section 74, the satisfaction should be not only of mismatch of ITC and short payment of tax having occurred, as is alleged in this case, the Officer should be satisfied that either fraud/willful misrepresentation/suppression had led to such mismatch or short payment of tax. … The foundational facts which led to the inference arrived at of fraud/willful misrepresentation/ suppression should be evident from the notice itself. The mere employment of such words will not indicate an application of mind, upon which alone the satisfaction can be arrived at.” 32.0 Tested against these principles, the SCN falls short. Paragraph 13 of the SCN records that, in the absence of the departmental verification, the discrepancy would have remained unnoticed, and infers that the Appellant “appears” to have wilfully suppressed the facts. The detection of a discrepancy by the Department is not evidence of suppression by the assessee. The word “appears” records a suspicion and not a foundational fact. 32.1 The SCN does not state what fact the Appellant was required to declare and did not declare, what information it was asked for in writing and did not furnish, or what act of the Appellant shows that it knew that the credit was inadmissible and availed it in order to evade tax. 33.0 The facts on record point the other way. The credit was availed in Table 4A(5) of Form GSTR-3B and was reflected in the annual return in Form GSTR-9. The Appellant disclosed the credit, replied to every communication, and sought time to place the certificates. The Respondent has not alleged that the invoices are not genuine, that the supplies were not received, or that the Appellant was in collusion with any supplier. 33.1 The Supreme Court, in Continental Foundation Joint Venture Holding, Nathpa v. Commissioner of Central Excise, Chandigarh-I, 2007-TIOL-152-SC-EX (Para 10), also reported as 2007 (8) TMI 11 (SC), as extracted in the order of the Delhi Bench of this Tribunal in Appeal No. 10 of 2026, held that mere omission to give correct information is not suppression of facts unless it was deliberate, and that suppression means failure to disclose full information with the intent to evade payment of duty. 33.2 The Thiruvananthapuram Bench, in Santhome Latex Enterprises (supra), held that, in the absence of any statutory presumption, the mere availment of ITC without due verification does not amount to suppression of facts, and that every act of transgression of section 16(2) cannot be termed a violation inviting proceedings under section 74 unless clear and categorical evidence has been adduced that the transgression was the result of fraud, wilful misstatement or suppression of facts with an intent to evade tax. We respectfully agree. 33.3 We have also noticed the observation of this Bench in Boilla Venkataramana Reddy v. M/s. Shivenari Canteen Services, APL/149/HYD/2026 dated 18.09.2026, that, in order to invoke section 74, there must be ingredients embedded in it, and the same have to be established in clear terms. 34.0 The AA recorded that the Appellant did not place any evidentiary material or certificates on record. The failure to produce evidence in defence of a notice cannot supply, after the notice, the ingredient of suppression that the notice itself did not disclose. In Santhome Latex Enterprises (supra), the Tribunal held that a failure to furnish information that was never a ground in the show cause notice cannot be relied upon to sustain section 74. The Appellant, moreover, had sought fifteen days to produce the certificates, and produced them in the appeal. 34.1 We are of the considered opinion that the charge of fraud, wilful misstatement or suppression of facts to evade tax has not been established against the Appellant. The notice under section 74(1) is therefore not sustainable. The inconsistency in the Order-in-Original noted in paragraph 13.0 above, where the statutory basis varies between section 73 and section 74, only fortifies this conclusion. 35.0 The consequence is provided by section 75(2), reproduced in paragraph 16.2 above. This Tribunal, being an Appellate Tribunal, may conclude that the notice under section 74(1) is not sustainable, in which event the tax payable is to be determined as if the notice had been issued under section 73(1). The Appellant has sought invoking section 75(2) in the alternative. 35.1 Circular No. 185/17/2022-GST dated 27.12.2022 clarifies that such a re-determination is available where the notice under section 74(1) was issued within the time limit specified in section 73(2) read with section 73(10). For FY 2017-18, the time limit under section 73(10) was extended by Notification No. 13/2022-CT dated 05.07.2022 to 30.09.2023 and by Notification No. 09/2023-CT dated 31.03.2023 to 31.12.2023. The Kerala High Court, in Faizal Traders Pvt. Ltd. v. Deputy Commissioner, Central Tax and Central Excise, Palakkad Division, WP(C) No. 24810 of 2023 dated 07.02.2024, upheld those notifications as issued within the power in section 168A. 35.2 The SCN was issued on 23.02.2023, and the Order-in-Original was passed on 13.12.2023, both within the extended period. The notice is therefore deemed to have been issued under section 73(1), and the question is whether any tax is payable under it. 36.0 For the reasons recorded under Issues (a) and (b), the Appellant has established its entitlement to the credit in respect of the amended B2B entry and the certificates. The Appellant has not established that the residual sum of ₹20,430/- was paid to the Government. We find that the ITC to the extent of ₹20,430/- is liable to be reversed by the Appellant under section 73 on the SCN. 36.1 Section 75(8) provides that where the Appellate Tribunal modifies the amount of tax determined by the proper officer, the interest and penalty shall stand modified accordingly. The interest under section 50(3), the penalty under section 122(2)(b) and the equal penalty under section 74(9) depend upon the existence of a tax liability. The interest and penalty to the extent upheld supra is to be computed accordingly. The proper officer is required to compute appropriate interest, and quantify the penalty and communicate the same to appellant within 4 weeks from the date of issue of this order. 37. CONCLUSION 37.0 We find that, on the material on record, the demand of ITC of IGST to the extent of ₹20,430/- is sustainable under Section 73 ibid. The appellants are liable to pay appropriate interest, if the said ineligible ITC has been utilised, and penalty, as is to be computed by the proper officer. The balance demand of ITC is not sustainable, and that the proceedings under section 74, together with the interest and penalty, cannot stand. Accordingly the demand of the balance ITC is set aside. 38.0 The order impugned, being Order-in-Appeal No. HYD-GST-HYC-APP1-45-24-25-GST dated 28.06.2024, stands modified to the above extent. 39.0 The issues framed in paragraph 18.0 above is answered accordingly. ORDER 40.0 On the above terms, the appeal filed by the taxpayer is partly allowed. 41.0 No order as to costs.

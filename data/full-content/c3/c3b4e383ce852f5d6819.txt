Articles 5 min read IRDAI commission caps may revive shadow payouts while hitting 70% of corporate broking revenue; broking jobs at risk: IBAI IBAI members argue the proposed architecture risks penalising customer-led broking while failing to adequately address forced insurance distribution through lenders and other channels. Anushka Sengupta ETBFSI Updated On Oct 7, 2026 at 02:55 PM IST Read by 100 Professionals var news_msid = 134741210; console.log(news_msid); Select ETBFSI as Preferred Choice on Google Copy Link Share on WhatsApp Share on Linkedin Share on X Share on Telegram Share on Facebook The Insurance Brokers Association of India (IBAI) has warned that the proposed overhaul of insurance distribution could put around 70% of corporate broking revenue at risk, while arguing that blanket commission caps could create unintended incentives for insurers and distributors to revive alternative payout structures.IBAI members on Tuesday said the regulator should distinguish between customer-driven distribution and forced sales, particularly lender-led insurance, rather than impose a common commission architecture across channels.The association also warned that hard product- and channel-level caps could recreate the conditions that had led to ₹824 crore of GST input credit at 15 insurers before the 2023 reforms, as insurers and distributors could look for alternative ways to compensate for capped commissions. Advt 70% revenue exposure, corporate broking jobs at riskAshok Jain, Treasurer, IBAI said that the proposed changes could have a significant impact on corporate broking economics, with around 70% of revenue potentially affected. Another estimate put the potential impact on corporate broking jobs at 60-70%, highlighting the employment implications of changes to distribution remuneration and architecture.The top brokers argued that the impact cannot be assessed merely by looking at the percentage commission paid to intermediaries, as the absolute economics of servicing policies determine whether distributors can continue servicing smaller accounts.IBAI members said corporate brokers often cross-subsidise smaller accounts through larger relationships, and a reduction in commission on individual policies could therefore affect the viability of servicing smaller businesses and customers.The concern comes against the backdrop of IRDAI's proposal to recalibrate commissions and reduce the Expense of Management limits for insurers through a phased glide path.‘₹824 crore GST problem could return’IBAI has strongly pushed back against the possibility that hard commission caps could recreate the incentives that existed before the 2023 EoM framework. Advt The association has said the earlier regime contributed to ₹824 crore of GST input credit involving 15 insurers, with additional payments being routed through mechanisms outside the formal commission structure. IBAI fears that new sub-caps could again encourage overriding commissions or other alternative payments.IBAI members argued that the 2023 reforms had brought such payments into the formal framework and that the regulator should be careful not to reverse that progress.The association's position is particularly focused on channels where insurers have historically had to compete aggressively for distribution. IBAI has argued that if commissions are artificially capped while insurance premiums remain market-driven, participants will have incentives to find other ways to compensate distributors. Advt It has also pointed to international examples where excess commissions have allegedly been disguised as marketing or other payments, warning that India could see similar workarounds if the new framework is too prescriptive.Sanjay Kedia: ‘Root cause is forced distribution’Sanjay Kedia, former IBAI President, said the regulator should first separate forced distribution from free-choice distribution.According to Kedia, customers using brokers or individual agents can compare insurers on price, coverage and service, whereas customers buying insurance alongside a loan may have significantly less choice. Advt “The fundamental issue is forced distribution. And that is where the problems exist. That is where an intervention could be done,” Kedia said.He argued that reducing commissions across the board and transferring the savings to insurers may not necessarily benefit policyholders. Instead, in forced-distribution channels, he suggested linking the economics of the product to claims experience.IBAI seeks claims-ratio trigger for customer refundsKedia proposed a mechanism under which a low claims ratio in forced-distribution products could trigger a refund or reduction in renewal premium for the policyholder.“There are two ways. You come and reduce the money of the distributor, which is the bank, and give it to the insurance company by reducing the commission by law. Or there's a smarter way,” he said.“The better way to transfer not the money to the insurance company, but to transfer the money to the policyholder by simply allowing a claims refund clause above or below a particular claim ratio,” Kedia said.IBAI has separately suggested that where insurance is force-sold and the claims ratio remains below a prescribed floor, such as 65%, insurers could be required to refund part of the premium or reduce renewal premiums.The association believes this would ensure that any excess economics actually reach the policyholder instead of simply shifting the benefit from the distributor to the insurer.General insurance expense ratio, PSU costs also questionedKedia also questioned whether the rise in commissions cited by IRDAI should be viewed in isolation from other components of insurer expenses.IBAI members pointed to the general insurance expense ratio falling from 28.2% to around 26.2% in one period following the 2023 reforms, while also flagging the impact of rising wage and pension costs at public-sector insurers.According to them, PSU wage and pension costs have increased by around ₹7,000 crore, which should be considered when assessing the underlying movement in insurer expenses.Top brokers also flagged the treatment of long-term motor insurance, where premium is recognised across policy years but commission can be recognised upfront. They argued that the commission should similarly be spread across the policy tenure.The central argument from IBAI is that the proposed architecture should distinguish between distributors appointed by customers and channels that effectively control access to customers.IBAI has therefore called for a deeper impact assessment before the proposed commission and EoM changes are finalised, arguing that reforms should focus on forced distribution and genuine mis-selling rather than applying uniform restrictions across fundamentally different distribution models.

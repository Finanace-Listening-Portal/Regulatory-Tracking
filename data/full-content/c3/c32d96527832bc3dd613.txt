Background of search assessments and revision proceedings: The Income Tax Appellate Tribunal, Nagpur Bench, comprising Judicial Member Shri V. Durga Rao and Accountant Member Shri K.M. Roy, decided two appeals filed by Maheshwari Coal Benefication & Infrastructure Pvt. Ltd. against the revision orders dated 20 March 2024 passed by the Principal Commissioner of Income Tax (Central), Bhopal, under Section 263 of the Income-tax Act, 1961. The appeals related to Assessment Years 2017-18 and 2019-20 and arose from assessments completed under Section 153A read with Section 143(3) on 29 September 2021. The assessee was engaged in coal trading, liaisoning and coal handling at railway sidings. A survey under Section 133A had been conducted on 26 September 2016, followed by a search under Section 132 on 11 July 2019. For AY 2017-18, the assessee had originally declared income of ₹2,09,04,440, and the Assessing Officer completed the search assessment after obtaining approval under Section 153D, making an addition of ₹7,59,397 towards interest expenses. Subsequently, the PCIT issued a show-cause notice dated 9 February 2024 and directed a fresh assessment through the revision order dated 20 March 2024. The principal issues concerned alleged non-examination of excess coal stock amounting to ₹2,18,17,729 for AY 2017-18 and the allowability of bad debts amounting to ₹1,53,80,308 for AY 2019-20. The assessee challenged the revision orders on several independent grounds, including invalidity of the foundational assessments, absence of the statutory conditions for revision, failure to examine the approval under Section 153D and lack of independent application of mind by the PCIT. Both appeals were heard together and disposed of through a consolidated order dated 14 May 2025. 2. Invalid assessment cannot provide jurisdiction for revision under Section 263: For AY 2017-18, the assessee raised an additional legal ground under Rule 11 of the Income Tax Appellate Tribunal Rules, 1963, contending that the assessment sought to be revised had already been quashed by the Nagpur Bench in the assessee’s own case through its consolidated order dated 26 December 2024 in ITA No. 113/Nag./2024 and connected appeals. The earlier assessment was held invalid because the approval granted by the Additional Commissioner under Section 153D suffered from a jurisdictional defect. Relying on National Thermal Power Co. Ltd. Vs CIT, 229 ITR 383 (Supreme Court), the Tribunal admitted the additional ground because it raised a legal question capable of determination from the existing record. The assessee argued that a non-existent assessment order could not be characterised as erroneous and prejudicial to the interests of the Revenue, which are the essential statutory conditions for invoking Section 263. The Tribunal accepted this submission and held that the PCIT could not revive an assessment already declared invalid by directing the Assessing Officer to undertake fresh proceedings. Such an approach would effectively extend the statutory limitation period and undermine the finality of the decision invalidating the original assessment. The Tribunal applied the legal maxim Sublato Fundamento Cadit Opus, meaning that when the foundation is removed, the structure built upon it must also fall. The foundational assessment under Section 153A read with Section 143(3) having ceased to exist in law, the subsequent revision order under Section 263 necessarily failed. The Tribunal relied upon PCIT Vs Badal Prakash Jindal HUF, Gigabyte Technology (India) (P.) Ltd. Vs CIT, Jagjeet Singh Vs ACIT, Krishan Kumar Saraf Vs CIT, Charbhuja Marmo (India) Pvt. Ltd. Vs PCIT, Pradeep Dattatraya Banginwar Vs PCIT, Classic Flour & Food Processing Pvt. Ltd. Vs CIT, Supersonic Technologies Pvt. Ltd. Vs PCIT and Westlife Development Ltd. Vs PCIT. These authorities supported the conclusion that a jurisdictionally invalid assessment cannot be validated through collateral revision proceedings. Consequently, the Tribunal quashed the revision order for AY 2017-18 on this independent jurisdictional ground. 3. Excess coal stock of ₹2.18 crore had already been examined during assessment: The Tribunal separately considered the merits of the PCIT’s allegation that the Assessing Officer had failed to examine excess coal stock valued at ₹2,18,17,729, discovered during the survey conducted on 26 September 2016. The assessee demonstrated that the Assessing Officer had specifically raised a query concerning the stock discrepancy through a notice dated 31 August 2021 issued under Section 142(1). The assessee furnished an explanation during September 2021, supported by its accounts and financial statements, showing that the excess stock had already been surrendered as additional income. The amount was separately reflected under extraordinary items in the Profit and Loss Account and included in the closing stock as on 31 March 2017. The assessee explained that the additional income had been considered in computing its taxable income and that the corresponding tax had been paid. Its books were audited under the Companies Act, 2013, and Section 44AB of the Income-tax Act. After examining these particulars, the Assessing Officer accepted the explanation and did not make a further addition on account of the surrendered stock. The Departmental Representative contended that the inquiry was inadequate and that the Assessing Officer had also failed to apply Section 115BBE to the surrendered amount. However, the Tribunal found that the assessment record clearly demonstrated an inquiry, a specific reply and consideration of the issue before completion of the assessment. It distinguished between lack of inquiry and alleged inadequacy of inquiry, observing that the PCIT had failed to identify a definite error in the Assessing Officer’s treatment of the excess stock. Merely directing further verification without demonstrating why the assessment was erroneous did not satisfy Section 263. The Tribunal relied on the principles explained in PCIT Vs Clix Finance India (P.) Ltd., CIT Vs Rajesh Mahajan, PCIT Vs Klaxon Trading (P.) Ltd., PCIT Vs Mohak Real Estate (P.) Ltd. and BNP Paribas Vs CIT. It therefore held that the revision order was unsustainable on the excess-stock issue as well. 4. Difference between lack of inquiry and inadequate inquiry under Section 263: The Tribunal undertook a detailed examination of the limits of revisional jurisdiction under Section 263, including Explanation 2, and reiterated that the existence of an assessment order considered erroneous and prejudicial to the interests of the Revenue is a jurisdictional prerequisite. Referring to the Delhi High Court’s decision in PCIT Vs Clix Finance India (P.) Ltd., 160 taxmann.com 357, it noted that an assessment order need not reproduce every question raised or every explanation considered by the Assessing Officer. The absence of elaborate reasoning in the assessment order does not necessarily establish non-application of mind where the assessment record demonstrates that the issue was examined. The Tribunal also considered the principles emerging from CIT Vs Sunbeam Auto Ltd., CIT Vs Gabriel India Ltd., CIT Vs Anil Kumar Sharma and CIT Vs Ashish Rajpal. These decisions recognise that a PCIT cannot invoke Section 263 merely because the assessment could have contained more detailed reasoning or because the Commissioner prefers a different approach to verification. The Tribunal further referred to Malabar Industrial Co. Ltd. Vs CIT, 243 ITR 83 (Supreme Court), which establishes that every loss of revenue does not automatically render an assessment prejudicial to the Revenue. Where the Assessing Officer adopts a legally permissible view after examining the relevant material, the PCIT must demonstrate why that view is unsustainable in law before exercising revisional jurisdiction. The Tribunal emphasised that a direction for a fresh assessment cannot substitute for the PCIT’s obligation to identify an actual error in the existing assessment. On the facts, the query concerning surrendered coal stock, the assessee’s explanation and the accounting treatment were available on record. The PCIT had not established that the stock income remained untaxed or that the Assessing Officer’s treatment was legally impermissible. Consequently, the Tribunal concluded that the essential conditions of erroneousness and prejudice had not been established. This finding furnished an additional reason for invalidating the revision order, independent of the jurisdictional defect affecting the original search assessment. 5. Revision of search assessments completed with approval under Section 153D: Another important issue concerned the effect of mandatory approval under Section 153D on the exercise of revisional jurisdiction under Section 263. The assessee argued that the search assessments had been completed only after the Additional Commissioner had granted statutory approval and that the PCIT could not simply revise the Assessing Officer’s order without addressing the legal effect of that approval. The contention was that the approval formed part of the statutory assessment process and that an order made after such approval could not be treated as erroneous without examining the approval and identifying a legally sustainable basis for interference. The Tribunal examined this objection separately from the question whether the foundational assessment was itself invalid. It considered the judicial authorities cited concerning revision of assessments completed with approval under Section 153D, including decisions dealing with the statutory hierarchy and the need for independent examination of the assessment record. The Tribunal accepted the assessee’s jurisdictional challenge and quashed the revision order on this ground as well. The proceedings also raised the question whether the PCIT had independently examined the assessment record or merely acted upon proposals forwarded by the Assessing Officer and the Range Head. The assessee referred to the proposals dated 2 May 2023 and 8 May 2023 and argued that the show-cause notice issued by the PCIT substantially reproduced the objections already formulated by subordinate authorities. The Tribunal considered the requirement that the revisional authority independently examine the record and reach its own satisfaction regarding the existence of an erroneous assessment prejudicial to the Revenue. It accepted the assessee’s challenge that the PCIT’s action was not supported by the requisite independent examination and application of mind. Thus, the revision order failed on multiple grounds: the invalidity of the foundational assessment, the absence of an established error, the objections concerning statutory approval and the failure to demonstrate an independent exercise of revisional jurisdiction. 6. AY 2019-20: Bad-debt claim of ₹1.53 crore and actual write-off principles: For Assessment Year 2019-20, the PCIT had questioned the assessee’s claim of ₹1,53,80,308 towards bad debts and directed the Assessing Officer to conduct a fresh assessment. The assessee contended that the PCIT had not recorded any finding explaining why the deduction was incorrectly claimed under Section 36(1)(vii). The Tribunal examined the relevant accounting entries, computation of income and the assessee’s explanation. It noted that the amount included ₹64,07,867 recovered from the concerned party during the year, while the balance of ₹89,72,441 had been reduced from the party’s account. The assessee argued that the net amount represented an actual write-off satisfying the statutory requirements. The Tribunal considered judicial authorities dealing with the treatment of doubtful debts and the circumstances in which a debit to the Profit and Loss Account accompanied by a corresponding reduction of receivables constitutes an actual write-off. These included Vijaya Bank Vs CIT, Vidras India Ceramics (P.) Ltd., Tainwala Chemicals & Plastics (India) Ltd. and CIT Vs Millennia Developers Pvt. Ltd.. The Tribunal recognised that, where the relevant accounting entries effectively reduce the debtors or trade receivables, the treatment may constitute a write-off rather than a mere provision for doubtful debts. Applying these principles, it found that the Assessing Officer had considered the relevant accounts and adopted a permissible view concerning the bad-debt deduction. The PCIT had neither identified a specific defect in the accounting treatment nor explained how the deduction was legally impermissible. Instead, the PCIT had simply set aside the assessment for fresh examination without recording a substantive finding of error. The Tribunal held that this approach did not satisfy the statutory requirements of Section 263 and quashed the revision order on the bad-debt issue. The Tribunal also applied its conclusions on the Section 153D approval issue, invalid foundational assessment and absence of independent examination of records to AY 2019-20, finding the relevant facts and legal objections substantially identical to those already considered for AY 2017-18. 7. Final ruling: Both revision orders quashed and assessee’s appeals allowed: The Nagpur Bench ultimately allowed both appeals filed by Maheshwari Coal Benefication & Infrastructure Pvt. Ltd. In ITA No. 247/Nag./2024 for AY 2017-18, it held that the PCIT could not revise an assessment already declared invalid for want of valid approval under Section 153D. It independently concluded that the excess coal stock of ₹2,18,17,729 had been examined during assessment and that the PCIT had failed to establish the statutory conditions necessary for revision. The Tribunal also accepted the assessee’s objections concerning revision of an assessment completed after statutory approval and the requirement of independent examination by the PCIT. Accordingly, the revision order dated 20 March 2024 was quashed. In ITA No. 248/Nag./2024 for AY 2019-20, the Tribunal held that the PCIT had not established any error in the deduction claimed for bad debts and had failed to provide a factual or legal basis for directing a fresh assessment. It applied its findings concerning Section 153D approval, the invalidity of the underlying assessment and the requirement of independent application of mind to the second assessment year. The revision order for AY 2019-20 was therefore also quashed. The Tribunal pronounced its consolidated order on 14 May 2025, allowing both appeals. The decision establishes that Section 263 cannot be employed to revive a jurisdictionally invalid assessment or to direct a fresh assessment merely because the PCIT considers the Assessing Officer’s inquiry inadequate. It further reinforces that the revisional authority must independently identify a definite error and demonstrate prejudice to the Revenue. Where the Assessing Officer has examined the relevant material and adopted a legally permissible view, a different opinion of the PCIT does not, by itself, justify revision. The ruling is particularly relevant to search assessments involving Section 153D approval, surrendered stock, bad-debt deductions and attempts to reopen matters through revision after the foundational assessment has been invalidated.

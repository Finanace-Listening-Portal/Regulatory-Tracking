(Supreme Court of India) Summary: The Supreme Court of India, in Baharul Islam & Ors. Vs The Indian Medical Association and Ors., examined the constitutional validity of the Assam Rural Health Regulatory Authority Act, 2004, which authorised the establishment of a three-year Diploma in Medicine and Rural Health Care and permitted diploma holders to practise limited forms of modern medicine in rural areas of Assam. The principal issue was whether the Assam Legislature possessed the legislative competence to enact a law governing medical education and the practice of allopathic medicine when Parliament had already prescribed national standards under the Indian Medical Council Act, 1956. The Court considered the constitutional distribution of legislative powers, particularly Article 246, Entry 66 of List I and Entry 25 of List III of the Seventh Schedule. The controversy originated when the Assam Legislature enacted the 2004 Act to address the shortage of qualified medical practitioners in rural areas. The legislation established a regulatory framework for registering Diploma in Medicine and Rural Health Care holders and regulating their medical practice. Pursuant to the legislation, the State established a medical institute at Jorhat and introduced a three-year diploma course. The Indian Medical Association, Assam State Branch, challenged the constitutional validity of the legislation and the admission process before the Gauhati High Court. By its judgment dated 30 October 2014, the High Court declared the Assam Act unconstitutional, principally on the ground that it conflicted with the Indian Medical Council Act, 1956, and the statutory requirements governing the establishment of medical institutions and introduction of medical courses. The appellants, comprising students admitted to the diploma programme, challenged the High Court’s decision before the Supreme Court. They argued that the diploma qualification was not a recognised medical qualification within the meaning of the Indian Medical Council Act, 1956, and consequently did not require prior permission from the Central Government under Section 10A. They further submitted that the Assam legislation operated within the State’s legislative authority over medical education and healthcare and did not interfere with the national standards prescribed for recognised medical qualifications. According to the appellants, the diploma holders were authorised to provide only restricted medical services in rural areas, particularly treatment of common diseases and prescription of specified medicines. They maintained that the legislation served an important public healthcare objective by addressing the shortage of medical practitioners in underserved rural communities. The Indian Medical Association and the Medical Council of India opposed the appeals, contending that Parliament had established a comprehensive regulatory framework governing medical education, recognised qualifications, registration and the practice of modern medicine. They submitted that permitting persons possessing a three-year diploma to practise allopathic medicine would undermine nationally prescribed standards and potentially compromise patient safety. They relied upon Section 10A and other provisions of the Indian Medical Council Act, 1956, and constitutional principles governing legislative competence. They also argued that the Assam legislation effectively introduced a parallel system of medical education and registration without complying with the regulatory requirements applicable to modern medicine. The respondents maintained that the objective of improving rural healthcare could not justify legislation inconsistent with constitutionally protected national standards. The Supreme Court examined the relationship between Entry 66 of List I, concerning coordination and determination of standards in institutions for higher education, and Entry 25 of List III, concerning education, including medical education. The Court explained that although Parliament and State Legislatures possess concurrent powers over medical education under Entry 25, that power is expressly subject to Entry 66 of the Union List. Consequently, Parliament possesses overriding legislative authority concerning the determination and coordination of standards in medical education. The Court discussed the principles of harmonious construction, pith and substance, legislative competence and constitutional repugnancy. It emphasised that the constitutional validity of legislation must be determined by examining its true nature, purpose and effect within the distribution of legislative powers established by the Constitution. Applying these principles, the Supreme Court held that the Indian Medical Council Act, 1956, and the rules and regulations framed thereunder constituted parliamentary legislation governing standards of education in modern or allopathic medicine. A State Legislature could not enact legislation conflicting with those centrally prescribed standards. The Assam Rural Health Regulatory Authority Act, 2004, was therefore beyond the legislative competence of the Assam Legislature and was liable to be declared null and void. Although the Supreme Court agreed with the Gauhati High Court’s ultimate conclusion invalidating the 2004 Act, it expressly disagreed with the High Court’s reasoning based on repugnancy under Article 254 and the doctrine of occupied field. The Supreme Court clarified that the decisive constitutional issue was the limitation imposed upon State legislative competence by Entry 66 of List I read with Entry 25 of List III, rather than repugnancy between two otherwise competent legislations under the Concurrent List. The Court separately examined the constitutional validity of the Assam Community Professionals (Registration and Competency) Act, 2015, enacted after the Gauhati High Court invalidated the earlier legislation. The subsequent Act provided a different statutory framework for persons who had completed or undertaken the diploma course, recognising them as Community Health Professionals. Under the revised framework, their professional role was distinct from the independent practice of allopathic medicine contemplated under the 2004 Act. The Supreme Court held that the 2015 legislation had removed the basis of the earlier constitutional defect and did not conflict with the Indian Medical Council Act, 1956, or the standards prescribed thereunder. Accordingly, the State Legislature possessed the competence to enact the 2015 legislation. The Court therefore upheld its constitutional validity while rejecting the challenges raised by diploma holders seeking restoration of their earlier professional status and privileges. In its final decision dated 24 January 2023, the Supreme Court upheld the invalidation of the Assam Rural Health Regulatory Authority Act, 2004, while affirming the validity of the Assam Community Professionals (Registration and Competency) Act, 2015. The Civil Appeals arising from SLP(C) Nos. 32592–32593 of 2015 and Transferred Case (Civil) Nos. 24 and 25 of 2018 were dismissed, with the parties directed to bear their respective costs. The judgment reinforces the constitutional distinction between the States’ authority to legislate on medical education and healthcare and Parliament’s overriding authority to prescribe national standards in medical education. It also establishes that State Governments may formulate alternative community healthcare frameworks to address rural medical needs, provided such legislation remains within their constitutional competence and does not conflict with centrally prescribed standards governing modern medicine. List of Cases Discussed 1. Association of Medical Superspeciality Aspirants and Residents & Ors. Vs Union of India & Ors., (2019) 8 SCC 607 (Supreme Court): Cited by counsel representing the diploma holders to emphasise the importance of rural healthcare and the State’s responsibility to ensure adequate medical services in underserved areas. 2. Chintpurni Medical College & Hospital Vs State of Punjab, (2018) 15 SCC 1 (Supreme Court): Relied upon by the Indian Medical Association in support of the statutory requirements governing the establishment and regulation of medical colleges. 3. Prof. Yashpal Vs State of Chhattisgarh, (2005) 5 SCC 420 (Supreme Court): Cited to support the importance of maintaining educational standards and the constitutional limitations upon State legislation concerning higher education. 4. Subhasis Bakshi Vs West Bengal Medical Council, (2003) 9 SCC 269 (Supreme Court): Considered in detail concerning the limited right of diploma holders in community medical services to prescribe medicines and issue medical certificates. The Supreme Court examined the distinction between the rights recognised under the relevant West Bengal framework and the broader medical practice authorised under the Assam Act. 5. Dr. Preeti Srivastava Vs State of Madhya Pradesh, (1999) 7 SCC 120 (Supreme Court): Relied upon by the Medical Council of India to establish that States cannot prescribe educational standards inconsistent with the standards determined under the applicable Central legislation. 6. Dr. Mukhtiar Chand Vs State of Punjab, (1998) 7 SCC 579 (Supreme Court): Extensively examined concerning registration of medical practitioners, the practice of modern scientific medicine and the circumstances in which practitioners of Indian medicine may prescribe allopathic medicines. The Court considered the decision in the context of the qualifications and registration requirements under the Indian Medical Council Act, 1956. 7. Medical Council of India Vs State of Karnataka, (1998) 6 SCC 131 (Supreme Court): Cited by the Medical Council of India to emphasise the binding nature of centrally prescribed standards governing medical education and professional qualifications. 8. Thirumuruga Kirupananda Variyar Thavathiru Sundara Swamigal Medical Educational and Charitable Trust Vs State of Tamil Nadu, (1996) 3 SCC 15 (Supreme Court): Relied upon to establish the scope of Section 10A of the Indian Medical Council Act, 1956, and the parliamentary framework governing the establishment of medical colleges. 9. Dr. A.K. Sabhapathy Vs State of Kerala, 1992 Supp (3) SCC 147; AIR 1992 SC 1310 (Supreme Court): Considered in the judgment’s discussion of the three principal medical-practice precedents. The decision concerned the qualifications necessary for practising allopathic medicine and its relationship with the subsequent decisions in Dr. Mukhtiar Chand and Subhasis Bakshi. 10. Gujarat University Vs Krishna Ranganath Mudholkar, 1963 Supp (1) SCR 112 (Supreme Court): Discussed concerning the division of legislative powers between Parliament and State Legislatures and the constitutional limitations imposed by Entry 66 of List I upon State legislation concerning higher education. 11. R. Chitralekha Vs State of Mysore, AIR 1964 SC 1823 (Supreme Court): Relied upon by the appellants concerning the extent to which State measures relating to education may operate without impermissibly interfering with Parliament’s power to coordinate and determine educational standards. 12. State of Bombay Vs F.N. Balsara, AIR 1951 SC 318 (Supreme Court): Discussed concerning the doctrine of pith and substance and the principle that incidental encroachment upon another legislative field does not necessarily invalidate legislation otherwise enacted within constitutional competence. 13. Hingir-Rampur Coal Co. Ltd. Vs State of Orissa, AIR 1961 SC 459 (Supreme Court): Relied upon by the State of Assam in its submissions concerning legislative fields, parliamentary declarations and the circumstances in which competing legislative provisions may give rise to constitutional repugnancy. 14. Atiabari Tea Co. Ltd. Vs State of Assam, AIR 1961 SC 232 (Supreme Court): Referred to in explaining the doctrine of pith and substance and the method of determining legislative competence by examining the true character of an enactment. 15. Calcutta Gas Company Vs State of West Bengal, AIR 1962 SC 1044 (Supreme Court): Discussed for the principle of harmonious construction of legislative entries and the need to reconcile apparently overlapping constitutional powers. 16. Hoechst Pharmaceuticals Ltd. Vs State of Bihar, AIR 1983 SC 1019 (Supreme Court): Referred to in the discussion of legislative competence and the constitutional distinction between general legislative subjects and taxation powers. 17. Ujagar Prints Vs Union of India, AIR 1989 SC 516 (Supreme Court): Cited concerning the liberal interpretation of legislative entries and the application of the doctrine of pith and substance when determining the constitutional validity of legislation. 18. Prafulla Kumar Mukherjee Vs Bank of Commerce, Khulna, AIR 1947 PC 60 (Privy Council): Discussed as an authority on the doctrine of pith and substance, particularly the proposition that incidental encroachment upon another legislative field does not necessarily invalidate an enactment. 19. United Provinces Vs Atiqa Begum, AIR 1941 FC 16 (Federal Court): Referred to for the principle that legislative authority over a specified subject extends to matters reasonably incidental and ancillary to that subject.

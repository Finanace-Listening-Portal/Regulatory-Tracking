Akon Phom and Others Vs State of Nagaland and Others (Supreme Court of India) Written Test Added After Recruitment Advertisement Not Automatically Arbitrary: Supreme Court Upholds Nagaland Constable Selection Introduction Does introducing a written examination after publication of a recruitment advertisement necessarily invalidate the selection process? The Supreme Court has upheld such a change where the governing rules permitted competitive examination or selection, the advertisement authorised corrective notifications, and candidates received equal opportunity without demonstrated prejudice. In T. Akon Phom and Others v. State of Nagaland and Others, 2026 INSC 1211, decided on 16 November 2026, the Supreme Court dismissed a challenge to the introduction of a written examination for recruitment of Excise Constables. The judgment recognises the recruiting authority’s discretion while examining statutory compliance, transparency and fairness. Background of the Recruitment The Department of Excise and Prohibition, Government of Nagaland, advertised 40 Excise Constable posts on 7 April 2025. Six additional posts were subsequently notified, taking the total vacancies to 46. Recruitment was governed by the Nagaland Excise Service (Revised) Rules, 1999, framed under the proviso to Article 309 of the Constitution. The prescribed educational qualification was Class VIII. The original advertisement contemplated a Physical Test and Suitability Test/Viva-Voce. However, through a corrigendum dated 4 June 2025, the Departmental Recruitment Board introduced a written examination. A subsequent notification specified that the examination would carry 100 marks, contain multiple-choice questions of Class VIII standard, and have no negative marking. Candidates Challenge the Additional Examination The appellants argued that introducing a written examination altered the selection procedure after recruitment had commenced. According to them, the authorities were bound by the original advertisement and a departmental memorandum dated 19 July 2016. They also questioned the Board’s authority to independently introduce the examination. Another objection concerned the written test carrying 100 marks, compared with 12 marks for the viva-voce. The State explained that it had received 11,521 applications for 46 posts. Of the 5,985 candidates who appeared in the Physical Test, 2,297 qualified. An objective written examination was therefore considered necessary to shortlist candidates fairly and manage the exceptionally large field. Single Judge and Division Bench Take Different Views The Single Judge accepted the challenge. Although the Government could prescribe a selection method where the rules were silent, the Judge held that the method should have been specified in the original advertisement. The corrigendum and Physical Test were quashed. The State was directed either to proceed under the original advertisement and memorandum or issue a fresh advertisement incorporating the written examination. The Division Bench reversed that decision. The unsuccessful challengers consequently approached the Supreme Court. Governing Rules Permitted Competitive Examination The Supreme Court found that Rule 5(i)(a) expressly contemplated direct recruitment through competitive examination/selection. Rule 7(1) provided for a Selection Board, while the relevant schedules prescribed direct recruitment and the Class VIII qualification. A written examination based on that educational standard fell within the recruitment method authorised by the rules. Consequently, Rule 5(ii), requiring consultation with the Commission for adopting a different recruitment method, was not attracted. The written test did not contravene the statutory framework. Advertisement Authorised Corrective Notifications The Court also examined the powers reserved in the advertisement. Clause 15 empowered the Departmental Recruitment Board to rectify omissions or commissions through notifications and addenda. Clause 16 authorised it to take final and binding decisions on disputes concerning recruitment. These provisions formed part of the circumstances supporting the Board’s action. The Court further accepted that the 2016 memorandum had neither been confirmed by the Selection Board nor notified by the State Government. It had not been strictly followed in earlier recruitments and, at its highest, prescribed certain minimum physical and medical standards. Objective Assessment and Equal Opportunity The Supreme Court rejected the contention that the written examination became arbitrary merely because it was introduced after the advertisement or carried greater weight than the viva-voce. An objective examination based on the prescribed Class VIII standard assessed candidates on a common basis and reduced disproportionate dependence on the comparatively subjective viva-voce. The corrigendum had been widely publicised, including in local newspapers. Every candidate qualifying in the Physical Test faced the same requirement and received an equal opportunity to participate. The appellants failed to demonstrate discrimination or prejudice. Their participation despite awareness of the revised procedure was also relevant. The Supreme Court accordingly dismissed the appeal. Author’s Comments The judgment should be read in its specific statutory and factual setting. It does not provide unrestricted authority to alter recruitment conditions after inviting applications. The decisive considerations were the permission contained in the governing rules, the Board’s powers under the advertisement, public notification, uniform application and absence of demonstrated prejudice. The chronology also matters: the written examination was announced before the Physical Test commenced, and candidates were informed of its format before it was held. For recruiting authorities, the practical lesson is to identify and disclose the complete selection procedure at the outset wherever possible. Where a later change becomes necessary, its legality must rest on lawful authority and demonstrable fairness. Candidates challenging such changes should identify concrete statutory violations or unequal treatment, rather than relying solely on the timing of the modification. FULL TEXT OF THE SUPREME COURT JUDGMENT/ORDER 1. Leave granted. 2. This Appeal assails the judgment and order dated 23.06.2026 of the Division Bench of the Gauhati High Court, Kohima Bench. By way of the impugned judgment, the High Court had allowed Writ Appeal No.39/2025 preferred by the respondent-State and set aside the judgment and order dated 16.10.2025 of the learned Single Judge in Writ Petition (Civil) No. 128/2025. 3. The dispute arises out of a recruitment process undertaken by the respondent-Department of Excise and Prohibition, Government of Nagaland, for filling the posts of Excise Constable. The recruitment was governed by the extant Nagaland Excise Service (Revised) Rules, 1999, framed under the proviso to Article 309 of the Constitution of India. By way of context, earlier, a meeting chaired by the Chief Minister and attended by other senior functionaries of the State had been held on 16.09.2024 to consider recruitment to uniformed services. Inter alia, it was resolved that departments would duly constitute their Departmental Recruitment Boards; that vacancies would be advertised in at least two local newspapers and the respective Departmental websites; that the constituted Departmental Recruitment Boards would maintain transparency and fairness while carrying out the recruitment process, etc. 4. On 04.03.2025 and 25.03.2025, approval was accorded by the State Government for the draft Advertisement and constitution of a Departmental Recruitment Board for recruitment to the posts of Excise Constable. The said Board of the respondent-Department was formally notified on 23.04.2025. An Advertisement No. EX.1/ESTT/1/2022/62-63 dated 07.04.2025 was issued inviting applications from indigenous inhabitants of Nagaland for 40 Excise Constable posts, prescribing Class VIII as the required educational qualification. Six additional posts were subsequently notified by an Addendum Order No. Ex.l/ESTT/1/2022/404-06 dated 14.05.2025, taking the total number of posts to 46. 5. Subsequently, in continuation of the original advertisement, the Board issued a Corrigendum dated 04.06.2025, notifying the inclusion of a written examination in the recruitment process, in line with other uninformed services. Candidates were asked to prepare accordingly. The Physical Test was conducted between 14.07.2025 and 24.07.2025. A Notification dated 25.07.2025 notified the release of names of candidates who had qualified for the written examination and further stated that the examination would carry 100 marks, consist of Multiple-Choice Questions, based on the Class VIII standard; have no negative marking; and was scheduled to be conducted between 10:00 am and 12:00 pm on 09.08.2025. 6. Ms. Diksha Rai, learned counsel for the appellants, contended that Clause 4 of the Advertisement dated 07.04.2025 stipulated that the recruitment process would consist of two stages: (a) Physical Test; and (b) Suitability Test/Viva-Voce. Therefore, introduction of a written examination was contrary to the terms notified in the initial advertisement. Relying on the 1999 Rules and a Memorandum No. EX-5/3/2000/6036 dated 19.07.2016 issued by the Commissioner of Excise and Prohibition, the appellants contend that the selection criteria could not be altered after the recruitment process had commenced. 7. Per contra, Mr. K.N. Balgopal, learned Advocate General for the State of Nagaland, would submit that the scale of the recruitment is relevant to the dispute at hand. The State has received as many as 11,521 applications against 46 posts, out of which, 5,985 candidates appeared in the Physical Test, and 2,297 candidates cleared it and became eligible to proceed to the next stage. It is, thus, argued that, in these circumstances, an objective written screening examination was necessary to reduce the large field of candidates and to ensure that the subsequent stage of selection was conducted fairly and objectively. It is also highlighted by the learned Advocate General that all the appellants, except appellant No. 4, participated in the Physical Test, and ten of the sixteen appellants herein, being appellant Nos. 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 7, 8, 12, 15 & 16, further participated in the written examination as admitted before the High Court. Therefore, it is argued that once having participated in the recruitment process, the appellants cannot challenge the same. 8. The learned Single Judge allowed the appellants’ writ petition on 16.10.2025, reasoning that although the Government could prescribe an appropriate selection method where the Rules were silent, the method had to be specified in the original advertisement and could not be introduced midway through recruitment. Further, the learned Single Judge found that though there was no illegality in the constitution of the Departmental Recruitment Board, given Government approval had preceded the advertisement and no prejudice was shown on that account, the Board lacked authority to independently prescribe a change in the selection procedure. The respondent-Department was bound by the terms of the Memorandum dated 19.07.2016 regarding the applicable physical and medical standards. Accordingly, the Corrigendum dated 04.06.2025 and the Physical Test were quashed, and the State was directed to conduct recruitment in accordance with the original advertisement and the Memorandum or issue a fresh advertisement if it wished to introduce a written examination. 9. Aggrieved, the State preferred Writ Appeal No. 39/2025. The Division Bench on 03.12.2025 permitted the recruitment process to continue in the meantime, subject to the outcome of the writ appeal viz. the results would not be declared without the leave of the Court. Ultimately, vide the impugned judgment dated 23.06.2026, the Division Bench allowed the State’s appeal and set aside the judgment and order of the learned Single Judge. 10. The State has drawn our attention to the fact that thereafter, the recruitment process continued and came to a conclusion on 30.06.2026, well before notice was issued and stay was granted by this Court on 07.07.2026. Specifically, medical tests were conducted on 29.01.2026 and 30.02.2026. Of the 2297 candidates, who passed the written examination, 211 were called for interview on 10.02.2026 (in an approximate ratio of 5:1) and interviews were conducted from 19.02.2026 to 21.02.2026. Finally, the Departmental Recruitment Board held a meeting on 24.06.2026 to complete the requisite formalities. Administrative approval was secured on 26.06.2026 and the list of the 46 selected candidates was notified on 29.06.2026. All the candidates so selected conveyed their acceptance between 01.07.2026 and 08.07.2026. The State has objected that said fact was not disclosed by the appellants before this Court and the successful candidates have also erroneously not been impleaded in the present Appeal. Owing solely to the pendency of this litigation, entire process has been kept in abeyance and status quo has been maintained. 11. In response, the appellants have pointed out that three of forty-six acceptance letters were only received on 08.07.2026 and mere publication of select-list does not render the instant Appeal infructuous, given candidates have yet to be appointed against the posts. 12. We have perused the record and are satisfied that there is no reason to interfere with the well-reasoned judgment of the Division Bench of the High Court, particularly that the various contentions raised by the appellants have already been considered and rejected by the court below. The recruitment was governed by the 1999 Rules, Rule 5(i)(a) of which provides for direct recruitment through “competitive examination/selection” and Rule 7(1) provides for a Selection Board for direct recruitment to the relevant posts. Schedule II prescribes direct recruitment as the method of recruitment to the Group C (Non- Gazetted) post of Excise Constable and Schedule III prescribes Class VIII as the requisite educational qualification for the same. Nothing in the recruitment process, therefore, contravened the 1999 Rules. We may add that the written examination, based on the Class VIII syllabus, falls under “competitive examination/selection” within the meaning of Rule 5(i)(a) and, therefore, Rule 5(ii), which requires the Government to consult with the Commission to adopt a different method of recruitment, is not attracted at all. 13. The appellants have vehemently contended that since 1999 Rules do not prescribe the detailed procedure for conducting the selection, the Memorandum dated 19.07.2016 held the field in this regard. However, we opine that the Division Bench had rightly accepted the State’s contention that the Memorandum had neither been confirmed by the Selection Board nor notified by the State Government and had not been strictly enforced in previous recruitment processes, and at its highest, merely prescribes certain minimum physical and medical standards for various athletic activities. The Departmental Recruitment Board, on the other hand, was constituted for purpose of recruitment to the posts of Excise Constable, consistent with the decision taken in the meeting dated 16.09.2024. The learned Single Judge, too, had accepted that the Departmental Recruitment Board was validly constituted under Rule 7 of the 1999 Rules and negatived the appellants’ contention that the constitution of the Board was irregular and caused prejudice to them. 14. The Advertisement empowered this Departmental Recruitment Board to rectify any omission or commission through notifications and addenda under Clause 15 and expressly vested in it the authority to take a final and binding decision in any dispute concerning the recruitment process under Clause 16. 15. Given these circumstances, we are unable to accept the appellants’ contention that the introduction of the written examination rendered the selection process arbitrary because it was introduced after the original Advertisement, or because it carried greater weightage of 100 marks than the Viva-Voce of 12 marks (as disclosed by respondent in response to an RTI query). In fact, our considered view is that an objective written examination, based on the already-prescribed Class VIII standard, provides a means of assessing candidates on a common basis and avoids placing disproportionate reliance on the comparatively subjective assessment inherent in a Viva-Voce. The policy decision to introduce the written examination was taken in the context of the exceptionally large number of applicants for the 46 posts, and the Corrigendum dated 04.06.2025 was widely publicised, including in the local newspapers Nagaland Post and Eastern Mirror, as previously resolved by the respondent- Department. Further, the Division Bench had correctly appreciated that all candidates who qualified in the Physical Test were subjected to the same requirement and afforded an equal opportunity to participate in the recruitment process. The appellants have not been able to demonstrate how the process operated discriminatorily or caused any prejudice to them as admittedly having participated in the selection process despite being aware of the revised procedure. 16. In these circumstances, and having regard to the discretion available to the competent recruiting authority in devising selection/shortlisting procedures where the applicable Rules do not prescribe a detailed method, we find no ground to interfere with the judgment and order dated 23.06.2026 passed by the Division Bench of the High Court. 17. The Appeal is, accordingly, dismissed.

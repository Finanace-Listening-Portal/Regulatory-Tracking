For banks, the requirement creates a funding cost even when a derivative is being used to hedge a genuine import payment rather than to take a speculative position. Cash maintained with the central bank cannot be deployed for other treasury activities while it remains subject to the reserve requirement. Lenders must therefore weigh the cost of maintaining the reserve against the income earnedfrom arranging the hedge. Market estimates suggest that the cost of hedging could rise by around 1.3 to 1.5 per cent for some contracts, although the actual impact will depend on the tenor of the hedge, banks' funding costs and how much of the additional expense they pass on to customers.The hidden cost is not a 20 per cent charge levied on customers. It arises because banks must maintain cash with the RBI against eligible contracts, creating an opportunity cost for funds that could otherwise be deployed elsewhere. The requirement is intended to curb excessive demand for forward dollars and ease pressure on the rupee. However, the extent to which banks will pass on the cost to customers remains uncertain as lenders seek clarification on the implementation of the framework. Advt How the reserve raises costsUnder the Foreign Exchange Risk Reserve (FERR) framework, authorised dealer banks must maintain a cash reserve with the RBI equivalent to 20 per cent of the rupee equivalent of the notional value of eligible rupee-linked foreign exchange derivative contracts exceeding $2 million. The framework covers specified contracts used to hedge current account transactions in which customers buy foreign currency against the rupee.For banks, the requirement creates a funding cost even when a derivative is being used to hedge a genuine import payment rather than to take a speculative position. Cash maintained with the central bank cannot be deployed for other treasury activities while it remains subject to the reserve requirement. Lenders must therefore weigh the cost of maintaining the reserve against the income earned from arranging the hedge.Market estimates suggest that the additional cost could increase hedging expenses by around 1.3 to 1.5 per cent in some cases, particularly for longer-tenor contracts. This is an estimate of the potential impact, not a uniform charge prescribed by the RBI. The actual cost will depend on the duration of the contract, the bank's funding costs and its pricing decisions.Banks seek clarity on implementationBanks have sought clarification on the calculation of the reserve, its implications for liquidity and regulatory ratios, and the extent to which the additional expense can be recovered from customers. The requirement could affect banks differently depending on their eligible derivative exposures and funding profiles. Advt Lenders have also raised concerns about the $2 million threshold and how it should apply to multiple transactions, complex options strategies and other derivative structures. The implementation of the framework will determine how these cases are treated and whether banks need to make changes to their systems, documentation and monitoring processes.The distinction between a reserve requirement and a direct transaction fee is important. A fee would impose an explicit cost on eligible transactions, while a cash reserve creates an opportunity cost for banks. The latter's impact on customers will depend on how lenders incorporate the cost into their pricing. Advt What it means for importers and the rupeeImporters face a trade-off between paying more to lock in an exchange rate and remaining exposed to movements in the rupee until their payments fall due. If forward hedging becomes more expensive, some businesses could reduce the size or tenor of their hedges, stagger transactions or postpone buying dollars forward. That could reduce demand in the forward market, but it could also leave businesses more exposed to sudden currency movements.The FERR requirement forms part of a wider set of RBI measures announced in October to manage pressure on the rupee and curb activity in the foreign exchange derivatives market. These include restrictions on rebooking cancelled rupee-linked derivative contracts and a reduction in the threshold for certain transactions without an underlying exposure from $100 million to $5 million. Advt The RBI has also arranged for the daily dollar requirements of three state-owned oil marketing companies to be met through a separate mechanism, reducing their need to purchase dollars directly in the spot market. The rupee strengthened by around 1 per cent on October 12, although a single day's movement does not establish that underlying pressure on the currency has eased.The effectiveness of the measures will depend on whether they reduce excessive demand for dollars without making routine currency protection prohibitively expensive for businesses. If banks pass on much of the additional funding cost, importers will bear more of the burden. If they absorb part of it, the impact will fall on lenders' margins. The RBI's challenge is to curb destabilising demand for dollars while preserving access to affordable hedging for companies with genuine foreign currency liabilities.

Summary: A registered person cannot ordinarily furnish a GST annual return after three years from its due date. Because the statutory due date for the FY 2022-23 annual return was 31 December 2023, the ordinary three-year period ends on 31 December 2026. This guide explains the legal basis, identifies the taxpayers for whom GSTR-9 or GSTR-9C may have applied, separates the time bar from late-fee and tax-payment questions, and sets out an evidence-led review for GSTINs that still show the year as pending. It also cautions that the proviso to section 44(2) allows only a notified relaxation and should not be treated as an automatic condonation power. Key Takeaways Show all 19 sections Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); The 31 December 2026 cut-off follows from section 44(2), not a fresh filing extension Section 44(2) of the Central Goods and Services Tax Act, 2017 bars a registered person from furnishing an annual return for a financial year after three years from the due date for that return. The provision was inserted by the Finance Act, 2023 and brought into force from 1 October 2023 through Notification No. 28/2023-Central Tax. The statutory calculation matters: the FY 2022-23 annual return was due on 31 December 2023 under rule 80, so the ordinary three-year period reaches its endpoint on 31 December 2026. The 2026 date is therefore not a new due date for FY 2022-23. A taxpayer filing now is already late and must separately consider applicable late fee, unpaid tax, interest and any consequence of prior non-compliance. The date instead marks the end of the ordinary statutory window in which the portal can accept the annual return. Businesses should not describe the exercise as an extension or assume that waiting until the final day changes the original default. Section 44 excludes specified categories such as an Input Service Distributor, a person paying tax under section 51 or section 52, a casual taxable person and a non-resident taxable person. Government notifications may also exempt classes of registered persons for a particular year. The reviewer should preserve the FY 2022-23 notification applied, the taxpayer category, aggregate turnover computation and the conclusion for each GSTIN. GSTR-9C is not a separate annual return that can be analysed in isolation. Rule 80 requires the self-certified reconciliation statement along with GSTR-9 where the turnover condition applies. A business should reconcile the PAN-level aggregate turnover test with GSTIN-level books and the form’s jurisdictional figures. The older article or software setting used in 2023 is not sufficient evidence if the underlying registration, merger, cancellation or turnover data has since been corrected. Voluntary filing and an exempt filing are different decisions Where a class was exempt from mandatory GSTR-9 for FY 2022-23, the team should verify whether the portal permits or records voluntary filing and whether a filing decision is appropriate. A voluntary return is not a casual clean-up step because GSTR-9 cannot ordinarily be revised after filing. Conversely, a taxpayer that was required to file should not treat an exemption applicable to a different turnover band, form or year as a defence. The file should record the exact legal basis for either action. Reconcile the annual return before the portal window closes Build the return from filed period returns, books and year-end adjustments A late annual return should still be prepared as a reconciliation document. Tie outward supplies to GSTR-1 and GSTR-3B, identify amendments reported in later periods, and separate taxable, zero-rated, exempt and non-GST supplies. Reconcile tax paid and input tax credit with the electronic ledgers and the books. Differences should be explained with transaction-level evidence rather than forced into the portal’s pre-filled amounts merely to obtain a successful submission. The annual return does not reopen a lapsed entitlement to input tax credit or permit a revision of every periodic return. If reconciliation identifies omitted liability, determine the legally available payment route and interest position under the law then applicable. If it identifies credit that was never validly claimed within the statutory time limit, do not present GSTR-9 as a mechanism to create that credit. The annual-return working should distinguish disclosure, payment and credit-entitlement conclusions. Portal readiness is an operational risk, not a change in the legal test Check the filing sequence and resolve technical blockers early GSTN advisories on the three-year restriction warned taxpayers that affected returns would be barred from filing after the statutory period. The portal may also require earlier returns to be furnished before the annual-return tile becomes available. A team should log in well before 31 December, confirm the correct financial year and GSTIN, download available data, verify authorised signatory access and test the digital signature or electronic verification process. A screenshot of a disabled tile should be supported by a helpdesk ticket and a legal analysis; it is not itself proof that the return was not required. Where a technical fault prevents filing, preserve timestamps, error messages, ticket numbers, attempted JSON uploads and correspondence. That evidence may be relevant in a later proceeding, but it does not guarantee condonation or override section 44(2). Escalation should occur while a remedy is still operationally possible. The authorised signatory should approve the final figures only after the preparer and reviewer have closed all material reconciliation items. Do not confuse a requested amnesty with an issued notification Trade bodies may seek an amnesty or extension and news reports may discuss such representations. The proviso to section 44(2), however, requires Government action by notification on the GST Council’s recommendation and may impose conditions and restrictions. Until an authenticated notification is available, the conservative compliance position is to work to the existing statutory endpoint. An internal note should identify the official source checked and the date and time of that verification. Consequences after the bar require a case-specific legal response Once the ordinary period expires, a taxpayer should not assume that an unfiled annual return can be cured by an ordinary portal request. The correct response depends on later notifications, portal implementation, pending notices, assessed liabilities and available legal remedies. The substantive tax, interest and penalty exposure does not disappear merely because the return is blocked. Equally, a blocked form should not lead to invented figures or duplicate payments without reconciling prior discharge and proceedings. The compliance file should include the applicability memo, return workings, management approval, filing acknowledgement or documented blocker, payment evidence, and a calendar for responding to any notice. If the taxpayer has multiple registrations, the file must show which GSTINs were completed and which remain exposed. This makes the conclusion reviewable by auditors, successors and counsel rather than dependent on an email saying that the portal was checked. Frequently Asked Questions on the FY 2022-23 filing bar Is 31 December 2026 an extended due date for FY 2022-23 GSTR-9? No. The original due date was 31 December 2023. The 2026 date is the endpoint of the ordinary three-year period under section 44(2). Does the three-year bar erase tax, interest or late fee? No. It restricts furnishing the annual return after the statutory period; it does not extinguish underlying liability or automatically waive consequences of delay. Can a helpdesk ticket allow filing after the bar? A ticket can document a technical problem but does not replace a Government notification under the proviso to section 44(2) or a competent legal remedy. Must every registered person file GSTR-9 for FY 2022-23? No. Statutory exclusions and any year-specific exemption must be checked. Applicability should be documented separately for every GSTIN. Can missed input tax credit be claimed through GSTR-9 now? GSTR-9 is not a general mechanism to revive credit that was not claimed within the applicable statutory time limit. The entitlement requires separate legal analysis. What if GSTR-9C applied but GSTR-9 is still pending? The reconciliation statement accompanies the annual return where rule 80 applies. Prepare both consistently and verify portal sequence and turnover thresholds before filing. Should a taxpayer wait for a possible amnesty? A representation or expectation is not law. Unless an authenticated notification grants relief, the safer operational course is to complete the review and filing within the existing window. Key Takeaways Section 44(2) creates a three-year outer limit measured from the original annual-return due date. For FY 2022-23, the ordinary period ends on 31 December 2026 because the due date was 31 December 2023. Applicability, late fee, tax payment, interest and input-tax-credit questions remain separate analyses. Only a notified relaxation under the statutory proviso can reopen filing after the bar; a request for amnesty is not enough.

The Finance Act, 2025 addressed the overlap between buyer-side TDS and seller-side TCS on ordinary goods transactions. For businesses reviewing FY 2026-27 purchases, the relevant question is therefore whether section 194Q requires the buyer to deduct tax, rather than whether the seller will collect TCS under the withdrawn subsection. Practical Application This change does not abolish every TCS provision in section 206C. Special categories of goods and transactions may be governed by other provisions. Accounts-payable teams should avoid broad ERP rules that treat all purchase invoices alike. A separate transaction-type assessment is essential. Check Buyer Turnover and Seller Threshold Independently Section 194Q generally applies to a buyer whose total sales, gross receipts or turnover from business exceeded ₹10 crore in the immediately preceding financial year. The purchase threshold is more than ₹50 lakh from a resident seller during the relevant financial year. Both tests must be applied: the buyer test is based on the previous year, while the seller-wise purchase threshold is tracked in the current year. Apply the Rate and Timing Correctly The ordinary rate under section 194Q is 0.1% of the purchase amount exceeding ₹50 lakh, subject to applicable statutory variations such as PAN-related rules. Deduction is linked to the earlier of credit of the sum to the seller’s account or payment. Advance payments therefore require attention even when the invoice has not yet been booked. Distinguish Section 194Q from Other Withholding Rules Transactions involving non-resident sellers, specified categories of goods, e-commerce arrangements and payments already covered by another applicable withholding provision can require different analysis. A buyer should test the statutory priority and exceptions before deducting under section 194Q. This is particularly important where the vendor’s description of the transaction differs from its legal substance.

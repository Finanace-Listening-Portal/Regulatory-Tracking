Articles 5 min read How much will the FCNR scheme cost the RBI? The question has gained importance after banks mobilised $127.23 billion through FCNR(B) deposits by August 31, far exceeding the $65-70 billion mobilisation assumed in an earlier assessment. ETBFSI Research ETBFSI Published On Sep 8, 2026 at 07:00 AM IST var news_msid = 133894330; console.log(news_msid); Select ETBFSI as Preferred Choice on Google Copy Link Share on WhatsApp Share on Linkedin Share on X Share on Telegram Share on Facebook The RBI could face a sizeable hedging cost, with an earlier SBI Research estimate putting the five-year cost at about $10.5 billion based on a $70 billion FCNR(B) corpus and a 3% annual hedging cost.A separate estimate puts the potential cost at around Rs 36,000 crore over three to five years, based on a 3% hedging cost on roughly Rs 12 lakh crore raised through the foreign-currency schemes.The $10.5 billion figure cannot be applied directly to the final $127.23 billion mobilisation, as the original calculation was based on a much smaller corpus and specific assumptions on hedging costs and maturity.The eventual net cost could be lower than the gross hedging expense, as returns earned on the foreign-currency assets can offset part of the cost; the final impact will depend on swap pricing, maturities and asset returns.The Reserve Bank of India’s special FCNR(B) swap scheme could entail a sizeable gross hedging cost over the life of the deposits, although the eventual impact on the central bank’s income will depend on the returns earned on the foreign-currency assets generated by the inflows and the actual cost of the swaps.The RBI’s special swap facility also attracted $5.26 billion through overseas foreign-currency borrowings and $3.89 billion through external commercial borrowings, taking total mobilisation under the facility to $136.38 billion. Advt The earlier assessment estimated that a 3% annual USD/INR hedging cost on a $70 billion FCNR(B) corpus would amount to about $2.1 billion a year, or $10.5 billion cumulatively over five years if the cost remained unchanged. With the actual FCNR(B) mobilisation nearly twice as large, that figure cannot be treated as the final cost of the scheme.Why the cost is not simply 3% of $127 billionThe structure of the RBI facility is important in assessing the cost. The central bank allows banks to swap eligible foreign-currency inflows for rupees at concessional terms, effectively taking on the hedging cost that banks would otherwise incur in the market.The cost therefore arises from the pricing of the currency hedge. It is not an upfront payment by the central bank equivalent to a fixed percentage of the entire $127.23 billion.The maturity profile also matters. The earlier $10.5 billion calculation assumed that the deposits would remain outstanding for five years and that the annual hedging cost would stay at 3%. Under those assumptions, the cost accumulated to 15% of the initial corpus.Applying that 15% assumption mechanically to the final $127.23 billion mobilisation would produce a figure of about $19.1 billion. But that should not be described as the RBI’s expected cost, as it applies assumptions used for a much smaller corpus. Advt A separate estimate puts the potential cost at around Rs 36,000 crore over three to five years, based on a 3% hedging cost on roughly Rs 12 lakh crore raised through the various foreign-currency schemes. This too represents a potential gross cost rather than a final loss for the RBI.Dollar assets provide an offsetThe RBI also acquires foreign-currency assets against the dollar inflows, providing a potential source of income that can offset part of the gross cost of the swaps.The central bank had recouped $31.2 billion of foreign-currency assets by August 7, equivalent to about 55% of the amount mobilised at that stage. A portion of these funds could be invested in US securities, allowing the RBI to earn returns on the dollars while the associated swap obligations remain outstanding. Advt The return on these assets is important because it can reduce the net financial impact of the concessional hedge. The higher the return relative to the effective hedging cost, the smaller the eventual impact on the RBI’s income.The precise offset cannot be established yet because it will depend on how the assets are deployed, the returns earned and the duration for which the funds remain with the central bank.Liquidity management adds another considerationThe scheme has also created a separate challenge for the RBI by injecting a large amount of rupee liquidity into the banking system.The $136.38 billion of total inflows under the special facility contributed to a sharp liquidity surplus, which reached about Rs 11.6 lakh crore in early September. The RBI responded by absorbing more than Rs 6 lakh crore through overnight and 30-day variable-rate reverse repo operations. Advt This is not the same as the hedging cost of the FCNR(B) scheme and should not be added to the $10.5 billion or Rs 36,000 crore estimates. It is a separate consequence of the large foreign-currency inflows and the RBI’s subsequent liquidity-management operations.Final cost remains uncertainThe eventual financial impact of the FCNR(B) scheme will depend on several moving parts: the actual maturity profile of deposits, the effective cost of the RBI’s swaps, the returns earned on the resulting foreign-currency assets and the path of the rupee over the life of the transactions.The scale of mobilisation means the eventual gross hedging cost could be larger than the earlier $10.5 billion estimate, simply because the actual FCNR(B) corpus turned out to be substantially larger than the $65-70 billion assumption.But it would be equally misleading to apply the 15% assumption mechanically to the final $127.23 billion and call the resulting $19.1 billion an RBI loss. The earlier estimate was a potential cost under a specific set of assumptions, not an estimate of the final loss on the actual corpus mobilised.For now, the most defensible conclusion is that the FCNR(B) scheme carries a potentially sizeable gross hedging cost, but the final net impact on the RBI will depend on the actual swap economics and the returns earned on the foreign-currency assets.The central bank’s eventual financial impact will become clearer only as the swaps run their course and the returns on the foreign-currency assets are realised.

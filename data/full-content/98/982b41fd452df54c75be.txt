The system-wide liquidity surplus is expected to be absorbed over the course of FY27, potentially bringing deposit mobilisation and funding costs back into focus if strong credit growth persists.Bank credit grew 18.1% year-on-year as of September 15, compared with 17.3% growth in deposits, indicating that loan growth continues to outpace the expansion of banks’ deposit base. Outstanding deposits fell by Rs 2.49 lakh crore during the fortnight, while outstanding bank credit declined by Rs 57,894 crore, according to RBI data. Advt The immediate pressure on deposit costs, however, has eased because banks received substantial foreign currency inflows through the FCNR(B) facility. FCNR(B) deposits accounted for $132.98 billion of the $143.596 billion mobilised through the special foreign currency facility, creating a large liquidity surplus in the banking system.The additional liquidity has reduced banks’ dependence on relatively expensive wholesale funding. Outstanding certificates of deposit fell to Rs 5.60 lakh crore as of September 15 from about Rs 6.90 lakh crore in mid-August, allowing banks to run down short-term borrowings as surplus funds entered the system.The effect is also visible in deposit pricing. The weighted average rate on fresh rupee term deposits declined to 5.67% in August from 5.85% in July, while the weighted average lending rate on fresh rupee loans rose to 8.61% from 8.52%. The gap between fresh lending and deposit rates therefore widened even before the October rate hike.Liquidity cushionThe liquidity cushion is not expected to be permanent. The RBI expects a large part of the surplus to be absorbed within the financial year through currency leakage, banks’ reserve requirements and its liquidity-management operations. The banking system has been running an average surplus of about Rs 7.3 lakh crore since early September. Advt Once the surplus declines, the combination of strong credit demand and slower deposit growth could force banks to compete more actively for deposits. That could push up the cost of fresh deposits and put pressure on margins, particularly if the tightening cycle extends beyond the October increase.For now, however, the immediate effect of the 25 bps repo-rate hike is unlikely to be a broad-based increase in deposit rates. The unusually large liquidity injection from FCNR(B) inflows has given banks room to absorb the initial tightening without immediately having to raise the price of deposits.

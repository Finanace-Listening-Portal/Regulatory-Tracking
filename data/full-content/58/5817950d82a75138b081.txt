Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); In re Jivraj Tea International Private Limited (GST AAR Gujarat) Summary: The Gujarat Authority for Advance Ruling held that Jivraj Tea International Private Limited’s proposed retail packs combining tea with stainless steel teaspoons, plastic teaspoons, plastic honey spoons, glass tea cups or wooden honey dippers constitute mixed supplies rather than composite supplies. Tea paired with stainless steel or plastic spoons, plastic honey spoons or glass cups attracts 18% GST, comprising 9% CGST and 9% SGST. Tea paired with wooden honey dippers attracts 5% GST, comprising 2.5% CGST and 2.5% SGST. The distinction follows the highest-rate rule under Section 8(b) of the CGST Act, 2017: the order records tea and wooden honey dippers at 5%, while the other four accessories attract 18%. The applicant manufactures, packs and supplies black tea, CTC tea, flavoured black tea, green tea and herbal tea under HSN 0902, stated to attract 5% GST. It proposed 21 product combinations covering 5 kg black-tea pouches, 250 g and 500 g CTC jars, saffron and rose tea, lemon, jasmine and mint green tea, Chai Vedic herbal tea and Pryds pyramid tea bags. Its application raised 18 combination-specific questions on the nature and rate of supply, followed by a question on the applicable HSN code. The application was filed on 9 March 2026, and its representatives reiterated the submissions at the hearing on 6 August 2026. The applicant submitted that the accessories were selected for functional suitability and consumer convenience: spoons assisted preparation, honey dippers facilitated adding honey, and cups enabled consumption. It claimed that tea was the sole principal product, the customer intended to purchase tea, and the entire MRP was attributable to tea. The accessory allegedly had insignificant value, carried no separate consideration or independent commercial objective, was not independently offered for sale by the applicant, and would not be advertised as a gift or incentive. Each pack would carry one MRP and one invoice line describing tea, without a separate value, HSN or tax rate for the accessory. Examining Sections 2(30), 2(90) and 2(74), the Authority explained that identifying a predominant product does not by itself establish composite supply. The goods must also be naturally bundled and supplied together in the ordinary course of business. It considered the indicators in CBIC Flyer No. 4 dated 5 August 2019, including consumer expectations, usual industry practice, the ancillary nature of components and whether removing a component changes the overall supply. Customers do not ordinarily expect these accessories when buying tea, comparable businesses do not routinely provide such bundles, and tea can be enjoyed without the particular accessory supplied. Removing a spoon, cup or dipper would not change the nature of the tea supply. The Authority therefore rejected composite-supply treatment. The Authority found the requirements of mixed supply satisfied because the goods were not naturally bundled, were offered together for a single price, and were independently available in the market and not dependent on one another. Its rate table classified tea under HSN 09024010, 09023020 or 09022090; stainless steel spoons under 82159900; plastic tea and honey spoons under 39241090; glass tea cups under 70139900; and wooden honey dippers under 44199090. Consequently, the spoon and cup combinations were taxable at 18%, while the three wooden-honey-dipper combinations with green tea, herbal tea and pyramid tea bags were taxable at 5%. On invoicing classification, the Authority held that a mixed supply should bear the HSN code of the component attracting the highest GST rate. Where both components attract the same rate, it observed that the CGST Act does not prescribe which HSN should be selected and permitted the HSN of either constituent supply. The operative ruling answered all 19 questions on that basis. No judicial precedent was cited or discussed in the order. Since, the definitions of principal supply, composite supply and mixed supply under Section 2 of the CGST Act, 2017 has already been reproduced earlier, we are not reproducing the same again here. As per the definition of composite supply under Section 2(30) of the CGST Act, 2017, a supply is said to be a composite supply if two or more taxable supplies of goods or services or their combination thereto, are naturally bundled and supplied in conjunction with each other in the ordinary course of business. Further, one of the services in a composite supply is a principal supply. As per Section 2(90) of the CGST Act, 2017, the supply of goods or services which constitutes the predominant element of a composite supply is the principal supply, the other supplies of the composite supply being ancillary. Thus, if we assume that the supply proposed to be made by the applicant is a composite supply, then the Jivvij Samaara Black Tea, Samaara Premium CTC black tea, black tea, Green tea, Herbal tea, flavoured Black tea and the pyramid tea bags would constitute the principal supply as it constitutes the predominant element of a composite supply and the various items/commodities such as stainless steel spoon, plastic tea spoon, plastic honey spoon, glass tea cup and wooden honey dipper which is supplied along with the various types/packages of tea mentioned supra, would constitute the ancillary supply. However, we find that apart from the above, there is another leg of the definition, which is important. In a composite supply, the two or more taxable goods or supplies should be naturally bundled and supplied in conjunction with each other in the ordinary course of trade. An illustration has also been provided below the definition of composite supply to explain the meaning of naturally bundled. The example provided in the illustration is supply of goods along with transportation and insurance when the goods are packed and transported with insurance, the supply of goods, packing materials, transport and insurance is a composite supply, the supply of goods being the principal supply. The rule therefore is, if various elements of a bundled service are naturally bundled in the ordinary course of business, it shall be treated as provision of a single service which gives such bundle its essential character. 13. The CBIC in its Flyer No.4, dated 05.08.2019 has listed some indicators to ascertain whether the bundled services are naturally bundled in the ordinary course of business, as under: > The perceptionof the consumer or the service receiver: If large number of service receivers of such bundle of services reasonably expect such services to be provided as a package, then such a package could be treated as naturally bundled in the ordinary course of business. > Majority of service providers in a particular area of business provide similar bundle of services. For example, bundle of catering on board and transport by air is a bundle offered by a majority of airlines. > The nature of the various services in a bundle of services will also help in determining whether the services are bundled in the ordinary course of business. If the nature of services is such that one of the services is the main service and the other services combined with such service are in the nature of incidental or ancillary services which help in better enjoyment of a main service. For example, service of stay in a hotel is often combined with a service or laundering of 3-4 items of clothing free of cost per day. Such service is an ancillary service to the provision of hotel accommodation and the resultant package would be treated as services naturally bundled in the ordinary course of business. > Other illustrative indicators, not determinative but indicative of bundling of services in ordinary course of business are – There is a single price or the customer pays the same amount, no matter how much of the package they actually receive or use. The elements are normally advertised as a package. The different elements are not available separately. The different elements are integral to one overall supply – if one or more is removed, the nature of the supply would be affected. 13.1. We find that none of the indicators mentioned above apply to the supplies intended to be made by the applicant. The bundling of various types/packages of tea with the other items/commodities such as stainless steel spoon, plastic tea spoon, plastic honey spoon, glass tea cup and wooden honey dipper (referred to by the applicant as ancillary items) is not the perception of the customer as the customer does not expect any such bunching when he buys tea. Majority of the businesses which are similar to that of the applicant do not provide such bundling as a matter of course. Items such as stainless steel spoon, plastic tea spoon, plastic honey spoon, glass tea cup and wooden honey dipper are not ancillary commodities which are required to be supplied along with the various types/packages of tea and without which the tea cannot be enjoyed. The applicant have themselves submitted that they are considering inclusion of the above items (which they have mentioned as ancillary items) to be included in a single pack with a view to facilitate the consumption of tea by customers and familiarising the new customers with the manner of preparation and use. It is only for testing the market that they have decided to bunch various types/packages of tea with the aforementioned items. Further, these items/commodities such as stainless steel spoon, plastic tea spoon, plastic honey spoon, glass tea cup and wooden honey dipper are not integral to the overall supply such that if it is removed, the nature of the supply is affected. We are therefore, of the view that the supply of various types/packages of tea along with the items/commodities mentioned above will not constitute a composite supply. 13.2 This brings us to the next question as to whether, such supply would fall under mixed supply. As per Section 2 (74) of the CGST Act, 2017, a mixed supply means two or more individual supplies of goods or service or any combination thereof, made in conjunction with each other for a single price where such supply does not constitute a composite supply. From the definition, we find that only those bundled supplies which do not constitute a composite supply, can fall under mixed supply. We have already discussed in the preceding paras as to why the supply in question is not a composite supply. We find that the illustration provided below the definition of mixed supply gives an example of a supply of a package consisting of canned foods, sweets, chocolates, cakes, dry fruits, aerated drinks and fruit juices, which when supplied for a single price is a mixed supply. The illustration further mentions that each of these items can be supplied separately and are not dependent on each other. Thus, for a bundled supply to be a mixed supply, the following elements are required: – It should not be a composite supply (in other words they are not naturally bundled). It should have a single price. Each of the items can be supplied separately and is not dependent on each other. The items should not be supplied separately. 13.3 As mentioned earlier, the various types/packages of tea and the other items such as stainless steel spoon, plastic tea spoon, plastic honey spoon, glass tea cup and wooden honey dipper are not naturally bundled and is therefore, not a composite supply. The supply is sold for a single price. The various types/packages of tea and the other items/commodities mentioned above are sold separately in the market and thus are not dependent on each other. The various types/packages of tea is provided along with the other items/commodities mentioned above and is not supplied separately. Thus, all the ingredients of a mixed supply are present in the supply made by the applicant. Therefore, we hold that supply of the various types/packages of Tea with the other items/commodities such as stainless steel spoon, plastic tea spoon, plastic honey spoon, glass tea cup and wooden honey dipper will fall under the ambit of mixed supply and taxed accordingly. 13.4 As per Section 8 (b) of the CGST Act, 2017, a mixed supply comprising two or more supplies shall be treated as a supply of that particular supply which attracts the highest rate of tax. Thus, when the various types/packages of tea is bundled with items/commodities like stainless steel spoon, plastic tea spoon, plastic honey spoon, glass tea cup and wooden honey dipper, the highest rate of the two tax rates, which each of them attract, will be the rate of tax at which the mixed supply is to be made. We find that the last question of the applicant is regarding the HSN code to be mentioned in such supplies. In a mixed supply, the supply is to be treated as of that particular supply which attracts the highest rate of GST. Therefore, the HSN code of that particular supply having the highest tax rate of GST is to be mentioned during the course of a mixed supply. However, we find that there are instances in the present case where the GST rate of both the supplies are equal. Here, the CGST Act is silent as to under which HSN code such supply would fall. We, therefore, feel that in such instances, HSN codes of any of the supplies in a mixed supply can be mentioned while supplying the goods. 15.

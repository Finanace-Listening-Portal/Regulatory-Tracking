Summary: The 57th GST Council meeting held on 8 October 2026 marked a significant shift in GST enforcement by recommending the removal of departmental arrest powers under Section 69 of the CGST Act and an increase in the prosecution threshold from ₹1 crore to ₹5 crore. The proposed changes reflect a movement away from coercive tax administration towards a system based on trust, technology and judicial oversight. Aijaz Hussain Malik examines the distinction between ordinary tax defaults and deliberate criminal fraud, questioning whether the threat of arrest has meaningfully improved compliance. The article highlights concerns that businesses may feel pressured to settle disputed demands rather than exercise their legal remedies. It also refers to the Supreme Court’s decision in Radhika Agarwal, which addressed safeguards against coercive enforcement. The author argues that modern invoice matching and GST data analytics can identify fraudulent input tax credit more effectively than indiscriminate arrest powers. The proposed reforms do not eliminate tax recovery, penalties or prosecution for qualifying offences. Instead, they would require enforcement agencies to strengthen evidence gathering, fraud detection and coordination with criminal justice authorities. The central argument is that a mature GST regime should protect taxpayer liberty while retaining effective mechanisms to combat organised tax fraud. Yet it may have taken one of its most consequential decisions since GST was launched in 2017: it recommended withdrawing the power of GST officers to arrest, by omitting Section 69 of the CGST Act altogether. Alongside, the threshold for prosecution goes up from ₹1 crore to ₹5 crore, the minimum jail term is dropped, and the choice between fine and imprisonment is left to the courts. Why Tax Collection Should Be Separated From Arrest What is the thinking behind this shift? Tax is, at heart, a civil obligation; a shortfall is ordinarily recovered with interest and penalty, not punished with custody. Section 69 blurred this line by letting a Commissioner order an arrest merely on “reasons to believe”. The Finance Minister summed up the new approach plainly: arrest has been taken out of the tax officer’s hands, and the department must first build its prosecution case before the law steps in. Liberty, in other words, is to be curtailed only through a judicial process, not an administrative one. Coercive Recovery and the Supreme Court’s Warning Industry has long complained that the threat of custody was used to push businesses into paying disputed demands rather than contesting them. The deterrent was thin anyway: central GST formations recorded 887 arrests across 72,393 offence cases between 2021-22 and 2024-25, and most of those arrested eventually secured bail. A power that frightens honest firms more than it convicts fraudsters fails its own test. The Supreme Court, in Radhika Agarwal (2025), had already cautioned against extracting payments under the shadow of arrest. From Arrest-Led Deterrence to Data-Led GST Enforcement In 2017, arrest was introduced as a deterrent against fake invoicing at a time when matching a seller’s invoice against a buyer’s claim was difficult. Today, invoice matching and data analytics on the GST Network can flag bogus credit close to its source, before it travels down the supply chain. The philosophy shifts from fear-led deterrence to detection-led enforcement. Earlier State Objections and the Wider Decriminalisation Drive Even before GST was rolled out, Maharashtra and West Bengal told the fifth Council meeting in December 2016 that arrest powers sat uneasily with ease of doing business and with VAT practice, under which tax officers had no power of administrative arrest. The decision also fits the wider decriminalisation drive, from the Jan Vishwas Act to the removal of arrest and detention from income-tax recovery rules earlier this year. GST Prosecution and Tax Recovery Will Continue None of this is an amnesty. Deliberate fraud above the threshold can still be prosecuted; where custody is warranted, it must now come through the general criminal law and the courts; and recovery of tax, interest and penalty continues as before. The Finance Minister has been explicit that criminal prosecution survives. Challenges for GST Departments After Arrest Powers Are Removed The real test lies ahead. Fake-invoice syndicates are often fly-by-night operators who vanish once detected. Without the option of a quick arrest, tax departments will need sharper analytics, faster provisional attachment of property and closer coordination with the police. Parliament and the state legislatures must also translate the recommendation into law. Conclusion: Let Data, Not Handcuffs, Police GST Still, the direction is right. A mature tax system presumes the taxpayer honest, reserves the criminal law for the genuinely criminal, and lets data, not handcuffs, do the policing. The 57th Council has taken GST a decisive step towards that ideal. ***** The author is a JKAS Officer presently serving as State Taxes Officer, Circle Budgam. Views expressed are personal.

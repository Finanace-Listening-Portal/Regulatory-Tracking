Summary: Search and seizure powers under Section 67(2) of the CGST Act, 2017 cannot be exercised merely on suspicion, unexplained cash holdings or estimated stock discrepancies. The author examines the statutory requirement of “reasons to believe”, distinguishing lawful investigation from tax determination, recovery and confiscation. Judicial decisions including R.J. Trading Co., Deepak Khandelwal, B. Kusuma Poonacha and Kanishka Matta demonstrate differing approaches to GST cash seizure, particularly whether currency can qualify as a “thing” under Section 67(2). The article further discusses Radhika Agarwal and J. Ramesh Chand concerning coercive payments through DRC-03, and Metenere Ltd. and Dinesh Kumar Pradeep Kumar concerning excess stock and confiscation. Special attention is given to scrap businesses, where unreliable physical estimation, mixed-material valuation and incomplete stock reconciliation can undermine tax demands. The discussion explains the distinct requirements of Sections 35(6), 73, 74, 74A and 130, including the applicable demand framework and procedural safeguards. Practical guidance covers search authorisations, cash ownership records, stock verification, provisional release, return of seized material, objections to confiscation and remedies against coercive recovery. The central conclusion is that GST investigation must remain within statutory limits, while taxpayers must preserve reliable evidence and challenge unlawful action through appropriate procedures. Introduction: A Search Is an Investigation, Not an Assessment Section 67(2) of the Central Goods and Services Tax Act, 2017 confers substantial powers of search and seizure. Those powers serve a legitimate purpose: detection of concealed transactions and preservation of material relevant to GST proceedings. But the power is conditional. The department must satisfy the statutory requirements before exercising it; the existence of a business, a large stock of goods or a substantial amount of cash does not, by itself, dispense with those requirements. The practical difficulty arises when investigation, determination of tax and recovery are treated as one continuous exercise. An estimated stock difference becomes an admission of suppression; cash found at the premises is treated as undisclosed turnover; and payment through DRC-03 is sought before the taxpayer has examined the records. The law requires these stages to be kept distinct. Section 67 concerns investigation and seizure. Tax liability must be determined under the applicable demand provisions. Confiscation under Section 130 requires its own statutory foundation. A payment described as voluntary cannot lawfully be extracted through force or threat of arrest. For a bona fide taxpayer, the appropriate response is neither obstruction nor unquestioning acceptance. It is cooperation supported by records, coupled with a timely objection wherever the statutory limits are crossed. 2. What Section 67(2) Actually Permits Section 67(2) applies where the proper officer, not below the rank of Joint Commissioner, has reasons to believe that: Goods liable to confiscation; or Documents, books or things useful for or relevant to proceedings under the Act, are secreted in any place. The officer may conduct the search personally or authorise another officer in writing to search and seize the relevant goods or material. Several consequences follow. 2.1 The belief must precede the search The authorising officer must possess material capable of supporting the statutory belief when the power is exercised. A search is not justified merely because officers subsequently discover something suspicious. In M/s R.J. Trading Co. v. Commissioner of CGST, Delhi North & Ors., W.P.(C) No. 4847/2021, decided on 20 July 2021, the Delhi High Court examined an authorisation arising from an inter-departmental request essentially seeking verification of the taxpayer’s existence. The Court found the jurisdictional ingredients absent and declared the search and consequential seizure and prohibition orders unlawful. The Court explained: “The expression reason to believe does not carry the same connotation as say reason to suspect.” Information from another formation can justify examination. It does not relieve the authorising officer of the responsibility to apply an independent mind. 2.2 A prior inspection is not compulsory in every case Section 67(2) uses the expression “either pursuant to an inspection carried out under sub-section (1) or otherwise”. A search can therefore be authorised without an earlier inspection, provided the requirements of Section 67(2) independently exist. The absence of a prior inspection is not, by itself, sufficient to invalidate a search. 2.3 The authorising rank and the searching rank are different matters The statutory belief must be formed by the proper officer of the prescribed rank. A duly authorised officer conducting the search need not personally hold the rank of Joint Commissioner. An objection based only on the searching officer’s lower rank may therefore miss the real issue: whether a competent officer issued a valid authorisation. 2.4 A residence is not automatically beyond the provision The expression is “any place”, not merely a registered business premises. Residential premises may fall within the provision where the statutory conditions and the scope of authorisation support the action. Conversely, an authorisation concerning one premises should not be treated as an unrestricted licence to search every connected property. 3. Information, Suspicion and “Reasons to Believe” There is a material difference between receiving information and forming the belief required by law. Suppose scrap dealer purchases goods from a supplier whose registration is subsequently cancelled. That information may warrant verification of invoices, movement of goods, payments and returns. It does not automatically establish that the purchaser has concealed confiscable goods or secreted relevant records. By contrast, contemporaneous evidence of invoice-less dispatches, parallel sales records and concealment of transaction records may provide a materially stronger foundation for authorisation. These are illustrations of the distinction between a reasoned belief and an assumption; the validity of a particular authorisation depends on the actual record. The controlling principle in R.J. Trading Co. is that the belief must be that of an honest and reasonable person, based on relevant material rather than surmise or conjecture. A taxpayer should also distinguish between asking to inspect the search authorisation and demanding immediate disclosure of the entire intelligence file. The jurisdictional challenge concerns whether the necessary material and belief existed; it should not be reduced to an argument that officers must disclose every confidential source at the premises. 4. Search, Seizure, Prohibition and Confiscation Are Not Interchangeable Action Legal character Search under Section 67(2) An investigative exercise subject to the prescribed conditions. Seizure Taking custody of goods or relevant material under the statutory power. Prohibition on dealing with goods Where physical seizure is impracticable, an order may restrict removal or dealing without permission. Tax determination Quantification through the applicable statutory demand and adjudication process. Confiscation under Section 130 A separate statutory consequence requiring the ingredients of the relevant clause and an opportunity of hearing. These distinctions arise from Sections 67 and 130 and the demand framework. A seizure memo is not a final determination of tax; nor does seizure, by itself, transfer ownership to the Government. Under Section 130(5), title vests in the Government upon confiscation. Rule 139 provides the prescribed search and seizure documentation, including authorisation in INS-01, seizure order in INS-02 and the applicable prohibition procedure. The practitioner should examine the actual documents rather than rely solely on an oral description of the action. 5. Huge Cash Found During Search: What Is the Legal Position? The first question is not merely, “How much cash was found?” It is, “What power permits its seizure, and what is its relevance to GST proceedings?” Money is excluded from the definition of “goods”. Courts have differed on whether, and in what circumstances, cash can fall within the expression “things” in Section 67(2). It is therefore unsafe to advise either that all cash can be seized or that cash can never be seized under any circumstances. NACIN’s April 2025 departmental training booklet expressly records this divergence. 5.1 The restrictive view: Section 67 is not a power to collect unexplained wealth In Deepak Khandelwal, Proprietor, M/s Shri Shyam Metal v. Commissioner of CGST, Delhi West & Anr., W.P.(C) No. 6739/2021, decided on 17 August 2023, the Delhi High Court considered seizure of cash and silver bars during a residential search. The Court distinguished an investigation into GST evasion from the collection of assets merely because they were allegedly unaccounted. The expression “things” had to be read in its statutory context, with reference to evidentiary relevance. Release was directed on the facts before the Court. The Revenue’s special leave petitions were dismissed by the Supreme Court on 14 August 2024. That dismissal left the relief undisturbed; it should not be described as a detailed Supreme Court judgment deciding every possible question concerning cash seizure. 5.2 Karnataka authority: B. Kusuma Poonacha In B. Kusuma Poonacha v. Senior Intelligence Officer, Directorate General of GST Intelligence, W.P. No. 25864/2023, decided on 20 February 2024, neutral citation 2024:KHC:7186, the Karnataka High Court considered seizure of ₹1,71,07,500. The Court quashed the seizure insofar as it concerned the cash and directed its return with accrued interest. Its reasoning confined “things” to the statutory context of material useful or relevant to GST proceedings. The decision is particularly important for taxpayers and practitioners in Karnataka. 5.3 The contrary view: Kanishka Matta In Smt. Kanishka Matta v. Union of India, W.P. No. 8204/2020, decided on 26 August 2020, the Madhya Pradesh High Court adopted a wider interpretation of “things”. The case involved ₹66,43,130 and an alleged admission connecting the cash to clandestine sales of pan masala. The Court upheld seizure and retention on the facts. A professional opinion on cash seizure should address this decision rather than omit it. 5.4 A recently reported development A report published on 18 October 2026 concerning Bhagwan Tirthani v. Principal Commissioner, Rajasthan High Court, records relief against seizure and retention of ₹47.45 lakh during a GST search. The reported reasoning rejects a general GST power to seize money merely as unexplained wealth or alleged sale proceeds, and states that a later adjudicatory finding cannot retrospectively cure an initial absence of jurisdiction. The publicly accessible report does not disclose the complete case number and judgment date. Accordingly, this development should be referred to as a reported decision until the authenticated judgment is obtained for formal citation. 6. Does Cash Found Automatically Become Taxable Turnover? No automatic equation can be drawn between cash in hand and suppressed taxable supplies. The decisions restricting cash seizure emphasise the need to connect the action to GST proceedings rather than merely to unexplained wealth. Illustration 1: Cash includes recorded business receipts and personal funds Assume officers find ₹40 lakh at a dealer’s premises. The taxpayer explains: Explanation Amount Recorded cash balance ₹12 lakh Recent bank withdrawal ₹10 lakh Collections against already invoiced sales ₹8 lakh Amount claimed to belong to a family member ₹10 lakh These explanations require verification. The practitioner should obtain the cash book, bank statement, invoice-wise collection details and independent evidence of ownership of the family funds. The correct enquiry is not to apply a GST rate to ₹40 lakh without examining those records. Equally, the taxpayer cannot expect an unsupported statement that “the money belongs to relatives” to settle the matter. Even if the cash is ultimately returned because the GST seizure lacked authority, that does not amount to a judicial declaration that its source is lawful or that proceedings under another applicable enactment are barred. The relief must be understood within the scope of the decision. 7. What the Taxpayer Should Do When Cash Is Found The immediate task is to preserve an accurate record, not to improvise an explanation. A practitioner’s response should include: Recording the exact amount, denomination-wise count and place of recovery. Identifying the person claiming ownership of each amount. Reconciling physical cash with the books as they stood at the relevant time. Preserving bank withdrawals, collection records and supporting documents. Requesting the written seizure documentation and recording any objection to its legal basis. Challenging unlawful retention promptly through an appropriate representation and, where necessary, judicial proceedings. The statutory and judicial framework makes the seizure record, relevance of the material and legality of retention central issues. Books must not be rewritten after the search to create an artificial explanation. Where a statement contains a factual error or was obtained through coercion, the correction or retraction should be prompt, specific and supported by the circumstances and documents. A general retraction, unsupported by evidence, is not a reliable substitute for a factual defence; Kanishka Matta illustrates the difficulty. 8. Forced DRC-03 Payments: A Search Is Not a Recovery Camp The Supreme Court’s decision in Radhika Agarwal v. Union of India, dated 27 February 2025, 2025 INSC 272, is important where payment is obtained through threat of arrest. The Court recognised the distinction between genuinely voluntary payment and payment extracted through force or coercion. It also recognised that affected taxpayers may approach the courts for refund where the law has been breached. CBIC Instruction No. 01/2022-23, dated 25 May 2022, similarly clarifies that there should be no occasion for coercive recovery of newly detected liabilities during search, inspection or investigation. Genuine voluntary payment remains permissible. The instruction also provides for enquiry into complaints of coercion and disciplinary action where wrongdoing is established. Recent Karnataka judgment: J. Ramesh Chand In Sri J. Ramesh Chand v. Union of India, W.P. No. 9890/2023, decided on 13 October 2025, neutral citation 2025:KHC:40313, the Karnataka High Court held that collection of ₹10 crore during search was not voluntary or based on self-ascertainment on the facts before it. The Court directed refund with interest at 6% per annum. The lesson is precise: filing DRC-03 does not conclusively establish free consent. But not every payment made during investigation is involuntary either. Timing, correspondence, computation, statements, threats alleged and the surrounding record all matter. A practitioner should not advise payment merely to end the search. Any admitted liability should first be identified, computed and explained to the client, including the legal consequences of the proposed payment. 9. Search of a Scrap Yard: Stock Cannot Be Determined by Appearance Alone In a scrap business, quantity and value must be examined separately. A heap may contain different metals, grades, non-metallic attachments, rejects and material belonging to customers. The legal weakness in a stock-based demand often begins with the measurement itself. If the quantity is unreliable, valuation and tax calculations built on that quantity are equally open to challenge. The Allahabad High Court line of decisions concerning excess stock has disapproved confiscation and tax quantification based on inadequately established stock differences, including eye estimation. The reported proceedings in Dinesh Kumar Pradeep Kumar specifically concerned excess stock determined on that basis. 9.1 What a defensible stock verification should address For a scrap-yard case, the practitioner should ask: Was the material actually weighed? Which weighbridge was used, and are the slips available? Were gross weight, tare weight and net weight distinguished? Were different materials and grades separately identified? Were goods belonging to customers or principals segregated? Were receipts and dispatches during the verification period adjusted? Were the books compared at the same cut-off time? Was the taxpayer’s objection to the method recorded? This is an evidentiary checklist, not an assertion that one mechanical method is compulsory in every situation. Its purpose is to test whether the alleged discrepancy has a reliable factual foundation. Illustration 2: An apparent excess disappears on reconciliation Assume the books show 100 metric tonnes of the dealer’s stock. Officers initially estimate the physical heap at 125 metric tonnes. A subsequent documented reconciliation shows: Component Quantity Dealer’s own stock 100 MT Customer-owned material awaiting processing 10 MT Non-stock material included in the initial estimate 5 MT Overstatement caused by visual estimation 10 MT Initial estimate 125 MT If those facts are proved, the apparent excess does not establish 25 MT of unaccounted taxable stock. Conversely, if reliable weighing and reconciliation establish an unexplained excess, the dealer must answer it. Bona fide conduct does not remove the statutory obligation to maintain proper stock records. Rule 56 specifically contemplates records covering stock, scrap and wastage. 10. Valuation of Scrap: One Rate for Every Heap Is Not an Answer After quantity comes classification and valuation. These should not be merged. An assessment working should identify the material, relevant classification, applicable rate for the period and the evidence supporting value. In a mixed-scrap case, applying the price of a valuable metal to the entire quantity invites a factual challenge. For example, a mixed lot containing iron scrap, aluminium scrap and non-metallic waste cannot reasonably be valued throughout at an aluminium price merely because aluminium is present. The practitioner should obtain grade-wise purchase invoices, recent comparable sales, sorting records and any technical evidence needed to dispute the department’s working. The principle drawn from the stock cases is that tax determination requires an adjudicatory exercise; an unsupported valuation cannot be cured merely by incorporating it in a confiscation notice. Any percentage used in a numerical illustration must be treated as an assumption. “Scrap” is not a sufficient description for selecting the tax rate in an actual case. 11. How Tax on Unaccounted Stock Should Be Determined Section 35(6) provides the statutory mechanism for determining tax where goods or services have not been accounted for, by treating them as if supplied. In Metenere Ltd. v. Union of India, Writ Tax No. 360/2020, decided on 17 December 2020, the Allahabad High Court emphasised the need to follow the applicable demand procedure rather than substitute confiscation for tax determination. Current drafting must also recognise the change in the demand framework: Sections 73 and 74 govern their respective categories for periods up to FY 2023-24. Section 74A applies to determination of tax for FY 2024-25 onwards. Older judgments naturally refer to Sections 73 and 74. Their references should not be copied mechanically into advice concerning a later financial year without checking the applicable amended provisions and corresponding State enactment. The essential sequence is: Establish a reliable physical quantity. Reconcile it with the relevant records. Identify what remains genuinely unaccounted. Establish classification, value and applicable tax treatment. Issue the appropriate notice. Consider the taxpayer’s explanation and pass a reasoned determination. The search supplies evidence. It does not eliminate adjudication. 12. Can Scrap Goods Be Confiscated Under Section 130? Scrap is not exempt from Section 130 merely because it is scrap. But neither does the presence of scrap, even in substantial quantity, establish confiscability. Section 130(1) specifies distinct grounds, including: Supply or receipt of goods in contravention of the law with intent to evade tax; Failure to account for goods on which the person is liable to pay tax; Taxable supplies without applying for registration; Contravention with intent to evade tax; and The specified misuse of a conveyance. 12.1 The precise clause matters It is inaccurate to state that every clause expressly requires proof of intention to evade. Clauses (i) and (iv) contain those words; clause (ii) is differently worded. Where clause (ii) is invoked, the department must establish the statutory failure to account for the relevant goods and the liability contemplated by that clause. Where clause (iv) is invoked, a contravention alone is not the complete statutory case: intent to evade must also be established. A reply should therefore answer the particular clause relied upon, not only assert that the dealer is honest. 12.2 Metenere: An assumption of future evasion was insufficient In Metenere, the department proceeded on the footing that failure to produce the prescribed records justified the belief that the stored goods would be cleared without payment of GST. The Court rejected the confiscation on the facts and emphasised the absence of the necessary statutory ingredients and proper tax determination. The decision does not excuse failure to maintain records; it prevents that failure from being converted, without the required legal foundation, into confiscation of the entire stock. 12.3 Excess stock is not a substitute for the confiscation case The reported Supreme Court order in Additional Commissioner Grade-2 (Appeal) v. S/S Dinesh Kumar Pradeep Kumar declined interference with the Allahabad High Court’s decision concerning excess stock and Section 130, while expressly preserving other lawful remedies for the authorities. That qualification is important. Setting aside an improperly invoked confiscation proceeding does not necessarily extinguish a tax liability that can lawfully be established through the correct process. It would therefore overstate the authorities to say that Section 130 can never apply to unaccounted stock. The sounder proposition is that an alleged stock difference does not dispense with the requirements of the particular confiscation clause. 13. Confiscation Procedure and Redemption Fine Section 130(4) requires an opportunity of hearing before an order of confiscation or penalty. The adjudication must identify the goods, the applicable clause and the factual basis for the proposed action. Section 130(2) also requires an option to pay a fine in lieu of confiscation: The fine cannot exceed the market value of the confiscated goods less the tax chargeable. Under the current provision, the aggregate of fine and penalty cannot be less than a penalty equal to 100% of the tax payable on the goods. These limits do not authorise an arbitrary figure. Quantity, market value and the tax working remain open to examination. A further drafting caution is necessary: Section 130(3), often reproduced in older articles as an additional tax, penalty and charges provision, was omitted with effect from 1 January 2022. It should not be quoted as current law. That omission does not, by itself, extinguish liabilities arising under other operative provisions. 14. Release of Goods and Return of Records A practitioner should examine release remedies immediately rather than wait for the investigation to conclude. Provisional release Section 67(6), read with Rule 140, provides for provisional release of seized goods. Rule 140 contemplates a bond in INS-04 for the value of the goods and a bank guarantee equivalent to the applicable tax, interest and penalty. Six-month safeguard for seized goods Under Section 67(7), seized goods must be returned if no notice in respect of them is issued within six months. The period may be extended, on sufficient cause, by a further period not exceeding six months. This safeguard concerns seized goods. It should not be indiscriminately applied to every document or electronic record. Documents and other material Section 67(5) permits copies or extracts of seized documents, subject to the statutory investigation-related exception. Section 67(3) requires return of documents, books or things not relied upon for the notice within 30 days of its issue. Retention under Section 67(2) is also limited to the period necessary for examination, enquiry or proceedings. 15. The Practitioner’s Responsibility: Preserve the Defence from the First Day A search case is often weakened before the notice is issued. The practitioner’s responsibility is to prevent factual inaccuracies from becoming an apparently undisputed record. During the search Verify officers’ identities and inspect the authorisation. Note the premises covered and the commencement and conclusion times. Facilitate lawful production of records without destroying or altering evidence. Ask that stock objections, ownership claims and measurement disputes be recorded. Ensure the client reads statements and inventories before signing. Do not permit an estimate to be described as an agreed quantity if it is disputed. Seek copies of the relevant documents and a clear inventory of seized material. These steps support later examination of the jurisdictional and evidentiary requirements identified in Section 67 and the search decisions. Immediately after the search Prepare a dated factual memorandum covering: What was searched and seized; What was actually weighed and what was estimated; Statements recorded and any identified errors; Payments made and the circumstances; Records produced, unavailable or requested; Specific objections and the supporting documents. Where coercion is alleged, preserve contemporaneous correspondence and other lawfully available evidence. The relief recognised in Radhika Agarwal and J. Ramesh Chand depends on the facts being placed properly before the court. Select the remedy according to the wrong Problem Remedy to examine Absence of statutory foundation for search Jurisdictional representation and, where appropriate, writ proceedings through counsel. Cash seized without authority Prompt return request supported by jurisdictional case law; judicial challenge if necessary. Goods blocking business operations Provisional release under Section 67(6) and Rule 140. Goods retained without timely notice Section 67(7), including verification of any valid extension. Inaccurate stock or valuation working Detailed factual objection, re-verification request and evidence-based reply. Confiscation order Appropriate statutory challenge, with separate consideration of urgent protection against disposal. Payment extracted through coercion Prompt protest, refund demand and appropriate judicial remedy. The remedy must be matched to the legal defect. A jurisdictional challenge is different from a dispute about weight or valuation. The Rajasthan cash report also illustrates that a court may examine a limited jurisdictional issue even where an appeal concerning other matters is pending; it is not a general licence to bypass statutory remedies. 16. Suggested Objections for Adaptation to the Facts Where stock has been visually estimated “The alleged excess stock is disputed. The quantity recorded during the proceedings was not established by actual weighment or another disclosed, verifiable method. We request disclosure of the measurement sheets, weighbridge records, material-wise classification and valuation basis. A reconciliation of our own goods, third-party goods, pending receipts and dispatches will be furnished with supporting records.” Where cash has been seized “The presence of cash does not, by itself, establish suppressed taxable supplies. The source and ownership reconciliation is enclosed/to follow with supporting documents. Without prejudice to our cooperation with the investigation, we dispute the statutory authority for seizure and retention of the currency and request a reasoned decision on its return.” Where confiscation is proposed “The notice must be examined with reference to the precise clause of Section 130(1) invoked. The alleged discrepancy, the liability attributed to the identified goods and, where required by that clause, the alleged intention to evade tax are specifically disputed. Tax determination cannot be replaced by an unsupported confiscation computation.” These are drafting starting points, not standard paragraphs to be filed irrespective of the record. Their legal basis lies in the statutory distinctions and the decisions discussed above. 17. Conclusion The department is entitled to investigate tax evasion. It is equally bound to establish the conditions on which its search, seizure, demand and confiscation powers depend. Section 67(2) does not permit suspicion to substitute for the prescribed belief. Cash cannot be treated merely as an available fund for satisfying a future demand. A scrap heap cannot become quantified suppressed turnover through an unexplained estimate. Confiscation requires the statutory case to be made under the clause actually invoked, and voluntary payment must remain voluntary. These are legal safeguards, not concessions to a taxpayer. For bona fide taxpayers, the strongest protection is a combination of reliable records and timely legal action. For the practitioner, the duty extends beyond preparing the eventual reply: it includes preserving the facts, preventing inaccurate admissions, testing the department’s computation and choosing the remedy that addresses the actual wrong.

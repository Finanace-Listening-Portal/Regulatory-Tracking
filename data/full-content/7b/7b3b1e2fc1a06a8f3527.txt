Vs Union of India (Gujarat High Court) Summary: The Gujarat High Court, in Trafigura India Pvt. Ltd. Vs Union of India, dismissed four connected Special Civil Applications concerning the denial of preferential customs duty exemption on tin ingots imported from Malaysia under the ASEAN-India Free Trade Agreement (AIFTA). The common judgment was delivered on 5 May 2023 by a Division Bench comprising Justice N.V. Anjaria and Justice Bhargav D. Karia. The petitions were filed by Trafigura India Pvt. Ltd., Lykos India Pvt. Ltd. and Talin International Pvt. Ltd. against orders passed by customs authorities demanding differential customs duty and imposing penalties. The principal controversy was whether customs authorities could independently initiate proceedings under the Customs Act, 1962, when Article 24 of the Operational Certification Procedures under the AIFTA prescribed a consultation and dispute-resolution mechanism between the governmental authorities of the importing and exporting countries. The petitioners contended that the authorities lacked jurisdiction to proceed without first following the treaty procedure, whereas the Revenue maintained that the statutory powers available under Indian customs legislation remained enforceable. Trafigura India Pvt. Ltd., engaged in trading non-ferrous metals, imported tin ingots manufactured by Malaysia Smelting Corporation and supplied through Trafigura Pte Ltd., Singapore. The company filed warehouse Bills of Entry in August and September 2015 and claimed concessional basic customs duty at 0% under Notification No. 46/2011-Customs dated 1 June 2011. The exemption was claimed on the basis of Certificates of Origin declaring Malaysia as the country of origin. Lykos India Pvt. Ltd. subsequently purchased certain imported goods on an ex-warehouse basis and also acquired tin ingots through a high-seas sale arrangement. Talin International Pvt. Ltd. purchased similar goods through Welcome Impex Pvt. Ltd. and claimed preferential duty treatment. The exemption depended upon compliance with the origin requirements prescribed under the applicable Rules of Origin, including the Regional Value Content (RVC) condition. The customs authorities alleged that the Certificates of Origin contained incorrect declarations of RVC and that the actual value content of the imported goods was below the prescribed threshold. Under the AIFTA origin criteria, goods not wholly produced or obtained in the exporting country were required to satisfy specified conditions, including an AIFTA content of not less than 35% of the FOB value and the prescribed change in tariff sub-heading. The petitioners relied on Certificates of Origin issued by the designated Malaysian authorities to establish eligibility for preferential treatment. However, the customs investigation indicated that the RVC had been overstated, with declarations in certain instances showing substantially higher percentages than the actual value content. The authorities treated these declarations as misrepresentation and suppression of material facts resulting in wrongful availment of customs duty exemption. Proceedings were accordingly initiated under Sections 28(4) and 46(4) of the Customs Act, 1962. The controversy was therefore not confined to the calculation of customs duty but also concerned the legal effect of origin certificates, the jurisdiction of customs officers to investigate preferential claims and the relationship between international treaty procedures and domestic statutory enforcement. In Special Civil Application No. 14028 of 2020, Trafigura India Pvt. Ltd. challenged the Order-in-Original dated 29 May 2020 passed by the Additional Commissioner of Customs, Kandla, imposing a penalty of Rs. 25,90,825 under Section 112(a) of the Customs Act. Lykos India Pvt. Ltd., in Special Civil Application No. 13365 of 2020, challenged the same order insofar as it confirmed differential customs duty of Rs. 3,18,194, together with interest and an equivalent penalty. In Special Civil Application No. 14937 of 2020, Lykos challenged another order dated 24 June 2020 involving recovery of balance customs duty of Rs. 4,54,979 and an equivalent penalty under Section 114A. Talin International Pvt. Ltd., in Special Civil Application No. 1835 of 2022, challenged an appellate order dated 15 February 2021 confirming differential customs duty of Rs. 8,52,700 and an equivalent penalty. These proceedings arose from separate import transactions but involved substantially common questions concerning preferential tariff treatment and the authority of customs officers to reject claims supported by Certificates of Origin. The petitioners argued that Article 24 of Appendix D to the AIFTA Operational Certification Procedures established a specific mechanism for resolving disputes concerning origin determination, classification of products and related matters. Under Article 24(a), the governmental authorities concerned in the importing and exporting countries were required to consult each other with a view to resolving such disputes and communicate the result to the other parties. Article 24(b) contemplated recourse to the ASEAN-India dispute-settlement mechanism where consultations failed to produce a mutually satisfactory solution. According to the petitioners, this procedure was an essential part of the preferential trade arrangement and could not be disregarded by domestic customs authorities. They submitted that the Certificates of Origin issued by the designated Malaysian authorities could not be independently rejected without following the agreed consultation process. Consequently, the show-cause notices and adjudication proceedings were challenged as being without jurisdiction and contrary to the international obligations undertaken by India. The question of jurisdiction had also arisen in related proceedings before the Bombay High Court involving Kothari Metals Ltd. and other importers. The Bombay High Court had earlier declined to entertain the writ petitions on the ground that alternative statutory remedies were available. The matter reached the Supreme Court, which, by order dated 25 November 2019, restored the concerned writ petitions for consideration on merits. The Supreme Court observed that the foundational issue concerning the competence of customs authorities to proceed in the context of Article 24 of Appendix D to the treaty could not be satisfactorily adjudicated by the departmental authorities themselves. The High Court was therefore required to examine the issue. Referring to this development, the Gujarat High Court considered the jurisdictional challenge raised by the petitioners instead of rejecting their petitions merely because appellate remedies under the Customs Act were available. The Court accordingly examined the treaty provisions and the domestic statutory framework governing preferential customs duty. The High Court analysed the Agreement on Trade in Goods under the Framework Agreement on Comprehensive Economic Cooperation between India and ASEAN, together with the Customs Tariff Act, 1975, and the Customs Tariff (Determination of Origin of Goods under the Preferential Trade Agreement between the Government of Member States of ASEAN and the Republic of India) Rules, 2009. These rules were notified through Notification No. 189/2009-Customs dated 31 December 2009 in exercise of powers under Section 5 of the Customs Tariff Act. The Court observed that Section 5 empowered the Central Government to provide preferential tariff treatment under trade agreements and prescribe rules for determining whether imported goods originated in the relevant foreign country. The Court also examined Notification No. 46/2011-Customs, issued under Section 25 of the Customs Act, granting preferential duty treatment subject to prescribed conditions. The legislative framework demonstrated that the AIFTA arrangement had been implemented through domestic statutory provisions and delegated legislation rather than operating entirely through the international agreement itself. A detailed comparison was undertaken between the AIFTA Rules of Origin, the Operational Certification Procedures and the corresponding provisions incorporated into the Indian Rules of 2009. The Court noted that provisions concerning origin criteria, wholly obtained products, non-originating materials, cumulative origin, direct consignment, Certificates of Origin and verification procedures had been substantially reflected in domestic rules. The comparison also showed that certain treaty provisions had not been incorporated. Significantly, Article 24 of the AIFTA Operational Certification Procedures, dealing with dispute resolution between governmental authorities, did not find a corresponding provision in the Indian Rules of 2009. The Court regarded this omission as material to the petitioners’ jurisdictional objection. It examined whether an international treaty provision that had not been incorporated into domestic legislation could nevertheless be enforced to restrict the statutory powers of customs authorities. The distinction between obligations undertaken internationally and rights or restrictions enforceable under municipal law became central to the determination of the dispute. The High Court considered the constitutional and legal principles governing the relationship between international treaties and Indian law. It explained that the executive may enter into treaties and international agreements, but treaty provisions requiring legislative implementation do not automatically acquire the force of domestic law merely because India has become a signatory. Parliament may enact legislation to implement international agreements, and the competent rule-making authority may incorporate relevant treaty provisions through subordinate legislation. Where an international obligation has not been incorporated into the applicable domestic legal framework, it cannot ordinarily override an express statutory provision or restrict powers conferred by legislation. The Court examined the relevant principles concerning treaty implementation and the authority of domestic law, distinguishing between international commitments and enforceable municipal obligations. Since Article 24 had not been incorporated into the Rules of 2009, the petitioners could not rely upon that provision as an independent statutory bar against proceedings under the Customs Act. The Court further considered the nature of the powers exercised by customs authorities under Sections 28(4) and 46(4) of the Customs Act. Section 28(4) permits recovery proceedings in specified circumstances involving collusion, wilful misstatement or suppression of facts, subject to the statutory requirements. Section 46(4) concerns the declaration made in connection with a Bill of Entry. The customs authorities had initiated proceedings on the basis that preferential exemption was wrongly claimed through incorrect origin declarations and misrepresentation of the Regional Value Content. The High Court held that the existence of an international consultation procedure, which had not been incorporated into domestic law, did not deprive customs officers of their statutory authority to investigate such allegations. It rejected the contention that customs authorities were necessarily required to await consultation between the governments of India and Malaysia before exercising their powers under the Customs Act. The statutory jurisdiction to examine the correctness of exemption claims and initiate recovery proceedings remained available. On the factual aspects, the Court considered the customs authorities’ findings that the imported tin ingots did not satisfy the prescribed Regional Value Content requirement. The Certificates of Origin had been relied upon to claim basic customs duty exemption, but the investigation indicated that the declarations concerning origin content were inaccurate. The Court accepted the findings supporting denial of the preferential duty benefit and did not find grounds to invalidate the proceedings on the jurisdictional objections raised by the petitioners. It also upheld the invocation of the extended limitation period in the circumstances recorded by the authorities, including findings relating to misrepresentation and suppression. The Court’s analysis made clear that the preferential duty benefit was conditional upon satisfaction of the applicable origin criteria and that the production of a Certificate of Origin did not, by itself, prevent the competent customs authorities from examining allegations of incorrect declarations under the statutory framework. The petitioners’ reliance on Article 24 was therefore rejected. The High Court held that the dispute-resolution mechanism contained in the AIFTA could not be treated as an enforceable restriction on the statutory powers of Indian customs authorities when the relevant provision had not been incorporated into the domestic Rules of Origin. The Court distinguished the treaty-based consultation mechanism from the procedures and remedies available under Indian customs legislation. It found that the customs authorities had acted within their statutory jurisdiction in initiating proceedings concerning the disputed preferential duty claims. The Court also rejected the argument that the challenged orders were liable to be set aside solely because the governmental consultation process contemplated by Article 24 had not been undertaken. The statutory scheme governing customs assessment, recovery and penalties continued to operate independently of that unincorporated treaty provision. Accordingly, the Gujarat High Court dismissed Special Civil Application Nos. 14028, 13365 and 14937 of 2020 and Special Civil Application No. 1835 of 2022. The Court upheld the challenged customs proceedings and the adjudication and appellate orders involved in the connected matters. The notices were discharged and interim relief was vacated. The judgment, bearing neutral citation 2023:GUJHC:28672-DB and reported as 2023-TIOL-737-HC-AHM-CUS, establishes that an international trade agreement does not automatically displace powers conferred by domestic customs legislation. Where a treaty-based dispute-resolution provision has not been incorporated into Indian law, importers cannot invoke it to invalidate statutory proceedings concerning allegedly incorrect Certificates of Origin, misrepresented origin criteria or wrongful claims of preferential customs duty exemption. The decision highlights the importance of complying with the substantive Rules of Origin while claiming benefits under free trade agreements. Cases Discussed Union of India Vs.

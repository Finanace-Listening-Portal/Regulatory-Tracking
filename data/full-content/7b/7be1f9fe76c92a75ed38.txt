Summary: India has reportedly witnessed a significant turnaround in foreign portfolio investment during November 2026, with foreign investors investing approximately USD 10.6 billion, equivalent to Rs. 99,158 crore, in Indian equities. The article examines this reported development against the backdrop of substantial foreign investment withdrawals during January–October 2026 and growing concerns over excessive capital expenditure and valuations in the global Artificial Intelligence sector. India is increasingly being considered an alternative investment destination because of its diversified corporate sector, relatively attractive valuations, improved corporate earnings and lower concentration in AI-related businesses. Other supporting factors discussed include declining crude oil prices, relative stability in the rupee, the underweight positioning of global investment funds in Indian equities and changes in international benchmark indices. The article also examines the reported decline in foreign ownership of BSE 500 companies, the growing influence of domestic institutional investors and the concentration of foreign investments in banking, financial services and other large-cap sectors. It contrasts India’s reported investment inflows with capital withdrawals from Taiwan, South Korea and other Asian markets. While these developments suggest improving international interest in Indian equities, the article cautions that the recent movement of capital may represent a temporary global portfolio rotation rather than a permanent shift. Sustained investment will depend on economic growth, corporate profitability, valuations, crude oil prices, currency movements and international financial conditions. A substantial proportion of global investment was channelled towards US-based companies engaged in Artificial Intelligence, semiconductor manufacturing, data centres and technology. According to certain estimates, nearly 70% of fresh investments in global equity markets flowed into the United States, particularly into the AI sector. Consequently, emerging markets such as India witnessed a slowdown in foreign investment inflows. However, November 2026 witnessed an unexpected reversal of this trend. Questions began to emerge about the enormous capital expenditure undertaken by US-based AI companies and whether such investments would generate the anticipated returns. Growing investor concerns led to some withdrawal of funds from the AI sector, with a portion of this capital finding its way into India. The impact was reflected in the Indian stock market in a historic manner. During November, Foreign Portfolio Investors invested approximately USD 10.6 billion, equivalent to Rs. 99,158 crore, in the Indian equity market. This represented the highest-ever foreign investment recorded in a single month, surpassing the previous record of USD 8.4 billion set in December 2020. Significantly, between January and October 2026, foreign investors had withdrawn approximately Rs. 2.95 lakh crore from the Indian market. Therefore, the November inflow appears to represent more than a gradual return of foreign capital; it indicates a significant shift in their investment strategy. In contrast, the other seven major Asian markets collectively witnessed an outflow of USD 22.6 billion during the same period. Against this adverse global backdrop, India attracted investments worth USD 10.6 billion, making its performance particularly noteworthy. Why Is Capital Flowing Towards India? India has emerged as an attractive investment destination for global investors. International financial institutions such as JPMorgan have described India as an ‘AI Hedge’. The underlying rationale is relatively straightforward. India offers a large and liquid market, but unlike the United States, it does not have excessive valuations of AI-related companies or an overwhelming concentration of investments in the AI sector. Consequently, if the AI investment boom encounters difficulties, India could emerge as a relatively safer investment destination. Some analysts describe this phenomenon as a ‘Reverse AI Trade’ or a rotation of investment from one sector or market to another. However, it would be premature to conclude that this shift represents a permanent investment decision by all global investors. How Much Do Foreign Investors Own in Indian Companies? While examining the record investment witnessed in November, another important aspect deserves attention. Despite the substantial inflow of foreign capital into India, foreign investors continue to hold a relatively modest ownership stake in Indian companies. Foreign Portfolio Investors’ ownership in BSE 500 companies has declined to 17.3%, its lowest level in the past 12 years. In contrast, the ownership share of Domestic Institutional Investors has increased to 18.8%, representing a 12-year high. India’s share in global foreign portfolio investments is also relatively small. The total value of foreign portfolio investment assets across global equity markets is approximately Rs. 4,234 lakh crore. Of this, the value of FPI assets in India stood at approximately Rs. 73.67 lakh crore as of October 2026. This means that India’s share in global investments is merely 1.7%. Furthermore, nearly 61% of global funds investing in major emerging markets continue to remain underweight in India. Their average overweight position in India is merely 0.1%, compared with the ten-year average of 2.2%. This indicates that despite substantial buying in November, global investors have not yet restored their Indian investments to normal levels. If they decide to increase their allocation to India, the Indian equity market could potentially attract considerably larger inflows in the future. Five Major Factors Driving Investment Towards India Growing Concerns About Investments in the AI Sector Over the past two years, substantial investments have been made in US-based AI companies, semiconductor manufacturers in Taiwan and data centres. However, investors are increasingly questioning whether such enormous expenditure will generate the expected revenues and profits. The use of various AI-based computer programmes has increased in stock market transactions, investment decisions and forecasting potential market movements. However, questions are also being raised about whether the accuracy and reliability of these programmes have been adequately tested. Consequently, the risks associated with AI-driven investment strategies have once again come under scrutiny. As a result, approximately USD 19.76 billion to USD 21 billion flowed out of Taiwan, while South Korea witnessed withdrawals ranging between USD 8.32 billion and USD 9.3 billion. During the same period, India attracted substantial investments. India is increasingly being viewed as a relatively safer alternative for investors seeking to reduce their exposure to potential risks in the AI sector. Decline in Crude Oil Prices India is one of the world’s largest importers of crude oil. Consequently, any increase in international crude oil prices substantially raises the country’s import bill. This, in turn, affects the current account deficit, inflation and the exchange rate of the Indian rupee. Brent crude prices reportedly declined from approximately USD 118 per barrel on October 30 to USD 93–97 per barrel by mid-November. This decline of nearly 21% provided considerable relief to India. Lower crude oil prices are expected to reduce India’s import expenditure, help contain the current account deficit and ease pressure on the rupee. These developments served as important positive signals for foreign investors. Stability in the Rupee’s Exchange Rate When the Indian rupee depreciates against the US dollar, foreign investors face not only the risk of losses in currency conversion but also the possibility of eroding their returns from equity investments. Therefore, depreciation of the rupee represents a significant risk for foreign investors. During November, the rupee demonstrated some stability, trading at around 93.92 against the US dollar. This helped improve foreign investors’ confidence regarding currency-related risks. Additionally, discussions were underway about facilitating greater convenience for foreign investors to undertake transactions in US dollars. There were also reports that India’s Finance Minister, Nirmala Sitharaman, had indicated the possibility of reviewing the taxation framework applicable to capital gains. These potential developments further contributed to India’s investment appeal. Attractive Valuations and Strong Corporate Earnings The decline in the stock market over the preceding two years, coupled with continuous selling by foreign investors, had made the valuations of several large Indian companies relatively attractive. At the same time, corporate results for the second quarter of Financial Year 2026-27 were better than expected. The aggregate profit after tax of 395 companies forming part of the NSE 500 index registered a year-on-year growth of 27.5%. This strengthened investor confidence in the profitability and financial performance of Indian companies. Furthermore, global funds had been maintaining lower-than-expected allocations to India. This created an opportunity for them to increase their exposure to the Indian market. Global Political Developments and Other Factors Following the US midterm elections, expectations emerged that there could be some restraint on the enormous expenditure being incurred in the AI and data centre sectors. In the November MSCI index rebalancing, four new Indian companies were included, while the weightage of another 11 companies was increased. This also provided an impetus to foreign investments linked to benchmark indices. At the same time, the possibility of easing tensions between the United States and Iran helped improve global investor sentiment. Why Did Other Asian Markets Witness Capital Outflows? To understand India’s performance in November, it is equally important to examine the developments in other Asian markets. Excluding India, the seven major Asian markets collectively witnessed an outflow of USD 22.6 billion. Taiwan experienced the largest withdrawal, amounting to approximately USD 19.76 billion to USD 21 billion, followed by South Korea, which witnessed outflows ranging between USD 8.32 billion and USD 9.3 billion. In both these countries, foreign investments were heavily concentrated in semiconductor manufacturing and AI-related technology companies. Japan also witnessed selling in shares of companies operating in the AI and technology sectors. Some capital outflows were also recorded from Thailand, Indonesia and Vietnam. Outside Asia, Brazil witnessed selling pressure in equities due to political and economic uncertainties following its elections. Against this background, India’s ability to attract substantial foreign investment becomes even more significant. Where Exactly Was the Foreign Investment Deployed in India? Foreign investors did not distribute the capital flowing into India equally across all companies. Their preference was primarily towards large companies with high liquidity and the ability to facilitate easy buying and selling of shares. The major beneficiaries included banking and financial services, telecommunications, automobiles, capital goods and pharmaceutical companies. The financial services sector deserves particular attention. Nearly 30% of the total FPI investments in India are concentrated in this sector, with the value of such investments estimated at approximately USD 206 billion. Consequently, larger companies in this sector witnessed a relatively stronger inflow of foreign investments. Foreign investors also preferred large-cap companies over small-cap companies. The impact of these investments was reflected in the benchmark indices as well. During November, the Nifty and Sensex registered gains of approximately 10%. This represented their best monthly performance in the past six years. Will This Bullish Trend Continue? The record investment witnessed in November is undoubtedly a positive development for the Indian economy and capital markets. However, it would be premature to conclude that foreign investors have decided to return to India permanently. Despite the substantial investments during November, foreign investors’ net selling for the entire calendar year 2026 remained around USD 21 billion. Thus, the year has been extremely challenging from the perspective of foreign portfolio investments. Interestingly, while foreign investors were making substantial purchases during November, domestic institutional investors sold shares worth approximately Rs. 52,000 crore, apparently to book profits. This highlights an important aspect of the current market situation. Although India’s investment appeal is increasing, the present inflow of funds is partly attributable to a global rotation of investments between markets and sectors. It cannot yet be regarded as a permanent increase in foreign investment. Developments in the global economy, interest rates in the United States, the strength of the US dollar, crude oil prices and the future earnings growth of Indian companies will all play important roles in determining the direction of foreign investments in the coming months. Overall, India is once again emerging as an attractive destination for global investors. India has provided an alternative destination for capital moving away from the high-profile AI investment boom. However, converting this opportunity into sustained long-term investment will require a healthy balance between economic growth, corporate profitability and stock market valuations. The record investment witnessed in November is certainly an encouraging signal for India. Nevertheless, the real test will be whether this momentum in foreign investment continues in the months ahead.

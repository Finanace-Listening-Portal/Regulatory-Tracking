(Bombay High Court) Summary: The Bombay High Court, adjudicating Official Liquidator Reports concerning Mafatlal Dyes and Chemicals Ltd. in liquidation, held that five identified paintings formed part of the company’s liquidation estate and permitted their sale through a process designed with the assistance of an art auction expert. The company had been ordered to be wound up on 19 July 2010. Forty-four paintings, seized by the Mumbai Police in January 2014 during investigations arising from disputes within the Mafatlal family, were subsequently preserved at the Sir J. J. School of Art in the deemed possession of the Company Court. The Official Liquidator ultimately pressed for sale of only five paintings, considering that their proceeds might suffice to discharge the company’s debts. Sheetal Mafatlal claimed all 44 paintings; Gayatri Jhaveri claimed the remaining 39 and excluded the five identified works; Yasmin Valibhoy sought intervention primarily to challenge Sheetal’s account through cross-examination. The Court rejected Sheetal’s reliance on the July 2012 family consent terms as establishing ownership against the company. Those terms settled criminal and matrimonial disputes among the participating family members and concerned the art covered by a complaint involving 35 paintings. They could neither establish entitlement to all 44 paintings nor bind the company or its Official Liquidator. Even if family members had agreed that Sheetal could retain and enjoy the paintings, they first needed title capable of being transferred. The related criminal proceedings had examined the truth of complaints rather than adjudicated ownership. The Court also recorded that Sheetal’s complaint alleging substitution of original paintings with reproductions had ended in a closure report, with her challenge ultimately unsuccessful before the Supreme Court, which had left all contentions open. The Company Court’s jurisdiction under Section 446(2)(b) and Section 446(2)(d), read with Section 456 of the Companies Act, 1956, had already been upheld by the order dated 7 December 2015. That determination had not been stayed or disturbed in appeal. Although the appellate court subsequently required the Official Liquidator to place his material first, he complied by filing an additional affidavit and documents in September 2023. Sheetal nevertheless failed to lead evidence despite repeated opportunities. Her eventual affidavit, notarised in London but defective in attestation, was withdrawn after she was allowed time to cure the defect. The Court rejected as false her later assertion that she had withdrawn it because the Official Liquidator had failed to comply. Her refusal to enter the witness box and expose her claim to cross-examination justified an adverse inference. The Court independently assessed the company’s fixed asset inventory, purchase vouchers and seizure list, together with the police affidavits and responses obtained from galleries. These materials established, on the civil standard of preponderance of probabilities, ownership of the five works at seizure-list serial numbers 2, 9, 12, 14 and 17: two M.F. Hussain paintings, a Biren De painting, an F.N. Souza painting and a Jogen Chowdhury painting. The asset register contained detailed accounting particulars, suppliers, voucher references and acquisition dates. Synergy Art Foundation’s confirmation corroborated the Jogen Chowdhury purchase. Where documentary descriptions could not uniquely identify other works, the police and Official Liquidator had refrained from attributing them to the company; the Court considered that restrained approach supportive of the reliability of the five identifications. Atulya Mafatlal’s reliance on later annual reports did not answer the acquisition-period records, and the explanation that company records had been destroyed in floods left unanswered how subsequent statutory audits had been conducted. The Official Liquidator’s earlier erroneous description of Ajay Mafatlal as an ex-director was immaterial to the ownership analysis. The Court found Sheetal’s applications devoid of merit and disposed of the three Official Liquidator Reports with directions enabling sale of the five identified paintings. Recognising that artworks required a different sale mechanism from industrial assets, it appointed Ms. Sonal Singh Naharwar as expert and requested advocate Mr. Ranjeev Carvalho to assist. They and the Official Liquidator were to formulate an auction mechanism through an art auction house and appropriate sale conditions to improve price discovery and maximise value. Ownership of the remaining 39 paintings was expressly left open. Sheetal and Gayatri were each directed to deposit ₹15 lakh with the Official Liquidator within four weeks of uploading the judgment for their preservation and maintenance, without creation of any equities. The matter was listed for an update on 20 November 2026. FULL TEXT OF THE JUDGMENT/ORDER OF BOMBAY HIGH COURT Context and Factual Background: 1. Mafatlal Dyes and Chemicals Ltd. (“Company”) was ordered to be wound up by the Company Court on July 19, 2010 in the captioned Company Petition No. 900 of 2005, presented by Vinyl Chemicals (India) Ltd. due to its inability to pay its debts, attracting the jurisdiction of the Official Liquidator (“OL”). 2. The captioned three Official Liquidator Reports (“OLRs”) fall for consideration along with the respective Interim Applications taken out in them. They are all filed at different stages of the same line of enquiry – relating to 44 paintings of reputed Indian artists (“Subject Paintings”). The Subject Paintings were seized by the Crime Branch under a panchanama dated January 18, 2014 in the course of criminal proceedings that flowed from inter se complaints by members of the family of the Late Mr. Yogindra Mafatlal, which is the promoter family of the Company. 3. The Subject Paintings are now stored under the care of the Sir J. J. School of Art, in the deemed possession of the Company Court. Five of the Subject Paintings (“Identified Paintings”), listed at Sr. Nos. 2, 9, 12, 14 and 17 of the seizure list that forms part of the record, have been identified by the Mumbai Police as belonging to the Company. 4. The Company’s promoters have been engaged in a three-way fight amongst Mr. Atulya Mafatlal (“Atulya”), the son of the Late Mr. Yogindra Mafatlal, his brother Mr. Ajay Mafatlal (“Ajay”, originally, Ms. Aparna Mafatlal, before gender reassignment) and Ms. Sheetal Mafatlal (“Sheetal”), who was married to Atulya. Another sibling, Gayatri Jhaveri (“Gayatri”), who claims through Ajay and through her parents is also in the fray in the proceedings before the Company Court. 5. Whether the Identified Paintings are assets of the Company, available for disposal, with proceeds being used for the benefit of its creditors and other stakeholders, or whether they are the private property of one or other members of the Mafatlal Family, falls for consideration. Overview of the OLRs: 6. An overview of what the three OLRs seek would be appropriate: A] OLR No. 457 of 2014 (“OLR 457”) is the first and the foundational one. The OL sought directions to the ex-directors of the Company to file the Statement of Affairs under Section 454 of the Companies Act, 1956 (“the Act”) and to hand over the books of account and records. OLR 457 also seeks directions for the purpose of ascertaining the status of ownership of the Subject Paintings, and once ownership of the Company is established, a direction to the police authorities to hand them over to the OL1. In the course of adjudicating OLR 457, the objection as to the jurisdiction of the Company Court to decide a question of title against a third party was raised by Sheetal and answered in the affirmative by an order dated December 7, 2015, and in which the directions were given enabling her to lead evidence; B] OLR No. 380 of 2016 (“OLR 380”) was taken out after the opportunity to lead evidence had been closed, seeking to move the process to the consequential stage – seeking leave to sell the Subject Paintings as property of the Company, the appointment of a valuer, and facility of inspecting the Subject Paintings2; and C] OLR No. 93 of 2026 (“OLR 93”) is restricted to the five Identified Paintings3, seeking determination of the reserve price and the earnest money deposit, and approval of the issuance and publication of the Sale Notice and the Terms and Conditions of Sale. 7. For completeness, it may be mentioned that two earlier and intermittent OLRs – OL Report No. 205 of 2015 and OL Report No. 53 of 2025 – were disposed of by orders dated June 24, 2025 and December 19, 2025 respectively. The former was disposed of with a direction to file a fresh Report for valuation and sale, and the latter was allowed on December 19, 2025, when Mr. Dadiba Pundole was appointed valuer. Before setting out the framework of analysis, the tortuous journey of facts that led to the aforesaid proceedings must be outlined, as briefly as possible, without losing out on material detail. Criminal Proceedings Inter Se the Mafatlals: 11. On August 17, 2011 Marushka Atulya Mafatlal, Sheetal’s step-daughter, lodged Magistrate Enquiry Criminal Register Case No. 11 of 201110 (“MECR 11”) at the Gamdevi Police Station under Sections 380, 405, 406, 411 and 414 read with Section 120B of the Indian Penal Code, 1860 (“IPC”), alleging theft (and related crimes) in relation to various paintings and an art chair from the residence of the Mafatlal Family (“Mafatlal Bungalow”). 12. On the very same day Sheetal too lodged a criminal complaint bearing Crime Register No. 188 of 2011 (“CR 188”) at the same police station under Sections 498A and 406 read with Section 34 of the IPC, alleging matrimonial cruelty, dowry harassment and withholding ‘stridhan’, against Marushka, Atulya and other members of the Mafatlal Family. Sheetal also commenced proceedings against them under the Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act, 2005 (“DV Act”) before the Metropolitan Magistrate, 40th Court, Girgaum. 13. It is noteworthy that MECR 11 complained of 35 paintings being stolen (not 44 paintings). The police recovered 31 paintings, of which 10 were found in Yasmin’s residence. At that stage, Sheetal and Yasmin, her friend, were aligned. They would have a falling out later. The paintings were not removed by the police. They were left where they stood, against a written undertaking from Sheetal and Yasmin that they would not be shifted or disposed of without the permission of the police11. 14. Meanwhile, Marushka filed Criminal Writ Petition No. 2770 of 2011 (“WP 2770”), while Gayatri and Kunti Shah, another sibling of Atulya, Ajay and Gayatri filed Criminal Writ Petition No. 2771 of 2011 (“WP 2771”), each seeking quashing of CR 188 set in motion by Sheetal. Atulya would later file Criminal Writ Petition No. 2861 of 2012 (“WP 2861”) towards the same end. 15. In the course of hearing WP 2770 and WP 2771, a Learned Division Bench referred the members of the Mafatlal Family to mediation by Mr. Rafique Dada, Learned Senior Advocate, and stayed the proceedings initiated by them against one another, by an order dated October 13, 201112 The mediation resulted in Consent Terms dated July 2, 201213 (“Consent Terms”), in which, Clause 7(b) read thus: “b] Further in this regard, it is agreed between the parties herein that the Art that is the subject matter of the said Complaint shall be retained by Mrs. Sheetal Mafatlal who shall be entitled to the possession and enjoyment thereof. Further it is agreed that Mr. Atulya Mafatlal, Ms. Marushka Atulya Mafatlal, Mr. Varun Atulya Mafatlal and Mrs. Kunti Shah expressly agree and declare that they do not have and shall not have nor make any claim, right or title over the same in any manner whatsoever nor shall any person claiming through or under them have, or claim any right, title or interest to or in respect of the said Art” [Emphasis Supplied] 16. Gayatri, who was a Petitioner in WP 2771 did not execute Consent Terms and stood outside the settlement, which is the basis of her pursuit of the Gayatri IA. However, as regards the others, pursuant to the Consent Terms, WP 2770 was allowed on August 7, 201214 and CR 188 was quashed as against Marushka. By order dated August 9, 201215, on Sheetal’s Criminal Application No. 429 of 2012 filed in WP 2771, MECR 11 lodged by Marushka against Sheetal was also quashed. By order dated August 22, 201216, WP 2861 was allowed to quash CR 188 as against Atulya. 17. Sheetal’s foundation of claim to the 44 Subject Paintings is Clause 7(b) of the Consent Terms (extracted above), which settled disputes among members of the Mafatlal Family with a declaration that their allegation against Sheetal would not be pressed and that Sheetal would not press her allegations of harassment and domestic violence against the Mafatlal Family members who executed the Consent Terms. 18. It is noteworthy is that the Consent Terms covered the art covered by MECR 11, which had levelled allegations about 35 paintings and not 44 paintings. It is also noteworthy that in her statement recorded before the Mumbai Police on July 12, 201117, Sheetal has confirmed removing 47 paintings and two artefacts from the Mafatlal Bungalow through her manager Manoj Shah. Therefore, Sheetal having admittedly removed 47 paintings and two artefacts, the Mafatlal Family was conscious only about 35 paintings having been removed. The Consent Terms settled the inter se disputes among them to the extent of 35 paintings that were subject matter of MECR 11. 19. On the face of the arithmetic, the Consent Terms cannot cover all 44 paintings even if one were to presume that the Consent Terms could somehow bind the Company. What is clear is that the Consent Terms did not purport to confer title on Sheetal on the face of the record. It was recorded that the members of the Mafatlal Family had disclaimed all right, title and interest in the Subject Paintings. They agreed that Sheetal would have possession and enjoy such possession of the paintings. This, in no manner, could be dispositive of whether the Company did or did not own the Subject Paintings and cannot bind the OL, more so bearing in mind the Consent Terms were among members of the promoter family under whose watch the Company had to move to liquidation. 20. If the members of the Mafatlal Family kept possession of paintings paid for by the Company in the Mafatlal Bungalow and later fought over the said paintings, trading criminal charges, to eventually settle their fight among themselves, the Consent Terms cannot add weight to the question of whether the paintings belonged to the Company or to anyone else. Evidently, apart from the Consent Terms and in particular Clause 7(b) therein, and the fact that eventually, the Mumbai Police seized the paintings from premises in her possession, Sheetal has nothing to lean on to support her claim for entitlement to the 44 Subject Paintings. At their highest, the Consent Terms (in settling inter se disputes among the Mafatlal Family) would cover the 35 paintings that were subject matter of MECR 11 without affecting anyone who was not a party to the Consent Terms. 21. Things would take a new turn later. On the strength of the Consent Terms, Sheetal would move the Learned Additional Chief Metropolitan Magistrate, Girgaum, for return of the 31 paintings. This prayer was granted by order dated September 24, 2012. On October 4, 2012, the Mumbai Police went to the flats where the paintings were directed to be kept along with Sheetal to take possession and hand the paintings over to her. At this point, Sheetal contended that fake paintings were being given to her and these were not originals. This led to the next round of criminal proceedings. 22. Sheetal filed a first information report being C.R. No. 277 of 2012 (“CR 277”) under Section 406 read with Section 34 of the IPC, alleging criminal breach of trust against Yasmin and two others. On Sheetal’s grievance that the investigation was not being properly conducted, CR 277 was transferred on December 6, 2012 to the V.P. Yasmin’s allegation was reported in the Mumbai Mirror on January 6, 201318 which led to the OL taking note and getting involved. The OL wrote to the Mumbai Police on January 29, 2013 asking for a report on the outcome of the investigation since the Company had been ordered to be wound up by order dated July 19, 2010, and if assets of the Company are found, an appropriate report would need to be made to the Company Court. 24. CR 104 was closed, with a finding that Sheetal’s complaint was false. Appropriate action was permitted to be taken against her. Analysis and Conclusions: 28. Against this factual backdrop, I have heard at length, Mr. Ranjeev Carvalho, Learned Advocate on behalf of the OL, Mr. Karl Tamboly, Learned Advocate on behalf of Sheetal, Mr. Bhavesh Parmar on behalf of Gayatri and Ms. Meena A. Ruparel on behalf of Yasmin. By consent of parties, a consolidated Convenience Compilation cutting across the material on record, and a detailed List of Dates compiled by Mr. Carvalho was taken on record and examined with the assistance of the Learned Advocates for the parties. 29. To summarise the positions of the parties, at this stage, in OLR 93, the OL only seeks directions for disposal of only the five Identified Paintings, particularly bearing in mind that disposal of the Identified Paintings may be adequate for discharging all the debts of the Company in the liquidation process. Sheetal contends that all Subject Paintings belong to her. Gayatri claims to be entitled to the Remaining Paintings and concedes that the five Identified Paintings belong to the Liquidation Estate of the Company. Yasmin, who has fallen out with Sheetal, is primarily interested in cross-examining Sheetal to prove her wrong. 30. Suffice it to say, none of the private parties has brought forth positive evidence to prove title. Each makes varying claims about the extent to which the Subject Paintings do not form part of the Liquidation Estate. One would need to examine if any of these Applicants is able to demonstrate, with any reasonable basis, the title they claim to the extent they claim it, and if they have locus to contend that the Company did not own the paintings, particularly bearing in mind their own conduct. 31. As a separate matter, whether the Subject Paintings or the Identified Paintings form part of the Liquidation Estate is to be considered. It is with a positive finding of the Identified Paintings forming part of the Liquidation Estate, that sale of the Identified Paintings and disposal of the corresponding proceedings can be approved under OLR 93. Each of these facets is dealt with in the following paragraphs. Sheetal’s Claim and Conduct: 32. Sheetal’s claims and conduct in these proceedings have been remarkable and speak for themselves. Sheetal was afforded multiple opportunities stretched over a decade to prove her claim. It would be necessary to outline her conduct and attitude to the Company Court to appreciate her claim and thereby deal with the Sheetal IAs. 33. Sheetal claims title to all 44 Subject Paintings but has positively refrained from entering the witness box despite having been given a very long rope by multiple benches of this Court, including in appellate rounds. Costs too have been imposed when enabling Sheetal to lead evidence even while rejecting her contentions about the lack of jurisdiction in the Company Court. Yet, no evidence was led. Eventually, she did purport to file an affidavit from London without compliance with due process, which too she was permitted to rectify. However, Sheetal withdrew even the non-compliant affidavit by confirming to the Company Court that she did not intend to lead any evidence. The journey of Sheetal’s multiple motions, appeals and liberties sought and granted would need to be outlined to get a sense of her conduct. 34. OLR 457 had been preferred on November 19, 201423. Company’s Claim to the Identified Paintings: 65. At the threshold, it is made clear that while the analysis in this judgement (primarily because of the nature of the factual matrix) may touch upon the 44 Subject Paintings in general, the analysis is only in aid of the five Identified Paintings, over which the OL asserts ownership by the Liquidation Estate, and seeks directions for disposal of the Identified Paintings, to use the proceeds to discharge claims adjudicated as owing to stakeholders. The OL’s claim is founded on Section 456 of the Act, which provides: “456. Custody of company’s property. (1) Where a winding up order has been made or where a provisional liquidator has been appointed, the liquidator or the provisional liquidator, as the case may be, shall take into his custody or under his control, all the property, effects and actionable claims to which the company is or appears to be entitled. … (2) All the property and effects of the company shall be deemed to be in the custody of the court as from the date of the order for the winding up of the company.” [Emphasis Supplied] 66. Evidently, where the Company appears to be entitled to any property, it shall be taken into custody by the OL, which is already noted in the December 2015 Order. Section 446(2)(b) and Section 446(2)(d) of the Act confer on the Company Court, jurisdiction to adjudicate the resulting question of title, as also held in that very order. Section 477 supplies the machinery to examine persons suspected of holding the Company’s property. The OL has also placed on record that he possesses no primary or personal knowledge of the ownership of the paintings, and the question is therefore confined to the records available and the statements of persons who were in charge of the affairs of the Company67. 67. In the teeth of the Asset Register, the purchase vouchers and the affidavits of the Mumbai Police, which make a clearly reasonable case for the Identified Paintings to belong to the Company, Sheetal and Atulya have chosen not to lead evidence and their conduct calls for adverse inference. 83. I have already articulated and set out in reasonable detail, Sheetal’s conduct and indeed Atulya’s conduct in their claim to title. As against that, there is the Asset Register, which does not appear to be fabricated – it has a detailed description of multiple assets and if it had to be fabricated, nothing would have prevented the person fabricating it to have come up with more fabrication to cover all the Subject Paintings. Yet, on the basis of what is available on record, considering that the OL’s prayers are restricted to the Identified Paintings, in my view, the OL has made out a reasonable case of showing title to the Identified Paintings. 84. At the risk of repetition, the standard of proof to be applied for these proceedings is not the criminal standard of beyond reasonable doubt but the civil standard of preponderance of probabilities. All that Sheetal has sought to create is some doubt and not even reasonable doubt. Her stance is undermined by her own conduct and her steadfast and consistent conduct with a complete lack of credibility. Sheetal has ensured a delay of over a decade in the conduct of these proceedings, challenging the jurisdiction of the Company Court; failing in the challenge; being given an opportunity to lead evidence; seeking multiple extensions of the deadline for leading evidence, only to eventually take a stand that she has no intention of leading evidence; and then filing IA 11880, on a false premise of her not having led evidence because the OL had allegedly not led evidence; and all over again contending that the Subject Paintings belong to her. 85. Worse, in the criminal proceedings, Sheetal’s conduct has been assessed and the Closure Report bringing to an end her case of having been cheated with duplicate paintings, has been upheld all the way to the Supreme Court. A party that does not come to the justice delivery system with clean hands has taken undue advantage of an indulgent long rope extended to her. Such conduct has to run out of steam at some point and that juncture has now come about. On the basis of the material on record, and the analysis and reasons set out above, I have no hesitation in holding that the Sheetal IAs are devoid of merit. 86. Atulya is no more and his own conduct as seen from the record leaves much to be desired on the subject of undermining the Company’s title. A careful and meticulous analysis of the Asset Register, the Purchase Vouchers and the Seizure List, presents a reasonable logical and commonsensical correlation to hold that the five Identified Paintings can be attributed to the liquidation estate of the Company 87. As to the five Identified Paintings, the OL also points to what has been conceded and recorded. On October 20, 2016 the Mumbai Police stated that five of the 44 paintings had been identified as the property of the Company, while there was no direct evidence of the Company’s ownership of the Remaining Paintings83. Sheetal’s own affidavit dated May 6, 2015 describes the dispute as one of “maximum 5 Paintings out of a total 44”, and the interim relief she sought in August 2024 was confined to the Remaining Paintings. Gayatri’s claim likewise excludes the Identified Paintings. Taking a holistic view of the matter, it is only fair and appropriate to hold that the OL has made out a case to allow OLR 93. Ajay’s Status – not an Ex Director: 88. One facet of the matter must be dealt with, since the OL’s own record addresses them even while Mr. Tamboly on behalf of Sheetal tables it as the basis of the foundation of OL’s approach being unreliable. It concerns Ajay’s description in OLR 457 as an ex-director of the Company. That footing was given up by the OL himself in the affidavit dated February 6, 2018, which records that the description was “truthfully incorrect” (essentially, a bona fide mistake). Ajay was the sibling of Atulya and Sheetal’s brother-in-law, who himself never held out as being an ex-director and only as a shareholder. In my view, Ajay not being an ex-director and only being a shareholder is of no consequence for the facts and analysis set out above, considering that he was an integral member of the Mafatlal Family. 89. The functioning of promoter-family-owned-and-managed companies essentially does not turn on who officially is a director and who is not. It is highly reasonable to conclude that the Mafatlal Family like many a promoter family had on the walls of their residence, paintings paid for by the company. The assessment in this judgement in any case does not place any emphasis on whether Ajay was an ex-director of the Company, but does factor in the fact that he was Atulya’s sibling and would be privy to how the Mafatlal Family conducted its affairs through its companies. Remaining Paintings: 90. I am pronouncing only upon the Identified Paintings, since those alone are the subject matter of the prayers presently pressed in the OLRs under consideration. No conclusive finding is rendered on the Remaining Paintings. 91. However, considering the need to preserve the Remaining Paintings, each of Sheetal and Gayatri who claim title to the Remaining Paintings are directed to deposit with the OL a sum of Rs. 15 lakhs respectively to enable the OL to ensure the upkeep and maintenance of the Remaining Paintings. It is necessary and reasonable for them to be called upon to deposit such a small sum considering the value of the paintings to which they stake their claim. No equities shall accrue to them since they are making competing claims and the Liquidation Estate can be reasonably preserved by making the two claimants contribute a pool of resources for the upkeep of the Remaining Paintings. 92. Such deposit shall be made by Sheetal and Gayatri within a period of four weeks from the upload of this judgement on the Court’s website. This is a fair and appropriate interlocutory measure. If either or neither makes such a deposit, it would potentially point to their level of seriousness in claiming title to the Remaining Paintings as opposed to denying and frustrating the claims of the other. Summary of Conclusions and Directions: 93. My findings may be summarised thus: A] The Company Court has jurisdiction under Section 446(2)(b) and Section 446(2)(d) read with Section 456 of the Act to adjudicate title to the Subject Paintings. The challenge to jurisdiction stands concluded against Sheetal by the December 2015 Order, which was never stayed and was not disturbed in appeal; B] Sheetal, on whom the onus lies, particularly after the OL filed the Additional Affidavit in compliance with the modified directions of the Learned Division Bench has led no evidence at all. She filed no affidavit in lieu of examination-in-chief within any of the six opportunities granted to her by multiple benches. She withdrew the only affidavit she did file in defect, and declined to enter the witness box. Her claim to the Subject Paintings rests on no evidence whatsoever; C] Clause 7(b) of the Consent Terms is confined to the art that was the subject matter of MECR 11, being 35 paintings, and cannot in any event found a claim to the 44 Subject Paintings. In any case, the Consent Terms settle disputes inter se the Mafatlal Family and neither bind the Company nor determine title as against it; D] The Asset Register, read with the Purchase Vouchers and the Seizure List, corroborated by the confirmation of Synergy Art Foundation and by Police Affidavit I and Police Affidavit II, establishes on a preponderance of probabilities that the Identified Paintings form part of the Liquidation Estate of the Company; E] OLR 93 is accordingly liable to be allowed in respect of the Identified Paintings, and the opposition of Sheetal to OLR 457 and OLR 380, including IA 11880, is liable to be rejected; and F] No finding is rendered on title to the Remaining Paintings, which is left open to be adjudicated. 94. Therefore, the following directions are passed: A] OLR 457 is finally disposed of since the prayers sought in it have either been granted, refused or overtaken, with even consequential action having been taken, with implications for non-compliance such as Atulya not providing books and records resulting in the consequences set out in this judgement. As such, with subsequent OLRs having been filed, nothing survives to keep OLR 457 pending; B] As regards OLR 380, while its prayers revolve around all the 44 Subject Paintings, the matter has now progressed and transformed into dealing with the five Identified Paintings, and more so with the filing of OLR 93. However, prayer clause (d) in OLR 380 is worthy of being granted. The asset class involved, namely, works of art, are not amenable to the routine manner of auction on an auction platform that is typically used to sell land, machinery, inventory and other industrial assets. In my opinion, for value maximisation and improved price discovery, it would be necessary to engage the services of an expert who can oversee the entire process of sale and disposal of the Identified Paintings; C] Towards this end, I hereby appoint Ms. Sonal Singh Naharwar (“the Expert”), a highly reputed expert in the field of art with a robust experience of two decades in the field of auctioning art. In my view, the Expert, having worked on valuation and sales of some of the most important South Asian works of art at auction, would be well suited to subserve the best interests of the sale process and the interests of the Liquidation Estate of the Company. Mr. Ranjeev Carvalho, Learned Advocate is also requested to actively assist in the process along with the Expert. Liberty to the Official Liquidator to seek such directions from the Company Court should there be a need to deal with any difficulties. In the aforesaid premises, OLR 380 is also finally disposed of; D] The conventional approach of the sale notice and standard terms and conditions for sale of land, machinery and other industrial assets would not be routinely adopted for the forthcoming disposal. Instead, the Official Liquidator along with the Expert and Mr. Carvalho shall formulate the mechanism for conduct of an auction by such art auction house as is finalised by them. The terms and conditions for the auction shall be formulated by them taking care to adopt the principles underlying the conventional terms and conditions as far as practicable. Liberty to mention the matter for any clarifications and directions. OLR 93 is therefore disposed of in the terms set out in this judgement; E] To get an update in the matter, stand over to November 20, 2026. 95.

Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); Bimal Poddar @ Bimal Kumar Poddar Vs State of Jharkhand (Jharkhand High Court) Deliberate Misdescription of Goods to Evade Sales Tax: Jharkhand HC Upholds Conviction Introduction The Jharkhand High Court has upheld the conviction of a partner of a trading firm for offences under Sections 49(2)(b) and 49(3)(d) of the Bihar Finance Act, 1981, concerning sales tax evasion through discrepancies in goods descriptions and statutory records. Dismissing the criminal revision, the Court found that the documentary evidence disclosed intentional discrepancies designed to evade commercial tax. It also emphasised the limited scope of revisional jurisdiction, which does not permit routine interference with carefully considered concurrent findings. The Court cancelled the petitioner’s bail and directed him to surrender within two months to serve the remaining sentence. Background of the Case The proceedings originated from an investigation into compliance with the Bihar Finance Act, 1981, and the Central Sales Tax Act, 1956, in transactions involving vanaspati and edible oils. A team of commercial tax officials collected information from out-of-State manufacturers and traders. These records, including Form F details, were compared with the records submitted by the Ranchi firm, Jokhiram Parmeshwar Lal, in which the petitioner was a partner. The prosecution alleged discrepancies between the suppliers’ records and those maintained or submitted by the firm. These included suppression of sales, undervaluation and incorrect descriptions of goods. In particular, the firm allegedly recorded mustard oil instead of vanaspati or dalda, which attracted a higher tax rate. The complaint also alleged use of certain Form F documents that had not been issued by the concerned commercial tax office. Allegations of Sales Suppression The prosecution alleged suppression of sales amounting to ₹12,45,180 during 1989-90. For 1990-91, the complaint described several irregularities, including suppression of sales of ₹72,91,200, allegedly resulting in avoidance of tax of ₹7,32,682. It further alleged that edible oil worth ₹18,49,400 had been imported using Form F documents not issued by the concerned office, involving alleged tax evasion of ₹1,86,604. The complaint placed the overall suppressed sale value for 1990-91 at ₹94,31,600. These allegations formed the foundation of the prosecution, supported by oral and documentary evidence. Trial Court’s Conviction The prosecution examined six witnesses and produced documents including comparison charts, transaction records, a seizure list and a sanction order. Several statutory forms and corresponding sale sheets were also produced as material exhibits. The trial court acquitted the petitioner of the IPC charges, but convicted him under the Bihar Finance Act. It imposed one year’s simple imprisonment and a fine of ₹1,000 under Section 49(2)(b), and two years’ simple imprisonment and a fine of ₹1,500 under Section 49(3)(d). The appellate court upheld the conviction and sentence. The petitioner then challenged these concurrent decisions before the High Court. Petitioner’s Objections The petitioner principally questioned the validity of the prosecution sanction. He argued that Section 49(5) required sanction from the Commissioner, whereas the sanction had been granted by the Joint Commissioner, who had also not been examined. He further contended that the firm had other partners and that the prosecution had failed to establish his individual responsibility for its day-to-day business affairs. Another objection concerned the non-examination of the second investigating officer, who had conducted a substantial part of the investigation. According to the petitioner, this prevented clarification of contradictions and discrepancies, causing prejudice to the defence. State’s Response The State defended the concurrent findings and maintained that the petitioner was responsible for the firm’s business activities. It argued that the objections concerning sanction and non-examination of the investigating officer did not establish any failure of justice. According to the State, the petitioner had shown no sufficient ground for interference in revision. High Court’s Findings After examining the records, the High Court found that the material exhibits revealed apparent discrepancies concerning the goods, intentionally introduced to evade commercial tax. The Court noted that the appellate court had examined the documentary evidence closely and identified the discrepancies supporting the finding of tax evasion. It relied on Amit Kapoor v. Ramesh Chander, (2012) 9 SCC 460, concerning the restricted scope of criminal revision. Revisional interference addresses matters such as patent legal defects, jurisdictional errors, findings unsupported by evidence, or arbitrary or perverse exercise of discretion. Finding no legal substance in the petitioner’s challenge to the concurrent findings, the High Court dismissed the revision. Conclusion The judgment reinforces the importance of accurate goods descriptions and consistent statutory records in tax compliance. On the evidence before it, the Court upheld the finding that discrepancies were deliberately created to evade tax. The ruling also illustrates that a criminal revision requires a demonstrated legal or jurisdictional defect warranting interference. The Court rejected the petitioner’s objections in this case; it did not separately formulate a general rule validating every sanction granted by a Joint Commissioner. Following dismissal, the petitioner was directed to surrender within two months, failing which the trial court was authorised to take coercive steps. Cases Discussed Amit Kapoor v. The factual matrix giving rise to this case is that Assistant Commercial Tax Commissioner, Special Area, Ranchi lodged a detailed a written complaint with Kotwali P.S under letter No 202 dated 18.04.1996 stating inter alia that the Government of Bihar has taken a decision to enquire as to whether the Provisions of Bihar Finance Act 1981 and Central Sales Tax Act 1956 are duly compiled with in case of purchase and sale of Vanaspati oil. It is alleged that several differences were found in the Form- F submitted by the partners of aforesaid Sarwasri Jokhiram Parmeshwar Lal in comparison to the prices declared in Form – F of aforesaid outside of the State manufactures and business men. They also submitted their statutory form – F fraudulently and under forged practice. It is alleged that the transaction table of impugned Sarwasri Jokhiram Parmeshwar Lal was also checked and it was found that in the statutory form – F correct name of foods imported was mentioned but to avoid tax wrong and false undervalued entries were made in transaction table with the wrong name of goods. Assailing the concurrent findings of the Courts below, the learned counsel for the petitioner has raised following points:- The main thrust of the petitioner is that for prosecuting under Bihar Finance Act, sanction is required in terms of Section 49(5) by the Commissioner but in the present case, sanction was granted by Joint Commissioner who was also not examined as a witness. The learned trial Court as well as appellate Court has failed to appreciate that no material evidence has been adduced to substantiate the charge against the petitioner alone whereas it is admitted that there were other partners of the firm also. Submissions on behalf of the State:- 15. Per contra, Learned A.P.P. has defended the judgment of learned trial Court as well as the appellate Court on merits by stating that both the Courts below have very wisely and aptly gone through the record of the case and rightly found the petitioner guilty. It has further been submitted that the petitioner was responsible for day to day work of the firm and it is also settled principal that every partners acts with each as an agent. There is no plea of the defence that he was not active partner in carrying out the business of the firm and doing all other business activities including the submission of sales tax etc. Similarly, the question of sanction accorded by joint commissioner also does not affect the judgment passed by the learned trial Court and appellate Court with concurrent findings. The petitioner has failed to point out as to how failure of justice has occasioned in this case due to non-examination of second I.O. and sanction accorded by joint commissioner for prosecution of the accused. There is no legal substance in the grounds raised on behalf of the petitioner challenging the concurrent findings of the learned trial Court as well as appellate Court. There is no merit in this revision, which is fit to be dismissed. Analysis, reasons and decision: 16. I have gone through the record of case along with impugned judgment passed by the learned Appellate Court as well as trial Court in the light of contentions raised on behalf of respective parties. 17. The sole point for consideration in this revision is as to “whether the conviction and sentence of the petitioner suffers from any error of law, calling for any interference in this revision? 18. Perusal of the material exhibits reveals that there are some discrepancies with regard to goods which are clearly apparent and these discrepancies with regard to goods has been intentionally made to evade commercial tax. The learned appellate Court in para-12 of its judgment has very minutely gone through the documentary evidence brought on record and has mentioned each of the discrepancies found with regard to goods to evade the commercial tax which makes it clear that the firm of the accused has played foul play to evade commercial tax which is punishable under Bihar Finance Act. 19. The Hon’ble Apex Court in the case of Amit Kapoor v. Ramesh Chander reported in (2012) 9 SCC 460 has held that : 12. Section 397 of the Code vests the court with the power to call for and examine the records of an inferior court for the purposes of satisfying itself as to the legality and regularity of any proceedings or order made in a case. The object of this provision is to set right a patent defect or an error of jurisdiction or law. There has to be a well- founded error and it may not be appropriate for the court to scrutinize the orders, which upon the face of it bears a token of careful consideration and appear to be in accordance with law. If one looks into the various judgments of this Court, it emerges that the revisional jurisdiction can be invoked where the decisions under challenge are grossly erroneous, there is no compliance with the provisions of law, the finding recorded is based on no evidence, material evidence is ignored or judicial discretion is exercised arbitrarily or perversely. These are not exhaustive classes, but are merely indicative. Each case would have to be determined on its own merits. 13. Another well-accepted norm is that the revisional jurisdiction of the higher court is a very limited one and cannot be exercised in a routine manner. 20. In view of the above discussions and reasons, I find no legal substance in the arguments of learned counsel for the petitioner regarding the guilt of the present petitioner in the concurrent findings of the learned Courts below. Accordingly, this revision stands dismissed. 21. Consequently, the bail granted to the petitioner vide order dated 19.03.2018 during pendency of this revision, is hereby cancelled and he is directed to surrender before the learned Trial Court within two months from the date of this Judgment to undergo the remaining period of sentence awarded to him by the learned trial Court, failing which, the learned trial Court shall take all coercive steps for arrest and detention of the petitioner for sustaining the remaining period of sentence awarded to him. 22. Pending I.A(s), if any, is also disposed of, accordingly. 23. Let a copy of this judgment along with trial Court record be sent back to the court concerned for information and needful.

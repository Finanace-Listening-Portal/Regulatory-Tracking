(NCLT Mumbai) Summary : The National Company Law Tribunal, Mumbai Bench-I, dismissed a petition filed by Parashram V. Kshatriya under Sections 241 and 242 of the Companies Act, 2013, alleging oppression and mismanagement in Madhushree Paper Mills Private Limited. The dispute concerned the company’s shareholding, alleged exclusion from management, irregular statutory filings and the failure to recognise additional shares claimed by the petitioner. The company was incorporated in 1987 by the petitioner, his brother Balram V. Kshatriya and their father, each initially holding 50 equity shares. Following their father’s death in December 1997, the company’s records for FY 2000-01 reflected 160 shares each in the names of the petitioner and Balram, and 201 shares in the name of their brother Mahendra V. Kshatriya. The petitioner challenged the legitimacy of this change and alleged that the relevant transfer or allotment lacked supporting documentation. The petitioner further contended that he had contributed ₹1,70,000 towards 170 additional equity shares around 2007, but the respondents failed to record the allotment properly. He also questioned a September 2022 board resolution proposing the allotment of 170 shares each to himself and Balram and 129 shares to Mahendra, purportedly to equalise their holdings at 330 shares each. Other allegations included unauthorised use of his digital signature, non-service of meeting notices, denial of access to statutory records and failures in ROC compliance. The respondents disputed these allegations, maintaining that the petitioner had himself controlled the company’s records and statutory filings for several years and had knowledge of the disputed shareholding. On the challenge to the historical shareholding, the Tribunal observed that the petition was filed in 2022, approximately 22 years after the disputed entries first appeared. It found no satisfactory explanation for this delay. Referring to Chairman, State Bank of India v. M.J. James and Abdul Wahid Abdul Gaffor Khatri v. Safe Heights Developers Pvt. Ltd., the Tribunal applied the principles of delay, laches and acquiescence. It also relied on Section 86(2) of the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023, concerning the presumption applicable to secure electronic signatures. The petitioner had not sufficiently explained how or by whom his digital signature was allegedly used without authorisation. Moreover, he had not established the documentary basis for the increase in his own shareholding from 50 to 160 shares, weakening his allegation that the disputed changes were exclusively attributable to the respondents. Regarding the additional share allotment, the Tribunal held that the objective of equalising shareholding could not dispense with compliance with statutory procedures. Nevertheless, procedural irregularities did not automatically constitute oppression. The petitioner had accepted share certificates and continued participating in the company’s affairs for several years. The Tribunal considered these circumstances relevant to acquiescence and directed that the company’s books and records be updated to reflect the shareholding arrangement discussed in its findings. It further observed that appropriate rectification remedies were available in relation to shareholding records under Sections 58 and 59 of the Companies Act, 2013. The Tribunal also rejected the allegation of deliberate exclusion from company meetings, finding insufficient evidence of a continuous course of oppressive conduct. Although it acknowledged a breakdown of trust among the brothers and considered winding up just and equitable, it refrained from ordering winding up because the non-functional company owned substantial agricultural land in Nashik, and liquidation could prejudice stakeholders. It suggested that the parties explore an amicable resolution concerning the company’s assets and future. Applying the principles explained by the Supreme Court in V.S. Krishnan v. Westfort Hi-Tech Hospital Ltd., the Tribunal concluded that careless conduct, procedural lapses or family disagreements alone were insufficient to establish oppression under Sections 241 and 242. The petitioner had failed to prove conduct lacking probity or causing substantial prejudice to his legal or proprietary rights as a shareholder. Accordingly, the petition was dismissed, without preventing the petitioner from pursuing other remedies available under law. Cases Discussed Chairman, State Bank of India v. M.J. Counsel and perused the material available on record. 5.2. The first issue for consideration is the allegation of the Petitioner that the shareholding pattern of the Respondent No. 1 Company was altered without any lawful basis after the demise of their father, Late Mr. Vishwanathsa Kshatriya, on 21.12.1997. The Petitioner contends that the Company’s records for the Financial Year 2000–2001 reflected the authorised share capital of Rs. 10,00,000/- divided into 1,000 equity shares of Rs. 1,000/- each and the subscribed and paid-up share capital of Rs. 5,21,000/- divided into 521 equity shares of Rs. 1,000/- each. The said records reflected 160 equity shares each in the names of the Petitioner and Respondent No. 2 and 201 equity shares in the name of Respondent No. 3. 5.3. It is the case of the Petitioner that there is no supporting record of allotment or transfer of shares in favour of Respondent No. 3, including any corresponding return of allotment or other statutory filing. The Petitioner further alleges that the shares held by their late father were dealt with contrary to his Will, which, according to the Petitioner, contemplated an equal division of the shareholding among the Petitioner and Respondent Nos. 2 and 3. The Petitioner has also alleged that his Digital Signature Certificate was used without his knowledge or consent for filing Online Form 32 for appointment of Respondent No. 3 as a Director of the Respondent Company. 5.4. Per contra, Ld. Counsel for the Respondents submits that the Petitioner, being the elder brother, was in charge of the Company’s records and was responsible for filing its statutory returns with the Registrar of Companies up to the Financial Year 2013–2014. It is contended that the Petitioner himself signed and filed the annual returns reflecting the shareholding pattern. The Respondents further submit that following their father’s demise, the shares he held were transferred or transmitted with the consent of all the brothers. 5.5. It is pertinent to note that the Petition is filed in the Year 2022 alleging the acts of Allotment of Shares in the Year 2000-2001, after lapse of 22 years. The Petitioner’s grievance is that the transmission of shares in the name of Respondent No. 3 was made contrary to the Will of the Petitioner’s Father. Further, the filings with the Registrar of Companies were done by the Petitioner, and admittedly the Filings bear the Digital Signature of the Petitioner. 5.6. It would be useful to refer to Section 86(2) of the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023, which provides that, unless the contrary is proved, a presumption shall arise that a Secure Electronic Signature affixed by a Subscriber with the intention of signing or approving an Electronic Record was so affixed by the Subscriber. 5.7. The Petitioner has only contended that without his knowledge the Digital Signature was affixed; however, in what manner his Digital Signature and in what form it was affixed is not pleaded. Neither is it pleaded as to who has affixed his Digital Signature without his consent and knowledge. Therefore, unless the contrary is proved by the Petitioner, the presumption that the Subscriber signed the documents digitally cannot be rebutted. Therefore, making a grievance of transmission of Shares in favour of the Respondent No. 3 after lapse of 22 Years lacks bona fides. 5.8. It is further pertinent to note that the Petitioner has challenged the increase in the shareholding of Respondent No. 3 from the earlier shareholding reflected in the Company’s records. However, the Petitioner has also not placed sufficient documentary evidence on record to establish the basis on which his own shareholding, as well as that of Respondent No. 2, increased from 50 shares each to 160 shares each. Thus, while the absence of supporting records regarding the disputed shareholding warrants consideration, the available material does not establish that the alleged irregularity was attributable exclusively to the Respondents or that it was part of a deliberate scheme to oppress the Petitioner. 5.9. Hon’ble Supreme Court in Chairman, State Bank of India v M J James, (2022) 2 SCC 301, held the following: “36. What is a reasonable time is not to be put in a straitjacket formula or judicially codified in the form of days, etc. as it depends upon the facts and circumstances of each case. A right not exercised for a long time is nonexistent. Doctrine of delay and laches as well as acquiescence are applied to non-suit the litigants who approach the court/appellate authorities belatedly without any justifiable explanation for bringing action after unreasonable delay.” Further, in Abdul Wahid Abdul Gaffor Khatri Director and Other vs Safe Heights Developers Pvt. Ltd. and Others, (2018) SCC OnLine Bom 693, Hon’ble Supreme Court held as under: “This Court, in the context of a similarly discretionary order under Section 111 of the Companies Act, 1956, in Sulochana Neelkanth Kalyani v. Takle Investments Co. &amp; Ors., has held that exercise of such discretion could certainly be affected by inordinate and unexplained delay and laches and that any such delay may render granting of the relief inequitable, as there could be equities arising in favour of other parties as a result of such delay and also such delay may give rise to a case of acquiescence, waiver or estoppel. These aspects would be for the CLB to assess and would depend upon the facts and circumstances of each case”. In view of the above, we are of the considered view that the Petitioner has failed to explain the inordinate delay of 22 years in challenging the alleged allotment or transfer of shares. The Petition, insofar as it seeks relief in respect of the aforesaid grievances, must be considered in light of delay and laches as was settled law in matters under the Companies Act, 1956, which had no express provision for limitation. In the absence of cogent evidence establishing the alleged acts of oppression and mismanagement, such delay weighs against granting relief in respect thereof. Nonetheless, the Companies Act, 2013 specifically makes the Limitation Act applicable to applications/petitions filed under the Act, and the Limitation Period thereunder is 3 years. On both grounds, the Petitioner has failed to bring on record reasons for not approaching this tribunal within the permissible time, even though the Petitioner, being a director of the Respondent Company, ought to be aware of the requirement of filing and could have diligently discovered the transmission of shares which is alleged to be contrary to the will. 5.10. The Petitioner has further alleged that, in or around the year 2007, he advanced urgent funds in the form of an unsecured loan to the Company at the request of Respondent No. 2, on the assurance that fresh equity shares would be issued to him in consideration of his contribution. According to the Petitioner, 170 equity shares were allotted to him, and the corresponding share certificates were handed over to him. However, the allotment was not properly recorded in the Company’s books of account and statutory records, and the prescribed procedure under the Companies Act was not followed. 5.11. The Petitioner has also referred to the letter dated 05.09.2022 issued by Respondent No. 2, enclosing a notice for convening a Board Meeting on 12.09.2022, wherein it was proposed to allot 170 equity shares each to the Petitioner and Respondent No. 2 and 129 equity shares to Respondent No. 3, with the stated objective of equalising the shareholding. The Petitioner objected to the proposed allotment on the ground that the procedure prescribed under the Companies Act, 2013, had not been followed. He also objected to the proposed allotment in favour of Respondent No. 3, whose existing shareholding was itself under dispute. The Respondents, on the other hand, contend that the shareholding pattern was within the Petitioner’s knowledge and that he himself was responsible for maintaining the Company’s records and filing its statutory returns for several years. 5.12. 5.12. The alleged allotment of 170 equity shares to the Petitioner dates back to 2007. The Petitioner has not satisfactorily explained why the alleged failure to record the allotment in the Company’s books and statutory records was not earlier challenged, particularly given that he remained associated with the Company and, as the Respondents contend, served as its Chairman for a considerable period. The Respondents have further contended that the proposed allotment was intended to equalise the parties’ shareholding and that no prejudice was caused to the Petitioner. 5.13. It is, however, noted that the proposed allotment of shares, as reflected in the notice dated 05.09.2022, was required to comply with the provisions of the Companies Act, 2013. The mere objective of equalising the shareholding cannot dispense with compliance with the prescribed statutory procedure. Nevertheless, the failure to follow the requisite procedure, by itself, does not establish that the Respondents acted with the intention of oppressing the Petitioner. It is further noted that, in the year 2007, Respondent No. 2 handed over to the Petitioner the original share certificates representing 160 equity shares, along with the freshly issued share certificates representing 170 additional equity shares of Rs. 1,000/-each. The said share certificates were issued and handed over when the Petitioner was actively involved in the affairs of the Company and continued to participate in its management, including by signing the financial statements, until FY 2014. Having accepted the share certificates and remained associated with the affairs of the Company for several years thereafter, the Petitioner is precluded, in the facts and circumstances of the present case, from challenging the said allotment at this belated stage on the principles of delay, laches, and acquiescence. 5.14. In the present case, the handing over and acceptance of the share certificates, coupled with the Petitioner’s continued participation in the affairs of the Company for several years thereafter, are relevant circumstances demonstrating the parties’ acceptance of the shareholding arrangement. Accordingly, he cannot have any grievance in relation thereto. Accordingly, the books and records of the Company shall, therefore, be updated to reflect the aforesaid shareholding pattern. 5.15. Further, considering that the proposed allotment contemplated the issuance of shares to the Petitioner as well as Respondent Nos. 2 and 3, and having regard to its stated objective of equalising the shareholding, the Petitioner has not demonstrated how the allotment, in itself, prejudiced his rights. In the absence of sufficient documentary evidence establishing the terms of the alleged 2007 transaction, the validity of the claimed allotment of 170 shares, and the manner in which the Petitioner’s rights were prejudiced, this allegation cannot, by itself, be treated as oppression or mismanagement by the Respondents. 5.16. There might be lapses in reflecting the Allotments in the Account Books or in making filings. Admittedly, no proof of filings has been placed on record. It is pertinent to note that the present issue pertains to Sections 58 and 59 of the Companies Act, 2013, which can be rectified further, since the Petitioner himself was in charge of the Company and was responsible for making filings with the Registrar of Companies; now the Petitioner cannot contend that he was subject to oppression by the Respondents. Moreover, shortcomings can be rectified by making an appropriate application with the Registrar of Companies. Further, the Petitioner cannot have any grievance if the interest of Respondent No. 3 was prejudiced by allotment of Shares in order to equalise the inter-se shareholding, in the absence of Respondent No. 3 being aggrieved by such allotment. 5.17. The next allegation concerns the non-service of notices of Board Meetings and Annual General Meetings upon the Petitioner. The Petitioner submits that the annual returns for the Financial Years 2014–2015 to 2019–2020 reflected only Respondent Nos. 2 and 3 as present at the Annual General Meetings. He further submits that he received notice of a Board Meeting scheduled for 29.10.2021 by email dated 22.10.2021, and notice of the 33rd Annual General Meeting scheduled for 29.11.2021 by email dated 04.11.2021, and that these were the first notices he received regarding the meetings. The Respondents, however, contend that the parties are brothers and that meetings were conducted on the basis of their mutual understanding. They further submit that the Petitioner had been associated with the Company’s affairs and that disputes regarding the meetings arose only subsequently. 5.18. We note that the material placed on record indicates that the Petitioner continued to remain in contact with the Respondents after 2014. The Petitioner has also filed the Company’s income-tax returns for certain years between 2014 and 2018 and has continued to be associated with the affairs of Bhikusa Papers Private Limited, in which the parties were also shareholders. In these circumstances, the Petitioner has not sufficiently explained whether he made any inquiry or raised any objection to the Respondents regarding the conduct of the Company’s meetings or his alleged exclusion from them before filing the present Company Petition. On the material available, the allegation of deliberate exclusion from the Company’s meetings has not been sufficiently established as part of a continuous course of oppressive conduct by the Respondents. 5.19. In the facts and circumstances of the present case, it is also necessary to consider the nature of the relationship between the Petitioner and Respondent Nos. 2 and 3. The Petitioner and Respondent Nos. 2 and 3 are brothers and were associated with the Company as shareholders and members of the same family. It clearly follows from the above discussion that there is a breakdown of trust and confidence between the Petitioner and Respondent Nos. 2 and 3. Accordingly, it is just and equitable to wind up the affairs of the Respondent Company. However, it is also noted that the Respondent Company is a non-functional company but owns agricultural land admeasuring approximately 21 hectares and 46 gunthas in Nashik, and that passing a winding-up order would be prejudicial to the interests of its stakeholders. Considering the family relationship between the parties and the nature of the Company’s assets, it would be appropriate for them to explore an amicable resolution concerning the future of the Company, including the sale or distribution of its assets in accordance with law, its continued existence, or its voluntary liquidation, as may be mutually agreed and legally permissible. 5.20. Hon’ble Supreme Court in V.S. Krishnan & Ors. v. Westfort Hi-Tech Hospital Ltd. & Ors., held the following: “From the above decisions, it is clear that oppression would be made out: i. Where the conduct is harsh, burdensome and wrong. ii. Where the conduct is mala fide and is for a collateral purpose where although the ultimate objective may be in the interest of the company, the immediate purpose would result in an advantage for some shareholders vis ‘- vis the others. iii. The action is against probity and good conduct. iv. The oppressive act complained of may be fully permissible under law but may yet be oppressive and, therefore, the test as to whether an action is oppressive or not is not based on whether it is legally permissible or not since even if legally permissible, if the action is otherwise against probity, good conduct or is burdensome, harsh or wrong or is mala fide or for a collateral purpose, it would amount to oppression under Sections 397 and 398.” 5.21. Unwise, insufficient, or careless conduct of the Director cannot give rise to a claim under Section 241-242 of the Companies Act, 2013. What is required for filing the Company Petition under Section 241-242 of the Companies Act, 2013 is conduct that lacks probity, is unfair and causes prejudice to the Petitioner in respect of his Legal or proprietary right as a Shareholder. Nothing has been demonstrated by the Petitioner to show that the Respondents’ conduct lacks probity or has caused substantial prejudice to the Petitioner’s legal rights. Therefore, the ingredients required to exercise powers under Sections 241-242 of the Companies Act, 2013 are not made out. 5.22. In terms of the above, CP No. 26/2022 is dismissed and disposed of. It is clarified that the disposal of the present Petition shall not fetter the Petitioner’s right to file an appropriate petition before the appropriate forum for their remedy, if otherwise available under the law.

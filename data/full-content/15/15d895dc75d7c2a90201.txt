(Supreme Court of India) Summary: The Supreme Court of India dismissed four connected criminal appeals challenging convictions arising from the murder of B.V. Girish, a 26-year-old software engineer employed with Intel. The judgment examined the reliability of eyewitness accounts, electronic call detail records, motive, recovery evidence and the circumstances establishing criminal conspiracy. While confirming the appellants’ convictions and life sentences, the Court separately considered the reformative purpose of criminal justice and permitted them to seek pardon from the Governor of Karnataka under Article 161 of the Constitution. The prosecution case arose from events in Bengaluru in December 2003. Shubha, then a 20-year-old law student, had become engaged to Girish on 30 November 2003, although she was unwilling to proceed with the marriage. According to the prosecution, she confided in Arun Verma, a fellow student, who contacted his cousin Dinesh. Dinesh subsequently involved Venkatesh in a conspiracy to eliminate Girish. On 3 December 2003, Shubha ac-companied Girish to dinner and thereafter to an airport viewing point. Girish was attacked with a steel rod and died the following morning. The prosecution alleged that Shubha communicated the couple’s movements to the other accused, enabling the assault. The Trial Court convicted the accused on 13 July 2010, relying on evidence concerning motive, eyewitness testimony, telephone communications and recovery of the weapon and scooter. The Karnataka High Court subsequently modified the convictions to include murder read with criminal conspiracy under Sections 302 and 120-B of the Indian Penal Code, while maintaining the life sentences. Shubha’s additional conviction under Section 201 IPC for destruction of evidence was also sustained. Before the Supreme Court, the appellants challenged the credibility of several witnesses, delays in recording statements, the admissibility of electronic records and the evidentiary value of the alleged recoveries. A central issue was the prosecution’s reliance on call detail records and SMS communications exchanged among the accused between 25 November and 4 December 2003. The appellants contended that the certificates accompanying records produced by the tele-communications companies failed to satisfy Section 65B of the Indian Evidence Act. The Supreme Court examined the evidence of the relevant service-provider officials and the objections concerning the preparation, certification and reliability of the records. It accepted the electronic evidence in the circumstances of the case, finding that the extensive communications, particularly during the period immediately preceding the assault, materially supported the prosecution’s allegation of coordination among the accused. The Court also scrutinised the testimony of witnesses relied upon to establish Shubha’s motive and the circumstances of the attack. Importantly, it did not accept every aspect of the prosecution’s evidence without reservation. The judgment expressly recorded disapproval of the evidence of certain witnesses, including PW-8, PW-11, PW-15 and PW-16. Nevertheless, the Court found that the remaining evidence was sufficient to establish the chain of circumstances. It placed particular reliance on PW-23’s evidence regarding motive, the telephone records proved through PW-24 and PW-25, and the recovery of the weapon. The accused had also failed to provide an acceptable explanation for the extensive communications disclosed by the records. In assessing the individual roles, the Supreme Court found that Dinesh had coordinated and supervised the operation, while Venkatesh’s involvement was supported by the recovery and communication evidence. Shubha’s participation was established through her knowledge of the plan, communications with the other accused and conduct relating to the destruction of evidence. The Court concluded that the circumstantial chain was sufficiently connected to sustain the findings of guilt, notwithstanding its reservations about certain witness testimonies. Accordingly, it dismissed the appeals, upheld the convictions under Sections 302 and 120-B IPC, maintained Shubha’s additional conviction under Section 201 IPC, and confirmed the life sentences. Despite affirming the convictions, the Supreme Court devoted substantial attention to the social circumstances surrounding the crime and the distinction between punishment and rehabilitation. It observed that family pressures and Shubha’s inability to exercise autonomy in marriage decisions formed part of the background, without excusing the murder of an innocent person. Referring to the constitutional power of pardon under Article 161, the Court explained that executive clemency is distinct from statutory remission and serves a broader reformative purpose. Considering the passage of more than two decades and the circumstances discussed in the judgment, it granted the appellants eight weeks to submit pardon petitions to the Governor of Karnataka. The Court directed that they should not be arrested and that their sentences would remain suspended until those petitions were considered and decided. The appeals were nevertheless dismissed, and no pardon was grant-ed by the Supreme Court itself.

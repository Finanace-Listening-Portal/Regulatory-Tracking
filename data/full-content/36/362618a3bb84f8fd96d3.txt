Summary: Physical verification of property, plant and equipment is an important management responsibility under clause 3(i)(b) of CARO 2020. Although the provision requires verification at reasonable intervals and appropriate accounting treatment of material discrepancies, it does not specifically mandate annual verification, asset tagging or outsourcing. Companies appointing external verification agencies should therefore establish clear contractual and operational expectations before fieldwork begins. This article explains nine practical considerations, including defining the engagement scope, adopting sheet-to-floor and floor-to-sheet verification approaches, preparing the Fixed Asset Register (FAR), establishing cut-off dates, monitoring asset movements and selecting suitable tags. It highlights the importance of allocating responsibilities between management and the agency, categorising discrepancies, investigating missing and additional assets, and determining appropriate accounting treatment. Particular attention is given to third-party assets, capitalisation, write-offs and recognition of right-of-use assets under applicable accounting standards. The article also discusses realistic project timelines, location-wise reporting, access to raw verification data, management review periods, reconciliation rounds and engagement completion criteria. Companies should compare quotations on the basis of the complete scope rather than merely the rate per asset, considering manpower, materials, travel, software access and additional visits. A practical pre-engagement checklist covering scope, responsibilities, confidentiality, data ownership and deliverables helps businesses avoid disputes and obtain verification reports that support effective fixed asset controls and statutory audit reporting. Notice what the clause does not say. It does not say assets must be tagged. It does not say verification must be outsourced. It does not even say it must happen every year. What it does say is that verification is management’s job. Many companies hand that job to an outside agency, especially when their assets are spread across plants, branches and warehouses. That is where things often go wrong. Companies tend to choose an agency the way they buy anything else: number of assets multiplied by a rate per asset. But when an engagement ends in an argument, the cause is almost never the rate. It is something nobody agreed on at the start. Here are the points worth settling before anyone sets foot on site. 1. Write down exactly what is included “Physical verification” means different things to different people. One agency may only count assets and stick tags on them. Another may also clean up your Fixed Asset Register (FAR), record serial numbers and condition, reconcile everything back to the books and give you a report for each location. Two quotations for the same number of assets cannot be compared unless they cover the same work. Sheet-to-floor and floor-to-sheet verification Also agree which way the verification will run: Sheet-to-floor: start with each line in the FAR and go find the asset. This tells you whether recorded assets actually exist. Floor-to-sheet: start with what is physically there and trace it back to the register. This tells you whether everything you own is actually recorded. Do only one of these and you have checked only half the story. 2. Sort out the register before the team arrives A register kept for accounting is often useless on the shop floor. You will find entries like “Being purchase of 25 executive chairs”, one line covering an entire project, quantities missing, locations blank. The number of lines in the FAR can be very different from the number of physical assets, so an estimate based on line count alone is often wrong. Classification of assets before physical verification Before fieldwork starts, agree how each type of asset will be handled: Taggable assets – checked and tagged one by one (computers, furniture, machines) Countable assets – counted, not tagged individually (light fittings, CCTV cameras) Assets you cannot physically see – checked from records and documents (concealed wiring, underground pipes) Assets already tagged – old tags checked and matched Assets belonging to someone else – listed separately (rented printers, vendor equipment) Do this clean-up first. Send a team out with an unprocessed register and you get slow progress, wrong matches and repeat visits. 3. Fix a cut-off date and keep track of movements Pick a cut-off date for the register and agree how new purchases, disposals and transfers during the exercise will be recorded. In a multi-location job, a laptop that moves from one office to another halfway through can be counted twice, or not at all. A simple movement log kept by the company while fieldwork is on solves this. 4. Choose the right tags The tag has to suit the surface and the environment. Polyester labels work well on furniture and IT equipment. Machinery and harsh conditions need metal tags. Laser-marked stainless steel tags are made to order and typically take 10 to 12 working days, so order them early. Always test a few samples on site before tagging thousands of assets. 5. Be clear about who does what Some things only the company can do. The final split should be in the contract, but it usually looks like this: Responsibilities of the company and verification agency Sharing the latest FAR – company Cleaning up the FAR, if included – agency Giving access to all locations – company Identifying specialised machines – company Verifying, tagging and recording assets – agency Reconciling with the FAR – agency Investigating differences – both Approving accounting entries – company Signing off each location – both In practice, the delays at the end of a job usually come from things only the company’s own people could have answered, like which machine is which, or where an asset was moved without anyone recording it. 6. Agree how differences will be reported The real value of the exercise is in the differences between the floor and the register. These are the usual categories: Classification of physical verification discrepancies Not found – in the FAR, but not located on site Additional – on site, but not in the FAR Wrong location – found somewhere other than recorded Quantity difference – the count does not match the books Description difference – the asset does not match its description Possible duplicate – two FAR lines may be the same asset Damaged or obsolete – found, but not in working condition Not verified – could not be checked because of access or other limits Accounting treatment of unrecorded and third-party assets Assets found on site but missing from the books need special attention. They may have been expensed instead of capitalised, buried inside one big project entry, moved in from another unit without a transfer entry, or they may belong to someone else. Keep third-party assets separate. Whether any of them should be recognised, for example as a right-of-use asset, depends on the contract and the accounting standards, and that call belongs to management. Finding a difference does not decide what happens to it. Whether an asset is capitalised, written off or removed is management’s decision, based on evidence. And that treatment is exactly what clause 3(i)(b) asks the auditor to report on. 7. Agree the reports and a realistic timeline Ask to see sample reports before you appoint the agency, not after the fieldwork is done. Make sure you will get the raw verification data too, not just a summary. Indicative timelines for physical verification How long it takes depends on the number and type of assets, the number of locations, the team size, the state of the register and site access. As a rough guide, one site with 500 to 2,000 assets may take 3 to 7 working days on the ground. A multi-location job with 10,000 or more assets may take 3 to 8 weeks. Plan it in stages: register clean-up, fieldwork, reconciliation, management review and final report. The stage that usually takes longest is the review. When assets are reported as not found, companies understandably search hard for them before writing anything off, and that search can easily run for a month. 8. Agree what “complete” means Companies and agencies often have very different ideas of when a job is finished. Write it down: which reports, which sign-offs, how many days for management to review, how many rounds of reconciliation and repeat visits are included. Also separate the differences the agency has reported from those still open because the company has not yet sent information or documents. Without this, jobs drag on for months and both sides lose. 9. Compare quotations like for like Pricing can be per asset, per man-day, per location, a fixed fee, or a mix. Check whether each quote includes tags, manpower, travel and stay, reconciliation, reports and software access. Check how extra assets, repeat visits and locations that cannot be accessed will be charged. A lower rate per asset does not mean a lower final bill if important work has been left out. Getting these points settled before the first team reaches site does more for the result than anything done during the fieldwork itself.

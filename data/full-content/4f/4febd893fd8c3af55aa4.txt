(Supreme Court of India) Summary: The Supreme Court considered appeals arising from the Madras High Court’s judgment dated 18 August 2020 in P.R. Jagannathan Vs Government of Tamil Nadu, concerning acquisition of privately owned land for expansion of Coimbatore Airport and disputes over compensation, unpaid lease rent and statutory interest. The Supreme Court’s supplied operative order records that Justice M.M. Sundresh pronounced a reportable judgment on behalf of the Bench comprising himself and Justice Nongmeikapam Kotiswar Singh. Leave was granted, the appeals were allowed in terms of the signed reportable judgment, and pending applications were disposed of. The supplied operative order does not disclose the Supreme Court’s reasoning or identify which findings and directions of the High Court were reversed or modified. Accordingly, the High Court’s findings discussed below provide the background to the appeals and should not be presented as findings affirmed by the Supreme Court. Read HC Judgment in this case: Landowners Entitled to 9% Interest on Delayed Airport Acquisition Compensation: Madras HC The underlying dispute originated in the acquisition of lands situated in Singanallur and Kalapatti villages of Coimbatore District. The predecessors of the landowners had leased their properties to the Defence Department under an agreement dated 8 August 1942. The lands were subsequently used for civil aviation purposes, with the Airport Authority of India continuing to occupy them. The landowners complained that substantial lease rent remained unpaid and that the authorities had neither completed compensation proceedings nor adequately addressed their grievances. The acquisition was undertaken for expansion of the Coimbatore Airport runway under the Tamil Nadu Acquisition of Land for Industrial Purposes Act, 1997. Statutory notices were issued in April 2011, followed by acquisition notifications in 2013 and 2015. The prolonged delay resulted in several writ petitions before the Madras High Court. The landowners sought payment of outstanding lease rent, determination and disbursement of acquisition compensation, and relief against continued occupation without payment. Certain petitioners also challenged acquisition-related actions and construction restrictions. During the litigation, interim orders restrained the authorities from using particular lands or undertaking construction. The High Court repeatedly examined the progress of compensation proceedings and required the State Government and aviation authorities to explain the delay. The litigation therefore involved competing considerations of private property rights, statutory compensation obligations and the public interest in expanding airport infrastructure. An important development was the negotiation of compensation between the Government and affected landowners. The authorities initially offered lower rates, which were subsequently increased. Negotiations culminated in a settlement dated 6 March 2018, under which compensation rates of ₹900 per square foot for rural lands and ₹1,500 per square foot for urban lands were accepted by several landowners. However, disputes persisted regarding whether the agreed compensation included all statutory benefits, whether additional solatium could be claimed, and whether interest was payable because the Government had delayed disbursement despite concluding negotiations. In its judgment dated 18 August 2020, the Madras High Court examined Sections 3, 4, 7 and 12 of the 1997 Act. It distinguished compensation settled through agreement under Section 7(2) from compensation determined by the Collector under Section 7(3). The High Court concluded that landowners who voluntarily accepted the negotiated compensation package were bound by its terms. According to the High Court, the agreed rates constituted a comprehensive settlement of compensation claims, and consenting landowners could not subsequently reopen the arrangement merely to demand separate solatium or additional compensation components. The High Court nevertheless recognised that agreement on compensation did not eliminate the consequences of delayed payment. Referring to Section 12 of the 1997 Act, which provides interest at 9% per annum where compensation remains unpaid or undeposited after possession is taken, it held that statutory interest was payable subject to appropriate adjustments. These included deductions for lease rent already paid for overlapping periods and exclusion of periods attributable to interim restraints obtained by landowners. The High Court considered the Government’s failure to release compensation within a reasonable period after the March 2018 settlement relevant to determining the landowners’ entitlement. The High Court also addressed claims raised by landowners who had not accepted the negotiated arrangements. Some sought passage through acquired airport land to reach their remaining properties. The Court declined to make such access a condition for receiving compensation, considering the security implications of creating an additional passage through airport boundaries. Other landowners demanded compensation of ₹3,000 per square foot. The High Court permitted those unwilling to accept the negotiated rates to pursue statutory determination before the Collector under Section 7(3), rather than compelling them to enter the settlement. The High Court ultimately directed consenting landowners to produce ownership documents and execute the necessary agreements before the Collector. It required disbursement of the compensation within one month and directed the release of money deposited before the Registrar General to the State Government for payment in accordance with its directions. The writ petitions were disposed of without costs. These directions formed part of the judgment subsequently brought before the Supreme Court by the Government of Tamil Nadu and other appellants. Before the Supreme Court, the matter was placed before a Bench comprising Justice M.M. Sundresh and Justice Nongmeikapam Kotiswar Singh. The supplied order records that Justice Sundresh pronounced the Bench’s reportable judgment. The Supreme Court granted leave and allowed the appeals in terms of the signed reportable judgment. It also disposed of all pending applications. The available operative order establishes that the appeals succeeded, but does not explain the grounds for allowing them. In particular, it does not disclose the Supreme Court’s conclusions concerning negotiated compensation, solatium, statutory interest, lease-rent adjustments, the effect of interim orders or the directions governing payment. Consequently, the Supreme Court’s decision must be distinguished from the reasoning recorded in the related Madras High Court judgment. Although the High Court had recognised entitlement to statutory interest and treated consensual compensation as a binding package, the supplied Supreme Court order does not establish whether those conclusions were rejected, modified or addressed on different grounds. The precise legal effect of allowing the appeals, including the continuing enforceability of individual High Court directions, can be determined only from the signed reportable judgment. The confirmed outcome is that the Government’s appeals were allowed, while the substantive reasoning and operative modifications remain unavailable from the supplied order.

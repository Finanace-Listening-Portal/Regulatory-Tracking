Summary: The loss of historical Structured Digital Database (SDD) records following software corruption, hardware failure or server crashes can expose companies to regulatory non-compliance under the SEBI (Prohibition of Insider Trading) Regulations, 2015. Regulations 3(5) and 3(6) require maintenance of records relating to unpublished price sensitive information (UPSI), supported by timestamps and audit trails, and their preservation for at least eight years after completion of the relevant transactions. The article examines practical responses to SDD failures through examples involving Indo US Bio-Tech Limited, PDS Limited, Winsol Engineers Limited, Arvind Port and Infra Limited, Aesthetik Engineers Limited and Aakaar Medical Technologies Limited. Companies should document technical failures, obtain root-cause analysis, examine available backups and reconstruct missing records using reliable documentary evidence wherever possible. However, management certifications regarding original timely entries cannot substitute the prescribed system-generated audit trails or automatically rectify historical non-compliance. Where records cannot be recovered, companies should document the extent of data loss and evaluate the regulatory consequences. Reportable cybersecurity incidents must also be assessed under the applicable CERT-In reporting requirements. The matter should be escalated to the appropriate Audit Committee or Risk Management Committee, considering applicable PIT and LODR Regulations. SEBI’s revised PIT FAQs dated November 5, 2026, reinforce that using third-party software or cloud infrastructure does not transfer regulatory accountability from the Board, heads of the organisation or Compliance Officer. Companies should therefore strengthen their backup arrangements, data security, recovery procedures, vendor agreements and internal controls to prevent recurrence and demonstrate continuing regulatory compliance. Loss of historical records may therefore constitute non-compliance even where entries were originally maintained. However, a temporary outage, recoverable data loss and permanent destruction of records present different levels of risk. Responsibility for SDD compliance remains with the Board or heads of the organisation and the Compliance Officer, within their respective obligations, even where third-party software is used[iii]. PIT regulations provide no automatic relaxation for technical failures. Radhika Varade-Deputy Manager What Is Recommended When SDD Data Is Lost? The response to an SDD failure should begin with identifying its cause, followed by efforts to recover the records, assess the compliance implications and prevent recurrence. Stock exchange disclosures provide useful examples at each stage: 1. Document the Incident and Establish Its Cause The first step is to identify whether the failure involves a temporary malfunction, database corruption or complete server failure. The company should document its cause, duration, affected records and extent of data loss, supported by system logs and a written root-cause analysis from the IT team or vendor. Indo US Bio-Tech Limited, in its disclosure dated 21 July 2026, reported that a technical issue had made certain historical SDD records unavailable and sought a root-cause analysis from its vendor. PDS Limited, in its disclosure dated 20 July 2026, reported hardware failure resulting in loss of data for November 2025 to June 2026, including SDD records. Despite engaging independent recovery specialists, the data could not be recovered. Where the incident involves a reportable cyberattack, such as ransomware or malware, the company must also examine its obligations under the CERT-In Directions issued under Section 70B(6) of the Information Technology Act, 2000, including reporting within six hours of noticing or being informed of the incident. 2. Examine Backups and Reconstruct Available Records Once the extent of the failure is established, the next step is to examine whether earlier SDD reports, database backups or exported records are available to restore or re-enter information into a new system. Winsol Engineers Limited and Arvind Port and Infra Limited, in their disclosures dated 26 May 2025, reported software corruption and data loss. Both migrated to new software in January 2025, retained earlier SDD reports containing records up to 30 September 2024, re-entered available records from 1 October 2024 and obtained software audits covering entries up to 31 March 2025. Where no SDD reports or backups are available, reconstruction may be attempted using emails, UPSI-sharing correspondence, Board records, transaction documents and records maintained by relevant departments or fiduciaries. Entries should be supported by verifiable evidence identifying the UPSI, persons sharing and receiving it, and the actual date of communication. Aesthetik Engineers Limited, in its FY 2024–25 Secretarial Audit Report, reported software corruption and re-entry of available records for 16 August 2024 to 31 March 2025. Its Secretarial Auditor relied on management certification regarding the original timely entries, although the disclosure did not specify the documents used for reconstruction. Where reliable reconstruction is not possible, the company should document the missing records rather than create unsupported entries. 3. Assess the Extent of Non-Compliance Where Records Cannot Be Recovered Even after reconstruction, the company must determine whether the original timestamps and audit trails are available. In the cases of Winsol, Arvind and Aesthetik, the certifying Practising Company Secretaries relied on management certifications regarding timely original entries. Such certification, however, cannot by itself replace the original system-generated audit trails required under Regulation 3(5) and would not rectify non – compliance. Where records are permanently lost, the company may be unable to demonstrate historical compliance with Regulation 3(5) and may face non-compliance with the preservation requirement under Regulation 3(6), even if the entries were originally maintained correctly. 4. Escalate the Incident and Assess Disclosure Requirements Under regulation 9A(4) of PIT regulations prescribes audit committee/ such analogous body of intermediary or fiduciary shall review the compliance with regulations and verify the systems of internal control. Under the SEBI (Listing Obligations and Disclosure Requirements) Regulations, 2015 (LODR Regulations), the Audit Committee oversees matters relating to internal controls, while the Risk Management Committee, where applicable, oversees risk management, including information and cybersecurity risks. Therefore, place the matter before relevant committee in compliance with applicable SEBI regulations. Aakaar Medical Technologies Limited, in its disclosure dated 9 May 2026, reported a temporary technical issue without data loss. All 52 UPSI events were captured, and the matter was placed before the Audit Committee. Conclusion Once the immediate incident and its compliance implications have been addressed, the company should review whether its existing safeguards are sufficient to prevent similar failures. Vendor agreements should specify responsibilities concerning data security, backup frequency, recovery timelines, incident reporting and regulatory cooperation.

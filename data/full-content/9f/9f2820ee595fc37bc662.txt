Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); Expedia Online Travel Services India Private Limited Vs Commissioner of Central Goods and Services Tax (GSTAT Delhi) Summary : The Goods and Services Tax Appellate Tribunal (GSTAT), Principal Bench, New Delhi, held that business support and software development services provided by Expedia Online Travel Services India Private Limited to its overseas group companies qualify as exports of services and not intermediary services under Section 2(13) of the IGST Act, 2017. The Division Bench comprising Justice Dr. Sanjaya Kumar Mishra, President, and Anil Kumar Gupta, Technical Member, allowed seven appeals concerning financial years 2019-20 to 2022-23 and set aside the rejection of unutilised Input Tax Credit (ITC) refund claims aggregating ₹55,99,74,202. The Tribunal directed the Revenue to refund the credit along with interest under Section 56 of the CGST Act, 2017. Expedia India, an indirectly wholly owned subsidiary of Expedia Inc., USA, provided contract software development and various business support services to overseas Expedia group entities under agreements executed in 2010 and 2016. These services included software development, supply sourcing support, supervision of third-party call centres, human resource support and product support. The company rendered the services on a cost-plus-mark-up basis and treated them as zero-rated exports under Section 16 of the IGST Act. However, the tax authorities rejected its refund applications on the ground that the services constituted intermediary services, with the place of supply in India. The appellate authorities upheld these rejections, reasoning that Expedia India facilitated transactions between overseas group companies and their customers. The Tribunal first rejected the Revenue’s objection concerning the jurisdiction of the Principal Bench. It held that the classification of services as intermediary services or exports necessarily involved determination of the place of supply. Consequently, the dispute fell within the exclusive jurisdiction of the Principal Bench under Section 109(5) of the CGST Act. Examining Section 2(13) of the IGST Act and CBIC Circular No. 159/15/2021-GST, the Tribunal explained that an intermediary ordinarily arranges or facilitates a separate supply between other parties. A person providing services independently on a principal-to-principal basis does not become an intermediary merely because those services support the business activities of a foreign customer. Relying upon its earlier decision in Navitas Eduservices Private Limited and judicial precedents including Genpact India, Columbia Sportswear India Sourcing, Blackberry India and FATEH Education Consulting, the Tribunal found that Expedia India was neither a broker nor an agent of its overseas group companies. The agreements established an independent contractual relationship, without authorising Expedia India to bind the foreign entities. The Tribunal also considered the Directorate General of GST Intelligence’s conclusion dated 21 May 2026 concerning the classification of Expedia India’s services. Although it clarified that the CBIC circular and DGGI findings were not binding upon its adjudicatory powers, the Tribunal considered them supportive of its conclusion. The Tribunal further held that Expedia India and its overseas parent company were separately incorporated legal entities and could not be treated as mere establishments of the same person for denying export status. It concluded that the relevant services satisfied the requirements of Section 2(6) of the IGST Act. Regarding one refund claim, the Tribunal also found that objections concerning Bank Realisation Certificate mismatches, non-reflection of invoices in GSTR-2A and the alleged absence of business nexus had not been adequately specified or supported by findings. The rejection on those grounds violated principles of natural justice. Accordingly, the Tribunal set aside the adjudication and first appellate orders and directed refund of the unutilised ITC with statutory interest. For our purpose, the 109(5) of the CGST Act, 2017, is of utmost important which is quoted below: – (5) The Principal Bench and the State Bench shall hear appeals against the orders passed by the Appellate Authority or the Revisional Authority: Provided that the cases in which any one of the issues involved relates to the place of supply, shall be heard only by the Principal Bench. Provided further that the matters referred to in sub-section (2) of section 171 shall be examined or adjudicated only by the Principal Bench: Provided also that the Government may, on the recommendations of the Council, notify other cases or class of cases which shall be heard only by the Principal Bench.] 16. In order to properly understand this Provision, we also have to take into consideration the definition of Place of Supply. Section 2(86) of the CGST Act, 2017, provides for place of supply and it lays down that “place of supply” means as referred in Chapter V of the IGST Act, 2017. The Chapter V of the IGST Act, 2017, provides place of supply for goods and services or both. Section 10 of the IGST Act, 2017, provides for place of supply of goods other than supply of goods imported into, or exported from India, which is not relevant for this case. Section 11 of the IGST Act, 2017, provides for place of supply of goods imported into, or exported from India and it has been laid down that (a) imported into India shall be the location of the importer; (b) exported from India shall be the location outside India. Section 12 of the IGST Act, 2017, provides for place of supply of services where location of supplier and recipient is in India. Section 13 of the IGST Act, 2017, provides for place of supply of services where location of supplier or location of recipient is outside India. Section 13 (8) of the IGST Act, 2017, is relevant for us in this case and it provides that the place of supply of the services mentioned therein from Clause (a) to Clause (d) (Clause (b) having been omitted by Finance Act, 2026 dated 30.03.2026). the place of supply shall be the location of the supplier of services. 17. Thus, a harmonious reading of all these Provisions leads to the conclusion that in case, a service provider who exports services outside India shall not have any GST liability on the payment received by for such recipients unless it is treated or found to be an Intermediary service provider. Section 2(13) of the IGST Act, 2017, lays down the definition of intermediary which reads as follows: – “intermediary” means a broker, an agent or any other person, by whatever name called, who arranges or facilitates the supply of goods or services or both, or securities, between two or more persons, but does not include a person who supplies such goods or services or both or securities on his own account; A carful and proper understanding of this definition lead us to hold that intermediary as Section 2(13) of the IGST Act, 2017, is a person: – ‘Intermediary’ – Section 2(13) of the IGST Act, 2017, I a. A broker, or b. an agent, or c. any other person, by whatever name called; II An Intermediary as above arranges or facilitates the supply of a. Good, or b. Services, or c. Both, or d. Securities III Such services as mentioned above is between two more person. IV It does not include goods and services, or both, or securities supplied on his own account. 18. Export of Services have been defined under Section 2(6) of the IGST Act, 2017, which reads as follow: – (6) “export of services” means the supply of any service when, – i. the supplier of service is located in India; ii. the recipient of service is located outside India; iii. the place of supply of service is outside India; iv. the payment for such service has been received by the supplier of service in convertible foreign exchange 1[or in Indian rupees wherever permitted by the Reserve Bank of India]; and v. the supplier of service and the recipient of service are not merely establishments of a distinct person in accordance with Explanation 1 in section -8; 19. Thus, in order to decide the case, whether supplier has been made in India or outside India and he doesn’t come under the definition of intermediary service provider, then only the issue of refund of tax can be properly decided. Though, the main issue may be whether the service provided by the Appellant to its parent company is in the nature of intermediary service or export, it does involve the question of place of supply as discussed above. Thus, the contention raised by the Revenue that this case does not pertain to the Jurisdiction of Principal Bench is not relevant and is hereby not accepted. 20. The seminal question that arises in this case and which is in fact the genesis of the entire tax dispute is, ‘whether the services provided by the Appellant to the parent company is intermediary service or export?’ In a recently decided case, viz., M/S NAVITAS EDUSERVICES PRIVATE LIMITED, IN APPEAL NOS. APL/23/PB/2026 along with other appeals, decided on 14.10.2026, we have held that education services supplied by the Appellant, i.e., Navitas Eduservices Pvt Ltd., India to Navitas Australia are export. After taking into consideration, the Judgment render by the Punjab and Haryana High Court in Genpact India Vs. Union of India; (2022) 1 Centax 226 (P&H), 2022 SCC OnLine P&H 4254, Hon’ble Karnataka High court in the case of Columbia Sportwear India Sourcing (P.) Ltd. v. Union of India, [2025] 174 taxmann.com 936 (Karnataka), 2025 SCC OnLine Kar 3484, Hon’ble High Court of Delhi in the case of Pr. Commissioner, Central Excise and CGST – Delhi South v. Blackberry India Private Limited,2023 SCC OnLine Del 4467, we have come to the conclusion that Navitas India Ltd., is not intermediary rather it is an exporter of services. 21. In that case, we have also considered the circular issued by CBIC bearing No. 159/15/2021-GST dated 28.09.2021, while considering in the matter of M/s. Dow Chemical International Pvt, Ltd. Vs. Commissioner of State Tax, Mazgaon, Mumbai, (2026) 1 GSTAT E- Journal 15 (Principal Bench). We take note of our observation made in para 33 of M/S Navitas Edu services Private Limited dated 14.10.2026, and reproduced it as follows: – “33. The CBIC circular further elucidates that upon perusal of the definition of the “intermediary” under IGST Act as well as under Service Tax Law, it is evident that there is broadly no change in the scope of intermediary services in the GST regime vis-à-vis the Service Tax regime, except addition of supply of securities in the definition of intermediary in the GST Law. The concept of intermediary services, requires basic requisites, which are discussed below: – i. Minimum of Three Parties: an intermediary is someone who arranges or facilitates the supply of goods or services or securities between two or more persons. It is, thus, a natural corollary that the arrangement requires a minimum of three parties, two of them transacting in the supply of goods or services or securities (the main supply) and one arranging or facilitating (the ancillary supply) the said main supply. An activity between only two parties can, therefore, NOT be considered as an intermediary service. It essentially “arranges or facilitates” another supply (the “main supply”) between two or more other persons and does not himself provide the main supply. ii. Two distinct supplies: there are two distinct supplies in case of provision of intermediary services; 1. Main supply, between the two principals, which can be a supply of goods or services or securities; 2. Ancillary supply, which is the service of facilitating or arranging the main supply between the two principals. This ancillary supply is supply of intermediary service and is clearly identifiable and distinguished from the main supply. iii. Thus, a person involved in the main supply on principal-to-principal basis to another person cannot be considered as a supplier of intermediary service. The definition of “intermediary” itself provides that intermediary service provider means a broker, an agent or any other person, by whatever name called….”. Thus, the role of intermediary is only supportive. In this case, admittedly, the Appellant is not as a broker or an agent. There is no dispute that he cannot act as broker or as an agent as he doesn’t have authority to bind the principal i.e. Navitas Australia. The only question that remains to be seen is whether he may be ‘any other person by whatever name called’. In this connection we have already taken a view in Dow Chemicals that interpretation of ‘any other person’ has to governed by principles of ‘ejusdem generis’ and it cannot be applied to any person or party who does not have the characteristics of a broker or an agent, having the authority to buying its principal and to act on behalf of the principal. iv. “Intermediary” does not include a person who supplies such goods or services or both or securities on his own account”. In this case it is admitted that Navitas India has the responsibility or duty to supply any services to Navitas Australia on his own account. v. The Circular clarify that Sub-contracting for a service is not an intermediary service, it is an important exclusion from intermediary. Navitas India provides service as sub-contractor. It provides the main supply and does not merely arrange or facilitate the main supply between the principal supplier and his customers, and therefore, clearly is not an intermediary. In fact, the notification of the CBIC provides an example, for the purpose of clarity, we are quoting the same: – “For instance, ‘A’ and ‘B’ have entered into a contract as per which ‘A’ needs to provide a service of, say, Annual Maintenance of tools and machinery to ‘B’. ‘A’ subcontracts a part or whole of it to ‘C’. Accordingly, ‘C’ provides the service of annual maintenance to ‘A’ as part of such subcontract, by providing annual maintenance of tools and machinery to the customer of ‘A’, i.e. to ‘B’ on behalf of ‘A’. Though ‘C’ is dealing with the customer of ‘A’, but ‘C’ is providing main supply of Annual Maintenance Service to ‘A’ on his own account, i.e. on principal-to-principal basis. In this case, ‘A’ is providing supply of Annual Maintenance Service to ‘B’, whereas ‘C’ is supplying the same service to ‘A’. Thus, supply of service by ‘C’ in this case will not be considered as an intermediary. This illustration fortifies the case in the sense that the Appellant is indulged in the main supply. vi. The notification further clarifies that a specific provision of place of supply of ‘intermediary services’ under section 13 of the IGST Act shall be invoked only when either the location of supplier of intermediary services or location of the recipient of intermediary services is outside India. 22. In the said decision, this Court held that where the Indian entity renders educational consultancy/marketing services to foreign universities, raises invoices upon such foreign universities, and receives consideration from them, such services would not constitute intermediary services merely because students in India are incidentally assisted in the process. 16. The said view is consistent with the judgment of the Bombay High Court in KC Overseas Education (P.) Ltd. (supra), wherein, in materially similar circumstances, it was held that the assessee was providing services to universities located outside India, which were paying consideration to the assessee, and that the assessee could not be treated as providing services to students in India merely by recommending their names to foreign universities. The Bombay High Court accordingly held that such services would not fall within the definition of “intermediary” and that the assessee would be entitled to refund, subject to receipt of consideration in foreign currency. The Special Leave Petitions preferred by the Union of India against the said judgment were dismissed by the Hon’ble Supreme Court, having regard to the judgment in CST v. Vodafone India Ltd [Civil Appeal Nos.10815-10819 of 2014, dated 6-5-2025] and the order in Commissioner, Central Excise, CGST-Delhi South Commissionerate v. Blackberry India (P) Ltd [SLP (C) No. 25992 of 2024, dated 4-11-2024]. 17. In Global Opportunities Private Limited, this Court also considered the line of judgments including Ernst & Young Ltd v. Additional Commissioner, Central GST Appeals-II [2023] 148 com 461/97 GST 820/73 GSTL 161 (Delhi), KC Overseas Education (P.) Ltd. (Supra), Vodafone India Ltd (supra), Blackberry India (P) Ltd (Supra)’. and other decisions, and held that a person who supplies services on its own account cannot be treated as an intermediary merely because such services may facilitate or further the business objective of the foreign recipient. The determinative factor is not the place where the incidental beneficiary may be located, but the contractual recipient of the service, the person liable to pay consideration, and the nature of the service supplied. This Court held that the respondent therein was engaged by foreign educational institutions for providing consultancy services and, upon rendering such services, received consideration from the said foreign universities and such relationship could not be characterised as intermediary services. 18. In the present case also, the petitioner has entered into agreements with foreign universities for providing education consultancy, marketing and recruitment support services. The impugned order itself proceeds on the basis that the petitioner promotes courses of the foreign university, identifies prospective students, assists in the recruitment/admission process and receives commission from the foreign university. Furthermore, the petitioner does not charge students for such services, has no authority to bind the foreign university, cannot guarantee admission, and receives consideration from the foreign university. The factual matrix, therefore, is materially similar to that considered by this Court in Global Opportunities (P) Ltd (supra) and by the Bombay High Court in KC Overseas Education (P.) Ltd. (supra). 19. It is also relevant that Ms. Anushree Narain, learned senior standing counsel appearing for the respondents, while adopting the submissions advanced by the Department in Global Opportunities Private Limited, has also not disputed that the nature of services rendered by the petitioner in the present case is materially similar to the services considered by this Court in Global Opportunities (P) Ltd (supra). Thus, even upon considering the submissions adopted on behalf of the Department, the controversy remains squarely covered by the judgment of this Court. In view of the settled position of law and its application to the facts of the present case, the impugned order dated 30.10.2025, which rejects the refund claim by treating the petitioner as an intermediary, cannot be sustained. 20. Accordingly, the impugned order dated 30.10.2025 is set aside. The refund shall be processed and granted to the petitioner along with applicable statutory interest, in accordance with law, within a period of two months.” 23. In course of hearing, learned Counsel appearing for the Appellant would also rely upon the Minutes of Conclusion of proceeding against Expedia Online Travel Services India Pvt. Ltd., it appears from the documents dated 21.05.2026 that Additional Director, DGGI, Ludhiana Zonal Unit have concluded the proceeding under proviso to Section 78 of Finance Act, 1994 read with para 2.5 of CBEC letter dated 18.08.2015. The detailed conclusion of proceedings is attached thereto. The investigation led to the conclusion that out of the total eight services provided by the Expedia India, viz., Lodging Partner Supply Support, EAN Marketing Support Services, HR Support Services, Supply of Support Services, Supervisory Support Services, Product Support services, Marketing Support Services, Contract Software Development Services. Five qualify as export. Lodging Partner Supply Support, EAN Marketing Support Services, HR Support Services does not qualify as export. The DGGI itself has communicated to Appellant that its services are export and not intermediary. It is not the case of the Revenue that the present case involves Lodging Partner Supply Support, EAN Marketing Support Services, HR Support Service. 24. Though, the circular issued by the CBIC or report of conclusion by DGGI do nothave a bind ing effect on adjudicating powers of the Tribunal, we have referred the aforesaid documents only to understand the issue in a clear manner and come to the conclusion that in fact CBIC circular and the conclusion of proceeding of DGGI buttresses our opinion and conclusion as given above. 25. In applying this principle to the case in hand, we further reiterate that as per the definition of intermediary under Section 2(13) IGST Act, 2017, it can be seen that the Appellant and its parent company have entered into agreement on principal-to-principal basis. There is no agency between them and Appellant is not acting as a broker for the parent company. There is no provision in the entire agreement which creates any obligation on the foreign company, undertaken by the Appellant, as we have held in Navitas India (Supra), that expression any person has to understood in the light of the doctrine of ‘ejusdem generis’, i.e., birds of same feather flock together and it cannot be treated to be including any person, by whatever name, to include a person who is not able to bind the foreign company with an obligation or a duty, acting on its behalf. 25. Though it facilitates the supplier service i.e. travel, etc., there is no privity of contract between a third party and the Appellant. The contract is strictly between the Appellant and the foreign Company. As far as the customers of the parent company are concerned, it shall be contracts between those two entities and present Appellant has nothing to do with it. It also does not include supply of goods, or services, or both, or security on his own account. 26. A quick reference to the definition of export services as mentioned in Section 2(6) of the IGST Act, 2017, also leads to the conclusion that supplier is located inside India. The recipient is located outside India. The place of supply as discussed earlier shall be outside India and the payment for such services has been received by the supplier of services on convertible foreign exchange, etc., and supply of services and recipient of services not merely establishment of distinct person as mentioned in Explanation of Section 8 of the IGST Act, 2017. Section 8 of the IGST Act, 2017, provides for Intra-State Supply, the explanation-1 of Section 8 of the IGST Act, 2017, provides: – Explanation 1.-For the purposes of this Act, where a person has, – i. an establishment in India and any other establishment outside India; ii. an establishment in a State or Union territory and any other establishment outside that State or Union territory; or iii. an establishment in a State or Union territory and any other establishment1[*****] registered within that State or Union territory, then such establishments shall be treated as establishments of distinct persons. However, this expression does not exclude the contract between parent company and subsidiary company in the sense that both the foreign company and the India subsidiary are registered companies, incorporated under the relevant laws guiding the registration as well as business, functioning, etc., of incorporated companies. It is settled principle of law that each individual company, incorporated, shall be a separate entity. Thus, it is clear that the services provided by the Appellant does not come under the definition of intermediary services rather it is an export services, and, therefore, needs to be meritorious and needs to be allowed. 28. As far as additional ground to reject the refund application, as involved in APL/15/PB/2026, we find that the Show Cause Notice has not specified the BRC mismatch, GSTR 2A not reflecting the details of the invoices and the kind of nexus that is lacking in certain inputs of the business. It was stated that the BRC are mismatching, however, it is not brought to our notice at this stage of second appeal, that how the same are not matching. It may also be stated here that this issue was totally ignored by the 1st Appellate Authority and there is no finding on this quote. Similarly, there is no specification regarding GSTR 2A lacking invoices. 29. Thus, it is clear that SCN was not a proper one in the sense that the grounds for rejecting the refund application on different scores has not been elaborated and specifications have not been communicated to the Appellant. Moreover, these issues were discussed and involved in the proceedings before the Authorized Officer and FAA and there is hardly any discussion on these issues no findings have been recorded thereon. 30. Hence, we are of the opinion that this is a case of not only travelling beyond the gamut of the SCN but is also of violation of the principles of natural justice and, therefore, impugned orders are not sustainable. 31. In view of the aforesaid discussion and reasoning, we come to the definite conclusion that the services provided by the Appellant i.e. Expedia India do not come within the purview of intermediary services rather it comes within the definition of export services under Section 2(6) of the IGST Act, 2017. Therefore, the learned Authorized Officer and the learned 1st Appellate Authority have committed errors on records by dismissing the claims of the Appellant for refund. Accordingly, the orders passed by the Adjudicating Authority and 1st Appellate Authority are set aside.

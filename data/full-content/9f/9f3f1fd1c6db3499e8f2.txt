The assessment could give the RBI more room to allow the rupee to adjust to external pressures while continuing to intervene to smooth excessive volatility rather than defend a particular exchange-rate level.In a post Monetary Policy press conference, RBI governor Sanjay Malhotra said that by a number of estimates, including the REER (real effective exchange rate), rupee is not overvalued, may be undervalued. Advt “We will ensure that the rupee stabilises, that the rupee finds its correct value, and we will support an orderly movement of the rupee in finding its correct value and at the same time in ensuring that there is no excessive volatility,” he said.The rupee touched 96.8450 against the US dollar on Wednesday, close to its record low of 96.96, before ending at 96.7750. The currency has been under pressure from foreign portfolio outflows, elevated oil prices, higher US Treasury yields and a stronger dollar. The RBI's assessment is based partly on measures such as the real effective exchange rate, which adjusts the rupee's value against a basket of trading-partner currencies for differences in inflation.What does an undervalued rupee mean?A currency is generally described as undervalued when its market exchange rate is weaker than the level suggested by certain measures of its value against trading partners and domestic price conditions. The REER is one such measure, but it does not establish a single exchange rate at which the rupee should trade.The distinction matters because a fall in the rupee against the dollar does not by itself establish that the currency is fundamentally weak. The dollar's global strength, capital flows, oil prices, interest-rate differentials and changes in the external balance can all influence the market exchange rate. Advt The RBI's assessment of the rupee as potentially undervalued does not mean it will stop intervening in the foreign-exchange market. The central bank has been using spot dollar sales and foreign-exchange swaps to manage periods of pressure and smooth excessive movements in the currency.India's foreign-exchange reserves stood at $734.6 billion in the week ended October 2, down from $785.71 billion on September 4. The decline reflects movements in reserves including foreign-exchange intervention and valuation changes. The size of the reserves nevertheless gives the RBI substantial resources to manage disorderly market conditions. Advt The policy distinction is important. Allowing the rupee to adjust does not require the RBI to tolerate abrupt or destabilising moves. The central bank can intervene when market conditions become disorderly while allowing the exchange rate to respond to changes in capital flows, oil prices and global financial conditions.Weaker rupee can improve export competitivenessA weaker rupee can improve the price competitiveness of Indian exports because foreign-currency revenues translate into more rupees and Indian goods and services can become relatively cheaper for overseas buyers.The benefit, however, varies across sectors. Exporters that rely heavily on imported raw materials, components or energy also face higher costs when the rupee weakens. India's dependence on imported crude oil makes this trade-off particularly important because a weaker rupee increases the domestic cost of dollar-denominated energy imports. Advt An undervalued currency can therefore support external competitiveness, but the benefit depends on the domestic value added in exports and the extent to which exporters depend on imported inputs.Currency depreciation can increase the rupee cost of imported goods and commodities, creating a potential channel for imported inflation. This is particularly relevant when international oil and commodity prices are already elevated.That creates a policy trade-off for the RBI. Allowing the rupee to adjust can help maintain external competitiveness and absorb changes in global financial conditions, but excessive depreciation can increase imported inflation and raise the cost of servicing foreign-currency liabilities.What it means for the rupeeThe RBI's assessment that the rupee may be undervalued should therefore not be read as a signal that the central bank wants a weaker currency or will actively push the rupee higher. Rather, it suggests that the RBI has room to allow the exchange rate to find its underlying value while intervening when movements become excessive or disorderly.The rupee's direction will continue to depend on oil prices, foreign capital flows, the global dollar, interest-rate differentials and India's external balance. For exporters, a relatively weak rupee can provide a competitiveness advantage, but higher imported input costs can offset part of that benefit.The immediate policy implication is therefore likely to be continued management of volatility rather than defence of a specific rupee level. The RBI's assessment of potential undervaluation gives it greater flexibility to allow market forces to determine the exchange rate while retaining intervention as a tool against disorderly movements.

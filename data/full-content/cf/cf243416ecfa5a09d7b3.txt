Vs San Commodities LLP (NCLT Cuttack) Summary: The National Company Law Tribunal, Cuttack Bench, in RKD Constructions Pvt. Ltd. Vs San Commodities LLP, admitted an application under Section 9 of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016, seeking initiation of the Corporate Insolvency Resolution Process (CIRP) against San Commodities LLP for an unpaid operational debt of ₹3.85 crore. RKD Constructions, engaged in infrastructure development, had placed purchase orders dated 10 January 2023 and 27 March 2023 for the supply of TMT bars to its construction project sites. The operational creditor paid advances aggregating to ₹7,42,46,430, against which the corporate debtor supplied materials worth ₹3,41,54,147. The remaining materials were not supplied, leaving an unadjusted advance of approximately ₹4.01 crore. Following negotiations, the corporate debtor issued two cheques dated 16 April 2024 for ₹1.10 crore and ₹2.75 crore towards repayment. Both cheques were dishonoured on 17 April 2024, with the respective endorsements “Payment stopped by drawer” and “Exceeds arrangement”. The operational creditor initiated proceedings under Section 138 of the Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881, and subsequently issued a statutory demand notice dated 19 February 2026 under Section 8 of the IBC. The corporate debtor neither repaid the outstanding amount nor raised any dispute in response to the demand notice. An application under Section 9 was filed on 8 April 2026, supported by purchase orders, bank statements, cheque dishonour memos and an affidavit confirming that no notice of dispute had been received. Despite service of notice in the insolvency proceedings and several opportunities to appear, the corporate debtor failed to submit a reply or contest the claim. Consequently, the Tribunal proceeded ex parte against it on 4 August 2026. The principal question before the Tribunal was whether an advance paid by a purchaser to a supplier for goods that were never delivered could constitute an operational debt within the meaning of Section 5(21) of the IBC. Relying upon the Supreme Court’s decision in Consolidated Construction Consortium Limited v. Hitro Energy Solutions Private Limited, (2022) 7 SCC 164, the Tribunal held that the expression “in respect of the provision of goods or services” must receive a broad and purposive interpretation. The definition does not require the operational creditor to be the supplier of goods or services. A purchaser who advances money for goods or services may also qualify as an operational creditor where the advance remains unrefunded following non-supply. The Tribunal also relied upon the NCLAT decision in Chipsan Aviation Private Limited v. Punj Lloyd Aviation Limited, which recognised unrefunded advances paid for goods or services as operational debt. Since the advance in the present case was directly connected with the procurement of construction materials, the claim satisfied Section 5(21). On the question of default and limitation, the Tribunal found that the two cheques issued by the corporate debtor towards repayment constituted contemporaneous documentary evidence acknowledging the outstanding liability. Their dishonour established default in payment of ₹3.85 crore, exceeding the statutory minimum threshold of ₹1 crore prescribed under Section 4 of the IBC. The Tribunal treated 17 April 2024, the date of dishonour, as the date of default for limitation purposes. Since the Section 9 application was instituted on 8 April 2026, it was within the three-year limitation period under Article 137 of the Limitation Act, 1963. Regarding the existence of a pre-existing dispute, the Tribunal applied the principles laid down by the Supreme Court in Mobilox Innovations Private Limited v. Kirusa Software Private Limited, (2018) 1 SCC 353, and Kay Bouvet Engineering Ltd. v. Overseas Infrastructure Alliance (India) Private Limited, (2021) 10 SCC 483. It observed that a genuine dispute must be supported by a plausible contention requiring investigation and cannot rest upon a speculative or unsupported defence. The Tribunal further relied upon Sudhi Sachdev v. APPL Industries Ltd., 2018 SCC OnLine NCLAT 775, to hold that proceedings under Section 138 of the Negotiable Instruments Act arising from dishonoured repayment cheques do not, by themselves, establish a pre-existing dispute under the IBC. In the circumstances of the present case, issuance of the cheques towards refund of the advance was treated as an acknowledgment of liability rather than evidence of a dispute. No prior correspondence, suit, arbitration or other material establishing a genuine dispute was produced. The Tribunal also considered the effect of the corporate debtor’s failure to participate in the proceedings despite valid service. Referring to Shubham Jain v. Gagan Ferrotech Ltd., Company Appeal (AT) (Insolvency) No. 1008 of 2019, it held that proceeding ex parte was justified where the corporate debtor failed to appear and furnish evidence of any pre-existing dispute. The Tribunal found that the operational creditor had established the existence of an operational debt, default exceeding the statutory threshold, compliance with the demand notice requirements and absence of a pre-existing dispute. Accordingly, the application satisfied Section 9(5)(i) of the IBC and was admitted. Consequently, the NCLT initiated CIRP against San Commodities LLP and declared a moratorium under Section 14 of the IBC. Mr. Biswajeet Bedamatta was appointed as the Interim Resolution Professional under Section 16(4). The Tribunal directed the IRP to make the statutory public announcement, invite claims, preserve the corporate debtor’s assets and manage its operations as a going concern. The operational creditor was directed to deposit ₹2 lakh towards initial CIRP expenses within three days of receiving the order. The Tribunal further directed communication of the order to the relevant parties and statutory authorities. The decision reinforces that an unrefunded advance connected with the supply of goods can constitute operational debt and that dishonoured repayment cheques, without evidence of an independent pre-existing dispute, do not prevent admission of a Section 9 insolvency application. FULL TEXT OF THE NCLT JUDGMENT/ORDER 1. The present application has been filed by M/ s. RKD Constructions Private Limited (hereinafter referred to as “Operational Creditor/OC”) under Section 9 of the Insolvency 86 Bankruptcy Code, 2016 (hereinafter referred to as “Code”) read with Rule 6 of The Insolvency & Bankruptcy (Application to Adjudicating Authority) Rules, 2016 against one M/s. San Commodities LLP (hereinafter referred to as “Corporate Debtor/CD”) seeking initiation of Corporate Insolvency Resolution Process (hereinafter referred to as “CIRP”) for a default of 3,85,00,000/ -. SUMMARY OF PLEADINGS 2. The Applicant/ Operational Creditor, has in its application made inter-alia the following averments: a. The OC is a Private Limited Company incorporated under the provisions of the Companies Act, engaged in the business of heavy infrastructure development such as construction, expansion and maintenance of roads, highways, Bridges, flyovers, etc. The CD is a Limited Liability Partnership registered under the Limited Liability Partnership Act, 2008, engaged in the wholesale supply and distribution of construction and industrial materials. b. It is submitted that the OC placed several Purchase Orders upon the CD, including Purchase Orders dated 10.01.2023 and 27.03.2023 (annexed as Annexure-4 Series), for the supply of TMT Bars of various specifications to multiple project worksites of the OC located at Sinapali, Bimalagarh, Chandiposh, Duduka, and Palma Gumla. As per the commercial terms agreed between the parties, the OC made advance payments to the tune of Rs. 7,42,46,430/- (comprising Rs. 2,83,75,124/- in FY 2022-23 and Rs. 4,58,71,306/- in FY 2023-24) to the CD towards the procurement and supply of the said materials. c. Pursuant to the aforesaid advance payments, the CD supplied materials amounting to Rs. 3,41,54,147/- across different dates. However, the CD abruptly stopped the supply of the balance materials worth Rs. 4,00,92,283/-. Due to non­delivery, the civil construction projects of the OC were adversely affected, causing substantial financial loss in its project. d. Upon repeated follow-ups and negotiations, the CD expressed its inability to deliver the balance materials and assured refund/reversal of the unadjusted advance. Towards partial discharge of its repayment obligation, the CD issued two CTS cheques drawn on IndusInd Bank, Bhubaneswar-II Branch: i. Cheque bearing No. 170286 dated 04.2024 for an amount of Rs. 1,10,00,000/-; ii. Cheque bearing No. 170339 dated 04.2024 for an amount of Rs. 2,75,00,000/-, aggregating to a total sum of Rs. 3,85,00,000/- (annexed as Annexure-5 Series). e. The OC presented the aforesaid cheques for clearance through its banker, State Bank of India, Commercial Branch, Bhubaneswar. However, both cheques were returned unpaid on 17.04.2024 vide Return Memos (Annexure-6) with remarks “Payment stopped by drawer” and “Exceeds arrangement”, respectively. f. Aggrieved by the dishonour, the OC issued statutory legal notices under Section 138 of the Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881 on 08.05.2024 (Annexure-7). When the same returned unserved with postal endorsement “No such person in the address”, the OC ascertained the current corporate office address of the CD and re-issued the notice on 29.07.2024 (Annexure-8). As no payment was forthcoming, the OC instituted criminal complaints under Section 138 of the NI Act bearing 1.C.C. No. 6961 of 2024 and 1.C.C. No. 6962 of 2024 before the Court of the learned JMFC, Bhubaneswar (Annexure-1 0 Series). g. Thereafter, the OC issued a Statutory Demand Notice in Form 3 dated 19.02.2026 under Section 8(1) of the Code demanding payment of the unpaid operational debt amounting to Rs. 3,85,00,000/- along with supporting documents (Annexure-9). Despite due service of the demand notice, the CD neither repaid the unpaid debt within the stipulated period of 10 days nor issued any notice pointing out the existence of any pre­existing dispute. h. Consequently, the OC filed the instant application under Section 9 of the Code on 08.04.2026 claiming an operational debt of Rs. 3,85,00,000/ -, citing the date of default as 16.04.2024. The OC has complied with Section 9(3)(b) by filing an affidavit stating that no notice of dispute has been received from the CD (Annexure-12) and has placed on record its ledger account and bank statements (Annexu re- 1 1). 3. Notice of the present application was duly issued and served upon the CD, as evidenced by the proof of service placed on record by both the Registry 86 Applicant. Despite granting multiple opportunities and sufficient time, neither anyone appeared on behalf of the CD nor was any reply filed. Consequently, vide order dated 04.08.2026, the Respondent/ Corporate Debtor was proceeded ex-parte. ANALYSIS AND FINDINGS 4. We have heard the Learned Counsel appearing for the Applicant, and perused the pleadings, written submissions, and documentary evidence placed on record. To adjudicate this Application filed under Section 9 of the Code, this Adjudicating Authority is required to determine: (i) Whether the advance payment made by the Operational Creditor for supply of materials constitutes an “Operational Debt” under Section 5(21) of the Code? ii. Whether the Corporate Debtor committed “Default” in terms of Section 3(12) of the Code, satisfying the threshold limit and limitation period? iii. Whether the pendency of proceedings under Section 138 of the Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881 constitutes a “Pre­existing Dispute” under the Code? iv. Whether the Application satisfies the statutory requirements under Section 9(5)(i) of the Code for initiation of CIRP? ISSUE-I: Whether the advance payment made by the Operational Creditor for supply of materials constitutes an “Operational Debt” under Section 5(21) of the Code? 5. In the present case, the OC placed multiple Purchase Orders dated 10.01.2023 and 27.03.2023 upon the CD for procurement and delivery of TMT Bars to its various project sites. As per the agreed terms stipulated in the Purchase Orders, the Operational Creditor paid an aggregate advance sum of Rs. 7,42,46,430/ -. While the CD supplied materials amounting to Rs. 3,41,54,147/ -, it failed to deliver the balance materials and retained the unadjusted advance of Rs. 4,00,92,283/-. 6. The legal question as to whether an unrefunded advance paid to a supplier/vendor qualifies as an “operational debt” is no longer res integra. The Hon’ble Supreme Court in Consolidated Construction Consortium Limited v. Hitro Energy Solutions Private Limited [(2022) 7 SCC 164] categorically held that the expression “in respect of the provision of goods or services” under Section 5(21) must be interpreted broadly and purposively. The relevant paragraphs are stated as follows: “50.1. First, Section 5(21) defines ‘operational debt’ as a ‘claim in respect of the provision of goods or services’. The operative requirement is that the claim must bear some nexus with a provision of goods or services, without specifying who is to be the supplier or receiver. Such an interpretation is also supported by the observations in the BLRC Report, which specifies that operational debt is in relation to operational requirements of an entity. 50.3. Finally, the judgment of this Court in Pioneer Urban, in comparing allottees in real estate projects to operational creditors, has noted that the latter do not receive any time value for their money as consideration but only provide it in exchange for goods or services. Indeed, the decision notes that 7e]xamples given of advance payments being made for turnkey projects and capital goods, where customisation and uniqueness of such goods are important by reason of which advance payments are made, are wholly inapposite as examples vis-à-vis advance payments made by allottees’. Hence, this leaves no doubt that a debt which arises out of advance payment made to a corporate debtor for supply of goods or services would be considered as an operational debt. 52. Similarly, in the present case, the phrase “in respect or in Section 5(21) has to be interpreted in a broad and purposive manner in order to include all those who provide or receive operational services from the corporate debtor, which ultimately lead to an operational debt. In the present case, the appellant clearly sought an operational service from the proprietary concern when it contracted with them for the supply of light fittings. Further, when the contract was terminated but the proprietary concern nonetheless encashed the cheque for advance payment, it gave rise to an operational debt in favour of the appellant, which now remains unpaid. Hence, the appellant is an operational creditor under Section 5(20) IBC.” The Hon’ble Apex Court observed that the operative requirement is that the claim must bear some nexus with the provision of goods or services, without restricting whether the creditor is the supplier or the receiver. It was held that an advance payment made to a CD for the supply of goods or services, when the supply is not completed and the advance is unreturned, constitutes an operational debt under the Code. 7. The aforesaid legal position was further reiterated and followed by the Hon’ble NCLAT in Chipsan Aviation Private Limited v. Punj Lloyd Aviation Limited [Company Appeal (AT) (Insolvency) No. 261 of 2022, wherein it was held that advance payments made for obtaining goods or services which were not supplied, and where the advance was not refunded, squarely fall within the definition of an “operational debt” under Section 5(21) of the Code. 8. In the present case, the remittance of advance funds was made directly in furtherance of underlying commercial purchase orders for the procurement of construction materials (TMT Bars) vital to the operational activities of the Applicant. The CD failed to supply the balance materials and subsequently issued two cheques dated 16.04.2024 aggregating to Rs. 3,85,00,000/- towards repayment of the unutilized advance. Therefore, the claim bears a direct nexus with the provision of goods, squarely falling within the ambit of Section 5(21) of the Code. Accordingly, Issue No. I is answered in the AFFIRMATIVE, holding that the unrefunded advance payment claimed by the Operational Creditor constitutes an “Operational Debt” under the Code. ISSUE-II: Whether the Corporate Debtor committed “Default” in terms of Section 3(12) of the Code, satisfying the threshold limit and limitation period? 9.A perusal of records reveal that the total operational debt claimed to be in default is Rs. 3,85,00,000/ -. The records demonstrate that upon the failure of the CD to supply the balance materials under the Purchase Orders, the CD acknowledged its default by issuing two cheques drawn on IndusInd Bank, Bhubaneswar-II Branch, bearing Cheque No. 170286 dated 16.04.2024 for Rs. 1,10,00,000/- and Cheque No. 170339 dated 16.04.2024 for Rs. 2,75,00,000/ -. Upon presentation for clearing, both cheques were returned unpaid on 17.04.2024 with the endorsements “Payment stopped by drawer” and “Exceeds arrangement”, respectively. 10. The issuance and dishonour of these cheques constitute unequivocal, contemporaneous proof of debt and default on record. The unpaid sum of Rs. 3,85,00,000/- squarely breaches the statutory minimum threshold of Rs. 1,00,00,000/- mandated under Section 4 of the Code for initiating CIRP. 11. As regards to limitation, the default occurred upon the dishonour of the cheques on 17.04.2024. The present application having been filed on 08.04.2026, is well within the three-year period prescribed under Article 137 of the Limitation Act, 1963. Accordingly, Issue No. II is answered in the AFFIRMATIVE. ISSUE-III: Whether there exists any pre-existing dispute between the parties prior to the issuance of the Demand Notice under Section 8 of the Code? 12. The law regarding the existence of a pre-existing dispute under Section 9 of the Code is well settled by the Hon’ble Supreme Court in Mobilox Innovations Private Limited v. Kirusa Software Private Limited ((2018) 1 SCC 353), wherein the Hon’ble Apex Court held at Para 51 as follows: “51. It is clear, therefore, that once the operational creditor has filed an application, which is otherwise complete, the adjudicating authority must reject the application under Section 9(5)(i)(d) if notice of dispute has been received by the operational creditor or there is a record of dispute in the information utility. It is clear that such notice must bring to the notice of the operational creditor the “existence” of a dispute or the fact that a suit or arbitration proceeding relating to a dispute is pending between the parties. Therefore, all that the adjudicating authority is to see at this stage is whether there is a plausible contention which requires further investigation and that the ‘dispute’ is not a patently feeble legal argument or an assertion of fact unsupported by evidence. It is important to separate the grain from the chaff and to reject a spurious defence which is a mere bluster. However, in doing so, the Court does not need to be satisfied that the defence is likely to succeed. The Court does not at this stage examine the merits of the dispute except to the extent indicated above. So long as a dispute truly exists in fact and is not spurious, hypothetical or illusory, the adjudicating authority has to reject the application.” The aforesaid principle was reiterated by the Hon’ble Supreme Court in Kay Bouvet Engineering Ltd. v. Overseas Infrastructure Alliance (India) Private Limited ((2021) 10 SCC 483), wherein it was reaffirmed that all the Adjudicating Authority is required to see is whether there is a plausible contention requiring further investigation and that the defense is not a patently feeble legal argument, mere bluster, or an assertion unsupported by any evidence. 13. In the case at hand, the OC served the statutory Demand Notice in Form 3 dated 19.02.2026 demanding payment of the outstanding operational debt. The CD neither replied to the statutory demand notice, nor did it dispute its liability to refund the unadjusted advance. The record is devoid of any prior communication, suit, or arbitral proceeding questioning the underlying liability. The Applicant has further placed on record an affidavit under Section 9(3)(b) of the Code affirming that no notice of dispute has ever been received from the CD. 14. Furthermore, following the dishonour of the two cheques bearing No. 170286 and No. 170339 dated 16.04.2024 aggregating to Rs. 3,85,00,000/ -, which were issued towards the refund of the unutilized advance, the OC instituted proceedings under Section 138 of the Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881. The question whether such proceedings constitute a “pre-existing dispute” under the Code is no longer res integra. The Hon’ble NCLAT in Sudhi Sachdev v. APPL Industries Ltd. [2018 SCC OnLine NCLAT 775], after relying on the decision of the Hon’ble Supreme Court in Innoventive Industries Ltd. v. ICICI Bank [(2018) 1 SCC 407], observed and held as follows: “5. In “Innoventive Industries Ltd. v. ICICI Bank” – (2018) 1 SCC 407, the Hon’ble Supreme Court observed as follows: 29. The scheme of Section 7 stands in contrast with the scheme under Section 8 where an operational creditor is, on the occurrence of a default, to first deliver a demand notice of the unpaid debt to the operational debtor in the manner provided in Section 8(1) of the Code. Under Section 8(2), the corporate debtor can, within a period of 10 days of receipt of the demand notice or copy of the invoice mentioned in sub-section (1), bring to the notice of the operational creditor the existence of a dispute or the record of the pendency of a suit or arbitration proceedings, which is pre­existing – i.e. before such notice or invoice was received by the corporate debtor. The moment there is existence of such a dispute, the operational creditor gets out of the clutches of the Code.” 6. In the present case, it is not in dispute that there is a debt payable to the Operational Creditor and default on the part of the Corporate Debtor. The pendency of the case under Section 138/441 of the Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881, even if accepted as recovery proceeding, it cannot be held to be a dispute pending before a court of law. Thereby we hold that the pendency of the case under Section 138/441 of Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881 actually amounts to admission of debt and not an existence of dispute. We find no merit in this appeal. It is accordingly dismissed. No Costs.” The ratio laid down in Sudhi Sachdev (supra) squarely applies to the facts of the present case. Here, the CD itself issued the cheques in discharge of its liability to return the unadjusted advance. The subsequent dishonour of these cheques and the consequent institution of criminal complaints under Section 138 of the Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881, rather than establishing any pre-existing dispute, operate in law as an unequivocal admission and acknowledgment of the debt by the CD. 15. In the absence of any reply to the Section 8 Demand Notice and having regard to the issuance of cheques towards repayment, this Adjudicating Authority finds that there was no dispute in existence between the parties prior to the service of the statutory demand notice. Accordingly, Issue No. III is answered in the NEGATIVE, holding that no pre-existing dispute exists between the parties. ISSUE-III: Whether the Application satisfies the statutory requirements under Section 9(5)(i) of the Code for initiation of CIRP? 16. Under Section 9(5)(i) of the Code, the Adjudicating Authority is mandated to admit the application if the application is complete, there is no repayment of the unpaid operational debt, the statutory demand notice has been delivered, no notice of dispute has been received, and no disciplinary proceedings are pending against the proposed Interim Resolution Professional (if proposed). 17. In the present case, the OC delivered the Demand Notice in Form 3 on 19.02.2026 under Section 8(1) of the Code, which was duly received. Despite receipt, neither repayment was made nor was any notice of dispute issued within the statutory period of 10 days. Upon he institution of the present proceedings, notices were issued and duly served upon the CD both by the Registry and the Applicant and sufficient opportunities were granted for appearance. Despite sufficient opportunity, the CD failed to enter appearance or file any reply controverting the averments in the petition, leading this Tribunal to set the CD ex parte vide order dated 04.08.2026. 18. The legal consequence of the non-appearance of a CD following valid service of the statutory demand notice and company petition is settled by the Hon’ble NCLAT in Shubham Jain v. Gagan Ferrotech Ltd. [(2021) ibclaw.in 40 NCLAT/Company Appeal (AT) (Insolvency) No. 1008 of 2019], wherein it was held: “8. As is evident from paragraph 10 of the Impugned Order, despite service of Demand Notice u/s 8 of the Code and service of the application u/s 9 of the Code, the Corporate Debtor did not appear before the Adjudicating Authority. Hence, we are of the considered opinion that the Adjudicating Authority had not erred in proceeding ex parte in the matter. 9. The Appellant though has alleged that the Operational Creditor’s claim is seriously disputed, has failed to adduce any evidence in support of alleged dispute before this Appellate Tribunal. Hence, in the absence of any evidence/ document before us showing the existence of dispute before issuance of Demand Notice, we cannot hold in favour of the existence of a dispute merely based on a single averment” The principle laid down in Shubham Jain (supra) is squarely applicable to the present facts. Despite valid service at both stages, first under Section 8(1) and subsequently in the Section 9 proceedings, the CD chose to abstain from the proceedings and did not place any material on record to demonstrate a pre-existing dispute or dispute the subsistence of the operational debt. In the absence of any rebuttal or contrary evidence, the statements of bank accounts, purchase orders, cheque dishonour memos, and the affidavit under Section 9(3)(b) stand unchallenged. The statutory prerequisites under Section 9 of the Code are fully met, and the petition warrants admission. Accordingly, Issue No. IV is answered in the AFFIRMATIVE, holding that the Application satisfies the statutory requirements under Section 9 of the Code for initiation of CIRP. 19. In light of the aforesaid findings, this Adjudicating Authority is satisfied that the OC has established the existence of an operational debt exceeding the statutory threshold, the factum of default, and the absence of any pre-existing dispute between the parties. The application is complete in all statutory respects and satisfies the requirements mandated under Section 9(5)(i) of the Code. Consequently, the present application deserves to be admitted. 20.

ETBFSI Research ETBFSI Published On Oct 16, 2026 at 08:07 AM IST var news_msid = 134980696; console.log(news_msid); Select ETBFSI as Preferred Choice on Google Copy Link Share on WhatsApp Share on Linkedin Share on X Share on Telegram Share on Facebook The Reserve Bank of India’s clarification of its foreign exchange risk reserve (FERR) rules has narrowed uncertainty over which currency derivatives attract the new requirement, but banks must still lock up cash without earning interest. The cost of maintaining the reserve is adding to the expense of hedging dollar purchases, while disruption in the forward market has pushed implied hedging costs to multi-year highs.The RBI will supply dollars to Indian Oil, BPCL and HPCL through designated banks to reduce pressure on the rupee.FERR requires banks to set aside 20 per cent of the rupee equivalent of eligible derivative contracts, with no interest earned on the reserve.Customer hedges of current account transactions attract the requirement when the customer buys foreign currency against the rupee and the notional value exceeds $2 million; eligible bank hedges are also covered.Banks are passing on additional hedging costs of 11-14 paise per month, prompting some importers to favour shorter-dated forward contracts.Implied hedging costs surged to 4.6 per cent for one-year contracts on October 15, amid liquidity strains and disruption in the forward market.The FERR applies to specified rupee-linked foreign exchange derivative contracts used by customers to hedge current account transactions when they buy foreign currency against the rupee and the notional value exceeds $2 million. Banks must maintain a reserve equal to 20 per cent of the rupee equivalent of each eligible contract’s notional value. The requirement also applies to eligible contracts through which banks hedge currency risks on their own assets and liabilities, without a minimum transaction threshold. Advt The reserve must be maintained in cash in the authorised dealer’s current account with the RBI on a daily basis, over and above the prescribed cash reserve ratio balance. It does not earn interest and must be maintained until a day after the derivative contract terminates. Banks must therefore account for the opportunity cost of funds tied up against eligible transactions when pricing hedging contracts.The clarification excludes banks’ market-making and proprietary trading transactions, as well as specified derivatives used to hedge repayments of overseas loans, including principal and interest. Customer contracts involving the sale of foreign currency against the rupee, derivatives that do not involve the rupee and hedges of capital account transactions are also outside the requirement. Certain current account payments arising from capital account transactions, including interest on external commercial borrowings, coupons on resident debt instruments held by non-residents and dividends payable to non-resident investors, are exempt from the customer-level requirement. Banks’ own qualifying hedges of currency risks arising from these exposures may still attract FERR.The additional cost is already feeding into customer pricing. Banks have been charging importers an extra 11-14 paise per month on forward contracts to buy dollars, with the cost varying according to funding expenses and contract tenor. Importers have increasingly favoured shorter-dated contracts of up to three months to limit the additional expense, although this requires them to renew their hedges more frequently. Advt The impactMarket disruption has added to the pressure. The one-year implied cost of hedging rose to 4.6 per cent on October 15, its highest level since January 2022, compared with roughly 2 per cent suggested by the India-US interest rate differential. The three-month implied cost also rose sharply, reflecting strained liquidity and imbalances in the forward market. These market-implied costs should not be confused with the 20 per cent reserve itself or with a uniform fee charged to every importer.For contracts combining two or more options, the RBI has clarified that the structure is treated as a single derivative contract and the notional value of the largest individual option is used to determine whether the $2 million threshold is crossed and to calculate the reserve. The clarification also cautions against splitting transactions across contracts or dealers to avoid the requirement. Advt The reserve is part of a broader set of measures aimed at curbing dollar demand and supporting the rupee. While the clarification specifies which transactions are covered, the requirement to maintain non-interest-bearing cash continues to impose a cost on banks and customers. The eventual impact on importers will depend on banks’ funding costs, the tenor and structure of contracts, and conditions in the foreign exchange market.

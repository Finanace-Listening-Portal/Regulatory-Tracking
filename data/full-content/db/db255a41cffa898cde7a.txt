ETBFSI Research ETBFSI Published On Oct 31, 2026 at 02:53 AM IST var news_msid = 135378116; console.log(news_msid); Select ETBFSI as Preferred Choice on Google Copy Link Share on WhatsApp Share on Linkedin Share on X Share on Telegram Share on Facebook Cyberattacks are exposing banks and non-banking financial companies (NBFCs) to direct financial losses, higher operating costs and potential regulatory liabilities. The alleged loss of around $2 million by two Mumbai-based NBFCs through unauthorised bank transfers highlights how weaknesses in payment infrastructure can translate into immediate financial damage. Data breaches involving State Bank of India (SBI) and AU Small Finance Bank have further exposed risks arising from lenders' dependence on external technology platforms.The financial impact extends beyond stolen funds. Lenders may incur costs on forensic investigations, system restoration, customer compensation, legal proceedings and regulatory compliance. Additional spending on cybersecurity infrastructure and technology upgrades can raise operating expenses. Substantial fraud losses could also erode the capital buffers of smaller lenders with limited capacity to absorb unexpected costs. Advt Two Mumbai-based NBFCs allegedly lost around $2 million through more than 700 unauthorised transfers on October 8 and 9 after hackers compromised an application programming interface (API) used to send loan-disbursement instructions to ICICI Bank. The details emerged from a police complaint reviewed by Reuters. The incident exposed how compromised credentials and weaknesses in transaction controls can be exploited to initiate fraudulent transfers through banking infrastructure.The Reserve Bank of India (RBI) has stepped up scrutiny of banks' cybersecurity preparedness following the incidents. It has sought details of lenders' compliance with existing cybersecurity requirements, vulnerabilities in APIs connecting banks with NBFCs and fintech companies, and preparedness for artificial intelligence-related risks. In an October 19 advisory, the RBI asked regulated entities to treat technology environments as compromised until they are forensically cleared following credential exposure or confirmed unauthorised access.Third-party technology widens exposureThe growing dependence on digital lending platforms, payment gateways and external technology vendors has increased the number of entry points through which attackers can access financial institutions. APIs allow banks, NBFCs and fintech platforms to exchange information and automate loan disbursements, repayments and other transactions. Compromised credentials or inadequate authentication controls can allow fraudulent instructions to pass through these systems.In an October 9 advisory, the Indian Computer Emergency Response Team (CERT-In) flagged attacks involving stolen credentials of payment aggregators, gateways and partner banks. These credentials were allegedly used to initiate transfers through financial institutions' infrastructure. The advisory identified tactics such as fictitious loan references, transactions exceeding prescribed limits and transfers to mule accounts. Advt SBI is investigating a suspected data exposure after around 3.75 lakh records surfaced on the dark web. A preliminary examination identified 3,897 records containing personally identifiable information. The bank has reported no material impact on its operations, while its investigation continues.AU Small Finance Bank is also investigating a potential data leak involving a third-party software platform holding limited information on vehicle-loan customers. The bank has said its own systems remain unaffected and banking services are operating normally. Claims made by a hacker group about the extent of the compromised information have not been independently verified. Advt CERT-In's October 15 advisory separately flagged incidents in which AI agents accessed live systems of Indian organisations using exposed credentials and permissive cloud settings. It highlighted the risks of granting automated tools excessive access to sensitive systems.Losses can spread across the financial systemThe exposure is not limited to the lender directly attacked. A compromised technology vendor, payment gateway or API connecting multiple financial institutions can create vulnerabilities across several banks and NBFCs. An attack on a shared platform could disrupt transactions across institutions, resulting in simultaneous financial losses and operational interruptions. Advt Smaller NBFCs could be particularly vulnerable because many depend on external technology providers and banking partners for loan origination, disbursement, collections and servicing. Their reliance on shared platforms exposes them to vulnerabilities beyond their direct control, while limited cybersecurity budgets can constrain their ability to maintain specialised teams and continuous monitoring.Customer data breaches can create further exposure through identity theft, fraudulent loan applications and unauthorised transactions. Even when core banking systems remain unaffected, lenders may incur costs on customer notifications, investigations and additional safeguards. Reputational damage can also affect customer retention and business growth.Lenders need stronger API access controls, multi-factor authentication, transaction authorisation and monitoring of unusual payment instructions. Regular security assessments of third-party systems and independent beneficiary verification are also necessary. Outsourcing technology does not transfer responsibility for protecting customer data and transactions away from banks and NBFCs.Cybersecurity failures can therefore affect lenders through fraud losses, higher operating expenses, regulatory exposure and damage to customer relationships. As financial institutions become more interconnected, their ability to secure third-party platforms and contain breaches will increasingly influence profitability and operational resilience, with severe incidents potentially affecting capital strength.

Summary: An Indian resident can generally become a director or officer of a UAE company, subject to the applicable UAE corporate regulations and Indian legal requirements. However, directorship and shareholding are distinct legal relationships. An individual appointed solely as a director without acquiring shares may not make an overseas investment merely by accepting the position. Conversely, acquiring equity in a Dubai company can trigger India’s Overseas Direct Investment (ODI) framework under the Foreign Exchange Management Act, 1999. The article examines how an individual’s ownership rights, capital contribution, management authority and financial interests determine the applicable regulatory obligations. The Foreign Exchange Management (Overseas Investment) Rules, 2022 permit eligible resident individuals to make overseas investments subject to prescribed conditions. Where a director also becomes a shareholder, the investment may require compliance with ODI eligibility, remittance, valuation, reporting and documentation requirements. The Liberalised Remittance Scheme (LRS), generally providing an annual aggregate limit of USD 250,000 for permitted transactions, can become relevant. The article also examines qualification shares, minority shareholding, contractual control and restrictions relating to foreign subsidiaries and step-down subsidiaries. For Indian entrepreneurs expanding into Dubai, the question of whether an Indian resident can become a director of a UAE company often arises at a very early stage. The short answer is generally yes. An Indian resident can, subject to the applicable UAE company rules and Indian foreign-exchange regulations, become a director or officer of a UAE company. But the practical position is more nuanced than simply being appointed to the board. The real issues begin when the directorship is connected with: ownership of shares; capital contribution; remuneration; management control; UAE banking authority; or active participation in the company’s business. These factors can create separate FEMA, Indian income-tax and UAE Corporate Tax considerations. For an Indian resident planning to become a director of a Dubai company, it is therefore important to distinguish between holding a board position and making an overseas investment. They are related in many cases, but they are not the same thing. Directorship and Shareholding Are Two Different Roles A person can be a director of a company without necessarily owning its shares. Likewise, a shareholder does not automatically have to be a director. This distinction is particularly important in India–UAE structures. Consider two situations. In the first: Indian resident → Director of Dubai Company → No shareholding In the second: Indian resident → Director + 100% shareholder of Dubai Company The UAE role may appear similar because the individual is involved in management in both cases. From the Indian FEMA perspective, however, the second arrangement introduces a direct overseas investment question because the Indian resident owns equity in the foreign entity. This is why the first question should not merely be: “Can I become a director?” It should be: “What rights, ownership and financial interests will I have in the UAE company?” UAE Company Law Does Not Generally Require Every Director to Be a UAE National The UAE has developed into an international business jurisdiction in which foreign individuals routinely serve as shareholders, managers, directors and officers of companies. Depending on the legal form and jurisdiction, a Dubai company may have foreign directors or managers. The exact rules vary according to the type of entity. A company may be incorporated: on Dubai mainland; in DMCC; in DIFC; in JAFZA; in Dubai South; in another free zone; or under another UAE corporate framework. The constitutional documents and rules of the relevant authority determine how directors or managers are appointed. The important point for an Indian resident is that UAE residency and UAE nationality are not, as a general proposition, prerequisites for every board position. However, the company should verify the specific rules applicable to its jurisdiction and legal form. Becoming a Director Does Not Automatically Mean Making ODI Under India’s current Overseas Investment framework, ODI broadly relates to acquisition of specified equity capital or control in a foreign entity. Directorship alone is not necessarily equivalent to ownership. Suppose an Indian resident is appointed as an independent director of a Dubai-based company and receives no shares. That appointment by itself may not constitute an equity investment. But the position changes if the person acquires shares. For example: Indian resident receives 20% shares and becomes director of a Dubai company. The share acquisition can constitute Overseas Direct Investment depending on the nature of the foreign entity and investment. The individual should then consider the Overseas Investment Rules, the applicable LRS framework and reporting requirements. It is therefore important not to confuse management designation with investment classification. What if Shares Are Required to Become a Director? Some companies or constitutional arrangements may require a director to hold a certain number of shares. If an Indian resident acquires shares merely because those shares are needed for the directorship, the investment still needs to be examined under the current FEMA framework. The amount may be small. That does not make the investment irrelevant. The modern Overseas Investment Rules define ODI broadly enough to cover subscription to the equity capital of an unlisted foreign entity. For a private Dubai company, even a relatively modest direct equity subscription can therefore create a FEMA investment obligation. The investor should not assume that shares described as “qualification shares” fall completely outside current overseas investment rules simply because the economic amount is small. Resident Individuals Can Make ODI Subject to the Rules The current Overseas Investment framework expressly permits resident individuals to make overseas investments subject to the prescribed conditions. This includes ODI in foreign entities. For an Indian resident becoming both shareholder and director of a Dubai company, the investment should therefore be structured under the applicable ODI provisions. A typical arrangement may be: Indian resident individual ↓ Equity investment in Dubai company ↓ Appointment as shareholder-director The individual should then review issues such as: available LRS limit; nature of the UAE business; method of remittance; ODI reporting; ownership percentage; control; and future subsidiaries. The directorship itself may be straightforward. The ownership attached to it requires the deeper FEMA analysis. The USD 250,000 LRS Limit Can Become Relevant For resident individuals, the Liberalised Remittance Scheme generally provides an annual ceiling of USD 250,000 for permitted transactions. If an Indian resident needs to invest money into a Dubai company in order to become a shareholder-director, the remittance may need to fit within the available LRS limit and the Overseas Investment framework. This is an annual aggregate ceiling. It is not reserved solely for UAE business investment. If the individual has already used LRS during the year for other permitted purposes, the remaining capacity may be lower. Therefore, an appointment should not be accepted on the assumption that the capital subscription can automatically be remitted later. Where shareholding is part of the role, the investment mechanics should be checked before corporate documents are finalised. Directorship Without Investment Is Simpler, but Not Compliance-Free Suppose an Indian resident joins the board of an existing Dubai company but does not acquire shares. The FEMA investment issues may be significantly reduced because there is no equity subscription. However, several other questions can still arise. For example: Will the director receive fees? Will remuneration be paid in the UAE or India? Will the director have authority over UAE bank accounts? Where will board decisions actually be made? Will the individual work physically in Dubai? Does the person need a UAE visa or work permit? Is the position executive or non-executive? These questions affect different areas of law. A board appointment should therefore be analysed according to what the director will actually do, not merely what the title says. Director Remuneration Can Create Indian Tax Consequences An Indian resident serving as director of a UAE company may receive: director fees; salary; bonus; board meeting fees; profit-linked remuneration; or another form of compensation. The Indian tax treatment depends on the facts, including the individual’s residential status, nature of remuneration and applicable treaty provisions. A Resident and Ordinarily Resident taxpayer in India is generally subject to Indian taxation on global income, subject to applicable law and treaty relief. Therefore, receiving remuneration into a UAE bank account does not automatically make the income outside Indian taxation. The key question is not where the money is physically received. The individual’s Indian residential status and nature of income are crucial. UAE Tax Treatment of Director Payments Also Matters On the UAE side, payments to directors can create Corporate Tax considerations for the company. The Federal Tax Authority treats a director or officer of a taxable business as a Connected Person for Corporate Tax purposes. FTA UAE Payments or benefits provided to connected persons should therefore be considered under the relevant UAE Corporate Tax rules. This means a Dubai company should not simply pay an arbitrary amount to a shareholder-director and assume it is automatically deductible. The remuneration should have a commercial basis and appropriate documentation. For owner-managed Dubai companies, this becomes especially important where the same person is: shareholder; director; general manager; and recipient of substantial remuneration. The different roles should be properly documented. Being a Director Can Create Control Even Without Majority Ownership Another important FEMA issue is the concept of control. Control does not necessarily require ownership of more than 50% of a company. Depending on the rights held, a person may exercise control through: the right to appoint directors; management rights; voting arrangements; contractual rights; or shareholder agreements. This becomes important where an Indian resident owns only a minority stake in a Dubai company but also has significant governance rights. For example, an Indian founder may own 30% of the UAE company but have contractual authority to appoint the majority of the board. The investment should not be analysed merely by looking at the 30% percentage. The actual governance rights matter. Under the Overseas Investment framework, control can affect how the investment is classified and what subsequent activities of the foreign entity are permitted. Why Control Matters for Step-Down Subsidiaries Suppose an Indian resident becomes a shareholder-director of a Dubai holding company. The Dubai company then establishes subsidiaries in Saudi Arabia and the United Kingdom. The overseas investment framework contains specific conditions for resident individuals where the foreign entity creates subsidiaries or step-down subsidiaries. A resident individual who has made ODI without control in a foreign entity that subsequently acquires or establishes a subsidiary cannot later acquire control in that foreign entity under the circumstances specified in the RBI framework. System Health This demonstrates why a directorship should not be viewed only as a title. Governance rights and control can have regulatory consequences for the entire international structure. An Indian Director Can Also Be an Authorised Bank Signatory In Dubai companies, directors and managers often receive authority over the corporate bank account. That can include powers to: approve payments; sign banking documents; initiate transfers; deal with relationship managers; or approve financing. This authority does not mean the company’s money personally belongs to the director. The UAE company remains a separate legal entity. However, bank-signing authority may have implications for: corporate governance; Indian foreign-asset disclosures where applicable; internal controls; and the evidence around where the company is actually managed. For a UAE business with Indian-resident directors, banking authority should therefore be documented carefully. Place of Effective Management Can Become a Serious Issue This is one of the most important tax risks for Indian-resident directors of UAE companies. A Dubai company is incorporated in the UAE. But where is it actually managed? Suppose the company has: a Dubai trade licence; a UAE bank account; a registered Dubai office; but every major decision is made by an Indian-resident director sitting in India. The Indian director decides: which customers to accept; which contracts to sign; which investments to make; who to hire; how much to borrow; and where the company’s money should be deployed. The UAE board merely signs formal resolutions afterwards. In such a case, the legal incorporation and the economic management may point to different jurisdictions. Indian tax law’s Place of Effective Management principles can become relevant where the key management and commercial decisions of a foreign company are effectively made in India. This is why management substance matters. A UAE Company Should Not Be Managed Entirely Through WhatsApp From India This may sound informal, but it reflects a real practical risk. Many small Dubai companies are operated almost entirely by founders living in India. The founder has: UAE licence; UAE bank account; UAE virtual office; but all decisions are taken from India through email and messaging applications. The UAE accountant records transactions. A local service provider handles licence renewals. But the actual business remains controlled from India. For a genuine UAE operating company, this model deserves careful review. If the commercial objective is to establish a real Dubai business, governance should support that objective. Board meetings, management authority, operating personnel and decision-making arrangements should reflect the intended structure. Does the Director Need a UAE Residence Visa? Directorship and immigration status are separate questions. In many circumstances, a foreign individual may hold a corporate position without automatically needing UAE residence merely because of that appointment. However, if the individual will: work physically in Dubai; manage daily operations; draw a UAE salary; regularly reside in the UAE; or require local employment/immigration status, the appropriate UAE visa and work-authorisation requirements should be considered. The correct arrangement depends on: company jurisdiction; role; nationality; frequency of physical presence; and employment structure. A corporate title should not be used as a substitute for appropriate immigration status where work authorisation is required. Shareholder, Director and General Manager Are Not Necessarily the Same Position Dubai company structures sometimes use these terms interchangeably in conversation. Legally, they can represent different roles. A person may be: shareholder but not director; director but not shareholder; general manager but not shareholder; or all three at the same time. Each role carries different rights and responsibilities. For example: Shareholder — owns equity. Director — participates in governance and board-level decision-making. Manager — may have authority to conduct daily operations. This distinction should be reflected in: constitutional documents; board resolutions; employment arrangements; banking authority; and remuneration. Clear roles reduce both governance and tax ambiguity. Can an Indian Employee Become Director of a Dubai Group Company? Yes, subject to the applicable corporate and regulatory requirements. This is common in international groups. An Indian company’s senior executive may be appointed to the board of its UAE subsidiary. The executive may not personally own any shares. In that situation, the Indian parent may remain the shareholder while the employee acts as a director. A structure may look like: Indian Parent Company ↓ 100% UAE Subsidiary ↓ Indian-resident executive appointed as director The employee’s directorship does not mean the employee personally owns the UAE business. This can be a clean structure where the group wants management oversight without requiring the executive to make personal ODI. Director Fees Should Not Be Confused With Dividend Another area requiring careful classification is payment. A shareholder-director may receive both: remuneration for services; and dividend as return on investment. These are conceptually different. Director remuneration compensates the individual for performing a role. Dividend is a distribution arising from share ownership. The company should not substitute one for the other simply because a particular tax treatment appears preferable. For a Dubai company, payments to a director or connected person should be appropriately supported. For the Indian individual, each type of receipt may have a different tax treatment. The corporate records and personal tax records should therefore use consistent descriptions. A Practical Example: Indian Founder Becomes Director and Shareholder Consider an Indian resident who wants to establish a management consultancy in Dubai. The founder proposes to: own 100% of the UAE company; act as director and general manager; invest AED 200,000; open the UAE corporate bank account; and continue spending significant time in India. This structure involves several layers. FEMA The share subscription is likely to constitute ODI. The investor should review available LRS capacity and complete the required overseas investment process. UAE corporate law The appointment should be properly reflected in the company’s documents and the requirements of the relevant Dubai jurisdiction. Banking The founder may be both beneficial owner and authorised signatory. Indian income tax Any salary, director remuneration, dividend or other personal income received from the UAE company requires appropriate analysis. POEM If all strategic and commercial decisions continue to be made from India, the UAE company’s management location could become relevant. A single appointment therefore intersects with several compliance systems. A Second Example: Indian Professional Becomes Non-Shareholder Director Now consider an experienced Indian engineer appointed to the board of an established Dubai manufacturing company. The person: owns no shares; makes no investment; attends quarterly board meetings; receives annual director fees; and has no authority over daily operations. The FEMA investment analysis is much lighter because there is no equity acquisition. The main issues become: whether the UAE appointment complies with company rules; how the director fees are documented; Indian tax treatment of those fees; and any UAE immigration or physical-presence requirements. This is fundamentally different from a founder owning and operating the UAE company. A Third Example: Minority Shareholder With Management Control Consider an Indian resident who acquires 25% of a Dubai technology company. The shareholders’ agreement also allows the individual to: appoint two of three directors; approve all significant spending; veto borrowing; and control the annual business plan. The person may be a minority shareholder economically, but the governance rights are significant. For FEMA and broader tax structuring, management should examine actual control rather than focusing only on the 25% number. This is particularly important if the Dubai company later acquires subsidiaries in other countries. UAE Corporate Tax Applies Regardless of the Director’s Nationality A company’s UAE Corporate Tax position does not depend on whether its director is Indian, British, Emirati or another nationality. The Federal Tax Authority states that UAE Corporate Tax generally applies to UAE-incorporated juridical persons without differentiating based on the nationality or residence of their owners. FTA UAE This is an important distinction. The director’s personal tax circumstances and the company’s UAE Corporate Tax position are separate matters. A Dubai company should therefore maintain its own: accounting records; Corporate Tax registration; tax computation; related-party documentation; and filing obligations regardless of where its director personally resides. Free-Zone Companies Follow the Same Basic Directorship Principle The same broad issues arise whether the UAE company is established in: DMCC; JAFZA; Meydan; IFZA; Dubai South; or another free zone. The particular free-zone regulations determine the corporate appointment process. But an Indian resident should still separately consider: whether shares are being acquired; whether ODI arises; whether LRS is involved; whether control exists; and how the role affects tax and management substance. The free-zone licence does not remove Indian FEMA obligations. Common Mistake: Becoming Director First and Sorting Out Shares Later A founder may sign UAE incorporation documents, become director and have shares issued in their name. Only afterwards does the individual approach the Indian bank. This is exactly the type of sequencing that can make an otherwise straightforward transaction unnecessarily complicated. Where directorship comes with share ownership, the Indian investment route should be understood before the shares are subscribed. The UAE corporate process and Indian FEMA process should be coordinated from the outset. Common Mistake: Assuming No Investment Means No Indian Tax Issue A non-shareholder director may have no ODI exposure. That does not mean there are no Indian tax consequences. If the individual is resident in India and receives UAE director remuneration, the income may need to be reported and taxed in India subject to the applicable rules and treaty position. Similarly, foreign bank accounts or signing authority can raise separate disclosure questions depending upon the individual’s facts. FEMA is therefore only one part of the analysis. Common Mistake: Using a Director Title to Justify Personal Withdrawals Owner-managed Dubai companies sometimes blur the boundary between company and shareholder. A director may withdraw company funds and later describe them as: salary; loan; reimbursement; or dividend. This is poor practice. Each payment should have a clear legal basis before it is made. For a UAE Corporate Tax payer, payments to directors and other connected persons can require particular attention. For an Indian resident, the personal tax treatment also depends upon what the payment actually represents. Corporate funds should not be treated as the director’s personal wallet simply because the individual owns the business. A Practitioner View: The Real Question Is Not “Can I Be Director?” For most Indian entrepreneurs looking at Dubai, the directorship itself is rarely the difficult part. The real questions are: Will I own shares? Will I invest money? Will I control the company? Will I receive remuneration? Will I manage the business from India or Dubai? Will the company have subsidiaries? Will I have UAE bank-signing authority? These questions determine the regulatory substance of the arrangement. A title alone tells very little. Two individuals may both be called “Director of a Dubai Company”, while one is an independent professional with no ownership and the other is the founder, sole shareholder, bank signatory and day-to-day decision-maker. Their Indian compliance positions can be completely different. A Better Structure for an Indian Resident Joining a UAE Board Before accepting or creating the appointment, the following sequence is useful. First, identify the role. Is the person independent director, executive director, manager or shareholder-director? Second, determine whether shares will be acquired. If yes, review the ODI implications. Third, establish FEMA residential status. Do not assume a UAE visa determines the answer. Fourth, determine the investment amount and LRS availability. If equity is involved, the remittance structure should be clear. Fifth, document governance rights. Understand whether the individual has control. Sixth, document remuneration. Separate salary, director fees, reimbursement and dividend. Seventh, review UAE immigration requirements. Particularly if the director will work physically in Dubai. Eighth, establish where real management will occur. Avoid creating a Dubai company that is managed entirely from India without analysing the consequences. Ninth, maintain proper corporate and banking records. The company’s money and the director’s personal money should remain clearly separated. Conclusion An Indian resident can generally become a director of a UAE company. The appointment itself is usually not the main regulatory obstacle. The real compliance consequences depend upon what comes with that appointment. If the individual also acquires shares, FEMA’s Overseas Investment framework can become relevant. If money is remitted from India, LRS and ODI requirements may need to be considered. If the individual receives remuneration, Indian income-tax consequences may arise. If the director controls the Dubai company’s strategic decisions from India, the management and tax-residency position of the UAE company may need closer examination. And if the individual is both director and owner, payments between the UAE company and that person should be properly documented under the UAE Corporate Tax framework. The central distinction is therefore simple: Being a director is a governance role. Owning and funding the UAE company is an investment role. For Indian residents entering Dubai business structures, the safest approach is to identify these roles separately and then make sure the FEMA, tax, corporate and banking documentation all reflect the same commercial reality.

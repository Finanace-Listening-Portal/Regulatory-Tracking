Articles 4 min read GFF 2026: Tokenisation at population scale needs public chains, interoperability, liquidity: Nandan Nilekani Tokenisation will only deliver its full potential if it moves beyond asset issuance to create liquidity and real-world transactions, Infosys Chairman Nandan Nilekani said at Global FinTech Fest 2026. Nilekani said public chains, interoperability and AI agents could help take tokenisation to population scale and give smaller businesses access to capabilities traditionally available only to large companies. Advt Tokenisation needs liquidity, not just issuanceNilekani said a key challenge is ensuring that tokenised assets are actually used after issuance.“A token is a trustable unit because it has all its attributes. But it still has to reach the right place on time,” he said, warning that without interoperability and liquidity, the ecosystem could end up with “a lot of token issuance, but no activity”.For regulated assets such as bonds, certificates of deposit and other financial instruments, the entire ecosystem needs to be designed around how tokens are used, how liquidity is created and how buyers and sellers can participate.This is one reason public chains become important, he said. While private chains may provide greater control, public chains can allow wider participation and enable multiple liquidity pools to interact.At the same time, putting assets on public chains creates data-security challenges. Information written to a public chain therefore needs to be unique but encrypted, Nilekani said.AI agents could drive tokenised transactionsNilekani said AI could become an important complement to tokenisation by creating demand and transaction activity through autonomous agents.“Tokenisation by itself will only handle issuance,” he said, adding that tokenisation on public chains supported by agentic transactions could create the volumes needed to justify such platforms. Advt For instance, an AI agent working 24x7 could identify an invoice, package it as a token, offer it to multiple lenders and help secure financing.The combination of tokens and agents could therefore create an ecosystem in which transactions are not dependent entirely on people initiating them manually.Three pilots demonstrate use casesOne of the pilots involves dairy and is being rolled out in Tamil Nadu. The model tokenises attributes of a cow, including its identity, owner, health and ownership status, allowing the token to be presented to lenders for credit.Nilekani said the open architecture means lending need not be restricted to a single institution, allowing any lender that accepts the token to potentially provide credit. Advt A second pilot focuses on warehouse receipts. Farmers who store wheat, rice or other commodities in warehouses can receive a token containing details such as quantity, quality, warehouse and insurance status. The token can then be shared with multiple lenders, allowing the farmer to choose between lenders rather than being dependent on a single financing source.Nilekani said this can increase the bargaining power of farmers by making warehouse receipts portable and widely acceptable.The third use case involves securitisation of assets in a marketplace, where standardised and interoperable documentation and smart contracts can help bring greater structure to an otherwise fragmented ecosystem. Advt Technology can level playing field for small businessesNilekani said the larger objective of tokenisation and AI is to reduce the gap between large corporations and small businesses.Historically, a large company could have a treasury desk, research team, credit-rating capabilities and sales infrastructure, while a small business lacked access to such resources.With AI agents, however, a small business could potentially have a 24x7 digital agent accessing analysis, packaging assets into tokens and taking them to marketplaces for sale or financing.“The main thing, why we believe that tokens and agents are important is actually about inclusion,” Nilekani said.He argued that India's future job creation could increasingly come from millions of small businesses rather than only a handful of large companies.Nilekani cited the growth of India's formal startup ecosystem from around 10,000 startups in 2015 to around 150,000 in 2025, and said the country could have a million startups by 2035.He also pointed to the more than 10 million businesses registered under GST as evidence of the scale of India's entrepreneurial ecosystem.“Therefore, we have to think of an economy with millions of small businesses and make them AI-proof,” he said.For Nilekani, the broader objective is to use digital public infrastructure to ensure that technology reaches millions of businesses and individuals, rather than remaining accessible only to large institutions.Click here to read our coverage of the Global FinTech Fest 2026.

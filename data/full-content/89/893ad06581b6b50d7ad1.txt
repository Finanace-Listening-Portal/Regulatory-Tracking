Background of the ₹8.74 crore freight expenditure dispute: The Gujarat High Court, comprising Justice Bhargav D. Karia and Justice Pranav Trivedi, in PCIT-3 Vs Sikandarkhan N Tunvar, decided on 27 July 2026, dismissed the Revenue’s appeal challenging the deletion of a substantial disallowance of ₹8,74,76,630 under Section 40(a)(ia) of the Income-tax Act, 1961. The assessee was an individual engaged in business as a transport contractor and commission agent. During Assessment Year 2007-08, he made freight payments aggregating ₹8,74,76,630 to transport subcontractors without deducting tax at source under Section 194C of the Income-tax Act. His explanation was that the subcontractors had furnished declarations in Form No. 15-I, thereby attracting the statutory exemption from TDS applicable to eligible small transport operators under the provisions governing the relevant assessment year. The Assessing Officer rejected this explanation, alleging that several transporters owned more than two trucks, that some declarations contained repeated names and inadequate particulars, and that a declaration had improperly been accepted from a partnership firm, M/s Sarkhej Motor Transport. The Assessing Officer further objected that the assessee had not furnished the prescribed consolidated statement in Form No. 15-J to the jurisdictional Commissioner of Income Tax within the prescribed time. On these grounds, the entire freight expenditure was disallowed. The Commissioner of Income Tax (Appeals) sustained the disallowance, but the Income Tax Appellate Tribunal, Ahmedabad Bench, granted substantial relief to the assessee. The Revenue consequently approached the High Court under Section 260A, raising questions concerning the legality of the deletion, the consequences of submitting Form 15-J before a Commissioner lacking jurisdiction, and the alternative disallowance of the expenditure under Section 37(1). 2. Assessing Officer’s objections to Form 15-I declarations and findings of CIT(A): The Assessing Officer’s principal objection was that the assessee had accepted Form No. 15-I declarations without adequately verifying whether the transport subcontractors satisfied the statutory condition concerning ownership of goods carriages. The assessment proceedings revealed that several names appeared repeatedly in the list of truck operators, with similarities in surnames, parentage and addresses. According to the Department, these circumstances indicated that certain persons owned more than two trucks and were consequently ineligible to furnish declarations claiming exemption from TDS. The Assessing Officer also noted that the assessee had failed to produce supporting documents such as registration certificates of trucks, driving licences, affidavits and permanent account numbers of the truck owners. The Commissioner (Appeals) examined a list containing 55 names and observed that several Form 15-I declarations were incomplete, undated or supported by vague addresses. He expressed serious doubts regarding their authenticity and observed that original declarations had not been produced before him. The CIT(A) also noted that Form 15-J had been filed with the JCIT (TDS) on 6 April 2007 rather than before the Commissioner having jurisdiction over the assessee. In his view, filing the statement before an incorrect authority deprived the Department of an opportunity to verify the transporters’ declarations. He consequently upheld the entire addition under Section 40(a)(ia). Significantly, the CIT(A) also held that the expenditure could alternatively be disallowed under Section 37(1) because the assessee had allegedly failed to establish its genuineness. He reasoned that incomplete declarations, repeated names and inadequate identification particulars rendered the freight payments unverifiable. The appellate controversy therefore involved two distinct questions: whether the alleged defects in statutory declarations created a liability to deduct TDS and whether the same alleged defects independently justified disallowing the freight expenditure as non-genuine business expenditure. 3. Tribunal’s findings that deductors were not required to independently verify declarations: The Income Tax Appellate Tribunal examined the statutory scheme applicable to transport subcontractors and concluded that the assessee could not be required to undertake verification obligations that the legislation did not impose upon the recipient of Form No. 15-I. The Tribunal relied upon the Supreme Court’s decision in CIT Vs ITI Ltd., 221 CTR 619, which concerned the scope of an employer’s obligation to collect supporting evidence in relation to employee declarations. The underlying principle was that a person responsible for deducting tax cannot automatically be burdened with independent verification requirements that are not prescribed by statute or rules. Applying this reasoning to the transport business, the Tribunal held that the declaration in Form 15-I was required to be verified by the subcontractor furnishing it, not independently certified by the contractor receiving it. The Tribunal also considered Kerala Transport Company Vs CIT, 54 taxmann.com 219, where it was held that an assessee could not be subjected to disallowance merely because it had not independently verified the contents of Form 15-I submitted by transport operators. The Tribunal emphasised that the object of the statutory exemption was to reduce the compliance burden on small truck owners, particularly individuals owning not more than two goods carriages. Under the then-applicable presumptive taxation framework, such operators could otherwise face unnecessary TDS deductions and subsequent refund claims despite having relatively modest taxable income. The Tribunal therefore considered that the exemption would be frustrated if every contractor receiving a prescribed declaration were required to undertake an independent investigation into the ownership history and personal particulars of the subcontractor. It further observed that, if the Department suspected false declarations, it possessed statutory powers under Sections 131 and 133(6) to summon persons, obtain information and verify the relevant facts. The Revenue had not undertaken those inquiries despite having access to truck numbers and addresses in the assessment records. 4. Form 15-J filing before a non-jurisdictional Commissioner was a procedural defect: A central question before the High Court was whether the assessee’s submission of Form No. 15-J before a Commissioner who did not exercise jurisdiction over him could retrospectively invalidate the exemption from TDS available on the basis of Form No. 15-I declarations. The Tribunal had held that such filing constituted a procedural irregularity rather than a substantive breach automatically attracting disallowance. It noted that the statement had been received by an income-tax authority and that the Commissioner receiving it had not objected to the absence of jurisdiction. The Tribunal relied upon the Karnataka High Court’s decision in CIT Vs Shridhar Shantinath Patravali, 81 taxmann.com 333, which recognised that a procedural failure relating to Form 15-J should not, by itself, result in disallowance where the underlying statutory conditions for non-deduction were satisfied. More importantly, it applied the Gujarat High Court’s earlier judgment in CIT Vs Valibhai Khanbhai Mankad, 28 taxmann.com 119. In that decision, the jurisdictional High Court had explained that the liability to deduct tax ceases once the statutory conditions for exemption under Section 194C(3) are satisfied. The subsequent obligation to furnish particulars in Form 15-J is separate from the substantive conditions governing deduction of tax at source. A failure to comply with that reporting obligation may have consequences independently prescribed by law, but it does not automatically recreate a TDS liability that had already ceased. The High Court in the present appeal accepted this distinction. It observed that Rule 29D of the Income-tax Rules, 1962, as applicable to the relevant assessment year, required the subcontractor to furnish a verified Form 15-I and required the contractor to submit the corresponding particulars in Form 15-J to the designated Commissioner by the prescribed date. However, the fact that Form 15-J had been furnished before an officer lacking territorial jurisdiction did not justify invoking Section 40(a)(ia), which applies where tax was legally deductible but was not deducted or deposited. The procedural reporting requirement could not be transformed into a substantive obligation to deduct tax where the statutory exemption was otherwise available. 5. Judicial precedents on TDS liability, transport contracts and departmental verification: The Tribunal and the High Court also considered judicial decisions addressing the circumstances in which payments to transport operators attract TDS obligations. The Tribunal referred to CIT Vs Valibhai Khanbhai Mankad, where the Gujarat High Court held that the operation of Section 40(a)(ia) presupposes an existing obligation to deduct tax under Chapter XVII-B. If the conditions of the exemption under Section 194C(3) are satisfied, the deduction obligation ceases and the disallowance provision cannot be applied merely because a subsequent reporting requirement was not fulfilled. The Tribunal also referred to DCIT Vs M/s Kataria Movers, ITA No. 1988/Ahd/2012, and the Gujarat High Court’s decision in PCIT Vs M/s Kataria Logistic Services, Tax Appeal No. 336 of 2017, dated 13 June 2017. In the latter case, the High Court had declined to interfere with the Tribunal’s finding that hiring trucks on a need basis did not establish the contractor-contractee relationship assumed by the Department. These authorities supported the broader proposition that liability under Section 194C must be established from the actual statutory conditions and factual arrangements, rather than presumed merely because payments were made for transportation. The Tribunal also recognised an important exception in the assessee’s own case. It upheld the disallowance relating to payments made to M/s Sarkhej Motor Transport, a partnership firm, because the relevant exemption was available to qualifying individual subcontractors and did not extend to that firm. Thus, the assessee’s appeal before the Tribunal had been allowed only in part. Regarding the remaining payments, the Tribunal observed that the Department had not exercised its powers to verify truck ownership, obtain information from the Regional Transport Office or examine the subcontractors. It also noted that truck ownership could change during the year and that the mere appearance of multiple truck numbers against a person’s name did not conclusively establish ineligibility without appropriate factual verification. The High Court agreed that the authorities could not substitute unverified suspicions for the inquiries available under Sections 131 and 133(6), particularly when the declarations had been received and the relevant records were available for examination. 6. Alternative disallowance under Section 37(1) rejected without rejection of books: The Revenue additionally contended that, even if disallowance under Section 40(a)(ia) could not be sustained, the entire freight expenditure should be disallowed under Section 37(1) of the Income-tax Act because the assessee had not proved the genuineness of the payments. The Commissioner (Appeals) had supported this alternative basis by pointing to allegedly defective Form 15-I declarations, incomplete addresses, repeated names and the absence of supporting records. The Tribunal rejected this reasoning after examining the nature of the assessee’s business and the treatment of corresponding income by the Department. It observed that the Revenue had accepted the income generated from the transport business and had not disputed the fact that freight expenditure was necessarily incurred in earning that income. The Tribunal reasoned that an assessee conducting transport operations could not ordinarily earn the accepted business receipts without incurring corresponding expenditure on transportation services. It further noted that the original addition was based principally on alleged defects in Form 15-I declarations and the filing of Form 15-J, rather than on independently established evidence that the transport services had not been rendered or that the payments were fictitious. The Tribunal held that, if the CIT(A) genuinely considered the accounts unreliable, the appropriate course would have been to follow the prescribed procedure for rejecting the books of account after granting the assessee an opportunity of hearing and thereafter estimating income on a reasonable, scientific and comparable basis. The entire freight expenditure could not simply be disallowed while the corresponding business income remained accepted. The Gujarat High Court endorsed this reasoning, observing that the CIT(A) had neither rejected the books of account nor undertaken a proper estimation of business profits. Consequently, the alternative invocation of Section 37(1) could not justify disallowance of the entire ₹8,74,76,630. The Court distinguished between a statutory TDS default and a substantive finding that expenditure was not incurred for business purposes. It held that neither basis had been established sufficiently to overturn the Tribunal’s factual conclusions concerning the eligible transport payments. 7. Gujarat High Court’s final decision dismissing Revenue’s appeal: After considering the rival legal propositions, the Gujarat High Court held that the Tribunal had correctly interpreted the applicable provisions of Section 194C(3), Rule 29D, Section 40(a)(ia) and Section 37(1). The Court reiterated that Form 15-I was required to be verified by the subcontractor furnishing the declaration, while the contractor receiving it was not subject to an independent statutory obligation to investigate every declaration before relying upon it. It also accepted that the submission of Form 15-J before a Commissioner lacking jurisdiction was a procedural irregularity and did not, by itself, create liability to deduct tax at source. The Court observed that the Assessing Officer had not disputed the receipt of Form 15-I declarations but had objected principally to the assessee’s failure to independently verify the particulars furnished by truck owners. Neither the Assessing Officer nor the CIT(A) had conducted the available statutory inquiries to establish that the declarations were false or that the relevant subcontractors were ineligible for the exemption. The Revenue relied upon CIT, Kolkata-III Vs Mitra Logistics (P.) Ltd., (2013) 357 ITR 657, but the High Court distinguished that decision because it concerned the improper admission and consideration of additional evidence by the appellate authority. The factual and procedural circumstances were materially different from those in the present appeal. The High Court further agreed with the Tribunal that the alternative disallowance under Section 37(1) was unsustainable in the absence of rejection of books of account and a proper determination of business profits. The Court also took note that the Tribunal had sustained the disallowance concerning payments to the partnership firm M/s Sarkhej Motor Transport, demonstrating that the relief granted was confined to payments qualifying under the applicable statutory framework. In paragraph 12, the High Court concluded that the Tribunal’s factual findings and legal reasoning justified deletion of the disputed disallowance. In paragraph 13, it held that no question of law, much less any substantial question of law, arose from the Tribunal’s order. Accordingly, the Revenue’s Tax Appeal No. 85 of 2020 was dismissed on 27 July 2026. The judgment reinforces that procedural defects in furnishing Form 15-J cannot automatically revive a TDS liability extinguished by compliance with the substantive exemption under Section 194C(3). It also underscores the importance of departmental verification before rejecting statutory declarations and the impermissibility of disallowing an entire category of business expenditure under Section 37(1) without following the legally prescribed procedure for questioning the reliability of accounts.

Chandrashekar (Karnataka High Court) Summary: The Karnataka High Court, in Office of Principal Commissioner GST and Central Excise Mysuru Vs R. Chandrashekar and Others, decided a batch of five connected writ appeals concerning the additional tax burden imposed on government works contractors following the introduction of the Goods and Services Tax (GST) regime on 1 July 2017. The Division Bench comprising Chief Justice Vibhu Bakhru and Justice K.S. Hemalekha examined whether contractors executing government works contracts entered into before the implementation of GST could obtain directions requiring government departments to reimburse the differential tax liability and whether GST authorities could be restrained from enforcing statutory obligations relating to tax, interest, penalties and filing of returns. The appeals arose from a common judgment dated 11 April 2023 passed by a learned Single Judge in a batch of writ petitions filed by contractors engaged by various departments, public authorities and government agencies in Karnataka. The contractors contended that their contractual rates had been determined under the earlier Karnataka Value Added Tax (KVAT) regime and did not adequately account for the increased tax burden arising under GST. The central controversy before the Division Bench was not merely the financial consequences of the transition from VAT to GST, but whether contractual disputes concerning reimbursement of additional taxes could justify judicial directions modifying the statutory obligations imposed under GST legislation. The underlying dispute originated from works contracts awarded before 1 July 2017, when the contractors had submitted their bids and entered into agreements based on the Schedule of Rates and the taxation structure prevailing under the KVAT regime. Several contractors had opted for the composition scheme under Section 15 of the Karnataka Value Added Tax Act, 2003, under which tax was payable at 4% of the relevant turnover, while other contractors were governed by the regular VAT assessment mechanism. The contract values were consequently determined with reference to the taxes applicable at the time of tendering and execution of the agreements. However, substantial portions of the contracted works remained incomplete when GST was introduced, and certain payments relating to works executed before GST were also received after its implementation. According to the contractors, the transition resulted in a materially higher tax burden because GST became applicable at 18% during the initial period from 1 July 2017 to 21 August 2017 and at 12% thereafter. They maintained that the additional tax liability had not been contemplated when the original contractual rates were fixed and that requiring them to absorb the differential burden would adversely affect the commercial basis of their agreements. The contractors therefore approached the High Court seeking appropriate directions against their respective government employers for reimbursement of the additional tax attributable to the change in the taxation regime. The learned Single Judge, while disposing of the batch of writ petitions on 11 April 2023, issued comprehensive directions intended to determine the differential tax burden arising from the transition from KVAT to GST. The concerned government departments and agencies were directed to calculate the value of works executed before 1 July 2017 and identify payments received in respect of those works. Such transactions were to be examined under the applicable KVAT composition or regular VAT scheme. The employers were further directed to determine the value of balance works completed or remaining to be completed after the introduction of GST, identify the materials and tax components included in the original contractual rates, deduct the applicable KVAT and service tax elements wherever relevant, and incorporate the GST applicable to the corresponding supplies. The directions also contemplated determination and adjustment of input tax credit on materials against output GST in cases involving contractors assessed under the regular VAT scheme. After calculating the resulting tax difference, the concerned department or authority was required to consider whether the original agreement needed modification. Where necessary, supplementary agreements could be executed to reflect the revised GST-inclusive value of the remaining works. If the revised contract value exceeded the original agreement value because of the additional tax burden, the concerned employer was directed to pay or reimburse the differential amount. The Single Judge also contemplated reimbursement where payments relating to works completed before GST were received after the commencement of the new tax regime. Apart from prescribing the methodology for calculating differential GST and considering contractual reimbursement, the learned Single Judge issued procedural directions affecting the administration of GST. The contractors were required to submit comprehensive representations to their respective employers within four weeks of receiving the judgment, irrespective of whether the relevant works had been completed before or after the introduction of GST or whether payments had already been received. The employers were directed to examine and dispose of those representations within eight weeks of submission. More significantly, the Single Judge permitted contractors who had not filed GST returns for periods after 1 July 2017 to file returns or amended returns following determination of the differential tax liability, without insisting upon interest, penalties or statutory limitation periods. The GST authorities were additionally directed not to take precipitative action against the contractors for six months from receipt of the judgment. Liberty was reserved to the contractors to challenge subsequent decisions or orders and pursue remedies available under law. These directions effectively connected the contractors’ contractual reimbursement claims with their statutory GST compliance obligations. The tax authorities challenged the judgment because, according to them, the directions concerning returns, interest, penalties, limitation and recovery were inconsistent with the statutory framework governing GST administration. Before the Division Bench, the appellants, representing the GST authorities, expressly confined their challenge to the directions issued against the tax administration and did not seek adjudication of the entire contractual dispute concerning reimbursement of additional GST. Their principal submission was that the Single Judge had exceeded the permissible scope of judicial directions by allowing contractors to file or amend GST returns in a manner not contemplated by the applicable legislation. They argued that the statutory provisions governing filing and amendment of returns prescribed their own conditions and limitations, which could not be displaced merely because contractors were pursuing reimbursement claims against government employers. Particular emphasis was placed on Section 50 of the Central Goods and Services Tax Act, 2017, under which the appellants submitted that interest liability was automatic and mandatory and that the statute did not confer discretion to waive or reduce such interest through the directions contained in the impugned judgment. The appellants further contended that the general waiver of penalties and limitation periods, together with the direction restraining precipitative action for six months, was incompatible with the statutory scheme. Their case was that the contractual arrangements between contractors and government departments could not alter the independent legal obligations arising under the Central Goods and Services Tax Act, 2017, the relevant State Goods and Services Tax Act, 2017 or the Integrated Goods and Services Tax Act, 2017. Consequently, the contractors’ claims for reimbursement, even if otherwise maintainable against their employers, could not justify directions preventing GST authorities from exercising powers conferred by statute. The Division Bench accepted the fundamental distinction between contractual reimbursement of an additional tax burden and statutory liability to pay GST. The Court observed that the dispute regarding whether the contractors were entitled to reimbursement of incremental tax paid or payable because of the introduction of GST was strictly a matter between the contractors and the employers with whom they had entered into works contracts. The contractual terms governing allocation of the additional financial burden could not modify the statutory scheme for levy and collection of GST. The Court emphasised that the contractors’ liability to pay GST, whether under the Central Goods and Services Tax Act, the State Goods and Services Tax Act or the Integrated Goods and Services Tax Act, had to be determined strictly in accordance with the relevant statutory provisions. Questions concerning levy, assessment, recovery and enforcement of GST were matters governed by legislation and could not be made dependent upon the outcome of contractual reimbursement disputes. Applying this reasoning, the Court held that directions permitting the filing of revised returns contrary to statutory provisions were impermissible. It further held that the blanket directions waiving penalties, interest and limitation periods for filing returns or revised returns were unsustainable. The Court also concluded that, in the context of a dispute concerning the additional tax burden arising from a change in the taxation regime, no directions could properly be issued to GST authorities regarding the levy, assessment or collection of tax, penalties or interest. The judgment therefore reaffirmed that contractual arrangements and disputes concerning the ultimate economic burden of taxation cannot override statutory tax obligations. In its final decision dated 10 June 2026, the Karnataka High Court held that the directions contained in the Single Judge’s judgment requiring reimbursement of differential GST must be construed as directions addressed only to the concerned government employers and not to the GST authorities. The Division Bench accordingly set aside the impugned judgment to the extent that it issued directions to tax authorities and disposed of the connected writ appeals in those terms. The ruling did not independently determine the amount of differential GST payable to any particular contractor or finally adjudicate the contractual entitlement of individual contractors to reimbursement from their respective employers. Instead, it clarified the legal boundary between contractual claims arising from the transition to GST and the statutory powers and obligations governing tax administration. The practical consequence is that contractors executing pre-GST works contracts may pursue reimbursement claims against their employers in accordance with the applicable contractual arrangements and the surviving directions concerning those employers, but such claims do not automatically suspend or modify their obligations under GST law. They cannot rely upon the contractual dispute itself to obtain a general exemption from statutory interest, penalties, return-filing requirements or tax recovery proceedings. Similarly, government employers’ responsibility to examine and address claims concerning the incremental tax burden remains conceptually distinct from the authority of GST officers to determine and enforce tax liabilities under the applicable legislation. The judgment establishes that the transition from KVAT to GST may create legitimate disputes regarding the financial allocation of additional taxes under existing contracts, but the resolution of those disputes must remain separate from the statutory framework governing GST levy, assessment, compliance and recovery. The Single Judge issued directions to government departments and agencies for determining and reimbursing the differential tax burden arising from the transition from KVAT to GST in respect of works contracts. The judgment also permitted contractors to file GST returns or amended returns without insisting upon statutory interest, penalties or limitation periods and restrained GST authorities from taking precipitative action for six months. The Division Bench held that contractual disputes concerning reimbursement of additional GST were matters between contractors and their employers and could not alter the statutory scheme governing GST. Consequently, the directions concerning filing of returns, waiver of interest and penalties, limitation periods and restrictions on statutory enforcement were set aside insofar as they applied to GST authorities. The directions concerning reimbursement were construed as applicable only to the respective government employers.

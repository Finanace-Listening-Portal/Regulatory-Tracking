Summary: India’s rapidly expanding digital economy is creating new investment opportunities for Real Estate Investment Trusts (REITs) through data centre leasing. Increasing demand for cloud computing, artificial intelligence and digital infrastructure requires specialised properties capable of supporting continuous computing operations. CRISIL Ratings projected that India’s third-party data centre operators would generate approximately ₹20,000 crore in annual revenue by FY2028, growing at 20–22% annually, while operational capacity could reach 2.3–2.5 GW. CareEdge Ratings separately projected approximately 24% annual revenue growth during FY2026–FY2030, with capacity reaching around 4 GW by FY2030. Recognising the rental potential of infrastructure assets, SEBI amended the REIT Regulations in April 2025, introducing Regulation 18(5B) and Schedule XI to permit qualifying infrastructure investments, including data centres, subject to prescribed conditions. Under this framework, REITs may own or develop data centre properties and earn fixed rental income but cannot operate the facilities or participate in operating profits and risks. Mindspace Business Parks REIT provides an example of participation in this emerging segment, while broader Indian REIT involvement remains limited in the disclosures reviewed. Data centre leasing can diversify rental income and support long-term portfolio growth, provided investments satisfy regulatory requirements and offer commercially attractive returns. REIT managers must carefully evaluate tenant creditworthiness, power availability, construction expenditure, lease commitments, operational boundaries and portfolio allocation limits before committing capital. This article considers the sector’s prospects, the scope for REIT participation and the investment choices that can turn an emerging opportunity into dependable cash flows. The entity running this data center, known as operator, manages the facility’s power, cooling, connectivity and technical systems, earning fees from customers for hosting their equipment or providing computing and storage services, depending on its business model. But since a data centre is classified as an infrastructure asset, a REIT can invest in it only as a landlord. It can own or develop the premises to the operator’s agreed specifications and lease them for fixed rent, with basic utilities where permitted. The operator runs the facility and earns the service fees. The REIT cannot take on the risks or share in the profits of running the data centre. Growth Supported by Measurable Demand CRISIL Ratings, in its assessment dated 25 November 2025, projected that the annual revenue of India’s third-party data centre operators providing colocation services—which earn fees by housing customers’ servers and providing supporting power, cooling and related facilities—would reach approximately ₹20,000 crore by FY2028, growing at 20–22% annually. It also expected their operational capacity, measured by the electrical power available to support IT equipment such as servers, to double to 2.3–2.5 gigawatts (GW) by March 2028.[1] Separately, CareEdge Ratings’ report dated 25 March 2026 projected approximately 24% annual compound growth in data centre industry revenue during FY2026–FY2030, with capacity reaching around 4 GW by FY2030.[2] These forecasts point to sustained demand for additional data centers. For operators, growth can come from accommodating more customers and expanding the capacity sold to existing users. This creates a corresponding requirement for suitable premises. A REIT can capture part of that expansion through new leases, additional buildings or acquisitions of leased properties. Its income grows as rent-paying space is added and agreed escalations take effect. How the Regulatory Route Developed As per definition of real estate property under reg 2(1)(zi), the REITs are prohibited from investing in infrastructure assets. This prohibition gave rise to a difficulty. The REITs were not able to invest in some rent generating assets as they fall under category of infrastructure assets. One such asset was data center. SEBI’s consultation paper issued on 30th October 2024 gave a solution to this difficulty. Paragraph 11.4 of the consultation paper proposed allowing infrastructure assets where the REIT earned fixed rent without assuming operating exposure.[3] The April 2025 amendment to REIT regulations implemented this principle through Regulation 18(5B), Schedule XI and the corresponding exception in Regulation 2(1)(zi). A REIT may hold qualifying infrastructure assets including data centers directly or through HoldCos/SPVs for fixed rental income, without risks or rewards connected with operating the asset.[4] What a REIT Can Do and Earn For a data centre, this means the REIT can lease the premises and earn fixed rent while the tenant runs the facility. Server hosting fees, computing charges and consideration linked to the operator’s business performance would therefore be unsuitable income streams under this route. Utility recoveries require separate assessment of their substance and applicable laws; calling them service charges does not establish permissibility. Portfolio allocation also matters. Regulation 18(4) requires at least 80% of asset value in completed, rent and/or income-generating properties. Regulation 18(5) limits the permitted residual investments to 20%. Accordingly, qualifying completed and leased data centre property can fall within the 80% category; eligible development exposure uses the residual allocation.[5] Participation Remains Concentrated Participation by Indian REITs in dedicated data centre leasing remains very limited in the public disclosures reviewed. Mindspace Business Parks REIT provides a concrete example of the opportunity. Mindspace is the one confirmed participant identified in this review. During its 6 August 2026 earnings call, management described it as the only Indian-listed REIT with a data centre portfolio, confirming two operational centres and an eventual portfolio of approximately 1.7 million square feet.[6] This example shows that owning and leasing data centre premises is a workable opportunity for Indian REITs. Their limited participation so far also suggests room for more REITs to enter the segment. Since as on date there is only 1 REIT providing rented property for data center, there is scope for more and more REITs to explore this new and growing area of investment. However, whether a particular investment is attractive will depend on its commercial terms and suitability for REIT. How Data Centre Leasing Can Benefit a REIT Data centre leases can diversify a REIT’s tenant base and connect rental growth with demand for digital infrastructure. A long-term commitment from a creditworthy operator can improve income visibility and support phased development of additional buildings. Where the tenant funds specialised equipment, the REIT can concentrate its investment on permitted property infrastructure. The benefit depends on rent covering the landlord’s capital and obligations adequately. A specialist tenant’s presence alone does not establish an attractive return. Evaluating a Proposal in Practice A REIT could develop a data centre property for an operator that commits to a long-term lease before construction begins. The REIT would fund the agreed property works and earn fixed rent. The tenant would fund and operate the specialised equipment. An early lease commitment gives the REIT more clarity on future rental income, but a building designed for one operator may be harder to lease to another. The investment committee should assess construction costs, the risk of delays in rent commencement and any expenses the REIT may have to bear. It should verify power availability through documents and review the tenant’s guarantees, early termination rights, responsibility for removing equipment and the cost of preparing the property for a new tenant. The committee should also compare the proposal with buying a completed property that is already leased. It should consider how much of the REIT’s portfolio would be committed to development, the property’s valuation, the approvals required and the impact on distributions. Any lease term requiring the REIT to provide computing capacity or guarantee operating performance should be examined against the requirement that the REIT’s role remain limited to leasing the property. A Case for Deliberate Diversification xREIT management should actively evaluate data centre leasing as a growth avenue. Credit-rating agencies project substantial sector expansion, while disclosed participation by Indian REITs remains concentrated. That combination creates scope to establish operator relationships and secure suitable sites before more REIT capital enters the segment. Limited REIT participation, however, does not mean limited competition from other property owners or infrastructure investors. The opportunity is to build a pipeline of compliant, commercially sound leases alongside the operator’s expansion. Management should pursue projects where tenant commitments, infrastructure readiness and rental returns justify the capital deployed. Selective entry now can diversify future income and position a REIT to participate as demand develops, while preserving the predictable property cash flows its unitholders expect.

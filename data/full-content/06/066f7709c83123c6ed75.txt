Summary: The article examines the legality of non-speaking orders under GST and the statutory obligation of adjudicating and appellate authorities to pass reasoned orders. An order may be considered non-speaking where the authority mechanically confirms a tax demand, disregards the taxpayer’s submissions, fails to examine documentary evidence or records conclusions without providing supporting reasons. Section 75(6) of the CGST Act, 2017 mandates that the Proper Officer state the relevant facts and the basis of the decision, while Section 107(12) requires the Appellate Authority to specify the points for determination, its decisions and the reasons thereof. Even the rejection of a rectification application under Section 161 may be challenged where the alleged error is not duly examined. The article discusses significant Supreme Court judgments in Shukla and Brothers, Kranti Associates and State of Punjab v. Bhag Singh, emphasising the importance of recording reasons in quasi-judicial proceedings. It also examines decisions of the Telangana and Allahabad High Courts reaffirming the necessity of reasoned GST adjudication. In Hari Shanker Transport v. Commissioner of Commercial Tax, the Allahabad High Court held that even the taxpayer’s failure to reply to a show cause notice does not absolve the Proper Officer of the statutory obligation to pass a reasoned order. Taxpayers may challenge non-speaking GST orders on grounds of violation of principles of natural justice, non-application of mind, failure to consider evidence and mechanical confirmation of tax demands. The order merely confirms the demand proposed in the SCN without recording independent findings. 2. The reply to the SCN is not discussed or considered. 3. The authority rejects the taxpayer’s submissions by stating only that “the reply is not satisfactory”. 4. Documents, invoices, reconciliations and other evidence submitted by the taxpayer are not examined. 5. The order does not explain the basis of computation of tax, interest and penalty. 6. The order merely reproduces the SCN, DRC-01 or audit objections. 7. The order contains conclusions but no reasons supporting those conclusions. 8. The order does not give findings on the specific allegations made in the SCN. 9. The order rejects ITC without examining invoices, GSTR-2A/2B, payment details or other relevant evidence submitted. 10. The order does not explain why the extended period under Section 74 has been invoked despite objections by the taxpayer. 11. The appellate authority dismisses the appeal without dealing with the specific grounds of appeal. 12. The rectification application under Section 161 is rejected without examining the alleged error apparent on the face of the record. Statutory Provisions Requiring a Speaking Order Section 75(6): Requirement for the Proper Officer Section 75(6) requires from the Proper Officer – The proper officer, in his order, shall set out the relevant facts and the basis of his decision. Section 107(12): Requirement for the Appellate Authority Section 107(12) requires from the Appellate Authority – The order of the Appellate Authority disposing of the appeal shall be in writing and shall state the points for determination, the decision thereon and the reasons for such decision. Section 161: Rectification of Errors Apparent on the Record Section 161 – Where the rectification application is rejected without dealing with the apparent error pointed out by the taxpayer, the rejection order may itself be challenged as a non-speaking order, depending on the facts. Courts’ Views on Non-Speaking GST Orders The Hon’ble Courts have consistently held in various judgments that a non-speaking order is not sustainable in law. Assistant Commissioner, Commercial Tax Department v. Shukla and Brothers — Supreme Court In Assistant Commissioner, Commercial Tax Department v. Shukla and Brothers [2010 (254) E.L.T. 6 (S.C.)] – the Hon’ble Supreme Court held that the requirements of recording of reasons is an essential ingredient of the principles of natural justice. A quasi-judicial authority is required to state the reasons for its decision so as to demonstrate that it has duly applied its mind to the issues arising for consideration. The Hon’ble Apex Court further held that an unreasoned order not only gives rise to an element of arbitrariness but also leaves the higher appellate forum in the dark as to the basis on which the decision has been arrived at, thereby frustrating effective appellate scrutiny. Kranti Associates Pvt. Ltd. v. Masood Ahmed Khan — Supreme Court In Kranti Associates Pvt. Ltd. v. Masood Ahmed Khan [2011 (273) E.L.T. 345 (S.C.)] – the Hon’ble Supreme Court laid down a definitive principle regarding the necessity of recording reasons, holding that a non-speaking administrative or quasi-judicial order is unsustainable in the eyes of law. State of Punjab v. Bhag Singh — Supreme Court In State of Punjab v. Bhag Singh, (2004) 1 SCC 547 – Failure to give reasons amounts to denial of justice. Reasons establish the link between the mind of the decision-maker and the conclusion reached. M/s RKBVSR JV v. Superintendent of Central Tax — Telangana High Court In M/s RKBVSR JV v. Superintendent of Central Tax (Telangana High Court), W.P. No. 25906 of 2023 – The Telangana High Court observed that the adjudicating authority passed a one-line order stating that the defence taken by the taxpayer was of a general nature and was not supported by law. The authority did not deal with the specific contentions raised in the reply. The Court held that reasons are the essence of an order passed by a quasi-judicial authority. The order was held to be non-speaking and unsustainable. M/s Hari Shanker Transport v. Commissioner of Commercial Tax, U.P. — Allahabad High Court In M/s Hari Shanker Transport v. Commissioner of Commercial Tax, U.P., Writ Tax No. 606 of 2025 – In this case the Appellant failed to submit the reply to the SCN before the Proper Officer. The order under Section 73(9) merely referred to the issuance of notices, non-submission of replies and raising of demand. The Allahabad High Court held that: 1. Section 75(6) requires the proper officer to set out the relevant facts and the basis of the decision. 2. Even where the taxpayer has not filed a reply to the SCN, the officer is required to pass a reasoned order. 3. Merely referring to earlier notices does not satisfy the requirement of a self-contained final order. The demand order was quashed and the matter was remanded for fresh adjudication. Conclusion A non-speaking or inadequately reasoned GST order may be challenged, as the facts and circumstances warrant, on one or more of the following grounds: 1. Violation of principles of natural justice. 2. Failure to record reasons for the conclusions arrived at. 3. Non-application of mind by the adjudicating authority. 4. Failure to consider the reply to the Show Cause Notice (SCN). 5. Failure to discuss documents and evidence submitted by the taxpayer. 6. Failure to give findings on the specific allegations made in the SCN. 7. Mechanical confirmation of the demand without independent examination.

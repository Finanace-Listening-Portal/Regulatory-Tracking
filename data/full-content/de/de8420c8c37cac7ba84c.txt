Fadnavis has estimated the value of land and other immovable assets that could potentially be addressed through the framework at around Rs 50 lakh crore. The state is also seeking inputs from SEBI, NSE and BSE as it works out the legal and regulatory architecture. Advt The proposal needs to be understood carefully because Maharashtra has not yet specified a final model under which individual pieces of land would simply be converted into tradable digital tokens. Rather, the government is trying to establish the legal framework under which the rights or economic value associated with physical assets can be represented digitally. Exactly what those tokens will represent, who can issue them, who can hold them and how they can be transferred will have to be determined through the legislation and subsequent regulations.What exactly is land tokenisation?In simple terms, tokenisation involves creating a digital representation of an asset or of defined rights associated with an asset. In the case of land, the physical property would remain where it is, while a digital token could represent a legally defined interest connected to that property.That is different from merely digitising a land record. Digitisation converts a paper or physical record into an electronic record, whereas tokenisation potentially creates a digital asset that can represent defined rights or economic interests and could, subject to the eventual legal framework, be transferred through digital infrastructure.Consider a property worth Rs 10 crore. Under the existing system, the property is generally treated as one asset for purposes of ownership and transfer, although it can of course have multiple legal owners. A tokenisation structure could potentially allow defined interests in the economic value of that property to be represented through digital units, making it possible to divide an interest in the asset into smaller portions. Advt The important caveat is that one token would not automatically mean ownership of a corresponding physical fraction of the land. The eventual DELTA framework will have to establish precisely what a token represents and what legal rights flow from holding it. It could potentially relate to ownership or another economic interest, but the Maharashtra government has not yet publicly finalised that structure.The blockchain itself does not create those rights. It provides a technology for recording and transferring digital units in a manner that can provide a transparent and auditable transaction history. The value of a tokenised asset will ultimately depend on the legal rights attached to it and the enforceability of those rights against the underlying asset. Advt Why does the government see an opportunity?The attraction is largely about liquidity. Land and real estate account for a huge amount of wealth in India, but they are relatively difficult to divide, transfer and monetise compared with financial assets such as shares and bonds.An owner of a property worth several crores cannot ordinarily sell a small economic interest in that property simply because they need a fraction of its value. They may instead have to sell the property or borrow against it. Tokenisation could potentially create another mechanism by allowing a defined interest in the asset to be represented digitally. Advt This is the broader idea behind the state's proposal to unlock what Fadnavis has described as the latent value of immovable assets. If legally recognised digital representations can be created and transferred, an asset that is currently difficult to divide could potentially become more flexible from a financing and investment perspective.However, these are potential applications rather than features that Maharashtra has already committed to implementing. The legislation and subsequent regulatory framework will determine the extent to which tokenised interests can actually be issued, transferred or used for financing.How could it work?A functioning tokenisation system would first need a reliable identification and verification process for the underlying asset. The property would have to be linked to an authoritative record establishing its ownership and other relevant legal characteristics before a digital representation could have meaningful value.The next stage would involve creating the digital representation and defining the rights attached to it. This is where the DELTA Act becomes particularly important: the government has to establish the legal relationship between the physical property, the official land record and the digital token.The framework would also need to determine what happens when the underlying asset changes status. Existing mortgages, transfers, inheritance claims, litigation, government acquisition and other restrictions could all affect the rights associated with a property. How such changes are reflected in the tokenised system will be an important part of the eventual architecture.The precise mechanism, however, is still being developed. It would therefore be premature to assume that Maharashtra has already decided on a particular blockchain, token structure or trading mechanism.Could tokenisation make property easier to finance?Potentially, although this will depend heavily on the final legal framework. Banks already lend against property, but lenders need to establish ownership, conduct due diligence and verify existing claims and encumbrances before accepting real estate as collateral. A more integrated digital property system could potentially make some of these processes faster and provide lenders with more standardised information about an asset.Tokenisation could also, in principle, allow owners to unlock part of the economic value of an asset rather than dispose of the entire property. A commercial property owner, for example, could potentially seek financing against a defined interest in the asset if the eventual framework permits such structures.But a token would not automatically become acceptable collateral merely because it exists on a blockchain. Banks would still need to know exactly what legal right they are receiving, how that right can be enforced and what happens to it if the underlying property becomes subject to a dispute or default.Why SEBI, NSE and BSE are involvedThe involvement of the country's financial-market institutions reflects the fact that land tokenisation could eventually cross the boundary between property transactions and financial markets.If a digital token merely records a property right, the principal questions concern land, property and registration law. If it represents an investment interest or economic claim that can be bought and sold, however, issues around securities regulation, disclosure, investor protection, custody and market infrastructure could arise.Fadnavis has said the state is taking inputs from SEBI, NSE and BSE, along with experts from technology, law, academia and industry. Their involvement suggests that Maharashtra is considering the financial-market implications of tokenisation while designing the framework, rather than treating it simply as a land-record modernisation exercise.This distinction will matter because the regulatory treatment of a token will ultimately depend on what rights it represents and how it is offered or transferred.Blockchain cannot solve land-title problemsThe biggest misconception around land tokenisation would be to assume that blockchain can eliminate the underlying problems associated with property ownership. It cannot. Blockchain can provide a reliable record of transactions after an asset has been entered into the system, but it cannot by itself establish that the original title was valid. If a property has competing ownership claims, an inheritance dispute, a mortgage or litigation, recording a token on a blockchain does not make those problems disappear.The success of the Maharashtra initiative will therefore depend on the quality of the legal and land-record infrastructure behind the technology. The state will have to create a system in which the digital representation is clearly linked to an authoritative record of the underlying property and in which changes to the legal status of that property are properly recognised.What happens if records conflict?One of the most important questions for the DELTA framework will be what happens if the digital record and the official land record do not agree.If a blockchain record shows that one person holds a token associated with a property while the government land records identify another person as the legal owner, the law will have to clearly establish which rights prevail and how the dispute is resolved.The same applies when a property is inherited, mortgaged, transferred, attached by a court or affected by a government order. The tokenisation system cannot operate independently of these legal processes; otherwise, the digital representation could become detached from the legal reality of the underlying asset.These are ultimately legal and institutional questions, not technology questions, which is why the proposed DELTA legislation is more significant than simply choosing a blockchain platform.Could land become a new digital financial asset?If Maharashtra is able to resolve these issues, the longer-term implications could be substantial. Tokenisation could potentially make defined interests in property more divisible and transferable, giving owners another way of unlocking capital and potentially allowing investors to obtain exposure to real estate without having to acquire an entire property.For banks and other financial institutions, it could eventually provide a more standardised digital representation of property interests and collateral. For companies holding large real-estate assets, it could create additional options for financing, while investors could potentially gain access to smaller interests in assets that are currently difficult to transact in fractional form.But none of this has been established as the final operating model yet. Maharashtra is currently building the legal framework, not launching a functioning market in land tokens.That distinction is crucial. The DELTA Act is an attempt to create the legal bridge between physical property and digital infrastructure. Whether that bridge eventually supports fractional ownership, new forms of property financing, wider investor participation or simply more efficient property records will depend on the rights that the legislation ultimately attaches to tokenised assets.The significance of the proposal, therefore, is not simply that Maharashtra wants to put land on blockchain. It is attempting to determine whether one of India's oldest and largest stores of wealth can acquire a legally recognised digital form that makes its economic value easier to identify, divide and potentially mobilise. If the state succeeds, the DELTA Act could make land-record digitisation only the first step in a much broader transformation of how property interacts with India's financial system.

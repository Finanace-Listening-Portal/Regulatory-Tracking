Summary: Section 112(9) of the Central Goods and Services Tax Act, 2017 provides for an automatic stay on recovery of the balance demand once a taxpayer files an appeal before the GST Appellate Tribunal and satisfies the prescribed pre-deposit requirement under Section 112(8). The statutory protection extends to the remaining disputed tax, interest and penalty, without requiring a separate stay application or judicial order. Nevertheless, instances of recovery through electronic cash ledgers and bank accounts continue even where the electronic liability register records the demand as stayed. Such deductions raise serious questions concerning the statutory limitations on recovery under Sections 78 and 79, the treatment of deposits maintained in electronic cash ledgers under Section 49, and constitutional protections under Articles 265 and 300A. The Allahabad High Court, in S.A.B. Engg Works v. State of U.P., disapproved premature recovery following rejection of the first appeal and directed restoration of the excess amount. In Manoj Kumar Richharia Contractor v. State of U.P., amounts recovered after filing the second appeal were refunded during the writ proceedings. These cases underline that the statutory pre-deposit mechanism protects taxpayers from coercive recovery while appellate remedies remain available or pending. Automated recovery mechanisms cannot override express legislative safeguards, and amounts recovered contrary to the statutory stay may be challenged before the constitutional courts. Section 112(9) says so in plain words. Yet in practice, across Uttar Pradesh, balances keep disappearing from electronic cash ledgers against demands that the portal itself marks as “Stayed”. The Tribunal has only recently begun to function, and thousands of second appeals have been filed in a short span. The software that sweeps ledgers has not caught up with the law that protects them. The result is a quiet, recurring illegality: small sums taken month after month, without notice, without any order, and often without the dealer noticing until the total has grown well beyond the pre-deposit. This article explains why such recovery is not merely irregular but wholly without jurisdiction. The Statutory Scheme The Act ties the right of appeal to a modest deposit and, in return, freezes recovery of everything else. Four provisions do the work. Section 107(6) and 107(7). A first appeal lies only after the appellant pays the admitted amount and ten per cent of the tax in dispute. Once that is paid, sub-section (7) deems recovery of the balance to be stayed. Section 112(8). For a second appeal before the Tribunal the appellant pays the admitted amount and a further ten per cent of the remaining tax in dispute. The figure was twenty per cent until the Finance (No. 2) Act, 2024 reduced it with effect from 01/11/2024. The combined deposit at both stages is therefore twenty per cent of the disputed tax. Section 112(9). The operative words are short: “Where the appellant has paid the amount as per sub-section (8), the recovery proceedings for the balance amount shall be deemed to be stayed till the disposal of the appeal.” Sections 78 and 79. Recovery itself is a regulated power. Section 78 gives the dealer three months from service of the order to pay. Section 79(1) then lists the modes by which the proper officer may recover, each of which has its own form under Chapter XVIII of the Rules: DRC-09 (Rule 143), DRC-13 and DRC-14 (Rule 145), DRC-16 and DRC-17 (Rules 147 and 151), and DRC-18 (Rule 155). Two features of this design matter. The deposit is computed on tax alone, but the stay covers “the balance amount”, which includes interest and penalty. And the stay is deemed: it arises from the statute the moment the deposit is paid and the appeal is filed, with no application, no hearing and no order. Why Such Recovery Is Without Jurisdiction Recovery after a deemed stay is not a procedural lapse that the department can cure later. It is the exercise of a power that, for the time being, does not exist. Six lines of reasoning support this. 1. The stay needs no order. Section 112(9) uses the word “deemed”. The legislature has created the legal fiction and attached it to two facts: payment of the deposit and filing of the appeal. Once both exist, the officer has nothing to decide. Nobody needs to apply for the stay, and nobody can withhold it. 2. The stay covers interest and penalty too. The deposit is a percentage of tax, but the stay is on “the balance amount”. Section 112(8)(a) itself speaks of “the amount of tax, interest, fine, fee and penalty arising from the impugned order”. There is no reading of the provision that leaves interest exposed. The adjudicated interest in an order under Section 74 is also not self-assessed interest under Section 75(12); it cannot be recovered while the adjudication itself is under appeal. 3. Recovery has only the modes Section 79(1) lists. The section says the officer shall recover “by one or more of the following modes”. That list is exhaustive. Each mode has its prescribed form. A recovery made outside those modes, without any form, is not a defective recovery. It is a taking with no legal source at all. 4. A cash ledger balance is the dealer’s money. A deposit in the electronic cash ledger is not payment of tax until it is debited against a liability under Section 49(3). Until then it belongs to the dealer. That is why the proviso to Section 50(1) charges no interest on tax lying in the cash ledger, and why Section 49(6) read with Section 54 allows a refund of the balance. Debiting it against a stayed demand is recovery from the dealer’s property, not an accounting entry. 5. The department’s own record defeats it. When Part II of the Electronic Liability Register marks a demand “Stayed”, the department admits that the demand is not enforceable. It cannot rely on that entry for one purpose and ignore it for another. The doctrine against approbation and reprobation applies to the State as it applies to anyone else. 6. The Constitution does not permit it. Article 265 forbids collection of tax save by authority of law. Article 300A forbids deprivation of property save by authority of law. A recovery made in the teeth of a statutory stay and outside Section 79 offends both. In Radha Krishan Industries v. State of Himachal Pradesh, (2021) 6 SCC 771, the Supreme Court held that coercive provisions in the GST law must be construed strictly and their conditions observed scrupulously. That principle applies with even greater force where the statute has expressly suspended the power. The pre-deposit is therefore both the price of the appeal and the ceiling on what the revenue may hold while the appeal is pending. Anything above it must come back now, not after the Tribunal decides. Strictly, the grievance is wider still: every rupee taken after the appeal was filed was taken without authority. The excess over the deposit is only the minimum that must be restored. What the Allahabad High Court Has Said The Allahabad High Court has already struck down recovery that defeats Section 112(9), and in a later case the State refunded the excess before the writ could even be heard. Before the appeal: M/s S.A.B. Engg Works v. State of U.P., Writ Tax No. 2254 of 2025, decided on 15.05.2025, 2025:AHC:80116-DB (Chief Justice’s Court). The first appeal was rejected on 24.01.2025. Just four days later, on 29.01.2025, Rs. 1,55,079 was debited from the dealer’s electronic credit and cash ledgers without any intimation. The State argued that, with the appeal dismissed and no interim order in force, it was free to recover. The Division Bench rejected that argument on two grounds: Under Section 78, the amount becomes payable only after three months from service of the order, and recovery within that period needs reasons recorded in writing. Recovery within four days of the appellate order was therefore impermissible. Allowing such recovery would make Section 112(9) “nugatory”, because a dealer may file the second appeal on the last day of limitation with the ten per cent deposit, and by then the money would already be gone. The Court also relied on the CBIC circular dated 11.07.2024, which allows the pre-deposit to be made even before the Tribunal is constituted. The recovery was quashed. The department was directed to credit back everything above ten per cent of the demand, about Rs. 60,000, within two weeks, and the amount already taken was to count as the pre-deposit. After the appeal: M/s Manoj Kumar Richharia Contractor v. State of U.P., Writ Tax No. 3814 of 2026. The tax demand of Rs. 6,12,000 was reduced in the first appeal to Rs. 4,72,168. The dealer had already deposited more than twenty per cent of that amount. Even so, after the second appeal was filed on 20.06.2026, further amounts were deducted, this time directly from his bank account. On 16.09.2026, the Division Bench asked the Standing Counsel for written instructions on the dates, the deposits and the recovery. By 30.09.2026 the State reported that the entire excess had been refunded on 28.09.2026, and the petition was disposed of as infructuous. Taken together, the two cases cover both sides of the appeal. S.A.B. Engg Works protects the period during which a second appeal can still be filed. Richharia shows that once the appeal is filed and the deposit paid, the State does not even try to defend recovery of the balance: it refunds the money as soon as the High Court asks for instructions. Conclusion The bargain in Section 112 is simple. The dealer pays twenty per cent of the disputed tax across the two appeals, and the State waits for the Tribunal. When the State takes more, it breaks that bargain and acts without any power to do so. Automated ledger sweeps do not change the law. A demand marked “Stayed” on the department’s own portal cannot be recovered through that same portal. Until the system is corrected, the writ court remains the dealer’s surest protection.

Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); Gautam Sharma Vs Union of India (Allahabad High Court) ₹38 Crore Fake ITC Allegation: Allahabad High Court Grants Bail, Notes Direction Against GST Arrests Introduction The seriousness of a tax allegation does not, by itself, justify continued imprisonment before conviction. In Gautam Sharma v. Union of India, Criminal Misc. Bail Application No. 39336 of 2026, decided on 6 November 2026, the Allahabad High Court granted bail in a case involving alleged fake input tax credit of ₹38 crore. Justice Vikram D. Chauhan considered the completed investigation, filing of the complaint, absence of criminal antecedents and likely delay in trial. The Court also took into account a circular dated 30 October 2026, recorded in the order as referring to withdrawal of GST arrest powers and directing that no arrests be made. Background of the Allegations The applicant was a partner of Aarti Industry. According to the submissions recorded in the order, the allegations concerned nine suppliers of the firm and wrongful availment of fake input tax credit amounting to ₹38 crore. The applicant had been in custody since 26 July 2026. His counsel submitted that the complaint had already been filed, he had no previous criminal history, and he would cooperate with the trial without misusing bail. The Directorate General of GST Intelligence opposed release, emphasising the amount of disputed credit. However, its counsel could not dispute the circular dated 30 October 2026 referred to before the Court. Personal Liberty and Presumption of Innocence The High Court emphasised that constitutional protection of liberty encompasses fair procedure, access to justice and a speedy trial. An accused awaiting trial enjoys the presumption of innocence. Detention before conviction serves to secure attendance at trial; it should not become punishment for an allegation that remains unproved. The Court observed that an accused cannot remain incarcerated indefinitely where the trial is unlikely to conclude within a reasonable period. It also recognised that bail may be justified on the particular circumstances even where a prima facie case exists. Investigation Completed and Complaint Filed Several features supported the applicant’s request. The investigation against him was complete, and a complaint had been filed before the Special Chief Judicial Magistrate, Meerut. The prosecution had not shown that charges had been framed. The Court noted that the prosecution was triable by a Magistrate and that the maximum punishment relevant to the case was five years. Even if the trial commenced shortly, it was unlikely to conclude within the following year. There was also no demonstrated criminal history. These circumstances required the Court to assess the necessity of further custody rather than treating the allegation’s financial magnitude as decisive. Supreme Court Decisions Relied Upon The High Court relied on Sanjay Chandra v. CBI, (2012) 1 SCC 40, which explains that bail secures the accused’s appearance and that imprisonment before conviction must not operate as punishment. It also referred to Atul Mehra v. Union of India, SLP (Criminal) No. 20996 of 2025, order dated 12 January 2026, concerning custody, delayed trial and the limited maximum sentence in a GST prosecution. In Vineet Jain v. Union of India, Criminal Appeal No. 2269 of 2025, decided on 28 April 2025, the Supreme Court had considered completed prosecution steps, documentary evidence, absence of antecedents and a five-year maximum sentence while granting bail. The Court further relied on Ratnambar Kaushik v. Union of India, (2023) 2 SCC 621, addressing completed investigation and predominantly documentary or electronic evidence, and Manish Sisodia v. Enforcement Directorate, (2024) 12 SCC 660, reiterating that bail is the rule and jail the exception. No Exceptional Grounds for Continued Custody The prosecution produced no material showing that the applicant would abscond, intimidate witnesses, tamper with evidence or obstruct justice. The High Court found no exceptional circumstances warranting denial of bail. Considering the evidence, detention period, completed investigation and the October circular, it allowed the application without expressing any opinion on the merits. Release was directed on a personal bond and two sureties, subject to conditions requiring attendance, cooperation and non-interference with evidence or witnesses. The applicant could not leave India without prior court permission. Breach could invite cancellation of bail. Author’s Comments The decision reinforces the need for an individual assessment of custody in GST prosecutions. The amount of alleged wrongful credit is relevant, but cannot substitute for examining flight risk, interference with evidence and the expected duration of trial. The reference to the 30 October 2026 circular requires precise reporting. The order records its effect but does not reproduce the complete instrument or identify its number. Accordingly, this bail order should not be presented as independently establishing that the statutory arrest provision had already been repealed. For practitioners, the stronger consolidated argument combines completed investigation, secured evidence, absence of antecedents, cooperation and likely trial delay. Bail protects liberty pending adjudication; it neither determines the admissibility of the disputed credit nor terminates the prosecution. The applicant’s substantive liability remains to be decided in the appropriate proceedings. The liberty guaranteed by Part III of the Constitution would cover within its protective ambit not only due procedure and fairness but also access to justice and a speedy trial. There is a presumption of innocence in favour of accused pending trial. The ultimate justification for such deprivation of liberty can only be, the accused being found guilty of the offences for which they have been charged. If such a finding is not likely to be arrived at within a reasonable time some relief becomes necessary. Timely delivery of justice is part of human rights and denial of speedy justice is a threat to public confidence in the administration of justice. No material has been shown by State to demonstrate that the conduct of applicant while in jail would disentitle the applicant to be released on bail. 6. It is well settled that at pre-conviction stage, there is presumption of innocence. The object of keeping a person in custody is to ensure his availability to face the trial and to receive the sentence that may be passed. The detention (pending trial) is not supposed to be punitive. Delay in commencement and conclusion of trial is a factor to be taken into account and the accused cannot be kept in custody for indefinite period if trial is not likely to be concluded within reasonable time. 7. In Sanjay Chandra Vs. CBI, (2012) 1 SCC 40 Hon’ble Supreme Court has held that accused cannot be in jail during trial as a punitive measure for his conduct, In this respect paragraph 21, 22 and 23 of Sanjay Chandra (Supra) is quoted herein below:- “21. In bail applications, generally, it has been laid down from the earliest times that the object of bail is to secure the appearance of the accused person at his trial by reasonable amount of bail. The object of bail is neither punitive nor preventative. Deprivation of liberty must be considered a punishment, unless it is required to ensure that an accused person will stand his trial when called upon. The courts owe more than verbal respect to the principle that punishment begins after conviction, and that every man is deemed to be innocent until duly tried and duly found guilty. 22. From the earliest times, it was appreciated that detention in custody pending completion of trial could be a cause of great hardship. From time to time, necessity demands that some unconvicted persons should be held in custody pending trial to secure their attendance at the trial but in such cases, “necessity” is the operative test. In this country, it would be quite contrary to the concept of personal liberty enshrined in the Constitution that any person should be punished in respect of any matter, upon which, he has not been convicted or that in any circumstances, he should be deprived of his liberty upon only the belief that he will tamper with the witnesses if left at liberty, save in the most extraordinary circumstances. 23. Apart from the question of prevention being the object of refusal of bail, one must not lose sight of the fact that any imprisonment before conviction has a substantial punitive content and it would be improper for any court to refuse bail as a mark of disapproval of former conduct whether the accused has been convicted for it or not or to refuse bail to an unconvicted person for the purpose of giving him a taste of imprisonment as a lesson.” 8. It is trite law that personal liberty cannot be taken away except in accordance with the procedure established by law. Personal liberty is a constitutional guarantee. However, Article 21 which guarantees the above right also contemplates deprivation of personal liberty by procedure established by law. Under the criminal laws, a person accused of offences which are non-bailable is liable to be detained in custody during pendency of trial unless he is enlarged on bail in accordance with law. Such detention cannot be questioned as being violative of Article 21 since the same is authorised by law. But even persons accused of non-bailable offences are entitled to bail if the court concerned comes to the conclusion that prosecution has failed to establish a prima facie case against him and/or if the court is satisfied for reasons to be recorded that in spite of existence of prima facie case there is a need to release such person on bail where fact situations require it to do so. 9. Article 21 of Constitution provides that no person will be deprived of his life or liberty except by procedure established by law. The procedure established by law should be just, fair and reasonable. The presumption of innocence is applicable to under trial accused. Even if prima facie case is made out, the constitutional Court while considering the bail application are required to examine whether it is reasonable to keep the accused in custody during trial and in only exceptional cases (like heinous crime) the bail can be denied. 10. In Special Leave to Appeal (Criminal) No 20996 of 2025 (Atul Mehra Vs. We do not undermine the gravity of the alleged offence. However, at the same time, we should not overlook the fact that the petitioner is in judicial custody as an under-trial prisoner past 8 months. The Trial Court is yet to commence. Charge is yet to be framed. Even if the trial commences in near future, it would not conclude within next one year. The offences are triable by Magistrate. The maximum punishment that the trial court may be in a position to impose upon the petitioner if held guilty would be upto 5 years.” 11. In considering the application for bail, it is noted that the petitioner was arrested on 21-7-2022 and while in custody, the investigation has been completed and the charge-sheet has been filed. The ocular evidence will be through official witnesses, due to which there can be no apprehension of tampering, intimidating or influencing. Therefore, keeping all these aspects in perspective, in the facts and circumstances of the present case, we find it proper to grant the prayer made by the petitioner.” 13.

Summary: Indian companies importing goods from overseas parent companies or fellow group entities must address both customs valuation and income-tax transfer pricing requirements. The two frameworks examine related-party pricing but serve different purposes, so a transfer pricing report does not automatically establish an acceptable customs value. Under Section 14 of the Customs Act, 1962 and the Customs Valuation (Determination of Value of Imported Goods) Rules, 2007, the declared transaction value remains the starting point. Rule 3(3) permits acceptance of a related-party price where the circumstances of sale show that the relationship did not influence it or the declared value satisfies the prescribed test-value comparison. If the proper officer has reasonable doubts about the truth or accuracy of the declared value, Rule 12 provides a procedure for seeking evidence and, where justified, rejecting that value before applying the alternative valuation methods in sequence. The Special Valuation Branch (SVB) may investigate the effect of relationships and relevant contractual payments under the applicable CBEC procedures; it does not automatically reassess every import. Year-end transfer pricing adjustments, royalties, licence fees and buyer-provided assists can have customs implications, but additions depend on the specific statutory tests and supporting evidence. Importers should retain agreements, invoices, comparable transaction data, cost records, royalty arrangements and transfer pricing analyses, and reconcile them with customs declarations and accounting records. A consistent, documented explanation of pricing differences is essential to reducing duty, interest and litigation exposure. Their pricing may be supported by an income-tax transfer pricing analysis, while Customs examines whether the declared value is acceptable for assessing import duty. These enquiries are related but not identical. A price that meets an income-tax arm’s-length analysis is relevant evidence, not a binding customs determination. Under Section 14 of the Customs Act, 1962 and the valuation framework, the price actually paid or payable for goods sold for export to India is the starting point, subject to the conditions and adjustments prescribed in the Customs Valuation (Determination of Value of Imported Goods) Rules, 2007. When Customs Must Accept a Related-Party Transaction Value Being related does not, by itself, make an invoice price unacceptable. Under Rule 3(3)(a), the transaction value is acceptable where examination of the circumstances of sale shows that the relationship did not influence the price. Rule 3(3)(b) separately permits acceptance where the importer demonstrates that the declared value closely approximates the specified test values, subject to appropriate adjustments. These are alternative routes for establishing acceptability; test values are not substitute customs values. Useful evidence may include prices charged to unrelated buyers, comparable transactions in identical or similar imported goods, pricing policies, cost and profit data, and explanations of differences in quantities, commercial levels and contractual terms. The interpretative notes to Rule 3 recognise that a detailed examination is not necessary in every related-party transaction where the proper officer is already satisfied about the price. What Happens When the Declared Price Is Questioned? Rule 12 governs the procedure where the proper officer has reason to doubt the truth or accuracy of the declared value. The officer may request additional documents and explanations. If reasonable doubt remains, the declared transaction value may be rejected following the prescribed procedure. On the importer’s request, the grounds of doubt must be communicated in writing and a reasonable opportunity of being heard provided. Rule 12 is not itself an alternative valuation method: after a valid rejection, the value must be determined by applying Rules 4 to 9 in the prescribed sequence, subject to the permitted reversal of deductive and computed value methods. The customs valuation principles therefore require a reasoned examination, rather than an automatic enhancement merely because the buyer and seller belong to the same group. Why Income-Tax Transfer Pricing Studies Are Not Conclusive Income-tax transfer pricing generally examines whether international transactions are at arm’s length under its own statutory methods. Customs valuation focuses on the imported goods, the circumstances of the sale and prescribed additions to the price actually paid or payable. Differences in transaction scope, benchmarking periods, product mix and adjustments can produce different outcomes without necessarily making either analysis defective. Year-End Price Adjustments and Customs Duty A year-end transfer pricing adjustment may require a customs review where it changes the price actually paid or payable for identifiable imported goods. An upward adjustment can create additional duty exposure, while a downward adjustment does not automatically establish entitlement to a refund. The importer must examine the contractual basis, linkage to particular imports, assessment status, evidence, applicable limitation provisions and any refund conditions, including unjust enrichment where relevant. Aggregate profit-level adjustments that cannot be attributed to particular imports require especially careful analysis. Royalties, Licence Fees and Buyer-Provided Assists Rule 10 specifies additions to transaction value. Under Rule 10(1)(c), royalties and licence fees relating to the imported goods may be includible to the extent that the buyer is required to pay them, directly or indirectly, as a condition of sale and they are not already included in the price. Other payments may require examination under Rule 10(1)(e). Buyer-provided materials, tools, designs or other qualifying assists may require appropriate apportionment under Rule 10(1)(b). Not every payment to a foreign group company is automatically dutiable. The precise contractual obligations, connection with the imported goods and the statutory conditions must be tested. Additions must rest on objective and quantifiable data. The distinction is illustrated in the TaxGuru discussion of royalty and technical know-how payments. How Special Valuation Branch Investigations Work The Special Valuation Branch (SVB) procedure under CBEC Circular No. 5/2016-Customs addresses investigation of specified related-party import cases and valuation questions. A relationship does not mean that every consignment must be referred for an SVB investigation. The applicable procedure distinguishes the investigation from the assessment of individual bills of entry by the proper officer. Importers should be ready with supply agreements, shareholding and group-structure information, pricing policies, comparable sales, cost sheets, royalty and licence agreements, details of other payments, and relevant transfer pricing documentation. The SVB documentation and investigation framework helps explain the information Customs may seek. Any provisional assessment, security, finalisation or subsequent adjustment must be handled under the applicable statutory procedure rather than assumed to follow automatically from an SVB reference. Practical Customs Valuation Checks for Importers Reconcile import values: Compare bills of entry, supplier invoices, accounting entries and transfer pricing calculations periodically, not only at year-end. Document price revisions: State whether retrospective changes are permitted, how they are calculated and whether they can be allocated to particular imported goods. Review additional payments: Examine royalties, licence fees, assists and other amounts against the individual conditions of Rule 10; do not add or exclude them solely because the recipient is related. Preserve comparable evidence: Keep relevant data on identical or similar imports, adjusting for differences in timing, quantities and commercial levels. Rule 3(3)(b) test values are a means of supporting the declared price, not a licence to substitute a different invoice value. Maintain audit-ready records: Preserve contracts, customs declarations, valuation explanations and supporting records for applicable statutory retention periods and while any enquiry or litigation remains open. Consistent records across Customs, income tax and the books are valuable, but identical figures are not always legally required. What matters is whether each difference has a documented commercial and statutory explanation before the department seeks one. Author: N.

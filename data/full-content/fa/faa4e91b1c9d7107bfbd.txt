Summary: The Vodafone case established an important distinction between the transfer of shares of a foreign company and the indirect acquisition of an Indian business. Vodafone International Holdings B.V., a Dutch company, acquired a Cayman Islands company’s share from Hutchison Telecommunications International Limited in a transaction involving approximately US$11 billion and substantial interests in Hutchison Essar Limited, an Indian telecom company. The Income Tax Department argued that the transaction effectively transferred control of Indian assets and attracted capital gains tax and withholding obligations under Section 195 of the Income-tax Act, 1961. On 20 January 2012, the Supreme Court ruled in Vodafone’s favour, holding that the law then in force did not authorise taxation merely by looking through the foreign shareholding structure to the underlying Indian assets. Parliament subsequently amended Section 9 retrospectively through the Finance Act, 2012, introducing express provisions for indirect transfers. The 2021 legislative amendment later provided conditional relief for indirect transfers undertaken before 28 May 2012. Under Section 9(10) of the Income-tax Act, 2025, the indirect transfer rules cover specified foreign shares or interests deriving substantial value from Indian assets, subject to the ₹10 crore value threshold, the 50% substantial-value test and applicable exclusions. Reporting by the Indian concern under Section 506 through Form 163 is a separate compliance requirement. The article explains the original dispute, the Supreme Court’s reasoning, the legislative developments, the present statutory tests and their application through numerical examples. Vodafone and Section 9(10) are the answer to that question. In 2012 the Supreme Court held in Vodafone that India could not tax the sale of a foreign company’s shares just because that company owned Indian assets. Parliament changed the law within months and made the change apply to past years, right back to 1962. That change is the “indirect transfer” rule: it taxes the sale of a foreign share that gets its value from India. Today it is Section 9(10). In one line: the Court said that taxing indirect transfers needs clear words in the law, and Parliament then wrote those words. The Deal in Simple Terms Vodafone, a Dutch company, wanted control of Hutchison Essar (HEL), an Indian telecom company, for about US$11 billion. HEL sat at the bottom of a chain of holding companies. Vodafone did not buy HEL’s shares in India. It bought one share of CGP, a Cayman Islands company higher up the chain that held HEL through Mauritius companies. The seller was HTIL, a Hong Kong-listed company. Both seller and buyer were non-residents, and the deal was done abroad. Vodafone bought one Cayman share, but the value sat in India Seller / Holding Structure Purchaser HTIL Seller, Hong Kong-listed Vodafone International Buyer, NetherlandsBuys the CGP share outside India ↓ owns CGP CGP Investments Cayman Islands ↓ holds HEL through Mauritius companies Hutchison Essar (HEL) Indian telecom company Price: about US$11 billion. The share sold was in the Cayman Islands. The business the buyer wanted was in India. That gap is the whole case. The Dispute The Income Tax Department said Vodafone should have deducted tax at source under Section 195 from the price it paid HTIL, because the real value was in India. Vodafone said it had bought a foreign share, so nothing was taxable in India and there was nothing to deduct. The Bombay High Court agreed with the Department. Issue Income Tax Department Vodafone What was sold? Control over an Indian business, held through a chain of companies One share of a Cayman company, which is a foreign asset “Capital asset situated in India” (Section 9(1)(i)) The Indian business gives the share its value, so the gain arises in India The share is in the Cayman Islands, so the section does not apply Control of the business Control is a separate thing of value, so selling it is taxable Not separate. It comes with owning the shares Section 195 Tax had to be deducted Tax must be deducted only if the payment is taxable in India What the Supreme Court Held On 20 January 2012, a three-judge bench of the Supreme Court led by Chief Justice S.H. Kapadia decided for Vodafone. The Court gave four reasons: 1 Look at the whole deal. It was one sale of a foreign share, not a transfer of Indian assets. The Court would not split it into pieces. 2 The old law could not look through. Section 9(1)(i) did not let the tax officer look past the foreign share to the Indian business behind it. The Court would not read the word “indirect” into it. If India wanted to tax such sales, Parliament had to say so in the law. 3 A real structure is respected. The Court will look behind a chain of companies only if it is fake (a sham) set up just to avoid tax. It found nothing fake here. 4 Section 195 needs taxable income. If the income is not taxable in India, no one has to deduct tax. So Vodafone had none. Result. The Court overturned the High Court’s judgment and cancelled the tax demand of about ₹11,000 crore, including interest. In plain words. A sale of foreign shares between two non-residents was not taxed in India just because the foreign company owned Indian assets, unless the law said so in clear words. Parliament’s Answer Parliament did say so. The Finance Act, 2012 amended the 1961 Act and made the change apply from 1 April 1962: Explanation 4 said “through” includes “by means of, in consequence of or by reason of”. That wording is now in Section 9(13). Explanation 5 said that shares of a foreign company are treated as Indian assets if they get most of their value from assets in India. That is the core of today’s Section 9(10). Explanation 2 to Section 195 said the duty to deduct tax applies to every payer, even a foreign company with no office or business in India. That answered the tax-deduction side of the dispute. Before 2012, many investors believed that a sale of foreign shares was safe from Indian tax. Because the change reached back into past years, it came as a shock and was heavily criticised. Vodafone took India to international arbitration under the India–Netherlands investment treaty, an agreement that protects investors from each country. In 2020 the tribunal ruled for Vodafone: India had not treated it fairly, as the treaty requires. In 2021 Parliament withdrew the tax on indirect transfers made before 28 May 2012. Taxpayers got back what they had paid, without interest, but only if they dropped their cases and claims against India. The rule itself stayed. In 2015, after a government expert committee (the Shome Committee) reviewed it, Parliament added the ₹10 crore and 50% tests, the testing date and the small-holder exception. These are now part of Section 9(10). Section 9(10) Today Under the 2025 Act, a share or interest in a foreign company or entity is treated as situated in India if it gets most of its value from Indian assets. In numbers, the Indian assets must be worth more than ₹10 crore and make up at least 50% of all its assets. Two kinds of seller are left out: a small holder (5% or less, with no say in running the company) and certain registered foreign portfolio investors. The Indian company in the chain must report the sale in Form 163 under Section 506. Now run the Vodafone deal through those tests. Assume CGP’s value came mainly from its Indian holding. Test Vodafone-type deal Indian assets above ₹10 crore and at least 50% of all assets Yes, on our assumption Small-holder exception No. HTIL was the controlling parent Foreign portfolio investor exception No Result The Indian share of the gain would very likely be taxable, subject to the valuation rules and any treaty That is the outcome the Supreme Court said the 1961 Act did not produce in 2012, and the one Parliament set out to change. Vodafone’s own 2007 deal is safe, though: it happened before 28 May 2012, so the 2021 law protects it. The 50% test is not met, so Section 9(10) does not apply In case 1, if the gain is ₹300 crore, the Indian share is ₹300 crore × 800 ÷ 1,200 = ₹200 crore, subject to the prescribed method and any treaty. Where It Goes Wrong Reading Vodafone as the law today. The 2012 judgment explains the old Act. Today Section 9(10) catches many sales made abroad. Stopping at the 50% test. The ₹10 crore floor, the testing date and the exceptions all need checking. Forgetting Form 163. The Indian company in the chain reports the sale, even though the seller does the deal abroad.

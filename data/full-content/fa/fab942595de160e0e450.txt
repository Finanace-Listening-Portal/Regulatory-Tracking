Articles 11 min read The Tata Sons boardroom battle and what happens next The Tata Sons board’s decision to give N Chandrasekaran another five-year term and move ahead with a potential listing has reopened a deeper question at the heart of India’s most closely watched business group: who ultimately decides the direction of Tata Sons when its board and its largest shareholder disagree? Tata Trusts, which owns about 66% of Tata Sons, has challenged the reappointment, while the RBI’s rejection of Tata Sons’ attempt to avoid listing has made the IPO issue equally contentious. Here is what happened, why the governance structure matters and what could happen next.What happened at the Tata Sons board meeting? The September 17 board meeting changed the trajectory of two issues that had appeared to be moving in opposite directions: N Chandrasekaran's succession and the proposed listing of Tata Sons. Chandrasekaran had told the board on August 12 that he would not seek another term after his current tenure ends on February 20, 2027. Tata Trusts accepted that decision and had begun the process of finding a successor. At Thursday's meeting, however, the board asked Chandrasekaran to reconsider and he agreed. The board then voted to reappoint him as executive chairman for another five years. The resolution passed 4-1, with Noel Tata, chairman of Tata Trusts and a Trust-nominated director, voting against it. Advt The board simultaneously decided to initiate steps towards listing Tata Sons, after the RBI rejected the company's application to surrender its Core Investment Company registration. Tata Sons said the company would seek guidance from the RBI, Tata Trusts and other stakeholders on complying with the central bank's requirements. Tata Trusts, however, said it had not agreed to the listing and wanted all alternatives to be examined. The two decisions therefore did not resolve the underlying conflict. They moved it from a question of succession and regulatory status into a direct contest over the authority of the board and the rights of the controlling shareholder. Why is Chandrasekaran's reappointment being challenged? Tata Trusts' objection is not simply that it prefers another chairman. Its argument is that the board's resolution itself did not comply with Tata Sons' Articles of Association. Noel Tata said Chandrasekaran had voluntarily decided not to seek another term, the majority shareholder had accepted that decision and a succession process had begun. In the Trusts' view, the board could not simply reverse that process and reappoint him while the status of his directorship itself remained subject to shareholder consideration.The more important legal issue concerns the Articles. Tata Sons has special governance provisions governing the role of Tata Trust-nominated directors in important decisions. Tata Trusts argues that the required Trust support was absent because Noel Tata voted against the reappointment. The board has proceeded on a different interpretation of the Articles and obtained legal advice supporting its position. The validity of the resolution is therefore contested and should not be described as legally settled either way. Advt If Tata Trusts owns 66%, why can't it simply remove Chandrasekaran?Tata Trusts collectively owns about 66% of Tata Sons, giving it majority shareholder power. But Tata Sons' Articles contain special governance provisions that govern how certain powers are exercised. The present dispute is essentially about how those provisions interact with the Trusts' majority ownership.This is why the issue cannot be reduced to either “the Trusts own 66%, so they can do whatever they want” or “the board voted 4-1, so the matter is settled”. The relevant questions include whether the chairman's reappointment required particular Trust-nominated votes, whether the board chairman's casting vote can resolve a deadlock, what shareholder approval is required, and how the Articles interact with the Companies Act. Advt The immediate dispute is therefore over the rules by which control is exercised, not merely over the percentage of shares owned.Why is corporate governance so important here?Tata Sons has an unusual governance structure because the largest shareholder has rights that are embedded not only in its shareholding but also in the company's constitutional documents. That creates an additional layer between ownership and management.In a conventional company, the majority shareholder can exercise substantial influence through shareholder votes, while the board runs the company within the framework of company law and its Articles. At Tata Sons, the Trust-nominated directors have a particularly important role in decisions affecting the company's leadership and strategic direction. The system works smoothly when the shareholder, board and management broadly agree. It becomes much more difficult when they do not. Advt That is what the current dispute exposes. Good corporate governance is not tested when everybody agrees; it is tested when the people with the power to make decisions disagree The question for Tata Sons is whether its governance architecture provides a sufficiently clear mechanism for resolving such a conflict without turning every major disagreement into a legal battle.Why did the succession process suddenly reverse?This is one of the most important parts of the story. Chandrasekaran announced on August 12 that he would not seek another term. Tata Trusts accepted his decision and a succession process was initiated. The Sir Dorabji Tata Trust had begun the process of constituting a selection committee to recommend a successor. The board subsequently asked Chandrasekaran to reconsider, and he agreed to continue. The board then voted for a fresh five-year term. The result is that a succession process that had already begun has effectively been overtaken by a board decision to retain the incumbent.That explains why the Trusts' objection is so strong. From its perspective, the issue is not merely a new vote on an old appointment. It is a reversal of a succession process that the majority shareholder says it had already accepted.Why has the listing suddenly become so important?The listing dispute predates the latest boardroom confrontation. Tata Sons has been classified by the RBI as an Upper Layer NBFC and a Core Investment Company under the central bank's scale-based regulatory framework. Upper Layer NBFCs are subject to enhanced regulatory requirements, including a listing requirement applicable to Tata Sons. The company had sought to escape that requirement by surrendering its CIC registration. The RBI rejected that application on September 11. That leaves Tata Sons with a much narrower route if it wants to remain compliant with the RBI framework. The board has therefore decided to initiate steps towards listing.The important point is that the IPO is no longer simply a strategic choice by Tata Sons. The RBI's decision has made the regulatory question central to the company's future structure.Why did Tata Sons want to surrender its CIC registration?The strategy was straightforward. If Tata Sons could cease to be a CIC and thereby exit the relevant regulatory classification, the regulatory basis for the mandatory listing requirement would fall away. The RBI has rejected that route. Its September 11 communication said the request to surrender the registration “cannot be acceded to” and directed Tata Sons to comply with the requirements applicable to an Upper Layer NBFC.This is why the RBI decision has transformed the listing debate. Tata Sons can still contest or seek clarification on aspects of the regulatory requirement, but it can no longer assume that deregistration will allow it to remain private indefinitely.Why does Tata Trusts oppose the listing?The Trusts' objection is about more than the inconvenience of becoming a listed company. Tata Sons is the apex holding company of a group that includes major listed businesses as well as large unlisted businesses. A listing would subject the holding company itself to continuous public scrutiny, disclosure requirements and market valuation.More importantly, it would introduce public shareholders into a company whose controlling shareholder has historically been the Tata Trusts. That changes the balance between long-term institution building, philanthropy, capital allocation and shareholder returns.This is particularly relevant because Tata is simultaneously investing heavily in aviation, electronics, semiconductors, digital commerce and other businesses where the returns may take time to emerge. The Trusts have argued for preserving the ability to take long-term decisions, while a listed Tata Sons would inevitably face greater pressure to explain its capital allocation to the market.What does the Shapoorji Pallonji stake have to do with this?The Shapoorji Pallonji Group owns about 18.37% of Tata Sons, making it the largest minority shareholder. A listing would establish a transparent market price for Tata Sons and create a potentially more liquid market for its shares. That gives the IPO another dimension. It is not just about RBI compliance or Tata Trusts' concerns. It also affects the value and liquidity of a substantial minority investment. So the listing has become simultaneously a regulatory issue, a valuation issue and an ownership issue.What will investors actually value if Tata Sons lists?They will not simply add up the value of Tata Sons' stakes in TCS, Tata Motors, Tata Steel and other businesses. They will have to value the holding-company structure itself. That means looking at the value of its investments, the discount or premium applied to a holding company, Tata Sons' own liabilities and cash flows, the capital requirements of newer businesses and the returns those investments can ultimately generate. The tension is already visible in the group's numbers. Tata Sons' standalone FY26 profit after tax rose 21.8% to Rs 31,961 crore, while Air India and Air India Express together reported a net loss of Rs 22,238 crore. Innovative Retail Concepts, which operates BigBasket's consumer-facing business, reported revenue of Rs 8,223 crore but widened its loss to Rs 3,073 crore. Tata Digital reported a loss of Rs 4,974 crore.The issue is not that these losses automatically make the strategy wrong. They show the different stages of businesses within the Tata portfolio. The question for a listed Tata Sons would be whether investors are willing to finance that long-term strategy and at what valuation.Does the listing therefore threaten the Tata model?It could put the model under greater scrutiny, but listing itself does not necessarily make long-term investment impossible. The bigger issue is the trade-off between private control and public accountability. Tata Trusts has a philanthropic purpose and has historically exercised control through a structure different from that of a conventional promoter family. Public shareholders, by contrast, would expect transparency, disciplined capital allocation and returns.These objectives can coexist. But when Tata Sons has to decide whether to continue funding a loss-making business, invest billions in a new industry or support a strategic project whose returns may take years, the disagreement between these philosophies becomes much more consequential.What happens to Chandrasekaran now?The board decision does not necessarily end the matter. The reappointment will have to go through the relevant shareholder process, and Chandrasekaran's position as a director is also important because the chairman's office is linked to his status as a director. The AGM is therefore likely to become a critical battleground. Tata Sons' previous AGM was adjourned after a quorum issue involving representation of the Tata Trusts, and the next meeting will have to deal with the outstanding shareholder matters. Tata Trusts' 66% shareholding gives it substantial voting power at the shareholder level. But the precise consequences will depend on the resolutions placed before the meeting, the Articles, the Companies Act and the status of the board's earlier decision.The dispute could therefore move through three levels: the boardroom, the shareholder meeting and potentially the courts.Can the courts settle the issue?They can potentially settle specific legal questions, but not the broader strategic disagreement. A court could be asked to determine whether the board followed the Articles, whether the relevant Trust-nominated voting requirement applied, whether the chairman's casting vote was valid in the circumstances, or whether a particular resolution should be stayed or set aside. It cannot, however, decide whether Tata Sons should pursue semiconductors, invest further in Air India or adopt one long-term capital-allocation philosophy over another. Those remain matters of corporate strategy and governance.That distinction is important because the present dispute combines legal questions with fundamentally strategic differences.Is this another Cyrus Mistry episode?There are obvious parallels, but the cases should not be treated as identical. The Cyrus Mistry dispute in 2016 also exposed tensions between Tata Trusts, the Tata Sons board and the chairman. It eventually travelled through the NCLT, NCLAT and Supreme Court. The Supreme Court in 2021 upheld Tata Sons' position in that litigation. The present dispute involves a different set of facts: Chandrasekaran had announced his intention to leave, a succession process had begun, the board reversed course, Tata Trusts objected, and the listing issue has simultaneously become a major point of disagreement. The earlier case therefore provides context, not an automatic legal answer. What are the possible next steps?The immediate focus is likely to be on the shareholder process for Chandrasekaran's directorship and reappointment, Tata Trusts' response to the board resolutions and the company's compliance with the RBI's Upper Layer NBFC requirements. At the same time, Tata Sons will have to work through the practical steps involved in a potential listing, including regulatory approvals, valuation, disclosures and the structure of the offering.The bigger question is whether the two sides can reach a durable understanding on governance before the listing process advances too far. If they cannot, Tata Sons could face parallel disputes over leadership, shareholder rights, Articles of Association and regulatory compliance.That is why Thursday's board meeting matters so much. It did not settle the Tata Sons dispute. It moved the dispute into a more complicated phase in which the board, the Trusts, shareholders, regulators and potentially the courts all have a role. The Supreme Court in 2021 upheld Tata Sons\u0027 position in that litigation.

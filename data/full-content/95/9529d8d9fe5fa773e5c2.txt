Summary: The amendments to Sections 15(3)(b) and 34(1) of the Central Goods and Services Tax Act, 2017, effective from 1 November 2026, simplify the GST treatment of post-supply discounts by removing the earlier requirements for a pre-existing agreement and specific invoice linkage. However, where a supplier seeks to reduce taxable value and corresponding GST liability through a statutory credit note, reversal of attributable input tax credit (ITC) by the recipient remains an important condition. This creates practical difficulties because the supplier may not have direct access to the recipient’s credit records or GST returns. The issue has become more significant following the withdrawal of CBIC Circular No. 212/6/2024-GST through Circular No. 253/10/2025-GST, which removed the earlier administrative certification mechanism for establishing recipient ITC reversal. Businesses must therefore distinguish between actual compliance with the statutory requirement and the documentary evidence available to demonstrate such compliance. Recipient declarations, credit-note registers, correspondence and return reconciliations may provide supporting evidence, but their sufficiency depends on the facts and applicable legal requirements. The article also examines situations where recipients have never availed the relevant ITC and distinguishes statutory GST credit notes from financial or commercial credit notes, for which different consequences may arise. It highlights the need for reliable documentation, appropriate reconciliation procedures and further clarification regarding the evidentiary standards applicable to post-supply discounts under the amended GST framework. Introduction Consider a manufacturer supplying goods to distributors across different States. After evaluating quarterly sales, the manufacturer grants additional discounts and issues GST credit notes. While the manufacturer maintains records of the credit notes and the corresponding tax adjustments, the attributable ITC reversal is undertaken by the recipients. How can the manufacturer verify whether each distributor has reversed the required ITC? What happens when a distributor does not respond to requests for confirmation? Would a declaration from the recipient be sufficient evidence during departmental scrutiny? These are practical questions that arise because the GST treatment of a post-supply discount may depend on compliance undertaken by two independent registered persons. The Finance Act, 2026 has simplified certain conditions governing post-supply discounts. However, the question of establishing recipient ITC reversal continues to deserve attention. 2. Legal position after 1 November 2026 Section 153 of the Finance Act, 2026 substituted Section 15(3)(b) of the CGST Act, while Section 154 amended Section 34(1). The changes came into force on 1 November 2026 through Notification No. 03/2026-Central Tax, dated 26 October 2026. Under the amended Section 15(3)(b), a discount granted after supply may be excluded from the value of supply where the supplier has issued a credit note and the recipient has reversed the ITC attributable to the discount, in accordance with Section 34. The earlier statutory requirements concerning a pre-existing discount agreement and specific invoice linkage have been removed. Section 34(1) now expressly recognises qualifying post-supply discounts as a ground for issuing credit notes. However, the amendments do not remove the statutory reporting limitation under Section 34(2) or the requirement concerning recipient ITC reversal. Therefore, the issuance of a GST credit note should be distinguished from satisfaction of all conditions relevant to the corresponding reduction in output tax liability. 3. Earlier verification mechanism and its subsequent withdrawal In June 2024, CBIC issued Circular No. 212/6/2024-GST to address difficulties in verifying reversal of ITC attributable to post-supply discounts. The circular recognised that, in the absence of a suitable facility on the GST portal, suppliers and tax officers faced difficulties independently verifying whether recipients had reversed the corresponding ITC. It prescribed an interim documentary mechanism. Where the total tax involved in discounts issued to a particular recipient during a financial year exceeded ₹5 lakh, the supplier was required under the circular to obtain a certificate from a Chartered Accountant or Cost Accountant, containing specified particulars of the reversal. Where the corresponding tax did not exceed ₹5 lakh, a certificate or undertaking from the recipient could be obtained instead, subject to the prescribed particulars. The mechanism was intended to facilitate evidence of compliance with the then-applicable Section 15(3)(b)(ii). However, CBIC subsequently issued Circular No. 253/10/2025-GST, dated 1 October 2025, withdrawing Circular No. 212/6/2024-GST. Consequently, the certification procedure prescribed under the withdrawn circular should not be presented as a continuing mandatory requirement. The withdrawal must nevertheless be distinguished from the statutory requirement concerning recipient ITC reversal, which continues under the amended Section 15(3)(b). 4. The practical issue: When the supplier does not possess the recipient’s records Consider a manufacturer granting discounts to three distributors through GST credit notes. The following amounts are assumed for illustration, with GST calculated at 18%. Particulars Distributor A Distributor B Distributor C Discount excluding GST ₹2,00,000 ₹5,00,000 ₹8,00,000 GST attributable to discount ₹36,000 ₹90,000 ₹1,44,000 Credit note issued Yes Yes Yes Reversal confirmation Received Pending Not received The aggregate discount is ₹15 lakh, with corresponding GST of ₹2.70 lakh. The manufacturer has issued the credit notes. However, its available evidence concerning recipient ITC reversal differs across the three transactions. For Distributor A, a declaration confirming reversal is available. For Distributor B, the manufacturer has requested confirmation but has not yet received it. For Distributor C, no confirmation has been obtained. These circumstances raise questions concerning the evidence required to establish compliance. Does the absence of a declaration necessarily establish that reversal has not occurred? Would a declaration, without supporting return information, conclusively demonstrate that the recipient has reversed the correct amount? How should a supplier examine an adjustment where the recipient subsequently revises its accounting or tax treatment? The statutory requirement relates to reversal of attributable ITC. However, the nature and sufficiency of evidence supporting that reversal may become relevant during verification. An important distinction exists between the actual reversal of ITC and the documentation available to establish that reversal. The absence of a particular document should not automatically be equated with an established failure to reverse ITC. Equally, the existence of a declaration should not automatically be regarded as conclusive evidence in every factual situation. The legal consequences depend on the applicable provisions and the evidence available in the individual case. 5. What if the recipient has not availed ITC? Consider another situation where a supplier issues a GST credit note, but the recipient had not originally availed the ITC attributable to the underlying supply. In such a case, there may be no previously availed credit requiring reversal in the ordinary accounting sense. However, the amended Section 15(3)(b) expressly refers to reversal of attributable ITC by the recipient. This creates a distinction between credit that has been availed and subsequently reversed, and credit that was never availed. The treatment of such a transaction requires examination of the statutory wording, recipient’s credit records, and any applicable clarification or judicial development. It would be premature to assume either that a separate reversal entry is invariably necessary or that non-availment automatically satisfies every statutory condition. This is a practical question that may require further clarification, particularly where suppliers cannot directly access the recipient’s complete credit records. 6. Financial credit notes and GST credit notes should not be confused The verification issue discussed above primarily concerns post-supply discounts where a supplier seeks to reduce the taxable value and corresponding GST liability through the statutory credit-note mechanism. A different position may arise where a supplier grants a discount through a financial or commercial credit note without reducing the original taxable value or GST liability. CBIC Circular No. 251/08/2025-GST, dated 12 September 2025, clarifies that where such a financial credit note does not reduce the original transaction value or corresponding tax liability, the recipient is not required to reverse ITC merely on account of that commercial discount. Therefore, before considering evidence of ITC reversal, the nature of the credit note and the actual GST treatment should first be identified. This distinction is important because the issuance of a commercial credit note does not necessarily have the same GST consequences as a credit note through which the supplier seeks a statutory tax adjustment. 7. Documentation during departmental scrutiny The issue of recipient ITC reversal may arise during scrutiny, audit or other departmental proceedings examining a supplier’s credit notes. The supplier may possess the original invoices, discount workings, credit notes and GST return particulars. However, the corresponding ITC reversal may be reflected in the recipient’s records and returns. The information relevant to the adjustment may therefore be distributed between two independent taxpayers. Following withdrawal of the earlier certification circular, businesses may consider maintaining supporting records such as: Credit-note registers containing recipient-wise discount and tax details. Communications informing recipients of the required credit adjustments. Recipient declarations or confirmations, where available. Reconciliations of credit notes with outward GST returns. Records of subsequent corrections, disputes or cancellations. These documents may assist in explaining the transaction and the steps undertaken to establish recipient compliance. However, such records should not be described as a replacement statutory certification mechanism or as universally conclusive proof. Their relevance and sufficiency would depend on the applicable legal requirements and the circumstances of the case. 8. An issue requiring further consideration The 2026 amendments reflect an effort to simplify the GST treatment of post-supply discounts. Nevertheless, the supplier’s ability to reduce the taxable value remains connected to an action undertaken by the recipient. This raises a broader practical question: where one registered person’s tax adjustment depends upon compliance by another registered person, what information can reasonably be expected from the first person to establish that compliance? The withdrawal of Circular No. 212/6/2024-GST removes the previously prescribed administrative procedure for obtaining evidence. It does not, by itself, determine the evidentiary standard applicable to every subsequent transaction. The distinction between the statutory condition and the manner of demonstrating compliance therefore remains important. Any subsequent clarification or judicial consideration specifically addressing this issue would be relevant to both suppliers and recipients. 9. Conclusion The amendments effective from 1 November 2026 simplify the treatment of post-supply discounts by removing the earlier requirements concerning pre-existing agreements and invoice-specific linkage. However, recipient ITC reversal remains an important condition where the supplier seeks the corresponding GST adjustment. The withdrawal of the earlier CBIC certification circular further highlights the distinction between statutory compliance and the documentation used to establish it. For businesses operating through distribution networks, the practical challenge lies in reconciling the supplier’s credit notes with tax adjustments undertaken by independent recipients. The issue is not merely whether a post-supply discount qualifies under the amended provisions, but also how the corresponding tax treatment can be reliably established when relevant information is maintained by different registered persons. The simplification of discount eligibility is a significant legislative development, but effective implementation continues to depend on reliable records and clarity concerning recipient ITC reversal.

Vs Commissioner of GST and Central Excise (CESTAT Chennai) Summary: The Customs, Excise and Service Tax Appellate Tribunal (CESTAT), Chennai, comprising Technical Member Vasa Seshagiri Rao and Judicial Member Ajayan T.V., allowed the appeal filed by Nobel King Purchase Solutions Pvt. Ltd. and set aside a service tax demand of ₹2,23,98,376, together with interest and an equivalent penalty imposed under Section 78 of the Finance Act, 1994. The dispute related to procurement support, vendor management, data management and marine equipment-related services supplied by the Indian company to its Singapore-based associated enterprise, Executive Ship Management Pte. Ltd. (ESM Pte), during the period from 1 October 2014 to 30 June 2017. The Department considered these activities to be intermediary services performed in India and therefore ineligible for the benefit of export of services. The assessee maintained that it independently rendered contracted support services to an overseas recipient and received consideration in convertible foreign exchange. The adjudicating authority, through Order-in-Original No. 24/2021(C) dated 26 October 2021, confirmed the demand by invoking the extended period under Section 73 of the Finance Act, 1994. The Tribunal, however, held that the entire demand was barred by limitation because the assessee had regularly disclosed the relevant export turnover in its statutory ST-3 returns and the Department had failed to establish any deliberate suppression or intention to evade tax. Having accepted the limitation objection, the Tribunal expressly refrained from deciding whether the services were actually intermediary services or exports. Business arrangement and services rendered to the overseas group company: The assessee was part of a corporate group headed by ESM Holdings, Hong Kong, which included entities providing ship management, technical management, crew management, accounting, payroll, software development and procurement-related services. Executive Shipping Service Pte. Ltd., Singapore, entered into ship management agreements with vessel owners, and Executive Ship Management Pte. Ltd., Singapore, performed technical and crew management responsibilities under back-to-back arrangements. ESM Pte subcontracted specific functions to different Indian group companies, including procurement and data management functions to Nobel King Purchase Solutions Pvt. Ltd. Under service agreements dated 1 April 2013 and 1 April 2016, the assessee performed activities relating to sourcing information about marine stores, spare parts, lubricants, equipment repairs and maintenance services, preparing supplier databases, obtaining and comparing quotations, maintaining procurement records and coordinating related documentation. The 2013 agreement contained extensive vendor liaison and procurement activities, whereas the 2016 agreement principally described market information, supplier database maintenance, contract records and document-management functions. The assessee received remuneration on a cost-plus basis, with contractual mark-ups of 8.70% and 5.30% under the respective arrangements. According to the assessee, these services were rendered independently on a principal-to-principal basis to ESM Pte and did not involve a separate contractual relationship with vessel owners. The Department, however, alleged that the assessee facilitated procurement transactions involving ESM Pte, vessel owners and vendors and therefore fell within the definition of intermediary under Rule 2(f) of the Place of Provision of Services Rules, 2012. Department’s allegations and assessee’s defence regarding export of services: Following an investigation by the Directorate General of GST Intelligence, Chennai, involving searches, examination of agreements and recording of statements, the Department issued Show Cause Notice No. 23/2020 dated 25 June 2020. It alleged that the assessee’s services were intermediary activities whose place of provision was India and that the requirements of Rule 6A(1)(d) and Rule 6A(1)(f) of the Service Tax Rules, 1994 had not been satisfied. The Department additionally argued that the Indian and Singapore companies were controlled by common individuals and could be regarded as establishments of distinct persons for the purposes of the export provisions. The assessee contested both propositions. It relied on the decision in SNQS International Socks Private Limited Vs Commissioner of Central Excise and Service Tax, subsequently affirmed by the Supreme Court, to argue that independently supplied services should not automatically be characterised as intermediary services. It also relied on Linde Engineering India Pvt. Ltd. Vs Union of India concerning the separate legal identity of Indian and foreign companies. The assessee further cited Circular No. 159/15/2021-GST, which clarified that subcontracting services on one’s own account does not by itself constitute intermediary activity, and Circular No. 161/17/2021-GST regarding separate legal entities. The assessee also referred to favourable GST proceedings and refund orders concerning similar post-GST services. The Tribunal recorded these rival submissions but ultimately did not adjudicate the intermediary classification or export eligibility questions on merits. Limitation as the preliminary jurisdictional issue: The Tribunal considered that the question of limitation went to the root of the Department’s jurisdiction to raise the demand and therefore required examination before the substantive classification dispute. It analysed Section 73(1) and Section 73(6) of the Finance Act, 1994, observing that the relevant statutory framework permitted recovery proceedings within the ordinary limitation period of thirty months from the relevant date, while the extended period of five years could be invoked only where the specified statutory ingredients existed. These included fraud, collusion, wilful misstatement, suppression of facts or contravention of statutory provisions with intent to evade service tax. The Tribunal emphasised the distinction between mere non-payment or an erroneous interpretation of law and deliberate conduct intended to evade tax. It considered the Delhi High Court decision in Bharat Hotels Ltd. Vs Commissioner of Central Excise (Adjudication), reported in 2018 (12) G.S.T.L. 368 (Del.), which examined the extended limitation provisions and explained that suppression requires deliberate withholding of material information with the requisite intention. The Tribunal also relied on the Supreme Court’s decision in Uniworth Textiles Ltd. Vs Commissioner of Central Excise, Raipur, reported in 2013 (288) E.L.T. 161 (SC), establishing that mere default or non-payment does not automatically amount to wilful suppression. The Tribunal further referred to judicial authorities holding that the burden of proving mala fide conduct lies on the Revenue. Consequently, a finding of suppression could not rest merely on the Department’s disagreement with the assessee’s treatment of its services as exports. Disclosure through ST-3 returns and departmental responsibility to scrutinise: An important factual finding was that the assessee had regularly filed statutory ST-3 service tax returns and had expressly disclosed the amounts claimed towards export of services. The Tribunal examined Part B of the returns, particularly the field designated for amounts charged against export of services provided or to be provided, and found that the assessee had declared the relevant figures, deductions, net taxable value and service tax payable. Significantly, even the adjudicating authority had acknowledged that the assessee regularly filed its ST-3 returns and had largely complied with service tax requirements, which was one reason for not imposing a separate penalty under Section 77. The Tribunal observed that the Department possessed statutory powers and responsibilities to scrutinise returns, examine deductions and call for supporting records wherever necessary. It referred to departmental instructions governing return scrutiny and the decision in Accurate Chemical Industries Vs Commissioner of Central Excise, Noida, reported in 2014 (300) E.L.T. 451 (Tri.-Del.), which emphasised that departmental failure to examine information already disclosed in returns cannot automatically justify invoking extended limitation. The Tribunal also considered Commissioner of Central Excise, Indore Vs Medicaps Ltd., reported in 2011 (24) S.T.R. 572 (Tri.-Del.), concerning the absence of deliberate suppression where relevant information was reflected in statutory returns. It held that the failure of jurisdictional officers to scrutinise disclosed export turnover could not be used against the assessee to extend the statutory limitation period. The Revenue had not demonstrated that the returns were scrutinised, that specific information was requisitioned or that the assessee deliberately withheld information sought by the Department. Absence of wilful suppression and computation of the limitation period: The Tribunal rejected the adjudicating authority’s allegation that references to vendors had deliberately been removed from the service agreement dated 1 April 2016 to avoid tax liability. It found no positive evidence supporting an intention to evade service tax and observed that the statement recorded from the assessee’s Head of Finance did not establish such deliberate conduct. The Tribunal distinguished lawful tax planning or an interpretational position from intentional tax evasion, noting that the assessee consistently maintained that it considered the services exported because the recipient was situated abroad and consideration was received in foreign exchange. The Tribunal also recognised that the classification of the services as intermediary services and the interpretation of the export conditions were contentious legal issues. In this context, it referred to Principal Commissioner of CGST and Central Excise Vs Securities and Exchange Board of India, reported in 2023 (385) E.L.T. 865 (Bom.), together with other authorities concerning suppression, bona fide interpretation and the evidentiary burden on the Revenue. Applying Section 73(6)(i)(a), the Tribunal found that the last relevant ST-3 return had been filed on 14 August 2017. The ordinary limitation period of thirty months therefore expired in February 2020. Since the show cause notice was issued only on 25 June 2020, and the Department had failed to establish grounds for extending limitation, the entire demand relating to October 2014 through June 2017 was time-barred. The Tribunal consequently concluded that the demand could not survive even independently of the dispute concerning intermediary services. Decision not to adjudicate merits and final relief: Having concluded that the demand was wholly barred by limitation, the Tribunal expressly declined to decide the substantive questions concerning intermediary classification and export of services. It observed that any finding on merits would be inconsequential once the recovery proceedings themselves were unsustainable, particularly because the dispute related to the erstwhile service tax regime and there was no question of recurrence of the same demand for a later service tax period. It also noted that Chapter V of the Finance Act, 1994 had been omitted following the introduction of GST, subject to applicable savings provisions, and that the Department had already dropped proceedings concerning similar subsequent transactions under the GST regime. The Tribunal relied on Commissioner of Customs, Central Excise and Service Tax Vs Monsanto Manufacturer Pvt. Ltd., reported in 2014 (35) S.T.R. 177 (All.), which held that an appellate forum should not proceed to adjudicate substantive merits after finding the proceedings barred by limitation. It also referred to Commissioner of Customs, Mumbai Vs B.V. Jewels, reported in 2004 (172) E.L.T. 3 (SC), Commissioner of Service Tax, Mumbai-IV Vs Rochem Separations (India) Pvt. Ltd., reported in 2019 (366) E.L.T. 103 (Bom.), and E.T.A. General Pvt. Ltd. Vs Additional Commissioner of Central Excise, Chennai, reported in 2016 (44) S.T.R. 409 (Mad.), concerning the same principle. Accordingly, the Tribunal set aside Order-in-Original No. 24/2021(C) dated 26 October 2021, quashed the service tax demand of ₹2,23,98,376 and the consequential interest and penalty, and allowed the assessee’s appeal with consequential relief in accordance with law. The ruling reinforces that disclosure in statutory returns, absence of affirmative evidence of tax evasion and mandatory limitation requirements are critical safeguards against belated tax demands. Importantly, the decision grants relief exclusively on limitation and must not be interpreted as a final judicial determination that the assessee’s services qualified as exports or were outside the intermediary category. Cases Discussed / Relied Upon Commissioner of Central Excise Vs H.M.M. Ltd. (Supreme Court); 1995 (76) E.L.T. 497 (SC) — Referred to in the discussion of extended limitation. The decision concerns the evidentiary and statutory requirements governing recovery of duty beyond the normal limitation period. Pushpam Pharmaceuticals Company Vs Collector of Central Excise, Bombay (Supreme Court); 1995 (78) E.L.T. 401 (SC) — Considered through the authorities discussing suppression of facts. The decision establishes that suppression, for purposes of extended limitation, requires deliberate withholding of material information and cannot be equated with every omission. Commissioner of Customs, Mumbai Vs B.V. Jewels (Supreme Court); 2004 (172) E.L.T. 3 (SC) — Relied upon for the principle that where departmental action is found to be beyond the limitation period, the appellate authority may dispose of the proceedings on that ground without examining substantive merits. State Bank of India Vs B.S. Agricultural Industries (I) (Supreme Court); (2009) 5 SCC 121 — Cited through the judgments dealing with time-barred proceedings. The Supreme Court held that deciding the merits of a proceeding after finding it barred by limitation constitutes a legal error. Commissioner of Central Excise, Indore Vs Medicaps Ltd. (CESTAT Delhi); 2011 (24) S.T.R. 572 (Tri.-Del.) — Relied upon concerning statutory returns and suppression. Disclosure of relevant figures in returns cannot ordinarily be treated as wilful suppression merely because the Department considers additional particulars necessary. Uniworth Textiles Ltd. Vs Commissioner of Central Excise, Raipur (Supreme Court); 2013 (288) E.L.T. 161 (SC); (2013) 9 SCC 753 — Extensively considered on extended limitation. The Supreme Court held that mere non-payment is not equivalent to wilful misstatement or suppression and that the burden of establishing mala fide conduct rests on the party alleging it. Accurate Chemical Industries Vs Commissioner of Central Excise, Noida (CESTAT Delhi); 2014 (300) E.L.T. 451 (Tri.-Del.) — Relied upon concerning departmental scrutiny of returns. The Tribunal held that failure by departmental officers to examine information already available in statutory records cannot, without additional evidence, justify invoking the extended limitation period. Commissioner of Customs, Central Excise and Service Tax Vs Monsanto Manufacturer Pvt. Ltd. (Allahabad High Court); 2014 (35) S.T.R. 177 (All.) — Specifically relied upon for the principle that once a tax demand is held barred by limitation, the Tribunal should not proceed to adjudicate the merits of the underlying controversy. E.T.A. General Pvt. Ltd. Vs Additional Commissioner of Central Excise, Chennai (Madras High Court); 2016 (44) S.T.R. 409 (Mad.) — Relied upon as the jurisdictional High Court authority holding that determination of merits after a proceeding has been found time-barred is legally unsustainable. Bharat Hotels Ltd. Vs Commissioner of Central Excise (Adjudication) (Delhi High Court); 2018 (12) G.S.T.L. 368 (Del.) — Extensively quoted concerning Section 73 of the Finance Act, 1994. The High Court explained that suppression requires deliberate conduct directed towards evasion and that mere omission or non-payment is insufficient. Linde Engineering India Pvt. Ltd. Vs Union of India (Gujarat High Court); 2022 (57) G.S.T.L. 358 (Guj.) — Cited by the assessee to support its contention that separately incorporated Indian and overseas group companies do not automatically constitute establishments of the same person under the service tax export provisions. The Tribunal did not decide the substantive issue. SNQS International Socks Private Limited Vs Commissioner of Central Excise and Service Tax (CESTAT Chennai); 2023 (11) TMI 898 — Cited by the assessee on the distinction between independently supplied principal-to-principal services and intermediary activities. The decision was cited as having subsequently been affirmed by the Supreme Court. The issue remained undecided on merits in the present appeal. Principal Commissioner of CGST and Central Excise Vs Securities and Exchange Board of India (Bombay High Court); 2023 (385) E.L.T. 865 (Bom.) — Referred to on the requirements for invoking extended limitation. The absence of suppression, misrepresentation or fraud weighed against the Revenue’s attempt to invoke the longer recovery period. International Merchandising Company, LLC Vs Commissioner of Service Tax, New Delhi (Supreme Court); 2022 (67) G.S.T.L. 129 (SC) — Referred to in connection with interpretational disputes and the absence of deliberate suppression, supporting the Tribunal’s conclusion that a disputed legal interpretation cannot by itself justify extended limitation. Commissioner of Service Tax, Mumbai-IV Vs Rochem Separations (India) Pvt. Ltd. (Bombay High Court); 2019 (366) E.L.T. 103 (Bom.) — Considered for the principle that where proceedings cannot survive on limitation, adjudication of the substantive tax controversy is unnecessary. Commissioner of GST and Central Excise Vs SNQS International Socks Private Limited (Supreme Court); Civil Appeal Diary No. 8343/2024; 19/03/2024 — The dismissal of the Revenue’s appeal was invoked in support of the assessee’s submissions concerning independently supplied procurement services and intermediary classification. The present Tribunal disposed of the appeal on limitation without deciding that classification question.

The statutory CRR remains at 3 per cent of net demand and time liabilities (NDTL). Advt The move comes after the RBI’s October monetary policy meeting, where the Monetary Policy Committee raised the repo rate by 25 basis points to 5.5 per cent and shifted its stance from neutral to “calibrated tightening”.The policy shift comes as the central bank is moving away from the easing cycle and placing greater emphasis on inflation and financial conditions. The RBI has also indicated that near-term rate cuts are off the table. Jefferies estimates that banks would carry around Rs 8 trillion in CRR balances if they were to maintain 100 per cent of the requirement. However, banks already maintain a buffer above the earlier 90 per cent daily threshold.On average, banks maintain around 10 per cent above the required 90 per cent threshold, according to Jefferies. It estimates that the latest change could therefore require banks to raise their balances with RBI by around 5-10 per cent, translating into a potential Rs 400-800 billion withdrawal of liquidity. The liquidity impact, however, is not expected to materially hurt bank profitability. Jefferies estimates the additional liquidity requirement could increase banks’ cost of funds by only 1-2 basis points, resulting in a small dilution of NIMs. The brokerage expects banks may be able to pass on part of the higher funding cost to borrowers, limiting the impact on their margins. Advt The implications could be more significant for NBFCs and corporates, as higher bank funding costs can feed into the broader cost of borrowing. Jefferies said the impact of the CRR tightening would therefore “flow through to NBFCs/corps”, while maintaining its preference for banks over NBFCs in a rising-rate environment. The distinction is important for NBFCs because their funding mix is more reliant on market and wholesale borrowings, making their cost of funds sensitive to changes in the broader interest-rate environment.

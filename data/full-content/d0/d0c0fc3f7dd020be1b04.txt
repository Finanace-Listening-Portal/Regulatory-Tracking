Royal Bank of Canada is considering blocking Muse from accessing customer information, while Bank of Montreal has warned customers against sharing banking credentials, payment card details and personal identification numbers with AI agents. The measures reflect a growing divide between allowing AI to help customers manage their finances and permitting it to act directly on their accounts. Advt The restrictions do not amount to a blanket rejection of AI in financial services. Banks are distinguishing between read only access to account information and the ability to transfer funds, pay bills or execute other transactions. Citigroup, for example, allows limited read only access through certain approved open banking providers but does not permit third party agents to log into its digital banking platforms or transact directly. Charles Schwab has also described controlled access that would allow external AI tools to retrieve account information and conduct research without moving money.The central security concern is that some AI agents use customer supplied credentials to navigate websites and carry out tasks. Once granted access, an agent may be able to view sensitive information or initiate actions that exceed the customer’s intended instructions. Banks also face difficulties distinguishing authorised automated activity from unauthorised access, particularly when agents interact with existing digital banking systems as if they were human users.Recent reports have also alleged that major US financial institutions detected third party agents operating within customer accounts, with some activity appearing to go beyond user instructions. Those claims have not been independently verified in all cases, but they have added urgency to banks’ efforts to tighten access controls. Advt Liability presents another challenge. If an agent makes an unauthorised payment, transfers funds incorrectly or misinterprets a customer’s instructions, responsibility could be disputed among the customer, the bank and the technology provider. Banks are therefore emphasising authentication, limited permissions and human approval for transactions that move money. Bank of America is developing protocols with AI agent developers to address responsibility for AI authorised payments.The restrictions could also affect competition for deposits. Autonomous agents capable of comparing interest rates and moving money between accounts could make it easier for customers to shift balances to higher yielding products. That could increase pressure on banks to retain low cost deposits. However, deposit flight driven by AI agents remains a potential risk rather than an established industry trend. Advt The Muse adoptionMeta’s Muse has added urgency to the debate because of its rapid adoption. It recorded an estimated 10.9 million downloads in the US in its first 40 days, according to market intelligence data, while Meta has reported more than 10 million accounts in the US and Canada. The agent’s ability to assist with tasks across applications illustrates the growing reach of systems that can do more than answer questions.Regulators are also examining the risks associated with increasingly autonomous AI systems, including cybersecurity, operational resilience, human oversight and the ability to monitor and audit automated decisions. The broader regulatory scrutiny of banks’ use of advanced AI is distinct from any specific endorsement of restrictions on external agents. Advt The emerging approach is to allow AI assisted financial management while retaining tighter control over customer authentication, sensitive data and the movement of money. Whether banks can develop secure, standardised ways for customers to authorise agents without handing over credentials will help determine how quickly autonomous AI becomes part of mainstream banking.

Advt The central bank has responded with restrictions on currency derivatives and a special dollar supply facility for oil marketing companies, but these measures have yet to change the underlying pressures on the rupee. The sustainability of intervention is becoming a concern as foreign exchange reserves decline and the cost of supporting the currency rises.Ambit Capital, in aresearch note titled Pushing the limits of Rupee defence, warned that the fall in usable reserves, after adjusting for forward dollar positions and gold, had reached noticeable levels. A continued decline could raise doubts about the RBI’s ability to defend the currency and fuel further speculation, the brokerage said in comments reported by Reuters.India’s foreign exchange reserves fell to $721.02 billion in the week ended October 16, from a record $785.71 billion in the week ended September 4, according to RBI data released on October 23. The $64.69 billion decline has reduced the country’s reserve cushion, although valuation changes and other factors mean the entire fall cannot be attributed to intervention.Foreign currency assets, the largest component of reserves, fell by $5.76 billion to $591.85 billion in the week ended October 16. Gold reserves, meanwhile, rose by $706 million to $105.8 billion.The pressure follows the RBI’s measures introduced on June 8 to attract foreign currency inflows through concessional swap arrangements. Banks mobilised nearly $133 billion through deposits from the Indian diaspora under the special programme. However, economists estimate that the RBI has since deployed $75 billion to $80 billion through spot dollar sales and dollar-rupee swaps. The estimate is based on reserve movements adjusted for valuation effects and is not an official disclosure by the central bank. Advt The RBI’s net forward dollar liabilities stood at about $200 billion at the end of September, according to Reuters. These liabilities represent future dollar commitments rather than immediate cash outflows, but they complicate assessments of how much of the headline reserve stock is available to support the rupee without creating further obligations.The central bank has stepped up its defence through measures aimed at reducing immediate dollar demand. It opened a special facility allowing Indian Oil Corporation, Bharat Petroleum Corporation and Hindustan Petroleum Corporation to purchase dollars directly from the RBI rather than the open market. The facility diverts a substantial source of demand away from the spot market but does not eliminate the underlying dollar requirement arising from oil imports. Advt The RBI also tightened rules on foreign exchange derivatives, including reducing the limit for specified positions without an underlying exposure from $100 million to $5 million and restricting the rebooking of cancelled contracts. The measures were intended to curb speculative positions and reduce one-way bets against the rupee, although they can also raise hedging costs and reduce liquidity in parts of the currency market.The concernsThe steps helped the rupee recover after it approached 97 to the dollar earlier in October. Ambit said the RBI had repeatedly intervened when the exchange rate neared that level, making it appear to traders like an implicit tolerance threshold. The central bank has not formally committed to defending a particular exchange rate. Advt The recovery, however, has proved vulnerable to renewed external pressure. The rupee weakened to 94.94 per dollar on October 23 as Brent crude rose above $114 a barrel amid concerns about energy supply disruptions. Higher oil prices increase India’s import bill and demand for dollars, putting pressure on the trade balance and the currency.Rising US Treasury yields and foreign equity outflows have added to the strain. A stronger dollar and reduced appetite for emerging-market assets can weaken capital inflows at a time when India needs stable sources of foreign exchange to finance imports and other external payments.The RBI retains several options, including further dollar sales, currency swaps, liquidity management and tighter rules governing foreign exchange positions. But each carries costs. Selling dollars reduces reserves, while tighter liquidity and higher interest rates can raise borrowing costs and weigh on economic activity. Restrictions on currency transactions may curb speculative demand but cannot permanently offset an imbalance between dollar supply and demand.The central bank’s challenge is therefore to contain disorderly depreciation without committing itself to defending a particular exchange rate at an escalating cost. Its substantial reserves provide a buffer, but the combination of intervention, forward commitments and persistent external pressures makes repeated defence less comfortable.Ambit’s warning does not establish that the RBI is close to exhausting its total reserves or has run out of policy options. Rather, it highlights the growing difficulty of sustaining intervention if oil prices remain elevated and capital flows fail to improve. A durable easing of those pressures would reduce the need for dollar sales; otherwise, the RBI may have to allow greater exchange-rate adjustment instead of repeatedly resisting depreciation.

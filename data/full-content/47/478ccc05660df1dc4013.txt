Summary: Section 16(2)(b) of the CGST Act requires a registered purchaser claiming input tax credit to establish actual or deemed receipt of goods, with the burden of proof resting on the claimant under Section 155. Disputes frequently arise where purchasers possess tax invoices, e-way bills and banking records but cannot produce freight vouchers, toll receipts or other transport documents, particularly when freight was arranged and paid by the supplier. This article examines the strict evidentiary approach adopted in Ecom Gill Coffee Trading and Malik Traders alongside subsequent decisions recognising that genuine transactions must be assessed on the complete documentary record rather than the absence of any single document. It analyses supplier-paid and buyer-paid freight, GTA reverse charge, bill-to-ship-to transactions and the consequences of retrospective cancellation of suppliers’ GST registrations. The discussion also covers legacy transactions, record-retention requirements, secondary evidence and practical documentation procedures. Drawing on decisions including Safecon Lifescience, LGW Industries, Gargo Traders, Suncraft Energy and Shaurya Alloys, the article explains how bona fide purchasers can substantiate receipt of goods and respond effectively to notices proposing ITC denial. Possession of the invoice, payment to the supplier and filing of returns can be shown from the portal and the bank statement. Receipt of goods is a question of fact, and it has to be proved from the purchaser’s own records, often years after the transaction. Departmental practice since 2017 has made this worse. Officers now routinely ask the purchaser to produce freight vouchers, toll receipts and transport payments even where the supplier delivered the goods at his own cost. Where the supplier’s registration is later cancelled from an earlier date, the purchaser is treated as having bought nothing. Many bona fide purchasers, particularly for FY 2017-18 to 2019-20, can no longer trace the transport papers of transactions that were genuine when they took place. This article looks at the statutory position, the conflicting lines of judicial authority, the reverse charge question on freight, and the records a purchaser should keep so that receipt of goods can be proved when it is questioned. 1. The Statutory Requirement Section 16(2) lists the conditions for ITC: (a) possession of the tax invoice or debit note; (aa) the invoice furnished by the supplier and communicated in GSTR-2B; (b) receipt of the goods or services; (ba) credit not restricted under Section 38; (c) actual payment of the tax to the Government by the supplier; and (d) filing of the return under Section 39. Two features of clause (b) are often missed in practice. First, the Explanation to clause (b) treats the registered person as having received the goods where the supplier delivers them to the recipient, or to any other person on the recipient’s direction, before or during movement. Delivery may be by transfer of documents of title or otherwise. Bill-to-ship-to transactions and delivery to a job worker or a customer’s site are therefore covered. Second, Section 155 places the burden of proving eligibility for ITC on the person who claims it. The real dispute is about how that burden is discharged and when it shifts to the department. 2. Two Lines of Judicial Authority (a) The Strict View: Ecom Gill and the Allahabad Decisions In State of Karnataka v. Ecom Gill Coffee Trading Pvt. Ltd. (13 March 2023), decided under Section 70 of the KVAT Act, the Supreme Court held that invoices and cheque payments are not enough. The dealer must prove the actual physical movement of goods through “the name and address of the selling dealer, details of the vehicle which has delivered the goods, payment of freight charges, acknowledgement of taking delivery of goods, tax invoices and payment particulars etc.”. The Court also said that a bare claim to be a bona fide purchaser is not sufficient and that the burden does not shift to the Revenue. The Allahabad High Court applied this reasoning to GST in Malik Traders v. State of U.P. (Writ Tax No. 1219 of 2021, 18 October 2023). Proceedings under Section 74 were upheld because the purchaser had produced invoices, e-way bills, GR and bank payments but not “payment of freight charges, acknowledgement of taking delivery of goods, toll receipts and payment thereof”. The same approach was followed in Shiv Trading v. State of U.P. (28 November 2023) and in M/s Anil Rice Mill v. State of U.P. (Writ Tax No. 886 of 2023). This is the line officers rely on when they call for freight vouchers. (b) The Balanced View: Genuineness Proved from Statutory Records Later decisions have put limits on the strict view. In Raghuvansh Agro Farms Ltd. v. State of U.P. (Writ Tax No. 3829 of 2025, 17 December 2025), the Allahabad High Court called the adverse inference drawn from non-production of toll plaza receipts “patently or apparently perverse”. The Revenue could not point to any provision of the Act or Rules requiring them. E-way bills, bilty, tax invoices, banking payments to the transporter and the transporter’s ledger had been produced, and no defect had been found in them. In M/s Safecon Life science Pvt. Ltd. v. Additional Commissioner (Writ Tax No. 389 of 2023, 9 September 2025), the same Court held: “Once actual movement of goods as well as payment of tax … have been proved by the petitioner to which no rebuttal has been brought on record at any stage, proceedings under section 74 of the Act cannot be justified.” It also held that intelligence inputs must be verified before they are used against the dealer. The GSTAT, Kolkata Bench, in Pr. Commissioner CGST, Siliguri v. Agarwala’s Bitumex Pvt. Ltd., followed Raghuvansh. It held that toll plaza data is not a statutory test for ITC and that goods need not start from the supplier’s registered premises in a bill-to-ship-to transaction. The most comprehensive statement of the law is now Shaurya Alloys (P) Ltd. v. State of Punjab (CWP No. 34296 of 2024, decided 1 October 2026 by the Punjab and Haryana High Court in a batch of 424 petitions). The Court upheld Section 16(2)(c) but laid down 14 guidelines. Three of them bear directly on receipt of goods: The burden under Section 155 lies on the purchaser. It may be discharged by producing tax invoices, e-way bills, transport receipts, weighbridge slips and stock and consumption records, and the officer must consider these materials and deal with them in the order. Retrospective cancellation of the supplier’s registration, a nil return or an alert may start an inquiry, but none of them is by itself a basis for denying ITC. Where Section 74 is invoked, the notice must state the foundational facts of fraud. The supplier’s fraud cannot be attributed to the purchaser without facts connecting him to it. Commentary on the judgment reads it as holding that once these documents are produced, the initial burden is discharged and the Revenue must establish complicity or collusion. (c) Reconciling the Two Lines The two lines do not truly conflict. Ecom Gill arose from transactions with suppliers whose existence itself was doubtful, and the purchasers had produced nothing beyond invoices and cheques. The later decisions concern purchasers who produced a complete chain of documents that the department did not rebut. The practical rule is that the burden is on the purchaser, but it is a burden of producing contemporaneous evidence, not of producing every document the officer chooses to list. Once a consistent chain is on record, the officer must either find a defect in it or accept it. 3. Freight Arrangements: Who Must Prove What The freight arrangement decides both the evidence of receipt and whether reverse charge applies. Officers often treat these two questions as one. Situation A: Freight Borne by the Supplier (Delivered at the Buyer’s Premises) The supplier engages the vehicle, pays the transporter and includes the freight in the price or in the invoice value. The transport documents and the freight payment are the supplier’s records. Evidence of receipt in the buyer’s hands: the buyer’s copy of the LR or bilty, the e-way bill with the vehicle number in Part B, the delivery challan acknowledged with the buyer’s stamp and date, the gate inward register, the goods receipt note, the weighment slip where relevant, and the stock or production register. The officer’s demand for freight vouchers: the buyer has not paid the freight, so he cannot produce a voucher for it. Ecom Gill lists “payment of freight charges” as illustrative evidence, introduced by “etc.”, and not as a mandatory condition. Raghuvansh holds that adverse inference cannot be drawn from the absence of documents that the law does not require. The appropriate reply is to produce the documents of receipt and, where available, a confirmation from the supplier or transporter, and to point out that the freight payment lies in the supplier’s books. Reverse charge: the buyer has no RCM liability. Under Notification No. 13/2017-Central Tax (Rate), the recipient of GTA service is “the person who pays or is liable to pay freight”. Here that person is the supplier, and any RCM is his liability. Situation B: Freight Borne by the Buyer (Ex-Works or Ex-Godown) The buyer engages the transporter or collects the goods himself. Engaging a GTA that issues a consignment note: if the buyer falls within the specified categories (a registered person, factory, body corporate, partnership firm and others), he pays tax under RCM at 5% unless the GTA has opted for forward charge. GTA under forward charge: RCM does not apply where the GTA is registered, has opted for forward charge through Annexure V, and has issued a tax invoice charging GST with the Annexure III declaration (proviso inserted by Notification No. 05/2022, effective 18 July 2022). The forward charge option is 5% without ITC or a higher rate with ITC. The higher rate, originally 12%, is shown at 18% in current guides. Own vehicle, or an individual truck owner who issues no consignment note: this is not GTA service, so there is no RCM. The buyer must still keep the vehicle log, the fuel and driver expenses and the e-way bill. Evidence of receipt: in this situation the freight documents are the buyer’s own. The GTA invoice or LR, the freight payment through the bank, the RCM payment in GSTR-3B, and the e-way bill showing the buyer’s vehicle or transporter are both the strongest proof of receipt and the records officers most often call for. A caution: the RCM payment on freight and the ITC on the goods support each other as evidence. A buyer who claims to have paid freight for inward goods but has no RCM entry, where RCM applies, weakens his own case on receipt. Situation C: Bill-to-Ship-to and Direct Delivery Where goods are delivered to a job worker, a customer or a project site on the buyer’s direction, the Explanation to Section 16(2)(b) treats the buyer as having received them. The buyer should keep his written instruction to the supplier, the e-way bill showing the ship-to address, and the acknowledgement from the consignee. 4. Retrospective Cancellation of the Supplier’s Registration This issue has hit genuine purchasers hardest since 2017. The settled position is as follows. Decision Holding LGW Industries Ltd. v. Union of India, Calcutta HC, WPA 23512 of 2019, 13 December 2021 ITC cannot be denied to a bona fide purchaser merely because the supplier’s registration was later cancelled retrospectively, provided the genuineness of the transaction is established Gargo Traders v. Joint Commissioner, Calcutta HC, WPA 1009 of 2022, 12 June 2023 At the time of the transaction the supplier was shown as registered on the portal, and payment including tax was made through the bank. Rejection based only on retrospective cancellation, without examining the documents, was set aside Shyamalmay Paul v. Assistant Commissioner, Calcutta HC, December 2025 The purchaser cannot be expected to foresee a future retrospective cancellation. The matter was remanded for verification of the documents Fathima Traders v. Deputy Commercial Tax Officer, Madras HC, 12 June 2026 Orders denying ITC only because the supplier’s registration was cancelled from 1 July 2017 were quashed. The authority must first examine the invoices, e-way bills and lorry receipts Shaurya Alloys, P&H HC, 1 October 2026 The officer must examine the grounds and the effective date of cancellation and whether they bear on the genuineness of the particular supply The same principle applies to supplier default in paying tax. In Suncraft Energy Pvt. Ltd. v. Assistant Commissioner (MAT 1218 of 2023, 2 August 2023), the Calcutta High Court held that ITC cannot be reversed from the purchaser without first proceeding against the defaulting supplier, except in exceptional cases such as collusion or a missing supplier. The Supreme Court dismissed the department’s SLP on 14 December 2023. That dismissal was expressly made “having regard to the facts… and the extent of demand being on the lower side”. It should therefore be cited as an affirmation on those facts, not as a declaration of law under Article 141. An important qualification: in every one of these decisions, relief depended on the purchaser proving that the transaction was genuine. Retrospective cancellation does not defeat a genuine purchaser, but it does not help a purchaser who cannot prove receipt either. 5. The Problem of Old Documents Purchasers for FY 2017-18 to 2019-20 often face the following difficulties: LR copies were kept in physical files that have since been destroyed; the transporter has closed down or cannot be traced; the supplier’s registration is cancelled and he does not respond; the e-way bill printouts were not preserved. The law on retention is in Section 36. Accounts must be kept until 72 months from the due date of the annual return for the year concerned. Where the person is a party to an appeal, revision or other proceeding, the records must be kept for one year after its final disposal or for the 72-month period, whichever is later. A purchaser whose ITC for FY 2017-18 is under dispute today is therefore still required to keep the records, and destruction of records is not a defence. Where the original transport documents are not available, the following secondary evidence may be built up from the purchaser’s own records and the portal: Bank statements showing payment of the invoice value together with GST, and payments to the transporter where the buyer bore the freight; GSTR-2A or 2B reflecting the supplier’s invoice, and the supplier’s GSTR-1 and 3B status for the period; E-way bill details downloaded from the portal, where still accessible, or obtained from the supplier or transporter; Stock register, inward register and production or consumption records showing that the goods entered stock and were used or sold; Corresponding outward supplies, with the buyer’s sales invoices and e-way bills, showing that the goods were onward supplied; The audited financial statements and tax audit report showing the purchase and the closing stock; Confirmation letters from the supplier or transporter, where they can still be obtained; For FY 2017-18 and 2018-19, Circular No. 183/15/2022-GST, which permits a certificate from the supplier’s CA or cost accountant for GSTR-2A mismatches above ₹5 lakh, and a supplier certificate for lower amounts. This circular deals with GSTR-2A mismatch only and does not replace proof of receipt. The reply should explain clearly why the originals are not available. It should then show how the secondary records, read together, establish movement and receipt. A consistent chain of business records is entitled to be weighed on its merits, as Raghuvansh and Shaurya Alloys require. 6. How to Keep Records of Receipt of Goods The following record-keeping system is recommended for every purchase on which ITC is claimed. Stage Document to be kept Purpose Before purchase Supplier’s GSTIN status printout or screenshot dated on the day of the first transaction; PAN and address verification; trade licence where relevant Shows due diligence and that the registration was valid on that date Order Purchase order or written quotation Links the invoice to a genuine commercial requirement Invoice Tax invoice, or e-invoice with IRN, and the e-way bill (Part A and Part B) Shows possession of the invoice and the start of movement Transport, supplier-borne freight Buyer’s copy of the LR or bilty; vehicle number; delivery challan acknowledged with stamp, signature and date Shows delivery at the buyer’s premises Transport, buyer-borne freight GTA invoice or consignment note; freight payment through the bank; RCM entry in GSTR-3B or the GTA’s forward charge declaration; own-vehicle log Shows movement and settles the RCM position Receipt Gate inward entry, goods receipt note, weighment slip, quality inspection note Shows actual receipt, quantity and date Stock Stock register entry with quantity, and its link to the production or consumption record Shows that the goods entered the business Payment Payment through the bank within 180 days of the invoice Satisfies the second proviso to Section 16(2) and supports genuineness Monthly reconciliation Purchase register reconciled with GSTR-2B, with the reconciliation sheet kept on file Detects missing invoices early Supplier follow-up Periodic check of the supplier’s registration status and return-filing status Gives early warning of cancellation or default Preservation Scanned copies indexed invoice-wise, kept in at least two places such as local and cloud Protects records for the full Section 36 period A practical suggestion: the simplest safeguard is a purchase file for each invoice holding, in a single PDF, the invoice, e-way bill, LR, GRN and payment proof. Prepared at the time of purchase, it takes a few minutes. Put together six years later, it may not be possible at all. 7. Points to Be Taken in Reply to a Notice 1. Identify the specific condition of Section 16(2) that the notice alleges was not satisfied. A general allegation of a “fake” or “non-genuine” purchase is not enough. 2. Produce the complete chain of documents and ask the officer to deal with each document in the order, as Shaurya Alloys directs. 3. Where the supplier bore the freight, explain the delivery terms and point out that the freight voucher is not the buyer’s document. 4. Where toll receipts are called for, rely on Raghuvansh Agro Farms and Agarwala’s Bitumex. 5. Where the supplier’s registration was cancelled retrospectively, show that it was valid on the transaction date and rely on LGW Industries and Gargo Traders, Fathima Traders and Shaurya Alloys. 6. Where the notice invokes Section 74, insist that it states the foundational facts showing the purchaser’s own intent to evade tax. The supplier’s fraud cannot be transferred to the purchaser. 7. Ask for copies of the intelligence report or third-party statements relied upon, and request cross-examination where statements are used against the purchaser (Safecon Lifescience; Shaurya Alloys). 8. Ask whether proceedings have been initiated against the supplier and what their status is (Suncraft Energy). Conclusion Receipt of goods under Section 16(2)(b) is a question of fact, and the burden of proving it rests on the purchaser under Section 155. The courts have not reduced that burden. In Ecom Gill and the Allahabad decisions that followed it, credit was denied because the purchaser had produced nothing beyond invoices and payments. The later decisions, Raghuvansh Agro Farms, Safecon Lifescience, the Calcutta and Madras rulings on retrospective cancellation, and Shaurya Alloys, settle the opposite side of the matter. Once a genuine purchaser has produced a consistent chain of contemporaneous records, he cannot be made to suffer for documents the law does not require, for freight he did not pay, or for a cancellation of the supplier’s registration that took place after the transaction. The bona fide purchaser is protected in the courts, but only through his own records. The difference between a purchaser who keeps his ITC and one who loses it is usually the LR copy, the GRN and the stock entry made on the day the goods arrived. For transactions still in dispute from 2017 onwards, the secondary evidence available today should be built up with care. For current transactions, the record-keeping system described above should be followed from now on. ****** Source note: The judgments are cited from their published texts or reliable reports linked above.

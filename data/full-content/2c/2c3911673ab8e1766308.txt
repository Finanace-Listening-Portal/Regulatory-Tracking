Blue Origin LLC founder Jeff Bezos also expressed enthusiasm about the idea, with his company filing paperwork with the US Federal Communications Commission to operate more than 50,000 such satellites. SpaceX Chief Executive Officer Elon Musk, whose company has been getting an increasing share of US national security and defence contracts, has said he envisions as many as 1 million orbital data centres. Although that number might not be realistic — given that operating data centres in orbit presents plenty of more prosaic challenges, such as cooling — even putting a fraction of those in the skies would pose a military defence problem, said former Harvard University astrophysicist Jonathan McDowell, who tracks launches and orbital debris. The US, China, Russia and India have tested ground-based anti-satellite weapons. The Space Force said last month it placed an operational weapon in orbit but declined to say what it was. “If you outsource a lot of your missile defense targeting to these data centers, for instance, then that makes them very much military targets,” McDowell said. “Kinetic anti-satellite weapons could directly target a data center. The other possibility is the vulnerability to jamming.” The worst-case outcome, he said, would be missile attacks against space-based centres that create enough debris to make those orbital areas unusable. Data centres would most likely use a sun-synchronous orbit over the Earth’s poles to stay constantly in sunlight, said Victoria Samson, chief director for space security and stability at the non-profit Secure World Foundation. That type of orbit is also used by many imaging satellites. Axiom Space Inc., a US startup that’s designing orbital data centres, said its systems were built around the assumption they’d be military targets, and “security is a design requirement for orbital compute, not an add-on.” Philip Johnston, chief executive officer of Starcloud Inc., which also aims to develop data centre satellites, said he saw them being less at risk in space than on the ground. The sheer number would distribute operations enough that destroying a few would have little effect, he said. Although the risk of orbital debris and escalation would be enough to dissuade major space powers from a kinetic attack, a nation with less to lose in orbit might not have such qualms. “If all this seems a little half-baked and not very well thought through, that’s because it’s completely half-baked and no one has thought about it at all,” said Jeffrey Lewis, a distinguished fellow at the Foreign Policy Research Institute based in Philadelphia.

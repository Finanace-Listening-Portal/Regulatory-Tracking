Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); Narayan Ramchandra Ghumatkar Vs Kiran Shah (NCLAT Delhi) CoC Approval Does Not Prove Cooperation by Suspended Management: NCLAT Upholds ₹25,000 Costs Introduction The National Company Law Appellate Tribunal, Principal Bench, New Delhi, has held that approval of a resolution plan by the Committee of Creditors (CoC) does not establish that the suspended management fully cooperated with the Resolution Professional. In Narayan Ramchandra Ghumatkar v. Kiran Shah, the Tribunal upheld costs of ₹25,000 imposed for non-cooperation and failure to provide corporate records. The judgment dated 30 October 2026 was delivered by Justice Sharad Kumar Sharma, Member (Judicial), and Arun Baroka, Member (Technical), in Company Appeal (AT) (Insolvency) No. 1766 of 2024. The Tribunal emphasised that cooperation under Section 19(1) of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016, is a continuing obligation. Background of the Proceedings The appellant was a suspended director of KSL & Industries Ltd., against which the Corporate Insolvency Resolution Process commenced on 6 September 2019. Kiran Shah was appointed Resolution Professional on 28 July 2020. He subsequently sought records necessary for conducting the insolvency process, including property title documents, fixed-asset registers, employee records and financial information. Alleging that complete records were not supplied despite repeated communications, the RP filed I.A. No. 350 of 2021 under Section 19(2) read with Section 60(5). The NCLT, Ahmedabad Bench, passed the challenged order on 11 June 2024, recording non-compliance and imposing costs. Ghumatkar appealed against that decision. Suspended Director Claimed Substantial Compliance The appellant maintained that he and the suspended management had supplied extensive information through emails, records and compliance affidavits. The material cited included litigation details, employee information, statutory dues, GST returns, bank statements, deposits, fixed assets and property-related information. He also explained that another director, Mansi Wadkar, who possessed substantial knowledge of the company’s affairs, had died on 2 May 2021. According to him, the remaining directors had limited information but continued to assist wherever possible. The appellant further argued that progress of the insolvency process, including the CoC’s approval of a resolution plan, indicated that sufficient documents had been made available. NCLT Found Documents Still Outstanding The RP maintained that the records supplied were incomplete. Through an affidavit dated 6 March 2024, he placed a further list of outstanding documents and information before the NCLT. The NCLT found that the suspended management had not complied with its direction dated 16 April 2024, that statements made on its behalf were incorrect, and that required documents remained pending. It imposed ₹25,000 each on the respondents identified in its order, payable to the Prime Minister’s National Relief Fund, and directed their appearance with the documents required by the RP. The NCLAT examined whether the costs challenged by the appellant could be sustained. CoC Approval Creates No Presumption of Cooperation The Tribunal expressly rejected the argument that approval of a resolution plan necessarily demonstrated full cooperation. It explained that CoC approval is a commercial decision. The insolvency process may progress using information gathered by the RP from other sources. That progress does not relieve suspended management of its independent responsibility to deliver the company’s records. Accordingly, neither CoC approval nor the pendency of a plan for approval establishes compliance with Section 19. The obligation must be assessed by examining whether the required information and documents were actually supplied. Section 70 Is Not the Exclusive Remedy The appellant argued that misconduct or non-cooperation should be addressed through Section 70 of the IBC, rather than through costs in a Section 19 application. The NCLAT rejected this contention. It distinguished punishment for misconduct during CIRP from the adjudicating authority’s power to impose costs for conduct in proceedings before it. Section 70 does not displace that procedural power. Consequently, the absence of proceedings under Section 70 did not prevent the NCLT from addressing the appellant’s conduct through an appropriate costs order. Wrong Provision Does Not Invalidate an Existing Power The Tribunal accepted that Section 19(2) enables the RP to seek directions but does not itself provide for costs. However, this did not invalidate the order. Relying on P.K. Palanisamy v. N. Arumugham and Ram Sunder Ram v. Union of India, it explained that citing an incorrect provision does not defeat an order where the authority possesses the relevant power. Here, the power to impose costs for conduct in the proceedings was traceable to Section 60(5) read with Rule 11 of the NCLT Rules, 2016. The reference to Section 19(2) therefore did not remove the NCLT’s jurisdiction. Appeal Dismissed The NCLAT found no reason to disturb the conclusion that the appellant had failed to cooperate fully despite earlier document-production directions. It stressed that partial compliance is not full compliance and upheld the ₹25,000 costs challenged in the appeal. The appeal was dismissed and pending applications were closed. The ruling confirms that suspended management must continue providing required records, even when the insolvency process has advanced and a resolution plan has received CoC approval. Cases Discussed 1. P.K. Palanisamy Vs N. Arumugham & Anr. (Supreme Court); Civil Appeal arising out of SLP (Civil) No. 2308 of 2009; 23/07/2009. Relied upon for the principle that an incorrect or omitted reference to the relevant statutory provision does not invalidate an order where the authority possesses the substantive jurisdiction to make it. 2. Ram Sunder Ram Vs Union of India & Ors. (Supreme Court); (2007) 13 SCC 255. Relied upon for the principle that citation of an incorrect statutory provision does not vitiate an exercise of power if that power is traceable to another valid source in law. Respondent No. 1 & 2 are directed to appear with all documents needed by the Resolution Professional on next date of hearing.” 21. Before we could carve out the reasons from the logic assigned is the impugned order of 11.06.2024, a reference to Section 19(2) of the Code becomes necessary to be considered under Section 19(2) reads as under: “Where any personnel of the corporate debtor, its promoter or any other person required to assist or cooperate with the interim resolution professional does not assist or cooperate, the interim resolution professional may make an application to the Adjudicating Authority for necessary directions.” 22. Section 19 obliges the personnel of the Corporate Debtor and the suspended management to assist and cooperate with the RP. Where they do not, Section 19(2) allows the RP to apply to the Adjudicating Authority for directions. The Appellant contends that the information sought was supplied, as shown by the responses of 24.06.2023 and 14.04.2024, and that Section 19(2) does not permit the imposition of costs. The question, therefore, is whether the cost imposed by the impugned order can be sustained. 23. The Appellant also contends that, since the CoC approved the resolution plan, it must be presumed that the documents supplied were sufficient. We do not agree. Approval of a plan by the CoC is a commercial decision. It raises no presumption that the suspended management has cooperated with the RP. The CIRP may proceed on information which the RP gathers from other sources, but that does not relieve the suspended management of its duty. 24. The Appellant next contends that the only remedy for non-cooperation lies under Section 70 of the Code. We do not agree. Section 70 provides for punishment for misconduct in the course of CIRP. It does not take away the power of the Adjudicating Authority to impose costs on a party for its conduct in the proceedings before it. 25. The Appellant relies on the affidavit of 24.06.2023 and the list of 14.04.2024. The RP, by his affidavit dated 06.03.2024, placed on record a list of the documents and information still outstanding, and the impugned order is based on that list. The Adjudicating Authority found that the statement made by the Appellant was not correct and that the documents sought by the RP had not been provided. We see no reason to differ. The pendency of the plan for approval did not relieve the Appellant of the duty to hand over the records of the Corporate Debtor. 26. We accept that Section 19(2) only enables the RP to seek directions, and does not provide for the imposition of costs. It was, therefore, not the appropriate provision, and the appropriate provision ought to have been invoked. However, the invocation of a wrong provision does not vitiate an order when the power exercised exists in law. The Hon’ble Supreme Court in P.K. Palanisamy v. N. Arumugham & Anr. (Civil Appeal arising out of SLP (Civil) No. 2308 of 2009, decided on 23.07.2009) has held that “mentioning of a wrong provision or non-mentioning of a provision does not invalidate an order if the court and/or statutory authority had the requisite jurisdiction therefor.” In doing so, it relied upon its earlier judgment in Ram Sunder Ram v. Union of India & Ors., (2007) 13 SCC 255, where it was held that a reference to a wrong provision of law does not by itself vitiate the exercise of a power, “so long as the power does exist and can be traced to a source available in law”. In that case, the reference to Section 20 of the Army Act, 1950, instead of Section 22, was held not to take away the jurisdiction of the authority under Section 22. Here, the power of the Adjudicating Authority to impose costs on a party for its conduct in the proceedings before it is traceable to Section 60(5) of the Code read with Rule 11 of the NCLT Rules, 2016. The reference to Section 19(2), therefore, does not by itself invalidate the impugned order. Having gone through the merits of the case, we find that the Appellant did not cooperate with the RP. Directions for supply of documents were issued on 08.06.2022 and 15.09.2022. The Ld. Adjudicating Authority found that the documents had not been provided and that the statement of the Appellant was not correct. The duty to cooperate under Section 19(1) is a continuing one, and partial compliance is not full compliance. The cost of Rs. 25,000/- is, therefore, justified. The Company Appeal is dismissed, and the impugned order dated 11.06.2024 passed on I.A. No. 350 of 2021 by NCLT, Ahmedabad, is upheld. 27. All pending interlocutory applications would stand closed.

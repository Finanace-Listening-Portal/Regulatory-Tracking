Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); Tejasvi Bhalla Vs ITO (ITAT Delhi) Verified Business Receipts Cannot Be Taxed in Full Under Section 68 Merely Because the Business Was Omitted From the Return Summary: The Delhi ITAT held that where bank statements and confirmations establish the nature and source of credits arising from the purchase and sale of Duty Entitlement Pass Book (DEPB) scrips, the Assessing Officer must consider the corresponding bank debits and tax the actual profit from the transactions. The assessee had knowingly omitted the business from her return. Even so, once the evidence explained the nature of the receipts, the omission could not justify taxing the entire business credits under section 68. The Tribunal confined the computation to the relevant bank entries and excluded other expenditure claimed by the assessee. Scrutiny Triggered by High-Risk Bank Transactions The assessee filed her return for AY 2017–18 on 13 March 2018, declaring income of ₹4,16,850. The case was selected for complete scrutiny after her PAN was flagged in a Suspicious Transaction Report. The Assessing Officer issued notices under sections 143(2) and 142(1), but the assessee furnished no response before completion of the assessment. The Assessing Officer identified ₹129,50,18,431 in transactions in an Axis Bank account and a ₹2,50,000 cash deposit in another bank account during demonetisation. In the absence of an explanation at that stage, he added the aggregate amount of ₹129,52,68,431 under section 68. The Tribunal condoned a 33-day delay in filing the appeal. Additional Evidence Before the Commissioner (Appeals) The assessee explained that she had traded in DEPB scrips through her proprietorship concern, Arshia Enterprises. She placed additional evidence before the CIT(A), who admitted it and called for the Assessing Officer’s comments. The Assessing Officer submitted two remand reports and emphasised the earlier failure to substantiate the credits. The CIT(A) upheld the addition, pointing to non-disclosure of the business in the return, non-production of books and an audit report, and the failure to discharge the onus under section 68. Bank Debits Had to Be Examined Alongside Credits Before the Tribunal, the assessee accepted that her return was incomplete, no revised return had been filed and only a revised computation was furnished. She argued that the DEPB scrip transactions were carried out through banking channels and should be assessed on the evidence available. The Tribunal noted that financial statements, bank records and confirmations furnished during remand explained the trading receipts. The authorities had focused on bank credits while overlooking corresponding debit entries representing purchases. Tribunal Directed Taxation of Actual Profit The ITAT directed the Assessing Officer to accept the additional evidence already verified during remand, consider relevant bank credits and debits and bring to tax only the actual profit from DEPB transactions. Given the conscious understatement of income, the Tribunal excluded other expenditure claims. The appeal was partly allowed. The order mentions a separate ₹2,50,000 demonetisation-period cash deposit in its factual narration but contains no separate operative finding on that deposit. Author’s Comments The decision illustrates the difference between omitting a business from the return and failing to explain the source of a credit. Once the banking records and confirmations identified the credits as trading receipts, the computation had to take account of both receipts and payments. Relief was limited: the Tribunal directed assessment of actual trading profit on verified bank entries and expressly disallowed consideration of other expenses claimed by the assessee. This appeal is filed by the assessee against the order of ld. Commissioner of Income-tax (Appeals)/National Faceless Appeal Centre (NFAC), Delhi [“Ld. CIT(A)”, for short] dated 30.12.2025 for the Assessment Year 2017-18. 2. At the time of filing of appeal, the Registry has pointed out a defect that appeal is time barred by 33 days. In response thereof, the ld. counsel for the assessee submitted that there was a reasonable cause for the delay in filing the appeal. Accordingly, she prayed that the delay in filing the appeal be condoned. We have heard both the counsels on the issue of condonation of delay. In our considered opinion, there was a reasonable cause for the delay in filing the appeal. Therefore, we condone the delay in filing the appeal before the Tribunal. 3. Brief facts of the case are, assessee filed her return of income for assessment year 2017-18 on 13.03.2018 declaring an income of Rs.4.16.850/-. The case was selected for scrutiny under CASS for complete scrutiny for the reasons of “High Risk Transaction (PAN reported in STR)”. Accordingly, notices under section 143(2) and 142(1) of the Income-tax act, 1961 (for short ‘the Act’) were issued and served on the assessee. In response, there was no response from the assessee till the completion of assessment under section 143(3) of the Act. The AO proceeded to complete the assessment after issuing several notices and the details are given in the assessment order itself. The AO observed that assessee has maintained high risk transaction amounting to Rs.129,50,18,431/- in the bank account maintained with Axis Bank Limited and made another cash deposit of Rs.2,50,000/- in another bank account during demonetization period i.e. 09.11.2016 to 30.12.2016. Since there was no satisfactory response from the assessee, the AO proceeded to make addition of Rs.129,52,68,431/- as unexplained money under section 68 of the Act. 4. Aggrieved with the above order, assessee preferred an appeal before the NFAC, Delhi. Before ld. CIT(A), assessee had filed additional evidences relating to a proprietorship concern in the name of Arshia Enterprise operated by the assessee for purchase and sale of DEPB scrips. It was submitted that the counsel (represented before AO on behalf of assessee) failed to prepare and present before the AO who had failed to submit the same during assessment proceedings. The ld. CIT(A) admitted the additional evidences and remanded the same to the AO. After considering the submissions of the assessee and the additional evidences, the AO submitted two remand reports as per which it was noticed that AO has given several opportunities to the assessee to substantiate the credits in the bank account but it was not substantiated. Further, the assessee has not disclosed these transactions in the return of income and not utilized the opportunity or filed any revised return of income. After considering the rejoinder to the remand report submitted by the assessee, ld. CIT (A) dismissed the grounds and plea of the assessee by observing as under :- “5.31 Looking to the present case it is seen that no detail was filed during the course of assessment proceedings. Further no business activity in the form of M/s Arshia Enterprises was declared in the ITR or the computation of income. Further there were no books of account to substantiate the transactions and no audit report was submitted during the course of assessment proceedings and appellate proceedings. The AO was left with no other option but to assess the credits of Rs.129,52,68,431/- u/s 68 of the IT Act, 1961 as no details whatsoever were filed during the course of assessment proceedings. Further the contentions of the appellant during the course of appellate proceedings have not been found to be cogent and credible by the AO in the remand proceedings. The AO has rightly added the amount of Rs.129,52,68,431/- as unexplained cash credit in the books of the appellant. 5.32 A genuine transaction must be proved to be genuine in all respect. The onus was on the appellant to prove that the transactions in M/s Arshia Enterprises were distinctly genuine and not unexplained. Once the appellant was made aware that the department is in possession of the information of high risk credits in the bank account of the appellant, as per section 101 of the Indian Evidence Act, 1972, the onus was on the assessee to prove that the transactions entered into by him were genuine business activities. 5.33 In the case of Shri Charan Singh vs. Chandra Bhan Singh (AIR 1988 SC 6370), the Hon’ble Supreme Court have clarified that the burden of proof lies on the party who substantially asserts the affirmative of the issue and not upon the party who denies it Section 102 of Indian Evidence Act makes it clear that initial onus is on person who substantially asserts a claim. If the onus is discharged by him and a case is made out, the onus shifts on to deponent It is pertinent to mention here that the phrase ‘burden of proof’ is used in two distinct meanings in the law of evidence viz, ‘the burden of establishing a case’ and ‘the burden of introducing evidence’. The burden of establishing a case remains throughout trial where it was originally placed, it never shifts. The burden of evidence may shift constantly as evidence is introduced by one side or the others. In this case, once the proposition that assessee has unexplained cash credits was proved by the AO, the burden of evidence shifted to the assessee. During the assessment proceeding “and even during the appellate proceeding, the assessee has failed to produce credible evidences to justify the credits of Rs.129,52,68,431/- in his bank accounts as genuine. 5.34 It is essential on the part of the AO to look into the real nature of transaction and what happens in the real word and contextualize the same to such transactions in the real market situation. Further, in the case of McDowell & Co. Ltd. [(1985) 154 ITR 148 (SC)],the Hon’ble Supreme Court have observed as under: ‘Tax planning may be legitimate provided it is within the framework of law. Coloureble devices cannot be part of tax planning and it is wrong to encourage or entertain the belief that it is honourable to avoid the payment of tax by resorting to dubious methods. It is the obligation of every citizen to pay the taxes honestly without resorting to subterfuges.” Every person is entitled to so arrange his affairs as to avoid taxation but the arrangement must be real and genuine and not a sham or make believe. 5.35 Further, in the case of Hersh Win Chadha vs. DCIT, Circle 1(1), International Taxation [(2011 135 TTJ 513 (Delhi)], the Hon’ble ITAT, Delhi has held as under- “‘6.13. It would, at this stage, be relevant to consider the admissibility and use of circumstantial evidence in income tax proceedings. Circumstantial evidence is evidence of the circumstances, as opposed to direct evidence. It may consist of evidence afforded by the bearing on the fact to be proved, of other and subsidiary facts, which are relied on as inconsistent with any result other than the truth of the principal fact It is evidence of various facts, other than “the fact in issue which are so associated with the fact in issue, that taken together, they form a chain of circumstances leading to an inference or presumption of the existence of the principal fact In the appreciation of circumstantial evidence, the relevant aspects, as laid down from time to time are- “(1) the circumstances alleged must be established by such evidence, as in the case of other evidence; 1. the circumstances proved must be of a conclusive nature and not totally inconsistent with the circumstances or contradictory to other evidence. 2. although there should be no missing links in the case, yet it is not essential that every one of the links must appear on the surface of the evidence adduced; some of these links may have to be inferred from the proved facts; 3. In drawing those inferences or presumptions, the Authorities must have regard to the common course of natural events, to human conduct and their relation to the facts of the particular case. 4. The circumstantial evidence can, with equal facility, be resorted to in proof of a fact in issue which arises in proceedings for the assessment of taxes both direct and indirect, circumstantial evidence can be made use of in order to prove or disprove a fact alleged or in issue. In fact, in whatever proceedings or context inferences are required to be drawn from the evidence or materials available or Jacking] circumstantial evidence has its place to assist the process of arriving at the truth.” 5.36 In view of the discussion in the preceding paragraphs, I am constrained to concur with the AO’s findings of fact and decisions thereof, more particularly in the absence of any meaningful and worthwhile submissions/documentations even during the instant appellate proceedings, to counter effectively the position adopted by the AO on the issue of addition of Rs.129,52,68,431/- u/s 68 of the IT Act, 1961. It is seen that the appellant has failed to discharge the onus u/s 68 of the Act and hence the addition of Rs.129,52.68,431/- made by the AO is upheld. The Grounds of Appeal No 1 & 2 are hereby dismissed.” 5. Aggrieved with the above order, assessee is in appeal before us raising following grounds of appeal :- “1. On the facts and in law, the Ld A.O erred in making an addition of Rs.1,29,52,68,431/- under section 68 read with section 115BBE of the Act by treating the entire credits appearing in the bank account of the Assessee as unexplained cash credits whereas the assessee had duly disclosed total turnover amounting to Rs.1,72,94,63,317/-, which includes the credits in the Axis Bank account amounting to Rs.1,29,52,68,431/-. Accordingly, the Learned CIT(A) erred in confirming the said addition. 2. On the facts and in law, the Learned CIT(A) erred confirming the said addition without properly appreciating the evidences on record, the remand proceedings, and the fact that substantial third-party verifications were carried out by the department itself without any adverse findings and treating turnover as unexplained cash credits under section 68. 3. The relief be given to assessee by quashing the hypothetical additions made and cancelling the demand.” 6. At the time of hearing, ld. AR for the assessee brought to our notice findings of the AO and ld. CIT (A), submitted that the return filed by the assessee is incomplete and he also accepted the fact that assessee has not disclosed the business transactions of Arshia Enterprise in the return of income and also accepted that no revised return of income was filed before the lower authorities, only revised computation is filed. He brought to our notice remand report filed by the AO which is placed at page 28 of the paper book and he also brought to our notice page 58 of the ld. CIT(A) order and the relevant findings of the ld. CIT(A). He prayed that the findings of the lower authorities are not proper and assessee has done genuine business transactions relating to purchase and sale of DEPB licenses which can be done only through banking channels. He prayed that the income derived by the assessee out of the above business transactions alone should be considered and whatever the credits in accounts were already stood explained. 7. On the other hand, ld. DR for the Revenue objected to the above submissions and he brought to our notice detailed findings of the lower authorities in remand report and relied on the detailed findings of the ld. CIT(A). 8. Considered the rival summations and material placed on record. We observed that no doubt, assessee has filed return of income without declaring any income earned by her in her enterprises. M/s. Arshia Enterprises is a proprietorship concern wherein assessee has carried on business of purchase and sale of DEPB scrips. It is also fact on record that assessee has not disclosed any profit earned in M/s. Arshia Enterprises. We are aware that assessee has under-disclosed income in her return of income. At the same time, we are of the view that the case of the assessee was selected for scrutiny to verify high risk transactions. As per the information gathered by the AO from Axis Bank, it amounts to Rs.129,50,18,431/- and deposit of Rs.2,50,000/- during demonetization period. Since assessee had not filed any details relating to above transactions, the AO without making proper inquiry disallowed the total credits in the bank. We noticed that there were no details available before the AO during assessment proceedings to appreciate that transactions carried on by the assessee in her bank account. However, in remand proceedings, assessee has submitted all the relevant details substantiating the transactions carried on by the assessee in Axis Bank. We also noticed that assessee filed a financial statement relating to the business carried on by the assessee in purchase and sale of DEPB licenses. It is fact on record that assessee has not disclosed the details of these transactions at the time of assessment proceedings. However, the relevant details were already submitted during remand proceedings. Since all the material evidences were available before the AO during remand proceedings and before learned CIT(A) in appellate proceedings, both the authorities have considered only the credit entries and conveniently overlooked to consider the debit entries in the bank account. Even though the assessee has not disclosed the above said transactions in her return of income, what is relevant to be considered is the actual profit earned by the assessee. Considering the bank statement and confirmation from various parties through whom assessee has purchased and sold DEPB license, credit entries in the bank are explained. Therefore, the AO is supposed to consider both the entries and he cannot consider conveniently only the credit entries. We are aware that assessee has not disclosed these transactions knowingly in her return of income. Still what is to be brought to tax is the real income of the assessee. Since the purchase and sale of DEPB license were routed only through banking channels and there was no involvement of any other method of settlement of the transaction, we are inclined to accept the additional evidences submitted by the assessee. Since the above said additional evidences were already verified by the AO, we have no hesitation to direct the AO to accept the additional evidences, both credit entries as well as debit entries and direct the AO to bring on record only the actual profit earned by the assessee in these transactions. Further, we direct the AO to consider only debit and credit entries in the bank account only. And we are consciously directing the AO not to consider any other expenditure claimed by the assessee as genuine business transactions for the reason that assessee has consciously understated the income earned by the assessee in these transactions. Therefore, we are inclined to partly allow the grounds raised by the assessee. 9. In the result, the appeal filed by the assessee is partly allowed as indicated above.

Joseph (Madras High Court) Struck-Off Company Can Continue Appeal Concerning Contractual Obligations: Madras HC The Madras High Court held that striking off a company under Section 248 of the Companies Act, 2013 does not prevent it from continuing an appeal against a decree enforcing its contractual obligations. The Court explained that Section 250 preserves the company’s capacity for specified purposes, including the discharge of its liabilities and obligations. On the merits, the Court set aside the decree for specific performance, finding that the purchaser had failed to establish continuous readiness and willingness to perform his contractual obligations. Facts of the Case The dispute arose from a sale agreement dated 21 April 2005 concerning properties described as measuring more than 300 acres across five villages. The agreed price was ₹16,250 per acre, and the transaction was scheduled for completion by 22 July 2005. The purchaser paid ₹10,00,001 when the agreement was executed and a further ₹20 lakh on 5 May 2005. Under the agreement, he was required to arrange a survey at his own expense to ascertain the actual extent available for sale. The final payment depended on the extent identified through that exercise. The purchaser claimed that the survey revealed only 205.15 acres and sought specific performance for that extent upon payment of the proportionate balance consideration. He also claimed possession and improvements to the properties. The landowners disputed the alleged survey, shortage of land, possession and readiness and willingness. Certain properties were subsequently sold to other purchasers. The trial court granted specific performance. During the appeal, a preliminary objection arose because the defendant companies had been struck off. Section 250 Permits Continuation of Proceedings Concerning Subsisting Obligations The High Court rejected the contention that the striking-off action could simply be treated as ineffective because the pending litigation had not been brought to the Registrar’s notice. It observed that a challenge to the striking off had to be pursued through the statutory remedy under Section 252. However, the consequences of striking off remained subject to Section 250. That provision preserves specified purposes relating to recovery of amounts due to the company and payment or discharge of its liabilities and obligations. The Court explained that discharge of a contractual obligation is not confined to performance. It can also involve termination or other legally recognised modes of bringing the obligation to an end. Consequently, defending the validity of the company’s refusal to perform the contract fell within the scope of the statutory exception. Since the appeal challenged a decree enforcing an obligation arising from the sale agreement, the company could continue the proceedings despite being struck off. The subsequent purchasers had also been brought on record and were available to pursue the appeal. Time Was Not Treated as the Essence of the Contract The High Court did not accept the landowners’ argument that the agreement became unenforceable merely upon expiry of 22 July 2005. Their subsequent conduct, including meetings and consideration of requests for extension, showed that they had not treated the stipulated date as an inflexible deadline. Nevertheless, the Court clarified that the purchaser still had to perform within a reasonable time and establish continuous readiness and willingness. Purchaser Failed to Prove Survey and Readiness and Willingness The purchaser’s case substantially depended on Exhibit A6, which he relied upon to establish that only 205.15 acres were available. The Court found material defects in that document. It was undated, omitted certain villages and survey numbers covered by the agreement, and included lands outside its schedule. It did not explain the survey methodology, participants or comparison between the contracted properties and the land allegedly available. The Court concluded that Exhibit A6 was not a genuine survey report and had been subsequently created for the suit. The purchaser had also failed to communicate the alleged survey results and shortage to the landowners. His legal notice omitted material particulars concerning the alleged survey, reduced extent and proportionate balance consideration. Considering these omissions, the delay and the inconsistencies in the evidence, the Court held that the purchaser had failed to prove continuous readiness and willingness. Payment of a substantial advance did not overcome those deficiencies. Agreement Recitals Did Not Establish Delivery of Possession The Court rejected the inference that possession must have been delivered merely because the agreement contemplated a survey and subsequent taking of possession. Independent supporting material was necessary to establish actual delivery. The purchaser’s oral evidence did not adequately prove possession or the claimed improvements. The subsequent purchasers, meanwhile, had produced title documents, revenue records and tax receipts supporting their possession and enjoyment. Claim for Reduced Extent Was Not Barred by Section 12 The Court separately rejected the landowners’ contention that the purchaser was impermissibly seeking specific performance of only part of the contract. The agreement itself contemplated determining the actual extent through a survey and calculating the final consideration accordingly. Therefore, seeking conveyance of the extent properly identified under that arrangement would not, by itself, attract the restriction under Section 12 of the Specific Relief Act, 1963. The purchaser’s failure lay in proving the alleged survey and performance of his obligations. Subsequent Purchasers Held to Be Bona Fide Purchasers Without Notice The High Court found that knowledge of the earlier agreement had not been established against the subsequent purchasers. The agreement was unregistered, and no public notice regarding it had been issued. Considering the evidence as a whole, the Court held that the subsequent purchasers were purchasers for value without notice of the earlier agreement. A lower purchase consideration did not, by itself, establish lack of bona fides. Decision The High Court allowed the appeal, set aside the trial court’s judgment and decree dated 29 April 2008, and dismissed the suit. Since the plaintiff had not sought any alternative or additional relief, the Court declined to entertain another claim in view of Section 22(2) of the Specific Relief Act. No costs were awarded. The judgment recognises the limited statutory capacity of a struck-off company to continue proceedings concerning subsisting contractual obligations. It also underscores that a purchaser seeking specific performance must substantiate compliance with essential contractual duties through reliable evidence and consistent conduct.

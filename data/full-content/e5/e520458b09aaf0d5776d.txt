Already Upgraded? Log in. When GSTN Fails, Can the Taxpayer Be Penalised? Bombay High Court Protects Import ITC Against ICEGATE–GSTN Integration Failure| An analysis of Maksur Analytical Systems v. Union of India and the development of the law on GSTR-2A mismatches, import IGST and the probative value of Bills of Entry. Summary: In Maksur Analytical Systems v. Union of India (Bombay High Court), W.P. No. 7551 of 2026, decided on 29 October 2026, the Bombay High Court examined the denial of import-related input tax credit arising from an alleged failure of data transmission between ICEGATE and GSTN. The importer stated that it had paid IGST of ₹1,19,38,621 on goods imported during FY 2019–20, but the corresponding amount was not reflected on the GST portal. It furnished Bills of Entry and reconciliation records, yet the adjudicating and appellate authorities proceeded on the electronic discrepancy. The Court considered the apparent systems-integration failure and the importer’s lack of control over the transmission. It set aside the orders dated 29 August 2024 and 29 January 2026 and directed reconciliation and appropriate reflection of the import IGST in the Electronic Credit Ledger. Crucially, the Court expressly left the substantive merits of ITC eligibility open. The article analyses section 16 of the CGST Act and Rule 36(1)(d), distinguishing import documentation from domestic supplier reporting. It compares the reasoning with Bharti Airtel on self-assessment and portal data, Suncraft Energy on supplier-reporting mismatches, Ecom Gill Coffee on the taxpayer’s evidentiary burden, and Amar Iron Udyog on Customs-side transmission failures. For importers, the practical response is to preserve Bills of Entry, IGST payment confirmations, Out of Charge records, accounting evidence and portal reconciliations, and to seek verification from Customs before accepting a demand founded solely on missing electronic entries. The decision supports evidence-based adjudication; it does not waive statutory conditions or automatically allow every import-credit claim. The GST portal has made tax administration more transparent and technology-driven. The issue arises when the electronic system fails to capture a transaction that has actually taken place. Can a taxpayer be denied Input Tax Credit simply because the transaction doesn’t appear on the GST portal, when the taxpayer has documentary evidence of tax payment? The Bombay High Court addressed this issue in Maksur Analytical Systems v. Union of India & Ors., W.P. No. 7551 of 2026, decided on 29th October 2026. The judgment is particularly pertinent for importers whose IGST payments (made at the time of import) are not reflected on the GST portal due to technical or integration issues between ICEGATE and GSTN. This decision matters not just for the relief it provides to the taxpayer. It’s that the Court has acknowledged that a taxpayer cannot be held responsible simply because of a technological failure in the Government’s own electronic infrastructure. The dispute: Tax paid at the port, credit missing on the portal The petitioner, Maksur Analytical Systems, imported goods in FY 2019-20 and paid IGST of ₹1,19,38,621 at the time of import. However, the corresponding credit was not reflected in the petitioner’s ECL on the GST portal. The petitioner said the issue arose due to a technical glitch in the integration between ICEGATE and GSTN. The petitioner contended that the IGST paid on imports was required to be electronically transmitted from the Customs system to the GST portal. The transmission had not occurred properly. Importantly, the petitioner responded to the discrepancy notice by providing the Bills of Entry and reconciliation statements demonstrating payment of IGST. Despite these documents, the authorities refused the corresponding ITC. The writ petition challenged the order of 29 August 2024 and the subsequent appellate order of 29 January 2026. The main complaint was that the taxpayer had paid the tax, had documentation to prove it, and was being denied credit because the Government’s electronic systems failed to pass on the information. What did the Bombay High Court hold? The Division Bench (Justices M.S. Karnik and Sandesh D. Patil) considered the facts in relation to the non-reflection of IGST. The Court observed that the non-reflection of IGST was, prima facie, due to a failure of integration between ICEGATE and GSTN (both electronic systems maintained and controlled by the respondent authorities). The Court also noted that the Petitioner had no part in such a transmission. The following observation captures the essence of the judgment: ‘The Petitioner therefore cannot be made to suffer as a result of technical or systemic failures which are the fault of the Authorities themselves.’ The Court also held that the challenged orders were unsustainable because they were based entirely on the non-reflection of IGST on the GST portal, despite the petitioner producing documents showing that tax had in fact been paid. Consequently, the Court: Quashed the adjudication order of 29 August 2024. Quashed the appellate order of 29 January 2026. Directed the authorities to reconcile and properly consider the IGST of Rs.1,19,38,621. Directed due reflection of the corresponding amount in the petitioner’s Electronic Credit Ledger on the GSTN portal. However, para 8 of the judgment contains an important qualification. The Court made it clear that it had not considered the merits of the ITC claim at all and that all parties’ contentions remained open. The judgment is not to be read as conferring an absolute right on the ITC simply on the production of a Bill of Entry. Its import lies in rejecting a purely mechanical refusal on the basis of a system discrepancy. The statutory question: Does s16 require portal reflection in all cases? This may well be the most important aspect of the whole controversy. Section 16 of the CGST Act deals with the eligibility and conditions for claiming ITC. Under s16(2), the registered person must satisfy the prescribed statutory conditions: possession of the prescribed document; receipt of the goods or services; actual payment of tax to the Government; and filing the relevant return. Section 16(2)(aa) (coming into effect 1st January 2022) also requires the supplier to provide and pass on to the recipient the details of the invoice or debit note referred to in section 16(2)(a). However, import transactions do require a separate look. Under Rule 36(1)(d) of the CGST Rules, a Bill of Entry, or a like document prescribed under the Customs Act for the purpose of assessing IGST on imports, is a document which is recognised for the purpose of availing ITC. This distinction matters. In a normal domestic B2B transaction, the recipient generally relies on the supplier’s invoice and the associated reporting mechanism. In an import transaction, the relevant tax is paid via the Customs mechanism and the Bill of Entry is the prescribed document. Hence, the statutory treatment of import IGST cannot be equated mechanically with the reporting obligations of a domestic supplier. Section 16(2)(aa) refers to the invoice or debit note referred to in Section 16(2)(a). A Bill of Entry is separately provided for in Rule 36(1)(d). Hence, the Department must look at the relevant statutory provisions on import credit rather than assume that every non-reflection in GSTR-2A or GSTR-2B amounts to automatic disqualification. Supreme Court’s approach in Union of India v. Bharti Airtel Ltd The Supreme Court’s decision in Union of India v. Bharti Airtel Ltd., (2022) 4 SCC 328 is relevant to the evidentiary value of the GSTR-2A. The Supreme Court has made it clear that the GSTR-2A is a facilitative document to assist the taxpayer in carrying out self-assessment. The Court made it very clear that the taxpayer’s books of account and primary records are the bedrock upon which the question of ITC eligibility is determined. However, you have to understand the context of Bharti Airtel. The issue was that you couldn’t correct the GSTR-3B returns for an earlier period, as GSTR-2A wasn’t in operation at that time. The Supreme Court didn’t rule that every discrepancy between GSTR-2A and GSTR-3B could be disregarded. Nonetheless, the decision establishes an important principle: information available electronically on the portal cannot substitute for the statutory framework or the taxpayer’s own records. This is particularly pertinent in Maksur Analytical Systems, where the taxpayer relied on Bills of Entry and evidence of actual IGST payment. The issue isn’t whether the taxpayer can simply ignore the GST portal. The issue is whether the portal’s failure can trump documentary evidence of a genuine payment. Suncraft Energy: Can ITC be denied simply because the supplier hasn’t reported the transaction? Another key decision is Suncraft Energy Private Limited v. Assistant Commissioner, State Tax, decided by the Calcutta High Court on 2 August 2023. The issue concerned ITC claimed by the purchaser where the corresponding transaction was not correctly reflected in GSTR-2A because the supplier failed to report it. The Calcutta High Court rejected a mechanical reversal without properly examining the transaction and the circumstances of the supplier’s default. The judgment is significant in that it acknowledges that a mismatch is not, by itself, a sufficient basis to rule out eligibility for ITC. The Department must look at the facts and then take the appropriate steps in accordance with the law. The Supreme Court dismissed SC Revenue’s challenge on 14th December, 2023. Dismissing an SLP without a judgment is not a declaration of law by the Supreme Court under Article 141. There’s an interesting comparison in that. At Suncraft Energy, the mismatch stemmed from the supplier’s failure to report. At Maksur Analytical Systems, the mismatch stemmed from a failure to integrate two government-owned electronic systems. If, in the first instance, we require proper fact-checking before we will go ahead and deny ITC, then we have equally good grounds to reconcile before we will go ahead and deny the credit in the second instance. Ecom Gill Coffee: The taxpayer must still establish the genuineness of the claim A balanced discussion must also consider the Supreme Court’s decision in State of Karnataka v. Ecom Gill Coffee Trading Pvt. Ltd., (2023) 3 SCC 629. The Supreme Court held that producing tax invoices and evidence of payment by cheque was not enough to establish the genuineness of the transactions under the Karnataka VAT Act. The buyer had to prove the actual movement and delivery of the goods with appropriate supporting evidence. The Revenue frequently cites this judgment in ITC disputes. However, its use must be read in context. At Ecom Gill Coffee, the authenticity of the underlying purchase transactions was in doubt. At Maksur Analytical Systems, the issue concerned the IGST paid on import at the point of entry and the subsequent failure of electronic transmission between ICEGATE and GSTN. The distinction is important. The taxpayer must establish the underlying transaction and meet the requisite statutory conditions for ITC. Once, however, the actual import and payment of IGST are supported by the requisite documentation, the Department cannot substitute a portal discrepancy for an examination of the documentation. Ecom Gill Coffee does not refute the reasoning in Maksur. It simply underlines the need for proper evidence-based verification. Amar Iron Udyog: The Calcutta High Court had already examined a similar import-credit problem An important judgment which came before Maksur Analytical Systems was M/s. Amar Iron Udyog Pvt. Ltd. & Anr. v. Union of India & Ors., WPA 19648 of 2025, decided by the Calcutta High Court on 13th January 2026. The case concerned ITC claimed on imported goods where the corresponding IGST payments were not reflected on the GST portal. The Customs authorities told the Court that certain Bills of Entry were processed manually. Although the goods had been cleared, the Out of Charge details hadn’t been entered into the electronic ICES system at the same time. The relevant info wasn’t passed to the GST portal. The Court directed Customs to provide reports on the actual payment of IGST and the reasons for its non-reflection. After considering the reports, the Court remanded the matter to the appellate authority for fresh consideration. Amar Iron Udyog’s importance lies in its practicality. Rather than treating the failure to reflect on the portal as being the end of the matter, the Court required the Customs authorities to confirm the actual payment and to explain the electronic discrepancy. The Bombay High Court has now adopted a similar approach in the Maksur Analytical Systems case. However, the relief afforded is different. While Amar Iron Udyog remitted the matter for reconsideration in light of the Customs reports, the Bombay High Court set aside the impugned orders and specifically directed that reconciliation and due reflection of the import IGST be made in the Electronic Credit Ledger. Both decisions show that Customs records and the original import docs must be given weight before an ITC claim is dismissed. The real distinction: Non-payment of tax versus non-reflection of tax In my view, this is the most significant legal distinction in the present dispute. These two scenarios must never be treated as being the same. Situation A: Tax has not in fact been paid. Where the taxpayer is unable to establish actual payment of IGST, the Department is entitled to go into the claim under the relevant statutory provisions, including Section 16(2)(c). Situation B: Tax has been paid, but the portal doesn’t show it. Where the taxpayer has a valid Bill of Entry, evidence of IGST payment and the other requisite import documents, the Department must look at the actual transaction and reconcile the difference. The mere absence of an electronic entry does not, by itself, establish that tax hasn’t been paid. This is, of course, an important distinction, as the importer has no control over data transmission between ICEGATE and GSTN. The Government is responsible for the e-infrastructure. The taxpayer’s statutory duties should not be equated with the administrative responsibility of ensuring that the various Government systems work together properly. Is this also a matter of administrative fairness? The judgment has implications which go well beyond the interpretation of s16. GST administration increasingly relies on automated systems, electronic matching, and data integration. These systems are designed to make compliance easier. They shouldn’t become independent sources of onerousness where the underlying transaction is supported by verifiable records. Consider the position of an importer who: Imports goods through the prescribed Customs procedure; Pays applicable IGST at the point of import; Obtains a Bill of Entry; Receives and records the imported goods; Maintains the relevant accounting records; and Subsequently, it is established that the IGST is missing from the GST portal. If the Department refuses to review these documents and instead relies solely on portal data, the taxpayer will effectively face a second financial burden. The Bombay High Court reasoned that such an approach would require judicial review. The general principle is that admin tech must enable the implementation of tax legislation. It must not override any substantive rights and obligations under the statute. Practical implications for importers and GST practitioners The judgment provides a useful basis for addressing import credit discrepancies. However, taxpayers should not rely on the judgment as a substitute for keeping proper records. In cases involving non-reflection of import IGST, the following documents should be compiled: Relevant Bills of Entry. Customs duty and IGST payment challans. ICEGATE payment confirmations. Import clearance and ‘Out of Charge’ records. Evidence of receipt and accounting of imported goods. GSTR-2A / GSTR-2B reconciliation statements where applicable. Electronic Credit Ledger extracts. Correspondence with Customs relating to non-transmission. Relevant import documentation and the associated supporting accounting records. Where the discrepancy is due to manual processing or a system integration failure, the taxpayer should specifically enquire with the Customs authorities. The response to the show cause notice should distinguish between the actual payment of tax and the electronic recording of that tax. A demand based purely on portal data, without regard to the relevant primary documents, may provide a basis for challenging the adjudication and the appellate orders. What this judgment does not decide It’s just as important not to overstate the ruling. The Bombay High Court clearly stated that it did not consider the merits of the ITC claim. Therefore, the judgment does not establish that: Every Bill of Entry automatically entitles the importer to Input Tax Credit. Every mismatch in a GSTR-2A or GSTR-2B must be ignored. The conditions under section 16 can be waived. Documentary evidence is unnecessary. The Department cannot verify actual IGST payment. The taxpayer will still have to establish compliance with the relevant statutory requirements. The judgment addresses a much more limited but very important issue: the Department cannot have a valid basis for an ITC denial simply on the grounds of non-reflection arising from a technical failure in its own electronic systems, without properly considering evidence of payment of tax. Cases Discussed Amar Iron Udyog Pvt. Ltd. & Anr. v. Union of India & Ors., WPA 19648 of 2025, decided 13 January 2026 (Calcutta High Court) — Customs records and electronic transmission discrepancies required examination before reconsideration of import-credit denial. Suncraft Energy Private Limited v. Assistant Commissioner, State Tax, 2023 SCC OnLine Cal 2226, MAT 1218 of 2023, decided 2 August 2023 (Calcutta High Court) — A domestic supplier-reporting mismatch required proper enquiry; the Supreme Court dismissed the related SLP on 14 December 2023. State of Karnataka v. Ecom Gill Coffee Trading Pvt. Ltd., (2023) 3 SCC 629, Civil Appeal No. 230 of 2023 (Supreme Court) — Under the Karnataka VAT law, the claimant had to establish genuine transactions, beyond invoices and cheque payments. Union of India v. Bharti Airtel Ltd., (2022) 4 SCC 328 (Supreme Court) — GSTR-2A was facilitative in the relevant period; self-assessment and statutory rectification restrictions remained controlling. Conclusion: Technology cannot become a substitute for the statute The GST regime was introduced to create a transparent, technology-driven indirect tax system. But technology is a means of delivering the law, not the law. A taxpayer who paid IGST at the time of import should not be deemed to have lost the corresponding credit simply because the information hasn’t moved from one Government portal to another. The Dept certainly has the power to verify the transaction’s authenticity, review the Bill of Entry, and confirm that the statutory conditions have been met. What it absolutely cannot do is treat the lack of an electronic entry as proof that tax wasn’t paid, especially where the taxpayer has produced the relevant primary documentation to support their position. Read alongside Bharti Airtel, Suncraft Energy and Amar Iron Udyog, the Bombay High Court’s decision provides an important basis for insisting upon evidence-based adjudication rather than mechanical portal-based denial. Ultimately, the distinction is simple: A failure of data transmission is not necessarily a failure of tax compliance. And where the taxpayer has fulfilled its statutory obligations, the consequences of an administrative or technological failure should not automatically fall on the taxpayer.

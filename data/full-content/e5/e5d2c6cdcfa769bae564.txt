Summary: The Central Board of Indirect Taxes and Customs (CBIC), through Instruction No. 02/2026-GST dated 30 October 2026, has directed Central GST field formations to implement three major taxpayer-friendly reforms recommended at the 57th GST Council meeting held on 8 October 2026, without waiting for the proposed statutory amendments scheduled to take effect from 1 April 2027. The Instruction directs that no further arrests be carried out under GST, restricts interception of goods in transit to cases involving specific intelligence and written authorisation by an officer not below the rank of Joint Commissioner, and discourages issuance of show cause notices under Sections 73, 74 and 74A where the aggregate amount involved is below Rs. 10,000. The reforms seek to reduce coercive enforcement, unnecessary detention of goods, low-value litigation and compliance costs while promoting a more trust-based GST administration. However, the Instruction is an interim administrative measure addressed to Central Tax officers and does not itself amend the CGST Act or automatically bind State GST authorities. Statutory powers relating to arrest, prosecution, summons, search, detention, confiscation and recovery continue to exist until the relevant legislative provisions are amended. The article examines the legal nature and operational reach of these directions, the safeguards applicable to e-way bill interception, the computation and interpretation of the Rs. 10,000 notice threshold, and the position of pending proceedings. It also discusses the practical limitations arising from jurisdictional restrictions, unresolved procedural questions and the absence of corresponding State-level instructions. The central takeaway is that CBIC has initiated significant administrative relief ahead of legislative reform, but taxpayers must distinguish an enforceable statutory amendment from an interim departmental direction. Conclusion Show all 16 sections Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); Introduction: Reform First, Amendment Later CBIC has told its officers to stop GST arrests, stop random truck checks and stop issuing show cause notices for less than Rs. 10,000 — with immediate effect, well before Parliament amends the law. Normally, a GST Council recommendation remains only a recommendation until the CGST Act is amended. The Council, in its 57th meeting on 08.10.2026, proposed that the amended provisions come into force from 01.04.2027. CBIC chose not to wait. Through Instruction No. 02/2026-GST dated 30.10.2026, the Central Government has asked its field formations to start following three of those reforms now, as an interim measure of trade facilitation. This article explains what the Instruction says, why the Government has taken this route, what it changes on the ground, and — equally important — what it does not change. Legal Nature and Reach of the Instruction The Instruction is an administrative direction to Central Tax officers; it does not amend the CGST Act, and it does not, by itself, bind State GST officers. This distinction matters in practice. Section 69, Section 129, Section 130 and Sections 73/74/74A remain on the statute book exactly as they stand today. What has changed is how CBIC officers are told to use those powers in the interim. The Instruction does not cite the provision under which it is issued; the Board’s general power to issue orders, instructions or directions to central tax officers for uniformity in implementation is found in Section 168(1) of the CGST Act, 2017. 1. Reform 1: No Further Arrests Under GST (Para 3.1) CBIC officers have been directed that “no further arrests may be carried out under GST”, in anticipation of the omission of Section 69 of the CGST Act. What the Law Says Today Section 69 allows the Commissioner, where he has reasons to believe that a person has committed a specified offence under Section 132 (broadly, cases above the monetary thresholds or repeat offences), to authorise the arrest of that person. Arrest has been one of the most feared tools in GST, particularly in fake-invoice and ITC cases. The Supreme Court in Radhika Agarwal v. Union of India (2025) upheld the constitutional validity of Sections 69 and 70, but laid down safeguards: “reasons to believe” must be recorded on tangible material, the grounds of arrest must be supplied, and the procedural protections of the criminal law apply. What Changes Now Aspect Before 30.10.2026 From 30.10.2026 (interim) After Amendment (proposed) Power to arrest (Section 69) Available to Commissioner Not to be exercised by CBIC officers Section 69 omitted Offences under Section 132 Exist Continue to exist To be rationalised (Council has also proposed narrower offences and a higher prosecution threshold) Prosecution With sanction under Section 134 Still possible Still possible, subject to amended thresholds Summons (Section 70), search and seizure (Section 67) Available Not affected Not affected by this reform Rationale Arrest is disproportionate for a tax dispute.GST is a self-assessed, documentation-based tax. Most disputes concern classification, valuation or ITC eligibility. Arrest in such matters causes hardship that cannot be undone even if the taxpayer later succeeds. The Instruction itself refers to “reducing hardships owing to arrests” (para 2). Trust-based law.The Instruction describes the aim as making the law “more trust based and forward looking”. This is consistent with the Government’s wider policy of decriminalising economic offences. Revenue is protected by other tools.Provisional attachment (Section 83), blocking of electronic credit ledger (Rule 86A), recovery provisions (Section 79) and prosecution remain available. Arrest is not needed to secure revenue. Reducing litigation.Arrest has generated a large volume of bail and writ litigation. Removing it frees both the department and the courts. Points to Note The word “further” means the direction applies to arrests after 30.10.2026. It does not, by its terms, release persons already arrested, nor does it withdraw pending prosecution complaints. Pending bail matters will continue on their own merits; the Instruction may, however, be placed before the court as a relevant policy development. The Instruction covers arrest under GST Proceedings under other enactments, where independently applicable, are outside its scope. For the broader legal position, see 57th GST Council’s Arrest and Prosecution Reforms: Do They Apply to Pending Cases? and GST Search, Summons, Arrest and Attachment: Limits on Department Powers. 2. Reform 2: E-Way Bill Interception and Verification (Para 3.2) A CBIC officer can now stop a vehicle only on specific intelligence approved in writing by a Joint Commissioner, can act only if the supplier or recipient falls in his own State, and cannot confiscate goods in transit at all. The Present Framework Section 68 read with Rules 138A to 138C of the CGST Rules empowers the proper officer to intercept any conveyance and verify the e-way bill and documents. Where goods are found moving in contravention, they may be detained or seized under Section 129 (with penalty of 200% of tax, or, for exempt goods, 2% of value or Rs. 25,000, whichever is less, where the owner comes forward). Section 130 provides for confiscation where there is intent to evade tax. Circular No. 64/38/2018-GST dated 14.09.2018 had already said that minor discrepancies should not lead to detention. The Six Directions Clause Direction What It Means in Practice (i) Interception only on specific intelligence, authorised by an officer not below Joint Commissioner, who must record in writing the reasons to believe that goods are moving in contravention Random or routine checking by CBIC officers stops. The authorisation and recorded reasons become part of the record and can be called for. (ii) The intercepting officer may inspect only if the supplier or recipient is registered (or, if unregistered, located) in his own State/UT. If contravention is found, goods may be detained/seized under Section 129 No inspection by officers of a “transit” State. (iii) If neither supplier nor recipient is in the jurisdiction, and a contravention appears prima facie, the officer must let the vehicle go and only pass the information to the officer having jurisdiction over the supplier or recipient The vehicle is not held up. The matter is examined later by the jurisdictional officer. (iv) Exception: where no e-way bill is generated and no document showing origin or destination is produced, the goods/conveyance may be inspected, detained or seized irrespective of jurisdiction Protects revenue against wholly undocumented movement. Both conditions must be met. (v) No detention or seizure for any reason other than the above Closes the door on detention for unrelated or technical reasons. (vi) No confiscation under Section 130 of goods or conveyances in transit Confiscation, with its harsher consequences, is off the table for goods in movement. Section 129 remains the only route. Illustration. Goods move from a registered supplier in Gujarat to a registered recipient in Tamil Nadu, passing through Maharashtra and Karnataka with a valid e-way bill. A CGST officer in Karnataka can stop the vehicle only on Joint Commissioner-approved specific intelligence. Even then, he cannot inspect it, because neither party is in Karnataka. If he has a prima facie view of contravention, he must release the vehicle and send the information to the Gujarat or Tamil Nadu jurisdictional officer. Notably, in most States, e-way bill checks on highways are carried out largely by State GST mobile squads. Until the States issue parallel instructions, the on-ground relief on e-way bill interceptions will be limited to checks by CGST officers. Rationale Free movement of goods.One of the stated objects of GST was a single national market. Random checks in transit States delay goods, add cost and create scope for harassment. The Instruction refers to “random checks of conveyances in transit” as a source of hardship (para 2). Technology has replaced the check-post.E-way bill data, e-invoice data and vehicle-tracking now give the department a digital trail. Physical checks can therefore be targeted rather than random. Requiring a Joint Commissioner’s written reasons adds a senior-level filter and a paper trail, similar in spirit to the “reasons to believe” standard for search under Section 67. Right officer, right forum.The officer with jurisdiction over the supplier or recipient has the returns, the records and the history. He is better placed to decide whether there is a real contravention. Confiscation under Section 130 is meant for cases of intent to evade. For goods in transit, detention and penalty under Section 129 is considered adequate. For further analysis of the proposed enforcement changes, see GST Arrest, Prosecution and E-Way Bill Reforms: What the 57th Council Recommended. 3. Reform 3: No Show Cause Notice Below Rs. 10,000 (Para 3.3) CBIC officers have been directed that show cause notices “may not be issued” under Sections 73, 74 or 74A where the amount involved is below Rs. 10,000, counting CGST, SGST/UTGST, IGST and Compensation Cess together. Rationale Cost of collection exceeds the revenue.Drafting a notice, granting a hearing, passing an order and defending an appeal costs the department more than Rs. 10,000 in most cases. The same is true for the taxpayer’s professional and time cost. Reducing low-value litigation.The Instruction states the purpose directly: “to reduce the low value litigation and compliance burden for taxpayers” (para 3.3). Focusing resources.Officers can spend time on high-value evasion instead of minor return mismatches. Consistency with existing litigation policy.CBIC already prescribes monetary limits below which the department does not file appeals (Circular No. 207/1/2024-GST dated 26.06.2024). A floor at the notice stage applies the same logic one step earlier. Interpretation Issues Issue Position / View Does “amount involved” include interest and penalty? The Instruction refers to the “sum of amounts” under the GST Acts and cess, without the word “tax”. Published summaries of the Council’s recommendation refer to the tax involved. Is the test per notice, per period or per issue? Not specified. Sections 73(10), 74(10) and 74A(2) operate per financial year; the department may aggregate issues for a year. Splitting a single issue to stay below the limit is not advisable. Pending notices already issued The Instruction speaks only of issuance. It does not direct withdrawal of notices already issued. The Council is reported to have recommended that pending small notices be dealt with as if the threshold applied; that will depend on the amendment and any transitional provision. “May not be issued” The wording is directory in form. Read with paras 3 and 4 (directions to be monitored by Chief Commissioners), it is intended to be followed by officers. Section 76 (tax collected but not paid) Not covered. The Instruction names only Sections 73, 74 and 74A. Does the liability vanish? No. The tax remains legally payable. The Instruction only stops adjudication proceedings. A taxpayer who finds a genuine short-payment should still pay it, with interest, through DRC-03. For further discussion of the threshold and its practical implications, see Rs 9,999 Gets a Pass, Rs 10,000 Gets a Notice: Reading CBIC’s Instruction No. 02/2026-GST. 4. Risks, Open Issues and Limitations The relief is real but partial: it binds only CBIC officers, rests on an instruction rather than the statute, and leaves several operational questions open. # Limitation Consequence for Taxpayers 1 Addressed only to Central Tax formations State GST officers, who handle a large share of assessments and road checks, are not bound until their State issues a similar direction. 2 Administrative instruction, not law Sections 69, 129, 130, 73, 74 and 74A continue unchanged until amended. A court will weigh the Instruction, but the statutory power technically survives. 3 Interim and time-bound It lapses when the amendments come into force. The final statutory text may differ from the Instruction. 4 Arrest — “further” arrests only Existing arrests, pending bail matters and filed prosecution complaints are not automatically affected. 5 Prosecution not abolished Offences under Section 132 and prosecution with sanction under Section 134 remain. 6 E-way bill exception in clause (iv) Where there is neither an e-way bill nor any document showing origin/destination, any officer may still act. Carrying proper documents remains essential. 7 “Located” for unregistered parties The Instruction does not define how location of an unregistered supplier/recipient is to be established. 8 Rs. 10,000 threshold — basis of computation Unclear whether interest and penalty are included, and whether the test is per notice, per year or per issue. 9 No withdrawal of pending small SCNs Notices already issued continue unless separately withdrawn. 10 Search, summons, inspection Powers under Sections 67 and 70 are not touched by this Instruction. 5. Conclusion Instruction No. 02/2026-GST moves GST enforcement from suspicion towards trust, five months ahead of the law. The three reforms share one idea: the State’s coercive power should be used only where it is truly needed. Arrest is replaced by civil recovery and prosecution where warranted. Random road checks give way to targeted, senior-approved interceptions. Petty notices give way to a sensible floor. For honest businesses, this means fewer disruptions and lower compliance cost. The relief is, however, limited to CBIC officers and to the interim period. Professionals should track three things: parallel instructions from the States, the text of the CGST (Amendment) Bill when introduced, and any transitional provision for pending matters. Until then, the Instruction is a useful shield, but not yet a statutory right. ****** This article is intended for general professional information and does not constitute legal or tax advice.

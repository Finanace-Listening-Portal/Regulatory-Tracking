Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); Sharad Keshri Vs PCIT (Central) (ITAT Patna) Section 263 Revision Survives on One Unexamined Issue: ITAT Grants Partial Relief to Jewellery Trader Relief on Two Issues Does Not Defeat Revision The Income Tax Appellate Tribunal upheld the exercise of revisionary jurisdiction under section 263 after finding that the Assessing Officer had failed to properly examine seized documents concerning transactions aggregating to ₹1,10,89,600. The assessee successfully challenged the revisionary findings concerning investment in immovable property and a jewellery repairing register. However, the Tribunal sustained the findings relating to the third issue—unreconciled transactions appearing in seized documents. Accordingly, the appeal was partly allowed, while the section 263 order was upheld on the surviving issue. Assessment Completed Without Any Addition The assessee filed his return for assessment year 2022–23 on 16 November 2022, declaring total income of ₹99,51,030. A search under section 132 followed on 19 November 2022. Subsequently, the AO completed the assessment under section 143(3) on 31 March 2024, accepting the returned income without making any addition. The PCIT examined the assessment records and considered the order erroneous insofar as it was prejudicial to the interests of the Revenue. His concerns related to three categories of seized material: HPB-06: Documents concerning an immovable property investment. HPB-14: A repairing register containing entries relating to 1,348 customers. SKLLP-02: Documents containing alleged unrecorded transactions. By an order dated 31 March 2026, the PCIT set aside the assessment and directed fresh enquiries. Property Investment: Assessee’s Explanation Accepted The first issue concerned property worth approximately ₹3.38 crore. The assessee explained that the property belonged to M/s Hira Panna Infraprojects LLP, a separate entity, and that the investment had been examined in the LLP’s assessment proceedings. He relied on the registered sale deed and assessment correspondence to support the ownership and source explanation. The PCIT had considered further verification necessary to determine whether the investment involved undisclosed funds of the individual assessee. The Tribunal, however, accepted the assessee’s challenge on this issue. It observed that the relevant query had been raised and answered, and that the response was supported by the facts of the case. Repairing Register: Free-Repair Policy Supported by Evidence The second issue concerned a register recording jewellery received for repair from 1,348 customers during financial year 2021–22. The assessee explained that the business followed a lifetime free-repair policy, supported by a published commercial advertisement. He disputed the suggestion that the absence of repair charges necessarily indicated undisclosed receipts. The PCIT considered the explanation insufficient and faulted the AO for not undertaking sample third-party verification. On examining the record, the Tribunal accepted the assessee’s position concerning this issue as well. It found that a query had been raised and the explanation was supported by the facts. The grounds relating to the property investment and repairing register were therefore allowed. Seized Transaction Documents Remain Unreconciled The third issue produced a different result. The seized documents marked SKLLP-02, particularly pages 100 and 104, contained transactions aggregating to ₹1,10,89,600 for the relevant year. The assessee maintained that these were supplier correspondence and purchase-rate reconciliations relating to Hira Panna Jewellers, rather than unrecorded cash transactions. However, the Tribunal noted that the assessment queries relied upon in support of this explanation had been raised in the case of Shri Satish Kumar Keshri. It held that this did not establish satisfactory examination of the issue in the assessee’s assessment. The entries had not been adequately explained or reconciled to demonstrate that the transactions were recorded in the books. Consequently, the Tribunal upheld the PCIT’s finding of failure to conduct the necessary enquiry on this issue. Explanation 2 to Section 263 Applied The assessee argued that the proceedings represented a change of opinion concerning the adequacy of enquiries already undertaken. The Tribunal nevertheless found that, for the third issue, the AO had accepted the explanation without properly examining the character of the entries. Applying Explanation 2 to section 263, it sustained the conclusion that the assessment was erroneous and prejudicial to the Revenue to that extent. Relying on Indian Textiles v. CIT, the Tribunal observed that revision could survive where at least one item justified its exercise. Success on the other two issues therefore did not invalidate the proceedings altogether. Author’s Comments The decision demonstrates the need for issue-specific examination of revisionary findings. Evidence supporting enquiries on some matters does not necessarily answer a separate allegation concerning unreconciled seized entries. It also highlights that correspondence from another person’s assessment must establish the relevant connection; its existence alone may not demonstrate verification in the assessee’s case. The surviving revision does not constitute a final addition of ₹1,10,89,600. It permits fresh examination of the transaction documents. The ultimate tax treatment must follow verification and an opportunity for the assessee to explain the entries. Cases Discussed Malabar Industrial Co. Ltd. v. As regards the issue of 2 show cause notices, it has been held in the case of Commissioner of Income-tax, Mumbai v. Amitabh Bachchan [2016] 69 taxmann.com 170 (SC) as under: “The power of appeal and revision is contained in Chapter XX of the Act which includes section 263 that confers suo motu power of revision in the Commissioner. The different shades of power conferred on different authorities under the Act has to be exercised within the areas specifically delineated by the Act and the exercise of power under one provision cannot trench upon the powers available under another provision of the Act. In this regard, it must be specifically noticed that against an order of assessment, so far as the revenue is concerned, the power conferred under the Act is to reopen the concluded assessment under section 147 and/or to revise the assessment order under section 263. The scope of the power/jurisdiction under the different provisions of the Act would naturally be different. The power and jurisdiction of the revenue to deal with a concluded assessment, therefore, must be understood in the context of the provisions of the relevant sections. While doing so, it must also be borne in mind that the legislature had not vested in the revenue any specific power to question an order of assessment by means of an appeal. [Para 9] Reverting to the specific provisions of section 263 what has to be seen is that a satisfaction that an order passed by the Authority under the Act is erroneous and prejudicial to the interest of the revenue is the basic pre-condition for exercise of jurisdiction under section 263. Both are twin conditions that have to be conjointly present. Once such satisfaction is reached, jurisdiction to exercise the power would be available subject to observance of the principles of natural justice which is implicit in the requirement cast by the section to give the assessee an opportunity of being heard. It is in the context of the above position that this Court has repeatedly held that unlike the power of reopening an assessment under section 147, the power of revision under section 263 is not contingent on the giving of a notice to show cause. In fact, section 263 has been understood not to require any specific show-cause notice to be served on the assessee. Rather, what is required under the said provision is an opportunity of hearing to the assessee. The two requirements are different; the first would comprehend a prior notice detailing the specific grounds on which revision of the assessment order is tentatively being proposed. Such a notice is not required. What is contemplated by section 263, is an opportunity of hearing to be afforded to the assessee. Failure to give such an opportunity would render the revisional order legally fragile not on the ground of lack of jurisdiction but on the ground of violation of principles of natural justice. [Para 10] {emphasis supplied} It may be that in a given case and in most cases it is so done that a notice proposing the revisional exercise is given to the assessee indicating therein broadly or even specifically the grounds on which the exercise is felt necessary. But there is nothing in the section to raise the said notice to the status of a mandatory show-cause notice affecting the initiation of the exercise in the absence thereof or to require the Commissioner to confine himself to the terms of the notice and foreclosing consideration of any other issue or question of fact. This is not the purport of section 263. Of course, there can be no dispute that while the Commissioner is free to exercise his jurisdiction on consideration of all relevant facts, a full opportunity to controvert the same and to explain the circumstances surrounding such facts, as may be considered relevant by the assessee, must be afforded to him by the Commissioner prior to the finalization of the decision. [Para 11] {emphasis supplied} In the instant case, there is no dispute that in the order passed by the Commissioner under section 263 findings have been recorded on issues that are not specifically mentioned in the show-cause notice though there are three issues mentioned in the show-cause notice which had specifically been dealt with in the order passed under section 263. [Para 12] Insofar as findings contained in the order of the Commissioner beyond the issues mentioned in the show-cause notice is concerned, the Tribunal taking note of the aforesaid admitted position held that the order under section 263 was violative of principles of natural justice as far as the reasons, which formed the basis for the revision but were not part of the show-cause notice issued under section 263 were concerned. [Para 12] The above ground which had led the Tribunal to interfere with the order of the Commissioner seems to be contrary to the settled position in law, as indicated above and the two decisions of this Court in Gita Devi Aggarwal v. CIT [1970] 76 ITR 496 and CIT v. Electro House [1971] 82 ITR 824. The Tribunal in its order had not recorded any finding that in course of the suo motu revisional proceedings, hearing of which was spread over many days and attended to by the authorized representative of the assessee, opportunity of hearing was not afforded to the assessee and that the assessee was denied an opportunity to contest the facts on the basis of which the Commissioner had come to his conclusions as recorded in the order passed under section 263. Despite the absence of any such finding in the order of the Tribunal, before holding the same to be legally unsustainable, the Court will have to be satisfied that in the course of the revisional proceeding the assessee, actually and really, did not have the opportunity to contest the facts on the basis of which the Commissioner had concluded that the order of the Assessing Officer is erroneous and prejudicial to the interests of the revenue. The above is the question to which the Court, therefore, will have to turn to. [Para 13] On consideration of the order of the Assessing Officer as well as the order of the Commissioner it appears that the Commissioner, in the course of the revisional proceedings, had scrutinized the record of the proceedings before the Assessing Officer and noted the various dates on which opportunities to produce the books of account and other relevant documents were afforded to the assessee which requirement was not complied with by the assessee. In these circumstances, the revisional authority took the view that the Assessing Officer, after being compelled to adjourn the matter from time to time, had to hurriedly complete the assessment proceedings to avoid the same from becoming time barred. In the course of the revisional exercise, relevant facts, documents, and books of account which were overlooked in the assessment proceedings were considered. On such re-scrutiny it was revealed that the original assessment order on several heads was erroneous and had the potential of causing loss of revenue to the State. It is on the aforesaid basis that the necessary satisfaction that the assessment order was erroneous and prejudicial to the interests of the revenue was recorded by the Commissioner. At each stage of the revisional proceeding the authorized representative of the assessee had appeared and had full opportunity to contest the basis on which the revisional authority was proceeding/had proceeded in the matter. If the revisional authority had come to its conclusions in the matter on the basis of the record of the assessment proceedings which was open for scrutiny by the assessee and available to his authorized representative at all times, it is difficult to see as to how the requirement of giving of a reasonable opportunity of being heard as contemplated by section 263 had been breached in the present case. The order of the Tribunal insofar as the first issue, i.e., the revisional order going beyond the show-cause notice is concerned, therefore, cannot have acceptance. The High Court having failed to fully deal with the matter in its cryptic order, the said order is not tenable and is liable to be interfered with. [Para 14]” 9. Further, it has been held in the case of CIT v. Leisure Wear Exports Ltd. [2011] 11 taxmann.com 54/202 Taxman 130 (Mag.) (Delhi) that if an order is based on incorrect assumption of facts or on incorrect application of law or without applying the principle of natural justice and without application of mind, it would be treated as ‘erroneous’ Further, revisional powers conferred on the CIT u/s 263 are of wide amplitude enabling the Commissioner to call for and examine the records under any proceeding of the 1961 Act and empowering him to make or cause to make such enquiry as he deems necessary in order to find out whether any order passed by the AO was erroneous and prejudicial to the interests of the Revenue. Therefore, when the powers conferred upon him are of wide amplitude enabling him to pass any order, it is not necessary for him to record his final conclusion regarding the allowability of the claim of the assessee on the merits of the case. It would be sufficient if he comes to the conclusion on materials that the order of the AO was erroneous and prejudicial to the interests of the Revenue and if such a conclusion is arrived at on materials on record, it is not necessary for him to record his final conclusion on the merits of the case and it is open to record his prima facie opinion in that matter and set aside the order of assessment and direct the AO to pass a fresh assessment order in accordance with law and so has been held in the case of CIT v. Shree Manjunathesware Packing Products & Camphor Works [1998] 231 ITR 53 (SC). It is also held in the case of Indian Textiles v. CIT [1986] 26 Taxman 677 (Mad.) that if at least in respect of one item the ITO’s order is found to be prejudicial to the Revenue, the initiation of proceedings u/s 263 cannot be questioned. Thus, though on 2 issues, the contention of the assessee appears to be correct but since on the third issue the findings of the Ld. PCIT are upheld, therefore, the initiation of proceeding under section 263 cannot be questioned. Hence, while Ground Nos. 7 and 8 are allowed, rest of the grounds raised are dismissed and in view of the findings of Indian Textiles v. CIT (supra) all other grounds of appeal are dismissed and we are inclined to uphold the order of the Ld. Pr. CIT. 10. In the result, the appeal filed by the assessee is partly allowed.

Vs Union of India, 2026 INSC 1195, considered a fundamental question concerning the constitutional right to personal liberty: whether the 24-hour deadline for producing a detained person before a Magistrate under Article 22(2) of the Constitution of India runs from the moment the authorities actually restrain that person’s freedom, or only from the time a formal arrest is entered in official records. The appeals were filed by Sujit Das, Suman Das and Rupak Sarkar against the common order dated 29 April 2026 of the Tripura High Court rejecting their regular bail applications in a prosecution under the Narcotic Drugs and Psychotropic Substances Act, 1985 (NDPS Act). The Court examined the legality of custody and constitutional safeguards independently of the ultimate merits of the criminal allegations. The prosecution alleged that, acting on secret intelligence on 29 September 2025, Customs officers followed a truck carrying a ginger consignment from the Khowai area towards Agartala. At Shanitala Noabadi, a motorcyclist approached the truck and allegedly received a sack from its occupants. The search team intercepted the persons concerned and recovered suspected methamphetamine, commonly called Yaba tablets, weighing 11.03 kg. Further inspection of the ginger consignment at the Customs Division office led to the recovery of another 49.76 kg in two trolley bags, bringing the total alleged seizure to 60.79 kg. Proceedings were initiated under the NDPS Act, including provisions concerning commercial quantities, possession, transport, abetment and conspiracy; the charge-sheet was subsequently filed on 27 March 2026. The critical chronology was undisputed in material respects. According to the investigating agency’s records, the appellants were intercepted and brought under the control of the search team at approximately 2:00 pm on 29 September 2025. The first seizure occurred at the interception point, while the second search and seizure at the Customs Division office concluded around 5:00 pm that day. Despite their continued presence with the authorities, Sujit Das was formally shown arrested at 11:00 am on 30 September 2025 and the other accused at 11:15 am. The appellants were produced before the Judicial Magistrate at about 4:45 pm on 30 September 2025 and remanded to judicial custody. If the starting time was the actual interception at 2:00 pm the previous day, production occurred beyond the constitutionally permissible 24 hours. Before the High Court, the appellants had also challenged compliance with Section 36 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023 (BNSS), particularly the arrest-memo safeguards, contending that the statutory breach implicated Article 21 and should prevail over the restrictions on bail under Section 37 of the NDPS Act. The respondent acknowledged the absence of the signature of a near relative or respectable locality witness on the arrest memo but argued that the stringent NDPS bail conditions still applied. Referring to State of Punjab Vs Sukhwinder Singh @ Gora, the High Court held that satisfaction of the statutory requirements under Section 37 was mandatory and rejected bail on the prima facie material against the accused. The separate issue concerning the actual start of custody under Article 22(2) was examined in depth when the appeals reached the Supreme Court. The appellants’ senior counsel argued that the Constitution does not permit an investigating agency to create an artificial interval between “detention” and “arrest” after a person has already been placed under its physical control and deprived of the freedom to leave. He submitted that the formal arrest times recorded on 30 September 2025 effectively shifted the constitutional clock forward and concealed the earlier restraint. Reliance was placed on Niranjan Singh Vs Prabhakar Rajaram Kharote, Ashak Hussain Allah Detha @ Siddique Vs Assistant Collector of Customs (P.), Bombay and other decisions addressing actual custody, coercive restraint and unlawful postponement of judicial scrutiny. The appellants submitted that their production at 4:45 pm on 30 September was unconstitutional because the 24-hour period had expired at 2:00 pm that day. The Union of India, through the Additional Solicitor General, maintained that the initial interception was merely a temporary restraint necessary for lawful search and investigation, and that custody commenced only with formal arrest. It referred to Sections 42, 43, 50 and 67 of the NDPS Act and Rule 3 of the Narcotic Drugs and Psychotropic Substances (Seizure, Storage, Sampling and Disposal) Rules, 2022, to justify the time spent on search, seizure, classification, weighing, sampling, questioning, documentation and travel. Alternatively, it argued that even if detention was deemed to commence at 5:00 pm on 29 September, when the second seizure concluded, production the next afternoon was within 24 hours. The Union relied on Arun K. Thomas Vs Narcotics Control Bureau, Kannan Vs State of Tamil Nadu and Aryaman Pattnayak Vs Union of India, which had adopted a distinction between preliminary investigative detention and formal arrest. Interpreting Article 22(2), the Supreme Court held that its command is binding and admits only one express exclusion when calculating the maximum 24-hour period: the time necessary for travel from the place of arrest to the Magistrate’s court. Investigation, interrogation, paperwork, testing, weighing or other administrative formalities cannot be deducted from that period. The Court examined Section 57 of the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973, and its successor, Section 58 BNSS, as well as the relevant arrest and search provisions. It emphasised that Article 22(2) is a protection against executive excess, arbitrary confinement and custodial abuse, and that an agency cannot displace this constitutional mandate by choosing when to prepare an arrest memo. The Court drew upon Khatri Vs State of Bihar, D.K. Basu Vs State of West Bengal, Directorate of Enforcement Vs Subhash Sharma, Niranjan Singh Vs Prabhakar Rajaram Kharote and Jaskaran Jeet Singh Deol Vs State of Punjab to stress that legal custody turns on the substance of coercive control, not the label applied by an officer. The constitutional protection would be undermined if an agency could keep a person in its physical hold as part of an investigation while describing that period as informal custody or a prolonged enquiry. The Court therefore expressly held that the contrary interpretations of Article 22(2) adopted in Aryaman Pattnayak (Orissa High Court), Kannan (Madras High Court) and Arun K. Thomas (Kerala High Court) did not lay down the correct law on this point. The Supreme Court further clarified that Section 58 BNSS, corresponding to Section 57 CrPC, contains a safeguard beyond the outer limit of 24 hours. Even detention that ends within that constitutional period must not continue longer than is reasonable in the circumstances. The duty of the Magistrate at first production is therefore not confined to mechanically checking the arrest memo or accepting the time of formal arrest written by the investigating officer. The Magistrate must establish the actual date and time when personal liberty was curtailed, verify whether the constitutional period has expired after excluding only necessary travel time, and separately assess whether the duration of pre-production custody was reasonable. Where the 24-hour limit has been breached, the person must be released; where detention within the limit was nonetheless unreasonably prolonged, the Magistrate may decline remand. Applying these principles, the Supreme Court found that the appellants’ liberty had been curtailed from about 2:00 pm on 29 September 2025, when they came under the control of the search team. They should consequently have been brought before the Magistrate before 2:00 pm on 30 September 2025. Their actual production at approximately 4:45 pm breached Article 22(2), rendering the arrest constitutionally defective and their continued custody illegal. In addition, although the authorities claimed to have completed search and seizure at 5:00 pm on 29 September, formal arrest was recorded only around 11:00–11:15 am the next day. The Court noted that the unexplained interval of approximately 18 hours was not a reasonable duration of custody under Section 58 BNSS and exposed the accused to a period without judicial oversight. In its nine operative conclusions, the Supreme Court affirmed that the 24-hour clock begins on actual curtailment of liberty, that only necessary journey time is excludable, that custody beyond the limit requires immediate release, and that both investigating authorities and Magistrates must ensure reasonable detention and meaningful judicial scrutiny. It set aside the Tripura High Court’s judgment dated 29 April 2026 and directed production of the appellants before the jurisdictional Special Court within a maximum of seven days from 5 November 2026, following which they were to be released on bail on appropriate terms and conditions fixed by that court. The Court also directed circulation of its judgment to relevant police and judicial authorities. The ruling adjudicated the legality of the appellants’ detention and their entitlement to release; it did not finally determine guilt or innocence in the NDPS prosecution.

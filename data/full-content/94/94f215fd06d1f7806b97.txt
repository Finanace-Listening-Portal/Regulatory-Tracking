(Supreme Court) Summary: The Supreme Court dismissed the Customs Department’s civil appeal against Samsung India Electronics Pvt. Ltd., bringing to a close the proceedings arising from a customs classification dispute involving imported front covers, middle covers and back covers of cellular mobile phones. The Supreme Court condoned the delay and dismissed the appeal by following its earlier order dated 10 July 2024 in Civil Appeal Diary No. 24349 of 2024. The Court did not deliver an independent, detailed judgment on the merits of the classification dispute. However, its dismissal left undisturbed the decision of the Customs, Excise and Service Tax Appellate Tribunal (CESTAT), Delhi, which had ruled in favour of Samsung and set aside the differential customs duty demand of ₹62,44,28,858 and penalty of ₹6 crore. Read CESTAT Judgment in this case: Mobile Phone Front, Middle and Back Covers Are Parts Under CTH 85177090: CESTAT Delhi The dispute originated from imports made by Samsung during the period from 6 February 2018 to 21 December 2019. The company imported various parts and sub-parts of cellular mobile phones, including battery covers, front covers, middle covers and back covers. Samsung classified the disputed covers under Customs Tariff Heading (CTH) 85177090, applicable to parts of telephone sets, including cellular mobile phones. The Customs Department disputed this classification and proposed to classify the goods under CTH 39209999, relating to specified plastic sheets and similar materials. The Department maintained that the goods were essentially articles of plastic and therefore attracted a different rate of basic customs duty. Following a post-clearance audit, the Department issued a consultative letter on 1 February 2020. As the dispute remained unresolved, a show cause notice dated 19 February 2020 proposed recovery of differential customs duty, interest, confiscation of the imported goods under Section 111(m) of the Customs Act, 1962, and penalty under Section 112(a)(ii). The Principal Commissioner of Customs, Air Cargo Complex, New Delhi, subsequently passed an order-in-original dated 23 February 2021, confirming the differential duty demand of ₹62.44 crore, directing payment of applicable interest and imposing a penalty of ₹6 crore. Although the goods were held liable for confiscation, no redemption fine was imposed because they had already been cleared for home consumption and were unavailable for physical confiscation. Samsung challenged the adjudication order before CESTAT Delhi. Its principal contention was that the imported goods were specifically designed and manufactured for incorporation into cellular mobile phones and performed essential mechanical and protective functions. The front covers provided structural support and accommodated components such as cameras, buttons and sensors. The middle covers housed and protected internal components and assisted in heat dissipation, while the back covers protected batteries and electronic assemblies from dust, moisture and other external elements. Samsung maintained that these products could not be treated merely as ordinary plastic sheets or general-purpose plastic articles. A significant aspect of Samsung’s defence concerned the manufacturing processes undertaken before importation. The company explained that the products underwent extrusion of plastic materials, printing, physical vapour deposition, hard coating, thermoforming and computer numerical control (CNC) milling. These processes transformed the original plastic sheets into specially shaped and finished components suitable for particular mobile phone models. Some components also incorporated metallic layers, including zinc, for strength and heat dissipation. Samsung argued that the goods had undergone substantial further working and therefore fell outside the scope of Heading 3920 and the relevant Chapter Notes. The Tribunal examined the competing tariff headings and the General Rules for Interpretation of the Customs Tariff. It observed that classification must primarily be determined according to the wording of the tariff headings and the relevant Section and Chapter Notes. Heading 3920 covered specified plastic plates, sheets, films, foils and strips, subject to restrictions concerning lamination and further working. The Tribunal found that the manufacturing processes undertaken on Samsung’s imported components went beyond mere printing, surface working or cutting of plastic sheets into regular shapes. In particular, CESTAT held that vapour deposition, treated in the context of the manufacturing process as lamination, and the subsequent processes of thermoforming and CNC milling took the disputed products outside the scope of Heading 3920. Thermoforming converted flat sheets into specifically shaped articles, while CNC milling created the necessary openings and configurations for incorporation into mobile phones. These processes were materially different from the limited operations contemplated by the relevant Chapter Note. Accordingly, the Tribunal concluded that the goods could not be classified under CTH 39209999 merely because plastic constituted their principal material. The Tribunal also examined the Department’s reliance on Notification No. 57/2017-Customs dated 30 June 2017 and the policy notifications issued by the Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology (MeitY). The Department had relied on references to mobile phone covers and their tariff headings in these instruments to support its proposed classification. CESTAT rejected this approach, explaining that an exemption notification issued under Section 25 of the Customs Act does not independently determine the classification of goods. Classification must first be established under the Customs Tariff, after which the applicability of any exemption notification may be examined. Similarly, the Tribunal held that a policy issued by MeitY could not conclusively determine classification under the Customs Tariff. The statutory responsibility for assessment and classification rests with the importer during self-assessment, the proper customs officer during reassessment, and the competent adjudicating and appellate authorities. Administrative policies issued by another ministry cannot substitute the statutory tariff classification exercise. Consequently, neither the exemption notification nor the MeitY policy provided a legally sufficient basis for reclassifying Samsung’s imported components. An additional issue concerned whether an allegedly incorrect tariff classification in a Bill of Entry justified confiscation and penalty. The Department argued that Samsung had misdeclared the classification of the goods, rendering them liable for confiscation under Section 111(m) and consequently attracting penalty under Section 112. CESTAT distinguished between factual declarations and matters involving legal interpretation or opinion. It explained that an importer must correctly disclose factual particulars such as the description, quantity, value and origin of goods, but tariff classification involves the application of legal provisions to those facts. The Tribunal reasoned that an importer cannot be expected to anticipate the classification subsequently preferred by a customs officer or an appellate authority. A genuine difference of opinion regarding the applicable tariff heading does not, by itself, establish that the goods were incorrectly declared in the manner contemplated by Section 111(m). Even where the Department ultimately adopts a different classification, that disagreement alone does not automatically make the goods liable for confiscation. On this basis, CESTAT held that the penalty imposed under Section 112 could not be sustained merely because the Department disagreed with Samsung’s classification. CESTAT accordingly accepted Samsung’s classification of the disputed mobile phone covers under CTH 85177090 and set aside the consequential duty demand, interest and penalty. The Department challenged the Tribunal’s decision before the Supreme Court. The Supreme Court, however, dismissed the civil appeal by following its earlier order dated 10 July 2024. The important distinction is that the detailed reasoning concerning tariff classification, manufacturing processes, exemption notifications, ministerial policies and penal consequences was recorded by CESTAT, while the Supreme Court’s brief order did not independently discuss those issues. The combined effect of the proceedings is that the Tribunal’s decision in favour of Samsung remained undisturbed. The case illustrates the importance of examining the actual characteristics and manufacturing processes of imported goods when determining their customs classification. It also highlights the distinction between statutory tariff provisions and administrative or exemption instruments, and between an incorrect factual declaration and a bona fide disagreement concerning legal classification. The broader reasoning on these issues belongs to the CESTAT decision; the Supreme Court’s contribution in the supplied proceedings was the dismissal of the Revenue’s appeal by reference to its earlier order.

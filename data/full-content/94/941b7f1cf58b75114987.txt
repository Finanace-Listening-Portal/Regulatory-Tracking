Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); Ajanta Manufacturing Private Limited Vs Union of India (Gujarat High Court) Summary: The Gujarat High Court, in Ajanta Manufacturing Private Limited Vs Union of India, considered whether the Central Excise Department could issue fresh show cause notices to recover Education Cess and Secondary and Higher Secondary Education Cess already refunded pursuant to orders of the Commissioner (Appeals) that had attained finality. The petitions, filed under Article 226 of the Constitution of India, challenged two show cause notices dated 8 October 2020. The central question was whether a subordinate adjudicating authority could disregard an unchallenged appellate order merely because the Supreme Court subsequently adopted a different interpretation of the applicable exemption provisions. The Court examined the principles of judicial discipline, finality of quasi-judicial decisions and the binding nature of orders passed by superior appellate authorities. The petitioner company manufactured electric energy-saving lamps, vitrified tiles and quartz clocks at its manufacturing unit in Kutch, Gujarat. Following the devastating earthquake of 2001, the Central Government issued Notification No. 39/2001-CE dated 31 July 2001, providing an area-based excise exemption for eligible manufacturing units in Kutch. The petitioner availed the notification and submitted refund claims for Central Excise Duty, Education Cess and Secondary and Higher Secondary Education Cess. The authorities initially sanctioned certain amounts but rejected the cess components, resulting in appeals and remand proceedings before the Commissioner (Appeals) and the Customs, Excise and Service Tax Appellate Tribunal. In Special Civil Application No. 3320 of 2021, the adjudicating authority rejected a refund claim of ₹54,10,810 relating to Education Cess and Secondary and Higher Secondary Education Cess for June 2007 to March 2008. The Commissioner (Appeals), Rajkot, subsequently allowed the claim by an order dated 19 December 2018, relying upon the Supreme Court’s decision in SRD Nutrients Pvt. Ltd. Vs Commissioner of Central Excise, Guwahati. The department did not challenge the appellate order. After verification and departmental pre-audit, the refund was granted. The connected petition involved another refund of ₹14,86,015, which had similarly been sanctioned following an appellate order dated 18 December 2018 and accepted by the department. The dispute arose when the Supreme Court delivered its decision in Unicorn Industries Vs Union of India on 6 December 2019. That decision held that exemption from basic excise duty did not automatically extend to Education Cess and Secondary and Higher Education Cess imposed under separate statutory provisions. It also declared the earlier ruling in SRD Nutrients to be per incuriam. Relying upon this subsequent development, the Assistant Commissioner issued fresh show cause notices on 8 October 2020 proposing recovery of the cess refunds already paid to the petitioners, together with applicable interest. The notices were issued approximately one and a half years after the relevant refund orders had been implemented. The petitioners contended that the recovery proceedings were without jurisdiction because the Commissioner (Appeals) had already decided their entitlement to refunds and the department had accepted those decisions. They argued that a subordinate officer could not reopen or effectively reverse an order of a superior quasi-judicial authority through a fresh show cause notice. Their submissions relied particularly upon Union of India Vs Kamlakshi Finance Corporation Ltd., which established that adjudicating authorities must follow binding appellate orders even when they disagree with their correctness. The petitioners further submitted that the department should have pursued the remedies provided under the Central Excise Act rather than attempting to circumvent the final appellate decisions. On behalf of the Union of India, the learned Additional Solicitor General acknowledged the importance of judicial discipline and fairly submitted that the notices had been issued because of the officer’s excessive enthusiasm following the subsequent Supreme Court judgment. He maintained that no mala fides should be attributed to the officer. The High Court examined several judicial precedents concerning the binding effect of appellate decisions, the finality of proceedings and the impermissibility of reopening concluded matters through collateral proceedings. It emphasised that the administrative objective of protecting revenue could not justify disregarding decisions of superior authorities. The Court also referred to Section 35E of the Central Excise Act, 1944, which provides a statutory mechanism for departmental review of orders considered legally or factually incorrect. The High Court held that the subsequent judgment in Unicorn Industries did not authorise the Assistant Commissioner to reopen refund proceedings that had already attained finality under unchallenged appellate orders. The Court distinguished the question of the correct legal interpretation of the cess exemption from the separate question of whether a subordinate authority could recover refunds granted pursuant to binding appellate decisions. It observed that the refunds had been sanctioned in accordance with the law prevailing at the relevant time and that the department had not exercised its statutory remedies against the appellate orders. Consequently, the later change in judicial interpretation could not, by itself, justify issuing fresh recovery notices in disregard of those orders. The Court considered such conduct inconsistent with judicial discipline in adjudication proceedings. By its judgment dated 14 October 2021, the Gujarat High Court allowed both Special Civil Applications and quashed the show cause notices dated 8 October 2020. The Court accepted that the officer’s conduct resulted from overenthusiasm rather than mala fide intention and therefore declined to impose costs or issue punitive directions. However, it stressed the necessity of appropriate training for departmental officers regarding judicial discipline and observance of binding appellate orders. The Court clarified that where the department disputes an appellate decision, it must resort to the statutory remedies available under Section 35E or other applicable provisions. The judgment establishes that final quasi-judicial decisions cannot be collaterally reopened by subordinate revenue authorities solely because of a subsequent change in judicial interpretation. List of Cases Discussed 1. Union of India Vs Kamlakshi Finance Corporation Ltd., 1991 (55) ELT 433 (SC): The Supreme Court established that adjudicating authorities are bound to follow orders passed by superior appellate authorities. The Gujarat High Court applied this principle to hold that the Assistant Commissioner could not disregard the unchallenged orders of the Commissioner (Appeals), even if a subsequent Supreme Court decision adopted a different interpretation of the law. 2. Unicorn Industries Vs Union of India, 2019 (370) ELT 3 (SC): The Supreme Court held that an exemption notification covering basic excise duty did not automatically exempt Education Cess and Secondary and Higher Education Cess imposed under separate legislation. The department relied upon this subsequent decision to initiate recovery of previously sanctioned refunds. The Gujarat High Court held that the decision did not permit a subordinate authority to reopen proceedings already concluded under binding appellate orders. 3. SRD Nutrients Pvt. Ltd. Vs Commissioner of Central Excise, Guwahati, 2017 (355) ELT (SC): This decision formed the basis upon which the Commissioner (Appeals) allowed the petitioners’ refund claims. Although subsequently held to be per incuriam in Unicorn Industries, the Gujarat High Court held that the earlier appellate orders passed in accordance with the then-prevailing legal position could not be collaterally reopened by the adjudicating authority. 4. Commissioner of Central Excise, Kanpur Vs Kothari Products Ltd., 2008 (229) ELT 12 (SC): Cited in support of the binding nature of appellate decisions and the obligation of subordinate departmental authorities to follow orders passed by superior forums. 5. Commissioner of Customs, New Delhi Vs Texcomash Export, 2015 (322) ELT 601 (SC): Considered in relation to the principles governing departmental proceedings, the authority of superior adjudicatory forums and the obligation to respect concluded decisions. 6. Union of India Vs Vicco Laboratories, 2007 (218) ELT 647 (SC): Relied upon concerning judicial interference with show cause notices issued without jurisdiction or in circumstances involving an abuse of statutory process. 7. Claris Lifesciences Ltd. Vs Union of India, 2013 (298) ELT 45 (Gujarat High Court): Considered in connection with the requirement that subordinate excise authorities respect decisions of superior appellate authorities and refrain from reopening matters in disregard of judicial discipline. 8. Claris Lifesciences Ltd. Vs Union of India, 2014 (305) ELT 497 (Gujarat High Court): Relied upon as a further authority concerning the limits of departmental action after a dispute has been decided by a superior appellate forum. 9. Lubi Industries LLP Vs Union of India, 2016 (337) ELT 179 (Gujarat High Court): Discussed in relation to the binding effect of superior appellate orders and the requirement that departmental authorities act consistently with the established hierarchy of adjudication. 10. Commissioner of Customs Vs Millat Fibers, 2011 (271) ELT 512 (Gujarat High Court): Cited concerning judicial discipline and the necessity of adhering to decisions of superior appellate authorities in customs and excise proceedings. 11. Lupin Limited Vs Union of India, 2013 (293) ELT 354 (Gujarat High Court): Considered in relation to the impermissibility of disregarding binding appellate decisions and the requirement that revenue officers act within the limits of their statutory authority. 12. Hope Plantations Ltd. Vs Taluk Land Board, (1999) 5 SCC 590 (Supreme Court): Relied upon concerning the principle of finality of judicial and quasi-judicial decisions and the importance of bringing litigation to a definite conclusion. 13. Union of India Vs Madras Telephone SC & ST Social Welfare Association, (2006) 8 SCC 662 (Supreme Court): Cited in the discussion concerning finality of judicial determinations and the legal consequences of subsequent developments affecting previously concluded proceedings. 14. Pradeep Kumar Maskara Vs State of West Bengal, (2015) 2 SCC 653 (Supreme Court): Relied upon in support of the principles governing finality of judicial decisions and the circumstances in which concluded proceedings may be reopened. 15. Subramanian Swamy Vs State of Tamil Nadu, (2014) 5 SCC 75 (Supreme Court): Cited in the context of the finality of judicial determinations and the requirement to respect binding decisions of competent authorities. 16. Union of India Vs Modi Rubber Ltd., (1986) 4 SCC 66 (Supreme Court): Discussed through the Supreme Court’s reasoning in Unicorn Industries concerning the interpretation of exemption notifications and the principle that exemption from one category of excise duty does not automatically extend to separately imposed duties or cesses. 17. Rita Textiles Pvt. Ltd. Vs Union of India, 1986 Supp SCC 557 (Supreme Court): Referred to in the discussion of Unicorn Industries as an earlier larger-Bench authority concerning the scope of excise-duty exemptions and the interpretation of statutory levies. 18. Bajaj Auto Ltd. Vs Union of India, (2019) 19 SCC 801 (Supreme Court): Considered in the discussion of the Supreme Court’s decision in Unicorn Industries, which held that the earlier ruling in Bajaj Auto, along with SRD Nutrients, had been rendered without considering binding larger-Bench precedents.

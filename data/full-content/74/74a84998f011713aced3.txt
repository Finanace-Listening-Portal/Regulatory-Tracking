Summary: The Delhi High Court, in V R Impex, held that retrospective cancellation of GST registration cannot be sustained where the show-cause notice does not propose such cancellation and the final order fails to provide reasons for selecting an earlier effective date. The Department had issued a notice on 4 April 2022 alleging that the taxpayer’s principal place of business was not found during a field visit, but subsequently cancelled registration retrospectively from 30 August 2019 through an order dated 16 March 2023. Applying Section 29(2) of the CGST Act, 2017 and earlier judicial decisions, the Court modified the effective date of cancellation to 4 April 2022. The judgment reinforces the requirements of adequate notice, objective satisfaction and a reasoned order, while recognising that the statutory power of retrospective cancellation remains available when lawfully exercised. But the existence of that power does not permit the officer to select an earlier date without notice to the taxpayer and without recording reasons. In M/s V R Impex Through Its Proprietor Mr Vishal v. Commissioner of Delhi Goods and Services Tax & Anr., the Delhi High Court examined precisely this issue. The Department had issued a show-cause notice proposing cancellation of registration. However, the notice did not propose retrospective cancellation. The final order nevertheless cancelled the registration from an earlier date. The Court interfered with the retrospective effect and modified the effective date of cancellation. The distinction is important. A taxpayer may have one explanation against cancellation of registration and a separate explanation against cancellation covering an earlier period. Unless the proposed retrospective action is disclosed, the taxpayer has no effective opportunity to contest it. Facts of the case The Department issued a show-cause notice dated 4 April 2022 alleging that the taxpayer’s principal place of business was not found or available at the time of a field visit. The notice required a reply within seven working days and recorded that the registration stood suspended with effect from 4 April 2022. It did not state that cancellation was proposed from 30 August 2019. Subsequently, by an order dated 16 March 2023, the Department cancelled the GST registration retrospectively from 30 August 2019. The taxpayer approached the Delhi High Court challenging the retrospective effect of that order. The relevant sequence was as follows: Particulars Date Date from which registration was retrospectively cancelled 30 August 2019 Show-cause notice and suspension of registration 4 April 2022 Cancellation order 16 March 2023 Effective cancellation date directed by the High Court 4 April 2022 These dates show the issue before the Court: whether a notice issued in April 2022, without proposing retrospective cancellation, could support cancellation going back to August 2019. Two defects noticed by the High Court 1. The notice did not propose retrospective cancellation The allegation in the notice concerned the non-availability of the principal place of business during a field visit. It did not inform the taxpayer that the Department intended to cancel registration from 30 August 2019. The Court specifically noted that the show-cause notice did not contemplate retrospective cancellation. The taxpayer had therefore not been put on notice regarding the retrospective consequence subsequently imposed. This is not a minor omission. An opportunity to reply to an allegation about the business premises is not, by itself, an opportunity to explain why registration should remain undisturbed for an earlier period. 2. The order did not explain the retrospective date The cancellation order also failed to assign reasons for giving retrospective effect to the cancellation. The Court treated this as a separate defect. An officer must explain not merely why registration is liable to cancellation, but also why cancellation should operate from the particular earlier date selected. The justification for cancellation and the justification for its retrospective operation are related, but they are not the same question. This distinction follows from the Court’s insistence on a reasoned exercise of the power under Section 29(2). Section 29(2): Power must be supported by reasons Section 29(2) of the CGST Act, 2017 permits the Proper Officer to cancel registration from such date, including a retrospective date, as the officer may deem fit, where the statutory circumstances are satisfied. However, the decisions discussed by the High Court make it clear that this discretion cannot be exercised mechanically. The officer’s satisfaction must rest on objective considerations, and the order must disclose why retrospective cancellation is warranted. The judgment reproduced the following observation from Ridhi Sidhi Enterprises: “The order under Section 29(2) must itself reflect the reasons which may have weighed upon the respondents to cancel registration with retrospective effect.” The requirement of reasons is therefore not satisfied merely by mentioning Section 29(2), reproducing an allegation, or entering an effective date in the cancellation order. The order must demonstrate a connection between the material considered and the retrospective consequence imposed. Earlier decisions relied upon The Court referred to its earlier decisions in Subhana Fashion, M/s Balaji Industries and Ridhi Sidhi Enterprises. These decisions addressed different defects in cancellation proceedings, but together emphasised the need for proper notice, an effective opportunity of hearing and a reasoned order. In M/s Balaji Industries, the reasons recorded in the cancellation order differed from those stated in the show-cause notice. Retrospective cancellation had also not been proposed. The Court modified the effective date of cancellation to the date of the notice and suspension. In Ridhi Sidhi Enterprises, the Court explained that retrospective cancellation cannot be a routine exercise. That decision, in turn, discussed Ramesh Chander and Delhi Polymers, which stressed objective criteria and the need to consider the consequences of cancelling registration for an earlier period. The common principle is straightforward: the final order must remain within the case that the taxpayer was called upon to answer. Why the retrospective date matters Retrospective cancellation is not merely a change in registration particulars. It may create disputes concerning supplies made during the affected period and the input tax credit claimed by recipients. The decisions reproduced in the judgment recognised these possible consequences. They required the Proper Officer to consider whether such consequences were intended and warranted before exercising the power retrospectively. However, the judgment should not be read as deciding that every recipient automatically loses input tax credit merely because a supplier’s registration is cancelled retrospectively. The quoted decisions expressly refrained from finally examining that wider issue. Their emphasis was on the seriousness of the possible consequences and the corresponding need for a properly reasoned cancellation order. Relief granted: Modification, not restoration The Delhi High Court directed that the taxpayer’s registration would stand cancelled with effect from 4 April 2022, being the date of the show-cause notice, instead of 30 August 2019. The scope of this relief must be understood correctly. The Court did not restore the registration for continued business. It modified the effective date of cancellation. Equally, the judgment does not lay down that every defective retrospective cancellation must necessarily be shifted to the date of the show-cause notice. That was the relief granted on the facts of this case. Points for examination in similar cases For practitioners, the judgment provides a useful basis for examining retrospective cancellation orders. The following questions should be checked against the actual notice, reply and order: What precise allegation was made in the show-cause notice? Did the notice disclose that retrospective cancellation was proposed? Was the proposed earlier date, and the basis for selecting it, made known to the taxpayer? Was there an effective opportunity to contest the retrospective action? Does the final order explain why cancellation should cover the earlier period? Do the findings in the order correspond with the allegations in the notice? Is the taxpayer seeking restoration of registration, modification of the cancellation date, or both? These are practical points arising from the notice-and-reasons requirements discussed in the judgment. The grounds and relief should be framed according to the records of each case, rather than by treating every retrospective cancellation as identical. Conclusion The decision in V R Impex does not take away the statutory power to cancel GST registration retrospectively. It requires the Department to exercise that power fairly and with reasons. An allegation that the business premises were not found during a field visit does not, without further explanation, establish why registration should be cancelled from a substantially earlier date. The taxpayer must be informed of the retrospective action proposed and given an opportunity to answer it. The final order must then explain why that action is justified. It was the absence of both notice and reasons that led the Delhi High Court to modify the cancellation date in this case.

Summary: The GST Appellate Tribunal (GSTAT), Thiruvananthapuram, in Pavithram Gold & Diamonds v. Commissioner of CGST & CE, Thiruvananthapuram, examined whether an audit-based GST demand involving reversal of input tax credit (ITC) for non-payment to suppliers could automatically sustain an equal penalty under Section 74 of the CGST Act, 2017. The taxpayer, a partnership firm engaged in retail trading of gold, silver and diamonds, had been audited for January 2019 to March 2021. The proceedings concerned the supplier-payment condition under Section 16(2), read with Rule 37 of the CGST Rules, 2017. The taxpayer had paid the disputed tax, accepted the interest liability and made a partial interest payment. Consequently, the surviving dispute before the Tribunal concerned the penalty imposed on the allegation of wilful suppression. The Tribunal found that the show cause notice merely alleged that the taxpayer had wilfully suppressed material facts with an intention to evade tax, without identifying the particular facts allegedly concealed. It rejected the reasoning that detection during departmental audit, by itself, established suppression. The Tribunal also held that the Revenue could not shift the burden to the taxpayer to disprove an unsupported allegation. In considering Explanation 2 to Section 74, it emphasised the need to identify the undisclosed information, the statutory document requiring disclosure and the legal obligation allegedly breached. Relying on the principles concerning the foundational role of a show cause notice, including those discussed in Brindavan Beverages and G.R. Infra Projects Limited Ratlam, the Tribunal declined to sustain the penalty on grounds introduced only during appellate arguments. The appeal was allowed to the extent of the Section 74 penalty, with consequential relief, while the taxpayer remained liable to pay the balance interest. The ruling distinguishes a proven tax compliance lapse from the separate statutory requirements for imposing a penalty based on fraud, wilful misstatement or suppression. It does not, by itself, justify an allegation of fraud. That distinction lies at the heart of the GST Appellate Tribunal’s decision in Pavithram Gold & Diamonds. The taxpayer had paid the disputed tax and accepted the interest liability. What remained was the department’s demand for an equal penalty under Section 74. The Tribunal set aside that penalty because the show cause notice had not made out a specific, sustainable case of suppression. For taxpayers dealing with audit-based GST demands, the order answers an important question: can the department move from “you failed to comply” to “you deliberately suppressed facts” without explaining what was suppressed and producing evidence to support that conclusion? The Tribunal’s answer was no. The dispute: unpaid suppliers and an ITC reversal lapse Pavithram Gold & Diamonds, a partnership firm engaged in the retail sale of gold, silver and diamonds, underwent a departmental audit covering January 2019 to March 2021. The audit identified non-payment of the value of supplies and the related tax to suppliers within the prescribed period. The dispute concerned the resulting ITC adjustment under the supplier-payment condition in Section 16(2), read with Rule 37. The adjudicating authority confirmed tax, interest and an equal penalty under Section 74. The first appellate authority upheld the demand. Before the Tribunal, the position had narrowed considerably: The Tribunal recorded payment of tax of ₹7,66,446. The taxpayer did not contest the interest liability. ₹2,50,000 had been paid towards interest. The surviving dispute was the Section 74 penalty. This was therefore not a decision allowing a taxpayer to retain ITC despite non-payment to suppliers. It was a decision examining whether the additional charge of suppression—and the penalty resting on it—had been established. A serious allegation needs more than statutory language The show cause notice alleged that the taxpayer had “wilfully suppressed the material facts with an intention to evade payment of tax.” The difficulty was that it did not specify which material facts had been wilfully suppressed. The Tribunal found the allegation vague and lacking in specificity. It also rejected the suggestion that a contravention of Section 16(2) would automatically attract Section 74. That is the practical importance of the order. Merely inserting the expressions “wilful suppression” and “intention to evade” into a notice does not explain the department’s case. Those expressions must be supported by identified facts and a clear connection between the conduct alleged and the provision invoked. The Tribunal also noticed an inconsistency within the notice: in relation to another ITC allegation, contravention of Section 16(2) had been linked to Section 73, while the disputed allegation was treated as attracting Section 74. The underlying contravention alone could not explain the harsher treatment. Detection during audit does not establish suppression The first appellate authority reasoned that the violation had been unearthed only during audit and therefore involved wilful suppression. The Tribunal rejected that reasoning. If detection during audit were enough, every audit objection could become a Section 74 case. The Tribunal expressly held that discovery by audit alone cannot justify invocation of the Section 74 penalty. There is a straightforward distinction here. Audit explains how the department discovered a lapse. It does not necessarily establish why the lapse occurred. An unpaid supplier balance may support an examination of the ITC adjustment. But the fact that an auditor discovered it does not, without more, prove deliberate concealment. The taxpayer cannot be asked to disprove an unsupported allegation The appellate authority had also observed that there was no evidence disproving the allegation that the contravention was wilful. The Tribunal found that this improperly reversed the burden of proof. In the absence of a statutory provision placing that burden on the taxpayer, the Revenue had to establish its allegation. In this case, the Tribunal found that the Revenue had not produced the necessary proof. The distinction must be kept precise. The claimant’s burden to establish ITC eligibility is separate from the department’s burden to establish fraud or suppression for invoking Section 74. Failure on the first question does not automatically answer the second. What was not disclosed—and where should it have been disclosed? The Tribunal’s discussion of Explanation 2 to Section 74 is particularly useful. For the non-declaration allegation before it, the Tribunal required identification of: The specific facts or information not declared. The return, statement, report or document in which disclosure was mandatory. The statutory provision creating that disclosure obligation. The appellate order had not identified these particulars. Its finding of suppression therefore failed. The judgment does not hold that return omissions can never amount to suppression. It requires the department to identify the omission and its legal basis rather than rely on a general accusation. A defective notice cannot be repaired at the Tribunal stage The Revenue’s representative advanced arguments concerning Rule 37 and the statutory definition of suppression. The Tribunal acknowledged that those arguments had considerable force, but found that the grounds were not part of the notice or the orders under challenge. The department could not improve its original case through fresh submissions at the second appellate stage. The Tribunal relied on Brindavan Beverages for the principle that the show cause notice forms the foundation of the department’s case. It also relied on G.R. Infra Projects Limited Ratlam, as reproduced in the order, for the principle that requirements necessary to sustain a notice or order must appear in that notice or order itself—not be supplied later through litigation submissions. What relief did the taxpayer actually receive? The Tribunal allowed the appeal concerning the Section 74 penalty and set aside the appellate order to that extent, with consequential relief. It expressly held that the taxpayer remained liable for the balance interest. Although the taxpayer requested three instalments, the operative order did not expressly grant that request. One reporting detail deserves care: paragraph 1.3 mentions ₹7,66,646 as the tax and penalty amount, whereas paragraphs 1.2 and 4.1 record ₹7,66,446. An article should flag this internal discrepancy rather than state a single penalty figure as undisputed. Why this judgment matters The decision is useful where a Section 74 notice merely repeats the language of suppression, relies on audit detection as proof of intent, or leaves the taxpayer to disprove an allegation unsupported by evidence. Its strength lies in requiring the department to distinguish a compliance failure from a properly established case of suppression. It is not a blanket cancellation of every demand in which fraud is unproved. Section 75(2) separately provides for determination under Section 73 where the statutory conditions are met. In Pavithram Gold & Diamonds, however, tax and interest were not the surviving contested issues; the Tribunal decided the penalty challenge before it. The message is measured but important: the department may establish a tax default through records. To sustain a Section 74 penalty, it must also make—and prove—the case that attracts that provision. One cannot be treated as a substitute for the other.

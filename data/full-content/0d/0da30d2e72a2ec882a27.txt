Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); Christy Friedgram Industry Vs Commissioner of GST and Central Excise (CESTAT Chennai) Summary: The Customs, Excise and Service Tax Appellate Tribunal (CESTAT), Chennai, comprising Judicial Member Sulekha Beevi C.S. and Technical Member Vasa Seshagiri Rao, decided two connected appeals involving Christy Friedgram Industry and the Commissioner of GST and Central Excise, Salem, concerning the availability of central excise exemption on nutritional food preparations supplied under the Integrated Child Development Services (ICDS) Scheme and the liability to service tax on associated technical and administrative activities. The principal controversy concerned the interpretation of Notification No. 12/2012-Central Excise, dated 17 March 2012, particularly the expression “intended for free distribution to economically weaker sections of society”. The assessee challenged the confirmation of central excise duty amounting to approximately ₹47.16 crore, together with interest and an equivalent penalty, while the Revenue challenged the dropping of a separate service tax demand of ₹7,16,80,505. Both appeals arose from Order-in-Original No. 78/2022-CE&ST dated 28 December 2022, covering the disputed period from July 2016 to June 2017. The Tribunal examined the manufacturing arrangements, the Government-approved nutritional programme, contractual obligations, exemption conditions, valuation of alleged services and invocation of the extended period of limitation before deciding both appeals through a common order. Background of the nutritional food supply arrangement: The assessee manufactured two products, namely Complementary Weaning Food (CWF) and Blend of Critical Processed Materials (BCPM), both falling under Heading 1901 of the Central Excise Tariff Act, 1985. These products were procured under the Tamil Nadu Government’s ICDS programme for providing nutritional supplements free of cost to children, pregnant women and lactating women. CWF manufactured directly by the assessee was supplied to Anganwadi centres, whereas BCPM was sold to the ICDS Department and delivered to 25 Women Industrial Cooperative Societies (WCS). These societies mixed BCPM with roasted wheat flour in the prescribed ratio of 58% BCPM and 42% roasted wheat flour, thereby preparing CWF for free distribution to the intended beneficiaries. The arrangement followed an expert committee’s recommendations after serious quality deficiencies were identified in the food preparations manufactured by the cooperative societies. The committee had concluded that these societies lacked adequate facilities for processing malted ragi, jaggery and vitamin premixes and therefore recommended procurement of BCPM from technically competent private enterprises. The assessee submitted that BCPM contained the essential nutritional ingredients of CWF and was an integral component of the Government-approved scheme. It further maintained that the subsequent addition of wheat flour did not result in a fundamentally different product and that BCPM, packed in 50-kilogram unit containers, was itself intended for the ultimate nutritional benefit of the weaker sections of society. Department’s objections and assessee’s defence: The Department accepted the exemption claimed on CWF supplied directly to Anganwadis but denied the same benefit to BCPM on the ground that the latter was delivered to cooperative societies for further processing instead of being distributed directly to beneficiaries. According to the Department, BCPM was merely an intermediate product used by WCS in manufacturing the final exempted food preparation. The assessee disputed this interpretation, arguing that the exemption notification did not impose any requirement of direct delivery to beneficiaries. It prescribed that food preparations falling under Heading 1901 should be packed in unit containers and intended for free distribution under a programme approved by the Central or State Government. The assessee submitted that the competent State Government authority had issued the certificates required under Condition No. 5, establishing that BCPM supplied through WCS was ultimately used in preparing CWF distributed free of cost. These certificates were furnished periodically to the jurisdictional excise authorities. The assessee also relied on the markings appearing on the 50-kilogram BCPM bags, including indications that the goods were intended for infant nutritional use, free distribution and not for sale. It further referred to clarifications issued by Tamil Nadu Government departments recognising the entire BCPM-to-CWF supply chain as part of the ICDS scheme. On that basis, it contended that denying exemption merely because cooperative societies performed an intermediate processing activity would introduce an additional condition not found in the notification and defeat the welfare objective underlying the exemption. Tribunal’s interpretation of exemption conditions: The Tribunal accepted the assessee’s principal contention and held that BCPM was entitled to exemption under Notification No. 12/2012-CE. It observed that the essential ingredients contributing amylase activity and micronutrients were already present in BCPM and that the subsequent addition of roasted wheat flour by WCS did not transform the preparation into a product possessing a new character and quality. BCPM was therefore treated as a food preparation forming an integral part of the ICDS nutritional distribution arrangement. The Tribunal emphasised that the notification employed the expression “intended for” rather than requiring direct supply to the final beneficiaries. It relied on the reasoning in Hindustan Zinc Limited, where exemption had been recognised for goods ultimately used in fertiliser manufacture despite an intermediate manufacturing stage, and referred to Fertilizer Corporation of India Limited and Gujarat State Fertilizers concerning the significance of ultimate intended use. The Tribunal also considered the Supreme Court’s decisions in Commissioner of Customs (Import) Vs Dilip Kumar and Company and Commissioner of Central Excise Vs Hari Chand Shri Gopal, which require strict compliance with exemption conditions. It distinguished those decisions because the assessee had fulfilled the stipulated conditions and no relevant ambiguity existed in the notification. Referring further to Government of Kerala Vs Mother Superior Adoration Convent, the Tribunal observed that the beneficial purpose of an exemption provision should receive due effect. Accordingly, the absence of direct delivery of BCPM to Anganwadis could not justify denial of exemption where its ultimate intended distribution stood established. Separate service tax demand on technical assistance: The Revenue’s appeal concerned the dropping of service tax demand of ₹7,16,80,505 on activities performed by the assessee at the premises of Women Industrial Cooperative Societies. The Department argued that the assessee had provided independently taxable services involving administrative support, technical assistance, quality control, fine-tuning of manufacturing processes and supervision of production standards. It sought to determine the taxable value of these alleged services from components disclosed in the tender cost sheet under administrative overheads and related expenses. The Tribunal examined the contractual arrangements and found that the assessee had undertaken these responsibilities as part of its obligation to supply BCPM and ensure the prescribed quality of the resulting CWF. The contract provided for a single composite price, with no separate payment or consideration received for the supporting activities. The tender conditions also established that the segregation of price components was intended for comparison of competing bids rather than creation of independently chargeable service contracts. The Tribunal relied on Bharat Sanchar Nigam Limited Vs Union of India, reported in 2006 (2) S.T.R. 161 (SC), and the relevant clarification in the CBEC Service Tax Education Guide concerning composite transactions. It also noted that VAT had been paid on the sale of BCPM involving transfer of title in goods. In these circumstances, the Tribunal concluded that the technical and administrative obligations were ancillary to the composite supply arrangement and that the contract price could not be artificially divided to levy service tax on activities for which no separate consideration was received. The adjudicating authority’s decision dropping the service tax demand was therefore upheld. Extended limitation, suppression and penalty: Independently of its findings on exemption eligibility, the Tribunal examined whether the Department was justified in invoking the extended period of limitation under Section 11A(4) of the Central Excise Act, 1944. The assessee submitted that it had regularly filed ER-1 monthly returns disclosing the manufacture, classification, quantity, value and clearance of both BCPM and CWF. The returns and accompanying records expressly disclosed that BCPM was being supplied to WCS for preparing CWF under the ICDS programme. The assessee also furnished the certificates issued by the competent Tamil Nadu Government authority, invoices and supporting records establishing the ultimate use of BCPM. It further pointed out that departmental internal audit teams and CERA audit officials had visited the factory, scrutinised relevant records, agreements and exemption documents, and raised no objection regarding the benefit claimed. The Tribunal found that the disputed demand was based substantially on statutory documents and records already available to the Department. It observed that the Revenue had not produced incriminating material establishing deliberate concealment, wilful misstatement or an intention to evade duty. The dispute principally arose from differing interpretations of the exemption notification, rather than any clandestine transaction or suppression of facts. Consequently, the Tribunal held that the extended period of limitation was not invocable. It also concluded that the penalty imposed under Section 11AC of the Central Excise Act, 1944 could not survive in the absence of the requisite ingredients for penal action. Thus, the excise demand was unsustainable both on substantive merits and on limitation. Final decision and legal significance: After considering the complete factual and legal position, the Tribunal recorded that BCPM manufactured by the assessee was a food preparation falling under Heading 1901, that its packaging in 50-kilogram bags satisfied the requirement of unit containers, and that the printed markings and contractual arrangements demonstrated its intended use for free distribution under the ICDS programme. The competent authority’s certificates established that BCPM was processed by the cooperative societies into CWF and ultimately distributed without charge to the eligible beneficiaries. Therefore, the exemption under Notification No. 12/2012-Central Excise could not be denied merely because the food preparation passed through Women Industrial Cooperative Societies before reaching the beneficiaries. The Tribunal further held that there was no legally sustainable basis for treating the technical assistance, administrative supervision and quality-control obligations as separate taxable services where they formed part of the overall supply arrangement and no independent consideration had been received. The Revenue had also failed to establish suppression of facts warranting invocation of the extended limitation period or imposition of penalty. Accordingly, the Tribunal set aside the central excise duty demand of approximately ₹47.16 crore, together with consequential interest and penalty, and upheld the adjudicating authority’s decision dropping the ₹7.16 crore service tax demand. The assessee’s appeal was allowed on merits as well as limitation, while the Revenue’s appeal was dismissed. The decision establishes, on the facts and wording of the applicable exemption notification, that the intended ultimate use of goods may satisfy an exemption condition even where intermediate processing occurs before distribution, and that ancillary contractual obligations do not automatically constitute separately taxable services. Cases Discussed / Relied Upon Fertilizer Corporation of India Limited (Patna High Court); 2000 (122) E.L.T. 343 (Pat.) — The Court considered the exemption available to LSHS/furnace oil intended for use as feedstock in fertiliser manufacture. It held that fuel used to generate steam forming an integral part of fertiliser production could qualify for the exemption despite its indirect use in the final manufacturing process. CESTAT Chennai referred to this principle in support of the proposition that the ultimate intended purpose of a product is relevant when interpreting an exemption notification. Bharat Sanchar Nigam Limited Vs Union of India (Supreme Court); 2006 (2) S.T.R. 161 (SC); (2006) 3 SCC 1 — The Supreme Court examined the distinction between sale and service elements in composite transactions. CESTAT relied on this decision, together with the CBEC Service Tax Education Guide, while examining the Revenue’s attempt to segregate technical assistance and administrative activities from the composite BCPM supply contract. Since no independent consideration was received for such activities, the Tribunal upheld the dropping of the service tax demand. Hindustan Zinc Limited (Rajasthan High Court); 2009 (236) E.L.T. 633 (Raj.) — The High Court considered exemption for sulphuric acid intended for fertiliser manufacture where the acid was initially consumed in producing phosphoric acid, which was subsequently used to manufacture fertilisers. The High Court recognised the relevance of ultimate use rather than insisting on direct use in the final product. CESTAT expressly found this reasoning applicable to BCPM supplied through cooperative societies for preparation of CWF. Gujarat State Fertilizers (Delhi High Court); 2009 (241) E.L.T. 190 (Del.) — The order refers to this authority while explaining the meaning of the expression “intended for” in an exemption provision. The Tribunal relied on the principle that exemption should not be denied merely because goods pass through an intermediate manufacturing stage, provided their ultimate use satisfies the intended statutory purpose. Commissioner of Central Excise, New Delhi Vs Hari Chand Shri Gopal (Supreme Court); 2010 (260) E.L.T. 3 (SC); (2011) 1 SCC 236 — The Supreme Court held that mandatory conditions prescribed for availing an exemption must be fulfilled. The adjudicating authority had relied on this principle to deny exemption. CESTAT distinguished the decision because Christy Friedgram Industry had fulfilled the specified requirements, including production of competent-authority certificates, and the notification did not require direct distribution to the beneficiaries. Commissioner of Customs (Import), Mumbai Vs Dilip Kumar and Company & Others (Supreme Court); 2018 (361) E.L.T. 577 (SC); (2018) 9 SCC 1 — The Constitution Bench held that exemption notifications must be construed strictly and that ambiguity concerning entitlement ordinarily operates in favour of the Revenue. CESTAT distinguished the precedent on the ground that there was no relevant ambiguity in the exemption notification and the assessee had complied with its conditions. The Tribunal declined to import an additional requirement of direct supply into the notification. Government of Kerala & Another Vs Mother Superior Adoration Convent (Supreme Court); (2021) 5 SCC 602; 2021 (376) E.L.T. 242 (SC) — The Supreme Court emphasised that the beneficial purpose underlying a statutory exemption should receive appropriate effect and that excessively formalistic interpretation should not defeat its object. CESTAT invoked this principle while concluding that nutritional food preparations intended for free distribution to economically weaker beneficiaries should not be excluded merely because of intermediate processing by cooperative societies.

Vs DCIT/ACIT (ITAT Rajkot) Disputed Subcontractor Balances Under Section 68 Require Verification of Confirmations and Subsequent Payments Summary: The Rajkot ITAT restored a ₹5.92 crore addition under Section 68 to the Assessing Officer for fresh adjudication where subcontractors acknowledged doing work for the assessee but denied that amounts were outstanding at year-end. A subcontractor also denied signing his confirmation, four others were unavailable for examination, and the assessee claimed to have settled the balances subsequently. The ruling draws attention to a practical distinction in scrutiny assessments. Evidence that work was performed and evidence that the corresponding amount remained payable on the balance-sheet date answer two different questions. Subcontractors Admitted Doing the Work but Denied Outstanding Balances Karan Construction Co., engaged in civil construction, filed its return for AY 2015-16 declaring income of ₹53,51,660. During limited scrutiny, the Assessing Officer examined sundry creditors of ₹5,91,65,673 appearing in the books. The Assessing Officer recorded statements from eight subcontractors whose balances totalled ₹4,15,54,209. They acknowledged carrying out labour or shuttering work but stated that nothing remained payable as on 31 March 2015. According to them, they had received payment during execution of the work or shortly thereafter. The Assessing Officer also questioned their creditworthiness, noted the lack of regular books or vouchers in most cases and found discrepancies concerning signatures on certain confirmations. The assessee furnished affidavits, bank statements, TDS certificates and income-tax return acknowledgements. The Assessing Officer regarded those documents as having been furnished after the event and found them insufficient to establish the genuineness of the balances. The remaining four subcontractors, whose balances aggregated to ₹1,76,11,464, were not produced despite opportunities. Drawing an inference from the discrepancies found in the first group, the Assessing Officer treated their balances as non-genuine as well and added the entire ₹5,91,65,673 under Section 68. The CIT(A) sustained the addition. A Denied Signature Required Proper Verification Before the Tribunal, the assessee relied on confirmations and supporting documents for all 12 subcontractors. It also submitted that the amounts had been paid subsequently, which, in its view, supported the genuineness of the transactions. The Revenue pointed out that only eight parties had been examined and that one of them, Rameshbhai Laxmibhai Ambala, had specifically denied signing the confirmation attributed to him. The Tribunal treated the denial of the signature as a material issue requiring proper verification. A confirmation can support a ledger balance when its authorship is established. Where the person to whom it is attributed disputes the signature, the Assessing Officer must examine that objection before relying on the document as evidence of the liability. The Tribunal also took note of the incomplete examination of the 12 parties. Subsequent Payments Had to Be Linked to the Year-End Liabilities The assessee’s claim of subsequent settlement required examination of the dates and modes of payment and their bearing on the genuineness of the original transactions. That enquiry matters because a payment in a later year may relate to an earlier outstanding bill or to fresh work. Its evidentiary value depends on whether it can be traced to the specific balance carried forward as on 31 March 2015. The Tribunal restored the entire Section 68 issue to the Assessing Officer for fresh adjudication after giving the assessee a reasonable opportunity of being heard. It directed the assessee to furnish the requisite confirmations, supporting evidence and details of subsequent payments. The appeal was allowed for statistical purposes, leaving the merits of the addition open for fresh determination. Author’s Comments The practical issue in this case is reconciliation of the outstanding liability. A TDS certificate can corroborate that a payment or credit was reported. A bank statement can show when money moved. An invoice or work measurement record can support the amount billed. When a subcontractor says the bill had already been settled, these documents become more persuasive if they are read together in a party-wise reconciliation showing the bills booked, payments made up to year-end, the closing balance and the later payment against that balance. The distinction matters equally for the four parties whose statements were never recorded. The Assessing Officer had drawn an inference about them from discrepancies identified in the other eight cases. On remand, the supporting records for each of those four parties can be examined against its own ledger and payment history. For the subcontractor who denied signing the confirmation, independent records linking the work, the bill and the eventual payment assume particular importance. The order’s significance lies in the scope of the fresh enquiry. Admission of work establishes the commercial transaction. Proof of the closing balance requires a further account of what was billed, what was paid and what remained due at year-end. In a case involving disputed confirmations, that reconciliation can provide the factual basis for deciding the Section 68 addition. FULL TEXT OF THE ORDER OF ITAT RAJKOT Captioned appeal filed by the assessee, pertaining to Assessment Year (AY) 2015-16, is directed against the order under section 250 of the Income-tax Act, 1961 [hereinafter referred to as ‘the Act’] passed by the National Faceless Appeal Centre [hereinafter referred to as ‘NFAC’], dated 26.08.2025, which in turn arises out of an order passed by assessing officer u/s. 143(3) of the Act, dated 29.12.2017. 02. Brief facts of the case are that the assessee, is engaged in the business of civil construction. For the Assessment Year 2015-16, the assessee filed its return of income on 27.09.2015 declaring total income of Rs.53,51,660/-. The case was selected for limited scrutiny, and notices under sections 143(2) and 142(1) of the Income-tax Act, 1961 were duly issued. The authorised representative of the assessee appeared before the Assessing Officer and furnished the books of account and other relevant details. During the course of assessment proceedings, the Assessing Officer examined the sundry creditors appearing in the books of account. The assessee had shown sundry creditors aggregating to Rs.5,91,65,673/-. On verification, the Assessing Officer found discrepancies in respect of several creditors. In particular, eight creditors, aggregating to Rs.4,15,54,209/-, were examined by recording their statements. Although the concerned persons admitted having undertaken certain labour or shuttering work for the assessee, they stated that there were no outstanding balances as on 31.03.2015 and that payments had been received during the execution period or shortly thereafter. The Assessing Officer also noted that most of them lacked proper creditworthiness and did not maintain regular books of account or vouchers. In certain cases, the confirmation letters were also found not to have been signed by the concerned persons. The assessee furnished affidavits, bank statements, TDS certificates and income-tax acknowledgements in support of the creditors’ balances. However, the Assessing Officer treated such documents as having been furnished post facto and considered them insufficient to establish the genuineness and creditworthiness of the creditors. Relying upon various judicial decisions, the Assessing Officer held that mere affidavits unsupported by corroborative evidence could not establish the genuineness of the credits. Further, in respect of four other creditors, namely Amitbhai Narshibhai (Rs.44.47 lakh), Hiteshbhai Dhirubhai (Rs.45.97 lakh), Manishbhai Naradbhai Sojitra (Rs.40.25 lakh) and Rashikbhai Manilal Luka (Rs.45.40 lakh), aggregating to Rs.1,76,11,464/-, the assessee failed to produce the creditors for examination despite opportunities being afforded. The Assessing Officer, considering the facts and circumstances and the pattern noticed in respect of the earlier eight creditors, held these balances also to be non-genuine. Accordingly, the Assessing Officer treated the entire amount of Rs.5,91,65,673/- as unexplained cash credits under section 68 of the Act and added the same to the total income of the assessee. The Assessing Officer also noticed that the assessee had claimed interest expenditure of Rs.1,69,421/- on account of delayed remittance of TDS. Since such interest was considered to be in the nature of a statutory levy and not an allowable business expenditure, the same was disallowed. The assessee conceded this disallowance, and consequently, the amount of Rs.1,69,421/- was added back to the total income. Thus, the assessment was completed by making, inter alia, the aforesaid additions and disallowances to the income returned by the assessee. 03. The assessee carried the assessment order in appeal before the Ld. CIT(A). The Ld. CIT(A), after considering the submissions of the assessee, dismissed the appeal and sustained the addition of Rs.5,91,65,673/- made by the Assessing Officer under section 68 of the Income-tax Act, 1961. 04. Aggrieved by the order of the Ld. CIT(A), the assessee is in appeal before this tribunal. 05. The Ld. AR submitted that the transactions entered into with the 12 subcontractors were genuine and duly supported by confirmation letters and other documentary evidence. He further submitted that the payments relating to the said transactions were subsequently repaid, which substantiated the genuineness of the transactions. Accordingly, he pleaded that the addition made by the Assessing Officer was not justified and order sustained by the Ld. CIT(A) deserved to be deleted. 06. The Ld. DR, on the other hand, submitted that the Assessing Officer had duly examined the facts of the case and arrived at the correct conclusion. He pointed out that out of the 12 subcontractors, statements of only 8 persons were recorded, whereas the remaining 4 persons did not appear for examination. He further submitted that one of the persons, namely, Shri Rameshbhai Laxmibhai Ambala, categorically denied having signed the confirmation letter attributed to him. Therefore, the confirmation furnished by the assessee could not be accepted at face value. He accordingly contended that the action of the Ld. CIT(A) sustaining the order of Assessing Officer deserved to be sustained. 07. We, after hearing the rival submissions of the parties and perusing the material available on record, we find that out of the 12 parties, only 8 parties were examined by the Assessing Officer, whereas the remaining 4 parties were not produced for examination. Further, the specific objection raised by the Ld. DR regarding the genuineness of the signature appearing on the confirmation letter of Shri Rameshbhai Laxmibhai Ambala requires proper verification. The denial of the signature by the concerned person is a material aspect which cannot be ignored. The Ld. AR also submitted that the transactions in question were subsequently repaid. This aspect, including the dates and mode of repayment and its relevance to the genuineness of the original transactions which clearly established the genuineness of transaction. In view of the above facts and circumstances, we deem it appropriate to restore the entire issue to the file of the Assessing Officer for fresh examination and adjudication in accordance with law, after providing a reasonable opportunity of being heard to the assessee. The assessee is also directed to furnish the requisite confirmations, supporting evidence and details of subsequent repayments before the Assessing Officer. Accordingly, the issue is restored to the file of the Assessing Officer for fresh adjudication. 08. In the result, the appeal of the assessee is allowed for statistical purposes. Order pronounced in the open court on this 17th day of November, 2026.

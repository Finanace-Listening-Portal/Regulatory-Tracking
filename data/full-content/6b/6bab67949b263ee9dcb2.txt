(Supreme Court of India) The Supreme Court’s five-judge Constitution Bench resolved conflicting interpretations of the time available to an opposite party for responding to a consumer complaint under Section 13(2)(a) of the Consumer Protection Act, 1986. The two referred questions were whether the District Forum could extend the initial 30-day period by more than the additional 15 days expressly permitted, and whether the limitation period started with receipt of notice alone or receipt of notice accompanied by the complaint. The conflict arose from divergent Supreme Court rulings on the mandatory or directory nature of the response deadline. The Court examined the Consumer Protection Act’s objective of simple and speedy consumer redressal and contrasted Section 13(2)(a) with provisions expressly allowing condonation of delay in complaints and appeals. Section 13(2)(b)(ii) provides for proceeding ex parte if an opposite party fails to respond within time, while Section 13(3) prevents a natural-justice challenge where the prescribed procedure is followed. In the absence of a statutory power to grant further time, the Court held that the 30-day period, extendable by no more than 15 days, is mandatory and cannot be enlarged beyond 45 days. The Constitution Bench rejected reliance on the flexibility available under Order VIII Rule 1 of the Code of Civil Procedure for ordinary civil suits. It distinguished Kailash v. Nanhku and Salem Advocate Bar Association v. Union of India on that basis, and discussed commercial suits, where a separate mandatory outer limit applies. The Court held that hardship and equitable considerations cannot override an unambiguous statutory deadline. It disagreed with the directory interpretation adopted in Topline Shoes Ltd. v. Corporation Bank and preferred the interpretation in Dr. J.J. Merchant v. Shrinath Chaturvedi and the earlier New India Assurance decision. On the commencement of limitation, the Court held that the opposite party must receive both the notice and a copy of the complaint before the 30-day clock starts, since a reply cannot meaningfully address allegations that have not been supplied. A party claiming non-receipt of the complaint copy must, however, raise that objection on the first date itself; it cannot wait until a later stage and thereby defeat the objective of speedy disposal. The Court answered the two references by holding that a District Forum has no power to extend the response deadline beyond 30 days plus 15 days, and that the 30-day period commences upon receipt of notice accompanied by the complaint, not notice alone. It expressly directed that its judgment would operate prospectively. The judgment answered the referred legal questions rather than individually determining the merits of every connected appeal.

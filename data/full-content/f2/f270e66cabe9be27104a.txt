Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); National Investigation Agency Vs Zahoor Ahmad Shah Watali (Supreme Court of India) Summary: The Supreme Court of India, in National Investigation Agency Vs Zahoor Ahmad Shah Watali, examined the restrictions on granting bail under Section 43D(5) of the Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act, 1967 (UAPA), particularly the requirement that courts assess whether accusations against an accused are prima facie true. The appeal was filed by the National Investigation Agency (NIA) against the Delhi High Court’s judgment dated 13 September 2018 granting bail to Zahoor Ahmad Shah Watali, who was accused in a case involving alleged terror funding and criminal conspiracy. The NIA had registered an FIR on 30 May 2017 under Sections 120B, 121 and 121A of the Indian Penal Code and Sections 13, 16, 17, 18, 20, 38, 39 and 40 of the UAPA. Watali was arrested on 17 August 2017 and was arrayed as Accused No. 10. The Special NIA Court rejected his bail application on 8 June 2018, but the Delhi High Court reversed that decision, prompting the NIA to approach the Supreme Court. The prosecution alleged that Watali was involved in a larger conspiracy to facilitate the transfer of funds from foreign sources to separatist leaders and persons allegedly associated with terrorist activities in Jammu and Kashmir. According to the NIA, funds were received through foreign remittances, banking channels and business entities and subsequently transferred to individuals connected with unlawful activities. The investigating agency relied on financial records, witness statements, documents recovered during searches, alleged communications between accused persons and records concerning transactions involving Watali’s businesses. The prosecution also referred to alleged connections with persons associated with Pakistan and separatist organisations. The Special Court examined the material and concluded that the accusations were prima facie supported by the evidence collected during investigation. It held that the statutory restriction under Section 43D(5) of the UAPA applied and rejected the application for regular bail. The Delhi High Court, while allowing Watali’s appeal, undertook a detailed examination of the prosecution material and questioned whether the documents established his involvement in terror funding. It examined financial transactions, business records, statements of witnesses and a document allegedly containing details of monetary transfers. The High Court expressed reservations about the evidentiary value of certain documents and considered whether the alleged transactions could reasonably establish links with terrorist activities. It also excluded statements of protected witnesses recorded under Section 164 of the Code of Criminal Procedure because copies had not been supplied to the accused. The High Court concluded that the material did not sufficiently demonstrate that the accusations were prima facie true. It additionally considered Watali’s age, medical condition, period of judicial custody and the absence of established circumstances indicating that he would abscond or interfere with the investigation. Consequently, the High Court granted bail subject to specified conditions. Before the Supreme Court, the NIA challenged the High Court’s approach, contending that it had effectively conducted a mini-trial while deciding the bail application. The investigating agency submitted that the High Court had improperly assessed the admissibility, reliability and sufficiency of individual pieces of evidence rather than examining whether the prosecution material collectively disclosed a prima facie case. The NIA argued that witness statements recorded under Sections 161 and 164 CrPC, financial documents, seizure records and other materials accompanying the charge sheet were relevant for the limited purpose of deciding bail. It further submitted that the questions concerning the ultimate admissibility and evidentiary weight of such material should ordinarily be determined during trial. Watali, on the other hand, defended the High Court’s judgment and maintained that the prosecution had failed to establish a sufficient connection between the financial transactions and the alleged terrorist offences. He contended that judicial scrutiny of the material was necessary before applying the stringent bail restrictions under the UAPA. The Supreme Court examined Section 43D of the UAPA and explained that the proviso to Section 43D(5) prohibits the release on bail of an accused charged with offences punishable under Chapters IV and VI where the court finds reasonable grounds for believing that the accusations are prima facie true. The Court distinguished this requirement from the bail provisions under other special statutes, including the Terrorist and Disruptive Activities (Prevention) Act and the Maharashtra Control of Organised Crime Act, where courts are required to consider whether there are reasonable grounds for believing that the accused is not guilty. Under the UAPA, the statutory inquiry is directed towards the apparent truth of the accusations rather than a conclusive finding concerning guilt or innocence. The Supreme Court clarified that the degree of satisfaction required at this stage is lower than the standard applicable when considering discharge or framing charges. The prosecution material must be assessed on its face to determine whether it sufficiently indicates the accused’s involvement in the alleged offences. Explaining the expression prima facie true, the Supreme Court held that the material collected by the investigating agency must, on its face, support the allegations and indicate the accused’s involvement unless contradicted or overcome by other evidence. The Court emphasised that the bail stage does not require a detailed examination of the prosecution evidence comparable to a criminal trial. Courts should not conduct an elaborate assessment of the credibility, admissibility or ultimate probative value of individual documents and witness statements. Instead, the material must be considered in its entirety to determine whether the statutory threshold has been satisfied. The Supreme Court observed that the Delhi High Court had adopted an incorrect approach by excluding certain prosecution materials and undertaking a detailed evaluation of documents and transactions. Such an exercise exceeded the limited scope of examination contemplated under Section 43D(5) of the UAPA. Applying these principles, the Supreme Court considered the allegations concerning foreign remittances, financial transactions, business entities, witness statements and alleged associations between Watali and other accused persons. It noted that the Special Court had examined the prosecution material and concluded that reasonable grounds existed for believing that the accusations were prima facie true. The Supreme Court found that the Delhi High Court had improperly substituted its own detailed evidentiary assessment for the statutory inquiry required at the bail stage. The Court emphasised that the question was not whether the prosecution had conclusively proved the alleged offences, but whether the available material, viewed collectively and on its face, satisfied the prima facie standard prescribed by the UAPA. It concluded that the Special Court had correctly applied the relevant legal principles and that the High Court’s judgment granting bail was legally unsustainable. Accordingly, the Supreme Court allowed the appeal filed by the National Investigation Agency, set aside the Delhi High Court’s order dated 13 September 2018 and restored the Special Court’s order dated 8 June 2018 rejecting Watali’s bail application. The Court held that the statutory restrictions under Section 43D(5) of the UAPA prevented the grant of bail in the circumstances presented. The judgment establishes that courts deciding bail applications involving offences under Chapters IV and VI of the UAPA must determine whether the accusations are prima facie true on the basis of the prosecution material, without conducting a detailed trial-stage evaluation of evidence. The ruling also clarifies that the assessment made at the bail stage is provisional and does not amount to a final determination of criminal liability. The decision remains an important authority concerning the interpretation of stringent bail restrictions under the UAPA and the limited scope of judicial scrutiny when considering such applications.

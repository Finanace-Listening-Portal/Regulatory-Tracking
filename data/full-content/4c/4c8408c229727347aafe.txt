Summary: The article examines the Uniform Civil Code debate through the interconnected questions of gender justice, constitutional rights and the allocation of legislative power between Parliament and State legislatures. Beginning with women’s equality and international human-rights instruments, it considers Article 44’s directive to secure a common civil code and discusses Goa’s civil-law framework, Uttarakhand’s legislation and the wider movement towards State-level enactments. Its central concern is the potential conflict between State civil codes and a subsequent Parliamentary law covering the same subject. The discussion addresses the Concurrent List, the doctrine of repugnancy under Article 254, the protection available to certain State laws receiving Presidential assent and Parliament’s continuing power to override that protection through subsequent legislation. It refers to Supreme Court authorities on repugnancy and personal-law reform, while also considering the constitutional balance between enforceable fundamental rights and Directive Principles. The article contrasts the case for a national code with the 21st Law Commission’s 2018 preference for removing discrimination through reforms within personal laws. It further identifies cultural diversity, tribal customs, religious freedom and transitional questions affecting pending proceedings as important implementation concerns. Ultimately, the author favours a centrally enacted Uniform Civil Code developed after considering the country’s traditions, customs and social circumstances, while recognising that its design remains a contested policy question. Concurrent Legislative Powers and the Doctrine of Repugnancy The Laws that are aimed to codified into one comes under Concurrent List which is enumerated in Seventh Schedule to the Constitution of India in which both the Union and State Governments have power to legislate the laws subject to that if there is repugnancy arises Union Law will always prevail over State Law unless otherwise. If the states in India legislating the Uniform Civil Code according to their state traditions, customs or it can be unified, what if the Union of India legislate Uniform Civil Code with the same context by then according Doctrine of Repugnancy comes into play then the Union Law will prevail over State Law as per Article 254 of the Constitution of India. The said Doctrine of Repugnancy has two sides of coin, one side will make state law as void and another side of the coin states if and only if the state law direct or inconsistency with the Union law has to prove to make the said Law to be void as also stated by the Apex Court of India in State of Kerala vs Mar Appraem Kuri Co.Ltd, 2012 (7) SCC 106. Time and again the Apex Court of India through its verdicts namely Mohd.Ahmed Khan vs Shah Bano Begum 1985(2)SCC 556, Sarla Mudgal vs Union of India 1995 (3) SCC 635, John Vellamattom vs Union of India 2003 SCC Online SC 716,Shayara Bano vs Union of India 2017(9) SCC 1, Jose Paulo Coutinho vs Maria Luiza Valentia Pere 2019 SCC Online SC 1196 manifested dismay about Uniform Civil Code and implored to Union of India to take necessary step to enact Uniform Civil Code. While Article 44 places the Uniform Civil Code within the Directive Principles of State Policy, it does not stand alone in the constitutional scheme. Part III of the Constitution guarantees the right to equality (Article 14), non-discrimination (Article 15), life and personal liberty (Article 21), and freedom of religion (Articles 25 and 26), all of which are justifiable rights enforceable before courts, unlike the non-justifiable directives of Part IV. The Supreme Court in Minerva Mills v. Union of India (1980) held that a harmonious balance between Part III and Part IV forms part of the basic structure of the Constitution, and it is precisely this balance that any UCC legislation must reconcile. Law Commission’s Approach to Personal-Law Reform The Law Commission of India has, in more than one occasion, examined this very question. In its Consultation Paper on Reform of Family Law released in 2018, the 21st Law Commission took the view that a Uniform Civil Code was neither necessary nor desirable at that stage, and recommended instead that the discriminatory practices within each personal law be identified and remedied through incremental, codified reform, so that gender justice could be achieved without eroding the diversity of personal laws. This position was not binding on the Union, being in the nature of a recommendation rather than law, but it remains an influential and frequently cited policy assessment of the subject. The debate has, however, continued to evolve, with subsequent Law Commissions inviting fresh public consultation on the desirability and contours of a Uniform Civil Code, reflecting the fact that this remains very much a live and unsettled question of policy rather than one on which a constitutional consensus has been reached.

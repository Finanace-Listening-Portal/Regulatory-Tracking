Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); Har Parsad Gaur Vs State (NCT of Delhi) (Supreme Court of India) Mere Recovery of Bribe Money Cannot Prove Guilt: Supreme Court Acquits Tehsildar as Official Records Contradict Alleged Demand Introduction Recovery of tainted currency and a positive chemical wash test cannot, by themselves, sustain a corruption conviction when demand and conscious acceptance of illegal gratification remain unproved. Reaffirming this principle, the Supreme Court acquitted a Tehsildar after finding that contemporaneous official records contradicted the very basis of the prosecution’s allegation. In Har Parsad Gaur v. State (NCT of Delhi), Criminal Appeal No. 571 of 2015, decided on 17 November 2026, 2026 INSC 1230, the Bench of Justice Ujjal Bhuyan and Justice Atul S. Chandurkar explained that the statutory presumption under Section 20 of the Prevention of Corruption Act, 1988 cannot substitute proof of the foundational facts necessary to attract it. Background of the Dispute The complainant, Puran Chand, had obtained a Labour Court award directing his employer to pay outstanding wages. Recovery proceedings were subsequently entrusted to the appellant, who was serving as Tehsildar, Kotwali. According to the complainant, the appellant initially demanded and received ₹3,000 for expediting recovery. He allegedly demanded a further ₹1,000 for forwarding the recovered cheques to the Labour Commissioner’s office. Following a complaint to the Anti-Corruption Bureau, a trap was arranged on 6 January 2003 near the State Bank of India branch at the Tis Hazari court complex. Two treated currency notes of ₹500 each were recovered from the appellant’s coat pocket. His hand wash and coat-pocket wash turned pink. The trial court convicted the appellant under Sections 7 and 13(1)(d), read with Section 13(2). The Delhi High Court upheld the conviction while reducing the imprisonment from two years to one year. Death of Witnesses Does Not Automatically Defeat Prosecution Both the complainant and the panch witness died before their evidence could be recorded. The Supreme Court clarified that this circumstance does not automatically result in acquittal. Following the Constitution Bench decision in Neeraj Dutta v. State (Government of NCT of Delhi), (2023) 4 SCC 731, demand and acceptance may be established through other witnesses, documentary material or circumstantial evidence. However, the absence of these witnesses does not dilute the prosecution’s burden. The remaining evidence must establish the necessary facts beyond reasonable doubt. Circumstantial evidence must support an irresistible inference of guilt. Trap Officer’s Account Was Hearsay The prosecution substantially relied upon the trap officer, Inspector S.S. Sandhu. Although he witnessed the appellant meeting the complainant, he had neither personally heard the alleged demand nor witnessed conscious acceptance of the bribe. His account of the transaction depended upon what the panch witness allegedly told him after giving the predetermined signal. The Court held that this part of his testimony was hearsay and inadmissible in the circumstances. Without the panch witness testifying to the correctness of that account, the officer’s narration could not establish demand and voluntary acceptance. Consequently, the principal surviving circumstances were the recovery of the currency notes and the positive chemical wash results. Official Records Contradicted the Alleged Demand The decisive evidence came from the official records. The complaint alleged that the appellant was withholding two cheques and demanding money as a condition for forwarding them. However, the order sheet and forwarding letter established that both cheques had already been dispatched on 28 December 2002 and received by the Deputy Labour Commissioner’s office on 1 January 2003. These dates were corroborated by departmental witnesses. The complainant subsequently collected the cheques on 7 January 2003. Thus, the work allegedly being withheld had already been completed before the trap on 6 January 2003. The Court found that the allegation of a demand for releasing the withheld cheques was contrary to contemporaneous official records. Presumption Cannot Replace Foundational Proof Relying upon P. Satyanarayana Murthy v. State of A.P., (2015) 10 SCC 152, the two Neeraj Dutta decisions and other authorities, the Court reiterated that mere recovery cannot sustain the charges where the necessary proof is absent. For the demand-based prosecution before it, demand and conscious acceptance had to be established before invoking Section 20. The presumption could not be used to fill that evidentiary gap. The Court also reiterated that Section 20 does not apply to offences under Section 13(1)(d)(i) and (ii) under the statutory framework considered. Plausible Defence and Final Decision The appellant’s explanation was that the complainant met him to express gratitude and attempted to thrust the notes into his pocket while shaking hands. Considering the records showing prior completion of work, the Court found this explanation plausible. While the prosecution must prove guilt beyond reasonable doubt, the accused can establish a defence or rebut the presumption on a preponderance of probabilities. The Supreme Court accordingly set aside both judgments and acquitted the appellant. Author’s Comments The judgment demonstrates why contemporaneous documents must be examined alongside trap evidence. Chemical tests establish contact with treated currency; they do not independently establish the circumstances of its receipt. Equally, the decision should not be read as granting automatic immunity whenever official work was completed or a complainant dies. Its significance lies in the combined evidentiary deficiencies. The ruling concerns the pre-amendment provisions applicable to the 2003 incident, and its reasoning must be applied with that statutory context in view. Section 2 of the PC Act provides various definitions of the words and expressions used in the PC Act. Section 2(c) defines the expression ‘public servant’ to mean and include any person in the service or pay of the government or remunerated by the government by fees or commission for the performance of any public duty; any person in the service or pay of a local authority etc. 24. There is no doubt, therefore, that appellant Har Parsad Gaur, who was serving as Tahsildar, Kotwali, was a public servant within the meaning of the PC Act. 25. As noticed supra, appellant was prosecuted and convicted under Sections 7 and 13(1)(d) of the PC Act. Occurrence had taken place on 06.01.2003. 26. Section 7 of the PC Act, as it stood at the relevant point of time, dealt with public servant taking gratification other than legal remuneration in respect of an official act. Section 7, at the relevant point of time, stood as under: 7. Public servant taking gratification other than legal remuneration in respect of an official act.- Whoever, being, or expecting to be a public servant, accepts or obtains or agrees to accept or attempts to obtain from any person, for himself or for any other person, any gratification whatever, other than legal remuneration, as a motive or reward for doing or forbearing to do any official act or for showing or forbearing to show, in the exercise of his official functions, favour or disfavour to any person or for rendering or attempting to render any service or disservice to any person, with the Central Government or any State Government or Parliament or the Legislature of any State or with any local authority, corporation or Government company referred to in clause (c) of section 2, or with any public servant, whether named or otherwise, shall be punishable with imprisonment which shall be not less than three years but which may extend to seven years and shall also be liable to fine. Explanations. – (a) ‘Expecting to be a public servant’. If a person not expecting to be in office obtains a gratification by deceiving others into a belief that he is about to be in office, and that he will then serve them, he may be guilty of cheating, but he is not guilty of the offence defined in this section. (b) ‘Gratification’. The word ‘gratification’ is not restricted to pecuniary gratifications or to gratifications estimable in money. (c) ‘Legal remuneration’. The words ‘legal remuneration’ are not restricted to remuneration which a public servant can lawfully demand, but include all remuneration which he is permitted by the Government or the organization, which he serves, to accept. (d) ‘A motive or reward for doing’. A person who receives a gratification as a motive or reward for doing what he does not intend or is not in a position to do, or has not done, comes within this expression. (e) Where a public servant induces a person erroneously to believe that his influence with the Government has obtained a title for that person and thus induces that person to give the public servant, money or any other gratification as a reward for this service, the public servant has committed an offence under this section. 27. In Jaswinder Singh Vs. State of Punjab10, this Court has analyzed Section 7 in the following manner: 10. As per Section 7, whoever being or expecting to be a public servant, accepts or obtains or agrees to accept or attempts to obtain from any person for himself or for any other person, any gratification other than legal remuneration as a motive or reward for doing or not doing any official act or for showing or not showing in the exercise of his official function(s) favour or disfavour to any person etc. with the Central Government or State Government or any authority mentioned in the said section or with any public servant, shall be punishable with imprisonment which shall be not less than three years but which may extend to seven years and shall also be liable to fine. 10.1. There are five explanations to Section 7. As per Explanation (a), the expression ‘expecting to be a public servant’ refers to and is in respect of a person not expecting to be in office, who obtains a gratification by deceiving others into believing that he is about to be in office and that he would then serve them, may be guilty of the offence of cheating but would not be guilty of the offence defined in Section 7. Explanation (b) clarifies that ‘gratification’ is not restricted to pecuniary gratification or a gratification which is estimable in terms of money. Explanation (c) clarifies that the words ‘legal remuneration’ are not restricted to remuneration which a public servant can lawfully demand but will include all remuneration which a public servant is permitted to accept. The expression ‘a motive or reward for doing’ has been explained in Explanation (d). It says that a person who receives a gratification as a motive or reward for doing what he does not intend or is not in a position to do or has not done, comes within the ambit of this expression. In terms of Explanation (e), where a public servant induces a person erroneously to believe that his influence with the Government has obtained the title for that person and thus induces that person to give the public servant, money or other gratification as a reward for this service, the public servant has committed an offence under this section. 28. Criminal misconduct by a public servant. – (1) A public servant is said to commit the offence of criminal misconduct, – * * * * * * (d) if he, – (i) by corrupt or illegal means, obtains for himself or for any other person any valuable thing or pecuniary advantage; or (ii) by abusing his position as a public servant, obtains for himself or for any other person any valuable thing or pecuniary advantage; or (iii) while holding office as a public servant, obtains for any person any valuable thing or pecuniary advantage without any public interest; or * * * * * * 29. For committing the offence of criminal misconduct in the aforesaid manner, appellant was also punished under sub- section (2) of Section 13 which is extracted hereunder: (2) Any public servant who commits criminal misconduct shall be punishable with imprisonment for a term which shall be not less than one year but which may extend to seven years and shall also be liable to fine. 30. Section 13(1)(d), as it stood at the relevant point of time, declared that a public servant would commit the offence of criminal misconduct if he by corrupt or illegal means, obtains for himself or for any other person any valuable thing or pecuniary advantage; or, by abusing his position as a public servant, obtains for himself or for any other person any valuable thing or pecuniary advantage; or, while holding office as a public servant, obtains for any person any valuable thing or pecuniary advantage without any public interest. For committing the offence of criminal misconduct as aforesaid, a public servant would be liable to be punished with imprisonment for a term which shall not be less than one year (four years with effect from 16.01.2014) but which may extend to seven years (ten years with effect from 16.01.2014) and shall also be liable to fine. 31. It would also be necessary to advert to Section 20 of the PC Act, inasmuch as, the High Court relied upon the aforesaid provision to draw the presumption against the appellant that he had committed the offence under Sections 7 and 13 of the PC Act. Section 20 was substituted with effect from 26.07.2018. Prior to its substitution, Section 20 stood as under: 20. Presumption where public servant accepts gratification other than legal remuneration.—(1) Where, in any trial of an offence punishable under section 7 or section 11 or clause (a) or clause (b) of sub-section (1) of section 13 it is proved that an accused person has accepted or obtained or has agreed to accept or attempted to obtain for himself, or for any other person, any gratification (other than legal remuneration) or any valuable thing from any person, it shall be presumed, unless the contrary is proved, that he accepted or obtained or agreed to accept or attempted to obtain that gratification or that valuable thing, as the case may be, as a motive or reward such as is mentioned in section 7 or, as the case may be, without consideration or for a consideration which he knows to be inadequate. (2) Where in any trial of an offence punishable under section 12 or under clause (b) of section 14, it is proved that any gratification (other than legal remuneration) or any valuable thing has been given or offered to be given or attempted to be given by an accused person, it shall be presumed, unless the contrary is proved, that he gave or offered to give or attempted to give that gratification or that valuable thing, as the case may be, as a motive or reward such as is mentioned in section 7, or as the case may be, without consideration or for a consideration which he knows to be inadequate. (3) Notwithstanding anything contained in sub- sections (1) and (2), the court may decline to draw the presumption referred to in either of the said sub- sections, if the gratification or thing aforesaid is, in its opinion, so trivial that no inference of corruption may fairly be drawn. 31.1. Sub-section (1) of Section 20 is relevant for our present purpose. It says that where in any trial of an offence punishable under Section 7 or Section 13 or clause (a) or clause (b) of sub-section (1) of Section 13 it is proved that an accused person has accepted or obtained or has agreed to accept or attempted to obtain for himself or for any other person, any gratification (other than legal remuneration) or any valuable thing from any person, it shall be presumed unless the contrary is proved that the accused public servant accepted or obtained or agreed to accept or attempted to obtain that gratification or that valuable thing, as the case may be, as a motive or reward as mentioned in Section 7 or, as the case may be, without consideration or for a consideration which he knows to be inadequate. 32. In P. Satyanarayana Murthy Vs. State of A.P.11, this Court held that proof of demand of illegal gratification is the sine qua non for the offence under Sections 7 and 13(1)(d)(i) and (ii) of the PC Act. Mere acceptance of any amount allegedly by way of illegal gratification or recovery thereof without the proof of demand would not be sufficient to record a conviction under Sections 7 and 13(1)(d)(i) and (ii) of the PC Act. This Court held that failure of the prosecution to prove the demand for illegal gratification would be fatal; mere recovery of the amount from the person accused of the offence under Sections 7 or 13 of the PC Act would not result in his conviction thereunder. Paragraph 23 of the said decision is relevant and is extracted hereunder: 23. The proof of demand of illegal gratification, thus, is the gravamen of the offence under Sections 7 and 13(1)(d)(i) and (ii) of the Act and in absence thereof, unmistakably the charge therefor, would fail. Mere acceptance of any amount allegedly by way of illegal gratification or recovery thereof, dehors the proof of demand, ipso facto, would thus not be sufficient to bring home the charge under these two sections of the Act. As a corollary, failure of the prosecution to prove the demand for illegal gratification would be fatal and mere recovery of the amount from the person accused of the offence under Sections 7 or 13 of the Act would not entail his conviction thereunder. 33. A Constitution Bench of this Court in Neeraj Dutta-1 considered the following question for determination: Whether, in the absence of evidence of the complainant, direct or primary evidence of demand of illegal gratification, is it not permissible to draw an inferential deduction of culpability/ guilt of a public servant under Section 7 and Section 13(1)(d) read with Section 13(2) of the PC Act based on other evidence adduced by the prosecution? 33.1. It is not necessary to delve into a detailed analysis of the aforesaid decision of the Constitution Bench. Suffice it to say that the question referred was answered in the following manner: In the absence of evidence of the complainant (direct/ primary, oral/documentary evidence) it is permissible to draw an inferential deduction of the culpability/guilt of a public servant under Section 7 and Section 13(1)(d) read with Section 13(2) of the PC Act based on other evidence adduced by the prosecution. 33.2. Thus, the Constitution Bench held that in the absence of evidence of the complainant, it is permissible to draw an inferential deduction of culpability of a public servant under Section 7 and Section 13(1)(d) read with Section 13(2) of the PC Act based on other evidence adduced by the prosecution. 33.3. The conclusions reached by the Constitution Bench are as under: 88. What emerges from the aforesaid discussion is summarised as under: 88.1. (a) Proof of demand and acceptance of illegal gratification by a public servant as a fact in issue by the prosecution is a sine qua non in order to establish the guilt of the accused public servant under Sections 7 and 13(1)(d)(i) and (ii) of the Act. 88.2. (b) In order to bring home the guilt of the accused, the prosecution has to first prove the demand of illegal gratification and the subsequent acceptance as a matter of fact. This fact in issue can be proved either by direct evidence which can be in the nature of oral evidence or documentary evidence. 88.3. (c) Further, the fact in issue, namely, the proof of demand and acceptance of illegal gratification can also be proved by circumstantial evidence in the absence of direct oral and documentary evidence. 88.4. (d) In order to prove the fact in issue, namely, the demand and acceptance of illegal gratification by the public servant, the following aspects have to be borne in mind: (i) if there is an offer to pay by the bribe-giver without there being any demand from the public servant and the latter simply accepts the offer and receives the illegal gratification, it is a case of acceptance as per Section 7 of the Act. In such a case, there need not be a prior demand by the public servant. (ii) On the other hand, if the public servant makes a demand and the bribe-giver accepts the demand and tenders the demanded gratification which in turn is received by the public servant, it is a case of obtainment. In the case of obtainment, the prior demand for illegal gratification emanates from the public servant. This is an offence under Sections 13(1)(d)(i) and (ii) of the Act. (iii) In both cases of (i) and (ii) above, the offer by the bribe-giver and the demand by the public servant respectively have to be proved by the prosecution as a fact in issue. In other words, mere acceptance or receipt of an illegal gratification without anything more would not make it an offence under Section 7 or Sections 13(1)(d)(i) and (ii), respectively of the Act. Therefore, under Section 7 of the Act, in order to bring home the offence, there must be an offer which emanates from the bribe-giver which is accepted by the public servant which would make it an offence. Similarly, a prior demand by the public servant when accepted by the bribe-giver and in turn there is a payment made which is received by the public servant, would be an offence of obtainment under Sections 13(1)(d)(i) and (ii) of the Act. 88.5. (e) The presumption of fact with regard to the demand and acceptance or obtainment of an illegal gratification may be made by a court of law by way of an inference only when the foundational facts have been proved by relevant oral and documentary evidence and not in the absence thereof. On the basis of the material on record, the court has the discretion to raise a presumption of fact while considering whether the fact of demand has been proved by the prosecution or not. Of course, a presumption of fact is subject to rebuttal by the accused and in the absence of rebuttal presumption stands. 88.6. (f) In the event the complainant turns ‘hostile’, or has died or is unavailable to let in his evidence during trial, demand of illegal gratification can be proved by letting in the evidence of any other witness who can again let in evidence, either orally or by documentary evidence or the prosecution can prove the case by circumstantial evidence. The trial does not abate nor does it result in an order of acquittal of the accused public servant. 88.7. (g) Insofar as Section 7 of the Act is concerned, on the proof of the facts in issue, Section 20 mandates the court to raise a presumption that the illegal gratification was for the purpose of a motive or reward as mentioned in the said Section. The said presumption has to be raised by the court as a legal presumption or a presumption in law. Of course, the said presumption is also subject to rebuttal. Section 20 does not apply to Sections 13(1)(d)(i) and (ii) of the Act. * * * * * * 33.4. Thus what the Constitution Bench held is that proof of demand and acceptance of illegal gratification by a public servant as a fact in issue by the prosecution is a sine qua non in order to establish the guilt of the accused public servant under Sections 7 and 13(1)(d)(i) and (ii) of the PC Act. The above fact in issue can be proved by direct evidence as well as by circumstantial evidence. Demand must precede acceptance. Mere acceptance or receipt of an illegal gratification without anything more would not make it an offence under Section 7 or Section 13(1)(d)(i) and (ii) of the PC Act. The presumption of fact with regard to the demand and acceptance or obtainment of illegal gratification may be made by a court of law by way of an inference under Section 20 only when the foundational facts have been proved by relevant oral and documentary evidence and not in the absence thereof. Section 20 does not apply to Section 13(1)(d)(i) and (ii) of the PC Act. In the event, the complainant turns hostile or has died or becomes unavailable to let in evidence during trial, demand of illegal gratification can be proved by letting in the evidence of any other witness. For the aforesaid eventualities, the trial does not abate nor does it result in the acquittal of the accused public servant. 34. A two-Judge Bench of this Court in Neeraj Dutta-2 examined the conviction of the appellant for the offences punishable under Sections 7 and Section 13(1)(d)(i) and (ii) of the PC Act following the declaration of law by the Constitution Bench in Neeraj Dutta-1. The Bench referred to earlier decisions of this Court dealing with the presumption under Section 20 of the PC Act and held that the presumption under Section 20 can be invoked only when the two basic facts required to be proved under Section 7 are proved. The two basic facts are ‘demand’ and ‘acceptance’ of gratification. Unless the above two basic facts are proved, the presumption under Section 20 would not be available. The demand for gratification and its acceptance must be proved beyond a reasonable doubt. In this regard, reference was made to the decision of the Constitution Bench in Neeraj Dutta-1 which held that only on proof of the facts in issue, Section 20 would mandate the court to raise a presumption that illegal gratification was for the purpose of motive or reward as mentioned in Section 7. That apart, the demand within the meaning of Section 7 cannot be a simplicitor demand for money but it has to be a demand of gratification other than legal remuneration. This Court held thus: 16. The presumption under Section 20 can be invoked only when the two basic facts required to be proved under Section 7, are proved. The said two basic facts are ‘demand’ and ‘acceptance’ of gratification. The presumption under Section 20 is that unless the contrary is proved, the acceptance of gratification shall be presumed to be for a motive or reward, as contemplated by Section 7. It means that once the basic facts of the demand of illegal gratification and acceptance thereof are proved, unless the contrary are proved, the court will have to presume that the gratification was demanded and accepted as a motive or reward as contemplated by Section 7. However, this presumption is rebuttable. Even on the basis of the preponderance of probability, the accused can rebut the presumption. 35. Aman Bhatia Vs. State12 is a two Judge Bench decision of this Court where it has been held that mere recovery of tainted money, by itself, would be insufficient to establish the charge against an accused under the PC Act. The Bench held that to sustain a conviction under Sections 7 and 13(1)(d) of the PC Act, it must be proved beyond reasonable doubt that the public servant voluntarily accepted the money, knowing it to be a bribe. It has been held as under: 52. It is well-settled that mere recovery of tainted money, by itself, is insufficient to establish the charges against an accused under the PC Act. To sustain a conviction under Sections 7 and 13(1)(d) of the Act respectively, it must be proved beyond reasonable doubt that the public servant voluntarily accepted the money, knowing it to be a bribe. The courts have consistently reiterated that the demand for a bribe is sine qua non for establishing an offence under Section 7 of the PC Act. 36. Similar view has been taken by another two-Judge Bench of this Court in A.K. Gaba where it has been held as follows: 16. The proof of demand of illegal gratification is the gravamen of the offence and in absence thereof, the presumption under Section 20 of the P.C. Act would not arise. Where prosecution evidence is inconsistent and material witnesses turn hostile, benefit of doubt must necessarily enure to the accused. 37. At this stage, let us briefly examine the judgments cited by the respondent. Ofcourse, four of the judgments relied upon by learned senior counsel for the respondent were delivered much before the decision of the Constitution Bench of this Court in Neeraj Dutta-1. Even in those cases also where reliance was placed on Section 114 of the Evidence Act, 1882, the conclusion reached by this Court is that the presumption under Section 20(1) of the PC Act is contingent upon the fact that during trial it should be proved that the accused had accepted or agreed to accept any gratification, though this condition need not be satisfied only through direct evidence. Let us therefore analyze the two judgments relied upon by the respondent which were rendered post Neeraj Dutta-1. 38. In P. Sarangapani, a two-Judge Bench of this Court relied upon the Constitution Bench judgment in Neeraj Dutta-1 and held that death of the complainant or non-availability of the complainant at the time of trial could not be said to be fatal to the case of the prosecution nor could it be said to be a ground to acquit the accused. In the absence of the complainant, it is open to the prosecution to prove the contents of the complaint and other facts in issue by leading other oral or documentary evidence. Referring to Section 20 of the PC Act, the Bench held that once the undue advantage i.e. any gratification whatsoever other than the legal remuneration is proved to have been accepted by the accused public servant, the court is entitled to raise the presumption under Section 20 that he had accepted the undue advantage as a motive or reward under Section 7 for performing or to cause performance of a public duty improperly or dishonestly. The Bench however clarified that such presumption is rebuttable. 38.1. In the facts of that case, the Bench held that the pre- trap and post-trap proceedings were duly proved by the prosecution. Both the courts below had recorded the findings that the prosecution had proved beyond reasonable doubt the conscious acceptance of the tainted currency by the accused and also the recovery of tainted currency from the accused- appellant. Therefore, the burden had shifted onto the appellant to dispel the statutory presumption under Section 20 of the PC Act. The explanation offered by the appellant was not sufficient to rebut the presumption. In the circumstances, the Bench while dismissing the appeal, confirmed the conviction and sentence of the accused-appellant. 39. A three-Judge Bench of this Court in Chandrasha also examined the operation and application of the presumption under Section 20 of the PC Act. Referring to various decisions of this Court including the Constitution Bench decision in Neeraj Dutta-1, the three-Judge Bench held that the presumption under Section 20 of the PC Act can be drawn only after demand for and acceptance of illegal gratification is proved. Section 20 does not apply to Section 13(1)(d)(i) and (ii) of the PC Act. After a thorough analysis, the Bench held that the two basic facts of ‘demand’ and ‘acceptance’ of gratification stood proved; thus, the presumption under Section 20 could be invoked to the effect that the gratification was demanded and accepted as a motive or reward as contemplated under Section 7 of the PC Act. While clarifying that such presumption is rebuttable, the Bench opined that even on the basis of pre-ponderance of probability, the accused public servant can rebut such presumption. In the facts of that case, the Bench held that prosecution had proved its case beyond reasonable doubt in respect of the demand and acceptance of the bribe amount from the complainant by the accused-appellant and recovery of tainted currency notes from the possession of the accused-appellant. Therefore, this Court held that the trial court had rightly drawn the presumption under Section 20 of the PC Act. 40. Having surveyed the statutory provisions and the relevant judgments, we may now revert back to the evidence tendered in this case. As noticed above, prosecution examined 10 witnesses. However, it is a fact that neither the complainant Puran Chand nor the panch (shadow) witness Ramesh Chand could enter the witness box as both of them passed away before their depositions could be recorded by the trial court. As a result, there is no direct or primary oral evidence of: (i) any prior demand of bribe allegedly made by the appellant to the complainant on 23.09.2002 or on 07.12.2002; (ii) any telephonic conversation or demand allegedly made by the appellant to the complainant on the morning of 06.01.2003; and (iii) the exchange of words, demand of bribe and/or conscious voluntary acceptance of illegal gratification by and between the complainant and the appellant at the spot outside the SBI building on the afternoon of 06.01.2003. 41. Thus, the prosecution case regarding the trap rests entirely on the testimony of the trap officer i.e. Inspector S.S. Sandhu (PW-10). In his examination-in-chief, he stated that at the relevant point of time, he was posted as Inspector in the Anti-Corruption Bureau, Government of NCT of Delhi. On 06.01.2003, at about 11:00 am, the complainant had come to the office where his complaint was recorded in the presence of the panch witness Ramesh Chand. The complaint was regarding demand of bribe of Rs. 1,000.00 by the appellant from the complainant. Appellant was serving as Tehsildar, Kotwali and he had demanded the bribe from the complainant for forwarding the two cheques received from M/s. Pooja Printers to the office of the Labour Commissioner. He then narrated the steps taken including chemical treatment of the two currency notes of Rs. 500 denomination furnished by the complainant. He stated that the complainant was instructed to do the transaction in such a manner that the panch witness should be able to see the transaction and also hear the conversation. Likewise, the panch witness was instructed to remain close to the complainant and to overhear the conversation between the complainant and the appellant and to see the transaction whereafter he was instructed to give the signal by hurling his hand over his head. 41.1. PW-10 stated that at around 12:30 pm of 06.01.2003, he alongwith the complainant, panch witness, Inspector H.N. Meena and other members of the raiding party left the office in a government Maruti van for the SBI building. On reaching the place, the government vehicle was left at some distance away with Inspector H.N. Meena and the driver staying behind. While the complainant and the panch witness went ahead, PW-10 and other members of the raiding party followed them whereafter they took suitable position. 41.2. While the complainant and the panch witness stood near a STD booth outside the SBI Branch, at around 01:05 pm, PW-10 saw one person, who was medium built and was wearing blue coat, coming near the complainant. He kept his hand on the back of the complainant. PW-10 saw the complainant shaking hands with that person and starting a conversation. He stated that at about 01:10 pm, he received the pre-determined signal from the panch witness whereafter he alongwith the raiding team rushed towards the spot where the panch witness told him that the appellant had specifically demanded and accepted bribe of Rs. 1,000.00. While the bribe was received by his right hand, appellant kept the same in the right side inner pocket of his coat. 41.3. In his cross examination, PW-10 stated that the panch witness had told him that the accused (appellant) had accepted the bribe money with his left hand and had kept the same in the inner right side pocket of his coat. 42. Notwithstanding the right hand left hand discrepancy, it is evident that the above statement of PW-10 regarding what was narrated to him by the panch witness is nothing but hearsay evidence. PW-10 himself had not seen or heard any demand for illegal bribe being made by the appellant or acceptance of the same. It was entirety relayed to him by the panch witness. In the absence of the panch (shadow) witness testifying as to the correctness of the said statement, such hearsay statement of PW-10 would be inadmissible in evidence and cannot establish the fact in issue viz. the demand for and voluntary acceptance of illegal gratification by the appellant. 43. What remains on record is the recovery of the tainted currency notes from the right side inner pocket of the coat worn by the appellant and the fact that the left hand wash as well as the coat pocket wash had turned pink. 44. We have already discussed supra, buttressed by various decisions of this Court, that mere recovery of tainted currency notes, absent proof of demand, cannot sustain a conviction under Sections 7 and 13(1)(d) of the PC Act. 45. In the absence of any primary evidence of demand, prosecution was under an obligation to prove the circumstances leading to an irresistible inference of guilt of the appellant. 46. An examination of the official record would belie the prosecution case regarding motive and demand. The crux of the complaint Ex P-10/A was that the appellant was withholding two recovery cheques received from M/s. Pooja Printers and was demanding a bribe of Rs. 1,000.00 as a condition precedent to forward the two cheques to the office of the Labour Commissioner. However, contemporaneous official record seized by the investigating agency belie this assertion. The original order sheet Ex. P-7/A and the forwarding letter Ex. P-7/F established that the appellant’s office had already dispatched both the cheques to the office of the Deputy Labour Commissioner on 28.12.2002. This fact is corroborated by PW-4 Sudhir Kumar who was serving as LDC in the office of the Deputy Labour Commissioner and PW-7 Suresh Kumar Verma who was also serving as LDC in the office of the Sub-Divisional Magistrate/Tehsildar. Both the witnesses deposed that the two cheques were dispatched on 28.12.2002 and were received in the office of the Deputy Labour Commissioner on 01.01.2003. Furthermore, the proceedings before the Assistant Labour Commissioner dated 07.01.2003 (Ex. P-14) revealed that the complainant Puran Chand had appeared before the Labour Department on 07.01.2003 and collected both the cheques which were lying deposited there since 01.01.2003. Therefore, no official work or proceeding in relation to the complainant was pending before the appellant on 06.01.2003. The official act of recovering the amount and transmitting the cheques to the Labour Department stood executed by the appellant on 28.12.2002 and had reached the Labour Department on 01.01.2003. In the above backdrop, the charge that appellant had telephoned the complainant in the morning of 06.01.2003 threatening that the cheques would be with-held unless Rs. 1,000.00 was paid to him as bribe, is contrary to the official record. 47. In the circumstances, the explanation offered by the appellant in his statement recorded under Section 313 CrPC that the complainant had met him near the SBI Branch to convey gratitude for forwarding the two cheques and while shaking hands, tried to thrust the currency notes into his coat pocket, cannot be discarded altogether. While the prosecution must prove its case beyond all reasonable doubt, the accused is only required to establish his defence on the touchstone of pre-ponderance of probabilities to rebut the presumption under Section 20 of the PC Act. In view of the official records showing prior completion of work, the defence version was plausible and satisfied the test of pre-ponderance of probabilities. 48. Thus, it is evident that neither the demand for bribe by the appellant nor the conscious acceptance of the bribe by the appellant can be said to have been proved. In the absence of such proof, drawing of presumption under Section 20 of the PC Act was impermissible. 49. That being the position, the impugned judgment and order of the High Court dated 19.12.2014 passed in Criminal Appeal No. 782 of 2008 and the judgment and order of the learned Special Judge, Delhi dated 28.08.2008 passed in CC No. 40 of 2003 are hereby set aside. Appellant Har Parsad Gaur is acquitted of the charges framed against him under Section 7 and Section 13(1)(d) read with Section 13(2) of the PC Act. Since the appellant is on bail, his bail bonds would stand discharged. 50. Consequently, the criminal appeal is allowed. All pending applications stand disposed of.

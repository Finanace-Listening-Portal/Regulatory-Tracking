With the recognition of Unified Fintech Forum as the second self-regulatory organisation for fintechs, the RBI is expanding an industry-led oversight mechanism. ETBFSI explains what powers these bodies have, where their authority ends, how FACE and UFF differ, and the challenges facing the framework. ETBFSI Staff ETBFSI Updated On Nov 10, 2026 at 10:59 AM IST var news_msid = 135700245; console.log(news_msid); Set ETBFSI as your preferred source on Google Copy Link Share on WhatsApp Share on Linkedin Share on X Share on Telegram Share on Facebook -Hashmeet Kaur The Reserve Bank of India’s (RBI) recognition of the Unified Fintech Forum (UFF) as the second Self-Regulatory Organisation in the FinTech Sector (SRO-FT) has expanded the central bank’s industry-led oversight framework for the sector. UFF joins the FinTech Association for Consumer Empowerment (FACE), which was recognised as the first SRO-FT in August 2024.The move comes as fintech businesses span an increasingly diverse range of activities, from digital lending and payments to wealth management and technology-led financial services. Monitoring every operational practice and emerging business model through direct regulatory supervision can be challenging for the central bank. Advt The SRO framework seeks to address this gap by giving industry bodies a formal role in setting standards, monitoring members and promoting responsible conduct. However, the RBI retains its statutory regulatory powers, while the SROs remain accountable to the central bank and operate within the boundaries of its framework.ETBFSI brings to you an explainer on what the Fintech SRO framework is, how powers are divided, how FACE and UFF differ, and what this dual-SRO setup means for the industry and consumers.What is a Self-Regulatory Organisation?A Self-Regulatory Organisation for the FinTech Sector (SRO-FT) is a not-for-profit industry body recognised by the RBI to establish and promote standards of conduct, governance and compliance among fintech companies.Unlike a conventional industry association or trade body, a recognised SRO-FT has a formal mandate under the RBI’s May 2024 framework. It is expected to develop codes of conduct, set industry benchmarks, monitor members and take action against violations of its standards.The RBI framework requires an SRO-FT to be registered as a not-for-profit company under Section 8 of the Companies Act, 2013. It also lays down requirements on membership representation, governance, independence, board composition and conflict-of-interest management.The objective is to ensure that an SRO does not merely represent the commercial interests of its members but also acts in the wider interests of the fintech ecosystem and its consumers. Advt What powers does an SRO-FT have?While an SRO does not possess statutory legislative authority like the RBI, it exercises significant oversight and enforcement powers over its members:Formulating Codes of Conduct: Establishing standardized rules for fair lending practices, transparent pricing, ethical collection practices, and data privacy.Monitoring & Auditing: Conducting regular reviews and compliance checks on member fintechs to detect unethical practices early.Dispute Resolution & Grievance Redressal: Operating streamlined mechanisms to resolve disputes between member entities, as well as handling escalated customer grievances.Enforcement Actions: Issuing warnings, imposing administrative fines, or suspending/revoking membership for entities that breach agreed-upon industry standards.Facilitating Regulatory Coordination: SROs are expected to act as a link between the industry and the RBI by sharing market intelligence, highlighting emerging risks and providing inputs on regulatory issues.However, these powers are not a substitute for statutory regulation. An SRO cannot cancel a bank or NBFC licence, override RBI directions or exercise the central bank’s enforcement powers under law. Advt How is it different from RBI?DimensionReserve Bank of India (RBI)Self-Regulatory Organisation (SRO-FT)AuthorityStatutory central bank and sovereign regulator.Industry-led body authorized and supervised by the RBI.Primary FocusSystemic stability, monetary policy, and macro-prudential regulation.Micro-level governance, conduct, and operational compliance.Rule-MakingEnacts legally binding circulars, acts, and master directions.Drafts operational codes, best-practice guidelines, and ethical standards.EnforcementCan cancel banking/NBFC licenses, impose direct legal penalties, or halt operations.Can counsel, caution, reprimand, impose reasonable monetary penalties and suspend or expel members.What role will the two recognised fintech SROs play?Feature / DimensionFinTech Association for Consumer Empowerment (FACE)Unified Fintech Forum (UFF)RBI Recognition DateAugust 28, 2024September 10, 2026 (Announced at GFF 2026)Origin & BackgroundFormed as an industry collective specifically focused on ethical digital lending and consumer protection.Formerly the Digital Lenders Association of India (DLAI), established in 2016; rebranded to broaden its scope for SRO recognition.Primary Domain FocusDigital lending, consumer protection, fair collection practices, and borrower grievance redressal.Diversified fintech coverage, spanning digital lending platforms, payments, wealthtech, and open finance.Key ObjectivesPreventing predatory lending, eliminating dark patterns, protecting data privacy, and setting ethical debt recovery standards.Harmonizing API standards, fostering regulatory dialogue, facilitating cross-sector fintech compliance, and scaling ecosystem growth.Governance & OwnershipNon-profit Section 8 company with independent directors and diversified member shareholding.Non-profit Section 8 company with independent board governance and multi-segment representation.Can self-regulation improve compliance and consumer protection?While the central bank remains the primary regulator, the fast-evolving nature of financial technology makes direct micro-regulation of every emerging business model difficult. By delegating operational monitoring and industry benchmarking to official SROs, the RBI aims to create a hybrid regulatory environment one where the industry participates in setting its own operational standards while remaining strictly accountable to the central bank. Because SROs are closer to day-to-day market innovations than a central regulator, they can identify mis-selling, deceptive app interfaces (dark patterns), or predatory lending mechanisms faster. Advt What challenges are ahead?The effectiveness of the framework will depend on how independently and consistently these organisations exercise their oversight powers. Some of the key challenges include:Conflict of Interest: Since SROs are industry-led and their members are the entities they are expected to monitor, maintaining independence will be critical. Strong governance, independent board representation and transparent enforcement will be necessary to prevent regulatory capture or preferential treatment.Jurisdictional Limits: Unregulated or rogue entities that refuse to join an SRO remain outside its direct reach, requiring direct intervention by the RBI and law enforcement.Enforcement credibility: The ability to issue warnings or expel members will matter only if SROs are willing to act against influential members when required. Consistent action against violations will be central to establishing credibility.Voluntary vs. Mandatory Compliance: The long-term effectiveness of the framework depends on whether the RBI eventually mandates SRO membership for all operating fintech entities.Overlapping regulatory responsibilities: Fintechs operate across activities that may already fall under different regulatory frameworks.

While easing US Treasury yields and lower crude oil prices have improved the conditions for overseas fundraising, issuers face a narrowing window to complete the documentation, rating and other formalities required to access international debt markets.Cumulative inflows through the external commercial borrowing (ECB) and overseas foreign currency borrowing (OFCB) routes stood at $10.616 billion as of September 18, according to the latest available Reserve Bank of India data. This was an increase of about 16% from the $9.151 billion reported as of August 31. The September 21 release put ECB inflows at $5.296 billion and OFCB inflows at $5.320 billion. Advt The RBI introduced the dollar-rupee swap facility on June 8 to attract foreign currency inflows and support the rupee. The ECB window is available to eligible public-sector undertaking borrowers, excluding banks, for borrowings with an average maturity of at least three years. The OFCB facility covers overseas foreign currency borrowings raised by authorised dealer Category-I banks with a minimum maturity of three years.The pace of mobilisation has slowed since the initial phase. Inflows through the two borrowing routes had reached $9.151 billion by August 31, before rising to $10.616 billion over the following three weeks. The increase came against a backdrop of elevated global borrowing costs, which had made overseas fundraising less attractive to Indian issuers.Conditions have since become more favourable. The US Treasury's three-year and five-year yields stood at 4.84% and 4.90%, respectively, on November 17, while Brent crude was trading at $93.10 a barrel on November 18. Lower global yields can improve the economics of dollar borrowing, although the final decision will depend on the all-in cost after accounting for the swap premium and other issuance expenses.Banks are preparing to tap overseas markets before the window closes. UCO Bank established a $1 billion medium-term note programme on November 17, following earlier steps towards foreign currency fundraising, including a non-deal roadshow. The programme provides an issuance framework but does not, by itself, establish that the bank has raised the full amount or that a particular bond issue has been priced. Advt Funding economics and documentation remain constraintsThe concessional arrangement allows eligible banks to sell dollars to the RBI at the prevailing FBIL reference rate and repurchase the same amount at the end of the swap period by returning the rupee funds along with a fixed premium. The premium is 1.5% per annum, compounded semi-annually, and transactions must be in multiples of $1 million. The maximum swap tenor is five years, subject to the underlying borrowing's repayment schedule or maturity.The arrangement provides borrowers with a defined swap cost, but it does not eliminate the need to compare the overall economics of overseas and domestic funding. Issuers must factor in the interest rate on the foreign currency borrowing, the swap premium and other transaction expenses. If the resulting cost is higher than the cost of raising funds in the domestic market, the incentive to borrow abroad diminishes. Advt Operational readiness is another hurdle. Banks need to establish medium-term note programmes, secure credit ratings and complete issuance documentation before accessing overseas debt markets. These programmes also require periodic updates, which can delay fundraising even when market conditions turn favourable. Institutions that postponed issuance when global yields were higher may now have to complete or refresh these processes before approaching investors.The remaining time before the December 31 cut-off could encourage eligible borrowers to accelerate plans, but the scale of additional inflows will depend on how quickly they can complete these formalities and whether overseas funding remains competitive. The RBI's circular allows eligible ECB drawdowns and OFCB inflows received up to December 31 to be brought into the swap facility until January 15, 2027. The latter date is the deadline for availing the swap, not an extension of the borrowing window. Advt The experience of the FCNR(B) scheme highlights the difference in response across the RBI's foreign exchange mobilisation measures. The central bank advanced the deadline for fresh FCNR(B) deposits to August 31 from September 30 after the scheme attracted substantial inflows. The RBI reported FCNR(B) mobilisation of $132.98 billion by August 31. In contrast, the ECB and OFCB windows remain open until December 31, with their combined inflows considerably smaller.The RBI has not announced an early closure or an extension of the ECB and OFCB facilities. With the FCNR(B) window already closed and the overseas borrowing deadline approaching, the coming weeks will indicate whether more public-sector borrowers and banks can convert improved global market conditions into actual foreign currency fundraising.

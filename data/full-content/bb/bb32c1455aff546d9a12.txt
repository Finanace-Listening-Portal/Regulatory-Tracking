Summary: The Mumbai Bench of the Income Tax Appellate Tribunal considered a consolidated group of cross-appeals filed by Asian Paints Limited and the Revenue for Assessment Years 2016-17, 2017-18 and 2018-19. The appeals arose from scrutiny assessments under section 143(3) and separate appellate orders passed by the NFAC. Since most grounds involved recurring issues and substantially identical facts, the Tribunal first examined the appeal for AY 2016-17 and applied its findings mutatis mutandis to the later years, except where a distinct issue required separate adjudication. The assessee was engaged in manufacturing, selling and distributing paints, coatings, home-décor products and bath fittings, besides providing related services. The disputes covered weighted deduction for in-house scientific research, expenditure on distribution of gift articles, disallowance under section 14A and Rule 8D, adjustment of book profit under section 115JB, provision for doubtful debts, evaluation of business opportunities, prior-period expenditure, trip schemes for dealers, Colour Idea Stores, post-invoice incentives and discounts, old balances written off, set-off of capital loss, foreign tax credit and deduction under section 80G for eligible donations forming part of CSR expenditure. The Tribunal ultimately held that the assessee’s and Revenue’s appeals were partly allowed for statistical purposes. The first major controversy concerned weighted deduction under section 35(2AB). For AY 2016-17, Asian Paints stated that it had incurred scientific research and development expenditure of Rs.75.16 crore at its approved in-house R&D unit at Turbhe, Navi Mumbai, comprising revenue expenditure of Rs.68.04 crore and capital expenditure of Rs.7.12 crore. It claimed a weighted deduction of Rs.129.94 crore. Although the R&D facility was approved by DSIR and the assessee furnished Forms 3CM and 3CL, DSIR quantified a lower eligible amount and the Assessing Officer consequently denied weighted deduction of Rs.12.41 crore. The CIT(A) confirmed the restriction on the reasoning that only the amount approved by the prescribed authority could be allowed and that the Assessing Officer could not alter DSIR’s quantification. The assessee relied on its own earlier-year orders and contended that, once the approved facility had incurred expenditure directly connected with scientific research, the Assessing Officer should examine the nature of the disputed items rather than mechanically adopt an unexplained reduction in Form 3CL. The Revenue, on the other hand, maintained that DSIR possessed the relevant technical expertise and that its certification was binding. The Tribunal analysed section 35(2AB), section 35(3), Rule 6(7A), Forms 3CM and 3CL, and the amendment under which the prescribed authority was expressly required to quantify eligible expenditure. It observed that the statutory provision focused on expenditure incurred on an approved in-house research and development facility, excluding land and building, and required the prescribed conditions concerning approval, accounts, audit and reporting to be fulfilled. For the period before the amendment, the Rule did not expressly prescribe DSIR quantification of eligible expenditure. The Tribunal also noted the practical grievance that DSIR could reduce the amount without seeking details or an explanation from the assessee, even where the disputed expenditure allegedly consisted of consultancy charges paid to scientists and engineers for work directly connected with R&D. It held that section 35(3) dealt with questions concerning whether an activity constituted scientific research or whether an asset was used for scientific research; the provision did not itself create a mechanism for unexplained quantification of expenditure. Accordingly, for AY 2016-17, the issue was restored to the Assessing Officer to examine the nature of the disputed expenditure and, if considered appropriate, obtain a DSIR report after placing the assessee’s details and explanation before that authority. For AYs 2017-18 and 2018-19, the Tribunal recognised that amended Rule 6(7A) specifically contemplated quantification by DSIR. Even so, it held that the process could not remain opaque or disregard natural justice. If DSIR alone was competent to quantify expenditure, the assessee had to receive an opportunity to substantiate why the expenditure related to scientific research and development. The order recorded that no clear mechanism had been shown under which DSIR called for details, examined the nature of each item and disclosed why expenditure falling outside the express exclusions in Rule 6(7A)(a) was rejected. The Tribunal therefore restored the issue for these years as well, directing the Assessing Officer to seek clarification, guidance or a report from DSIR and to ensure that the assessee received an opportunity to support its claim. The ground was thus treated as allowed for statistical purposes, without the Tribunal itself finally quantifying the deduction. On the section 14A issue, the Assessing Officer had invoked Rule 8D and made a disallowance relating to exempt income. The assessee argued that its investments were made from substantial interest-free funds and that no borrowed funds were used for making those investments. It also contended that no further administrative expenditure was attributable to earning exempt income. The Tribunal followed the position accepted in the assessee’s own earlier years and the judicial principle that where interest-free funds exceed the investments, a presumption operates that the investments were made from those funds. It accordingly decided the recurring section 14A ground in favour of the assessee. For AY 2018-19, it expressly recorded that the same issue permeated that year and applied the earlier finding. The linked question was whether the amount computed under section 14A read with Rule 8D could be added while determining book profit under section 115JB. Following the Special Bench ruling referred to in the order, the Tribunal held that the computational mechanism in Rule 8D could not be imported into clause (f) of Explanation 1 to section 115JB. The corresponding book-profit adjustment was therefore deleted. On the remaining recurring adjustments, the Tribunal held that a provision for doubtful debts already added back by the assessee under the normal provisions could not be disallowed again. The related book-profit issue was also decided in the assessee’s favour under the jurisdictional High Court ruling cited in the order. Expenditure for evaluating business opportunities in the course of the existing business was treated as revenue expenditure although individual proposals did not culminate in completed investments. Prior-period expenditure was also allowed where the liability crystallised during the relevant year. A substantial group of grounds related to Asian Paints’ dealer network: trip-scheme expenditure, Colour Idea Store expenditure, and post-invoice target-based incentives or discounts. The tax authorities had treated these arrangements as giving rise to an agency relationship and, consequently, as commission attracting tax deduction under section 194H. The assessee maintained that sales to dealers were on a principal-to-principal basis, that title and risk in the goods passed under the sale arrangements, and that the schemes were designed to promote sales rather than remunerate agents. The Tribunal examined the commercial relationship, the dealer price list, the terms and conditions and the assessee’s earlier-year decisions. It held that the dealers were not acting as agents of Asian Paints. Trip schemes offered on achieving sales targets were business-promotion measures; post-invoice discounts and incentives were linked to purchases or targets under principal-to-principal transactions; and none of these payments acquired the character of commission merely because they encouraged higher sales. The Tribunal separately examined the nature of Colour Idea Stores. Under these arrangements, dealers provided designated space in their shops where the assessee installed décor, displays, creative material, product literature, counters and other items intended to give customers a better understanding and experience of its products. The dealer and the assessee shared the cost of setting up the area. The store remained the dealer’s premises, and on termination the dealer was generally not required to return the civil work, furniture or fittings, except specified books, literature and intellectual property belonging to the assessee. The Tribunal regarded the arrangement as a joint sales-promotion activity benefiting both Asian Paints and its dealers. The expenditure did not create an enduring asset belonging to the assessee and did not evidence a principal-agent relationship. It was therefore revenue expenditure incurred for business and allowable under section 37(1). On this reasoning, the assessee’s grounds concerning trip schemes and Colour Idea Store expenditure were allowed, while the Revenue’s challenge to the treatment of Colour Idea Store costs as revenue expenditure was dismissed. The Revenue also challenged relief for old and sundry balances written off. The Rs.2.54 crore comprised ordinary business items such as CENVAT in transit, security deposits, vendor advances, open balances and service tax paid on traded goods. The Tribunal upheld the claim as a business loss or expenditure even though it did not necessarily satisfy the technical conditions governing bad debts. The Revenue’s ground was dismissed, and the conclusion was followed for AY 2017-18. For AY 2017-18, an additional issue arose from the sale of a depreciable flat held since 1997. The assessee computed a deemed short-term capital gain of Rs.1.09 crore under section 50 but sought to set it off against brought-forward long-term capital loss. The Assessing Officer denied the set-off by relying on section 70(3), and the CIT(A) upheld that action. The Tribunal observed that section 70(3) did not apply at the threshold because the assessee had not incurred a current-year long-term capital loss. More importantly, it held that section 50 creates a limited deeming fiction for computation under sections 48 and 49. The provision does not convert a long-term capital asset into a short-term asset for every other purpose of the Act. Following the Bombay High Court rulings in CIT v. Manali Investment and Commissioner of Income-tax v. Parrys (Eastern) (P.) Ltd., which in turn applied the principle stated in CIT v. Ace Builders (P.) Ltd., the Tribunal allowed the brought-forward long-term capital loss to be set off against the gain deemed short-term under section 50. The foreign tax credit dispute arose because Asian Paints received royalty, dividend and service-related recoveries from overseas subsidiaries. Those receipts were offered to tax in India and were also subjected to withholding abroad. The assessee claimed credit under sections 90 and 91 and filed Form 67 under Rule 128. It stated that no credit was claimed on pure reimbursements and that, for recoveries from subsidiaries, credit was confined to the mark-up component rather than gross receipts. The Assessing Officer disallowed the credit on the ground that expenses attributable to the foreign income were not properly demonstrated and separately recorded; the CIT(A) restricted the claim to 50%. The assessee further explained that some withholding certificates were received only after filing the original or revised return, leaving an additional claim before the Assessing Officer as the practical remedy. The Tribunal did not grant the whole amount outright. It directed the Assessing Officer to verify the expenses connected with foreign income, examine the supporting material and allow credit in accordance with law. It also held that an additional credit claim based on certificates received later should be examined and allowed if otherwise permissible. The relevant grounds were therefore restored or allowed for statistical purposes. The final distinct controversy concerned deduction under section 80G for qualifying donations included within the assessee’s corporate social responsibility expenditure. For AY 2018-19, Asian Paints incurred CSR expenditure of Rs.46.51 crore and added it back while computing business income under section 37. Out of the CSR outlay, it claimed the eligible portion of donations made to institutions covered by section 80G. The Assessing Officer denied the deduction on the reasoning that mandatory CSR expenditure under section 135 of the Companies Act, 2013 lacked the voluntary character ordinarily associated with a donation and that allowing section 80G relief would defeat the legislative treatment under section 37. The CIT(A) rejected this approach, observing that the assessee voluntarily selected the recipient organisations and that their activities retained a charitable character. The Tribunal upheld the CIT(A)’s conclusion. Relying principally on Societe Generale Securities India (P.) Ltd. v. Principal Commissioner of Income-tax, it treated section 37 and Chapter VI-A as distinct computation stages. Explanation 2 to section 37(1) bars CSR expenditure as a business deduction but does not impose a general prohibition on an otherwise eligible section 80G deduction. The statute expressly excludes CSR-linked contributions to specified funds in particular clauses of section 80G(2), without extending that restriction to every eligible category. Once the CSR amount had been added back under section 37, an eligible donation could not be denied merely because it also fulfilled a CSR obligation. The Revenue’s ground was dismissed. Overall, the ruling granted relief on section 14A, book-profit adjustment, dealer promotion, sundry balances, capital-loss set-off and CSR donations; reduced the gift-article disallowance from 25% to 10%; and remanded the R&D and foreign-tax-credit issues for verification. We have heard both the parties and also perused the relevant finding given in the impugned orders. The ld. AO has denied the set off relating to Section 70 of sub-Section 3 which for the sake of ready reference is reproduced hereunder:- “Set off of loss from one source against income from another source under the same head of income. 70(3) Where the result of the computation made for any assessment year under sections 48 to 55 in respect of any capital asset (other than a short-term capital asset) is a loss, the assessee shall be entitled to have the amount of such loss set off against the income, if any, as arrived at under a similar computation made for the assessment year in respect of any other capital asset not being a short-term capital asset.” 74. First of all assessee has not incurred any long term capital loss in A.Y.2017-18 and therefore, at the threshold the provision of Section 70 sub-Section 3 will not apply. Assessee has derived the income from sale of flat which is a long term capital asset as it was held from 1997-2017, however, by virtue of dealing provision of Section 70, the same was treated as short term capital gain. The provision to Section 70 makes exactly clear for deeming fiction restricted only to the mode of computation of capital gains contained u/s.48 and 49 of the Act and cannot be extended beyond that. Further, as per section 74(1)(b) any loss related to long term capital asset, it shall be setoff against income under the head of ‘Capital Gains’ assessable for that assessment year in respect of any other capital asset not being short term capital asset (i.e. long term capital asset), accordingly, the assessee has set-off capital gain from long term capital asset (i.e. Flat) against brought forward long term capital loss. 75. It is as settled position in law that deeming fiction needs to be strictly constructed and cannot be extended to any other provision of law. Section 50 does not change the character of capital gain from being long term capital gain to short term capital gain for the purpose other than section 50 of the Act. 76. This issue is now covered by the decision of the Hon’ble Bombay High Court in the case of CIT vs. Manali Investment (2013) 219 Taxman 113 and the Hon’ble High Court observed as under:- “2. The respondent – assessee had during the subject assessment year sold its meters and transformers on which it had claimed depreciation. On sale, the respondent – assessee claimed long term capital gains and sought to set-off the same against Its carried forward long term capital loss in terms of Section 74 of the Income Tax Act, 1961 (‘Act’ for short). The assessing officer disallowed the claim and held that in view of Section 50 of the Act, the gain is in the nature of short-term capital gain. The Commissioner of Income Tax (A) upheld the order of the assessing officer. 3. On further appeal, the Tribunal by the impugned order has allowed the claim of the respondent – assessee to set-off its long term losses in terms of Section 74 of the Act against the long term capital gains on sale of transformers and meters. This was by following the decision of this Court in the matter of CIT v. Ace Builders (P) Ltd [2006] 281 ITR 2 10/[2005] 144 Taxman 855 (Born). In the case of Ace Builders (P) Ltd (supra), this Court held that by virtue of Section 50 of the Act only the capital gains is to be computed in terms thereof and be deemed to be short-term capital gains. However, this deeming fiction is restricted only for the purposes of Section 50 of the Act and the benefit under Section 54E of the Act which is available only to long term capital gains was extended. In this case, the Tribunal held that the position is similar and the benefit of set-off against long term capital loss under Section 74 of the Act is to be allowed. Further, an identical issue with regard to set off against long term capital loss arose in an appeal filed by the Revenue in the matter of CIT v. Hathway Investments (P.) Ltd, being Income Tax Appeal (L) No. 405 of 2012. This Court by its order dated 31 January 2013 refused to entertain the appeal filed by the Revenue. The Revenue has not been able to point out any distinguishing features in the present case warranting a departure from the principles laid down by this Court in the matter of Ace Builders (P) Ltd. (supra) and in our order dated 3l January, 2013 in Income Tax Appeal (L) No. 405 of 2012. 3. In view of the above, we see no reason to entertain the proposed re-framed question of law. Accordingly, the appeal is dismissed with no order as to costs 77. Further Hon’ble Jurisdictional High Court in the case of Hon’ble Jurisdictional High Court in the case of Commissioner of Income-tax v. Parrys (Eastern) (P.) Ltd [2016] 384 ITR 264 held as under:- “6. We find that the issue stands concluded by the decision of this Court in ACE Builders (P) Ltd. ‘s case (supra) in favour of the Respondent-Assessee. Moreover, the impugned order relies upon the order of the Tribunal in Komac Investments & Finance (P) Ltd. ‘s case (supra) to dismiss the Revenue’s appeal before it. The deeming fiction under Section 50 is restricted only to the mode of computation of capital gains contained in Sections 48 and 49 of the Act. It does not change the character of the capital gain from that of being a long term capital gain into a short term capital gain for purpose other than Section 50 of the Act. Thus, the respondent-assessee was entitled to claim set off as the amount of Rs. 7.12 Crores arising out of sale of depreciable assets which are admittedly on sale of assets held for a period to which long term capital gain apply. Thus for purposes of Section 74 of the Act, the deemed short term capital gain continues to be long term capital gain. Moreover, it appears that the Revenue has accepted the decision the Tribunal in Komac Investments and Finance (P) Ltd. ‘s case (supra), as our attention has not been drawn to any appeal being filed from that order.” 78. Thus, following the aforesaid law laid down by the Hon’ble High Court we allow the set off of long term capital loss against deemed short term capital gain u/s.50. 79.

Summary: The Supreme Court of India, in State of Bihar v. Phulpari Kumari, Criminal Appeal No. 5212 of 2026, decided on 2 November 2026, clarified that the permissible gold jewellery quantities specified under CBDT Instruction No. 1916 dated 11 May 1994 and the CBDT Press Release dated 1 December 2016 cannot independently establish the source of acquisition of jewellery in a disproportionate-assets prosecution under the Prevention of Corruption Act, 1988. The dispute arose after the Vigilance Department initiated proceedings against the respondent and her husband for allegedly possessing assets disproportionate to their known sources of income. The Patna High Court quashed the order taking cognizance, reasoning that the respondent’s jewellery fell within the permissible quantities recognised by CBDT guidelines. The Supreme Court rejected this approach, distinguishing guidelines governing the seizure of jewellery during income-tax searches from the obligation to explain assets in criminal proceedings. It held that merely relying on a circular or press release permitting possession of specified quantities of gold does not amount to accounting for those assets. Accordingly, the Supreme Court set aside the High Court’s order, restored the order of cognizance passed by the Special Judge, Vigilance, Patna, and directed the criminal proceedings to continue. The Court expressly preserved the respondent’s right to pursue other remedies before the Trial Court. Importantly, the judgment neither invalidates CBDT Instruction No. 1916 nor determines the respondent’s guilt; it establishes that permissible possession and explanation of the source of acquisition are distinct legal considerations. Background and Facts of the Case The present judgment concerns the interpretation and evidentiary significance of CBDT Instruction No. 1916 dated 11.05.1994 and the Ministry of Finance, Department of Revenue, CBDT Press Release dated 01.12.2016, in proceedings relating to disproportionate assets under the Prevention of Corruption Act, 1988. The material facts emerging from the judgment are as follows: Date Particulars 11.05.2015 FIR registered at Vigilance Police Station, Patna, against the respondent and her husband for possessing assets disproportionate to their known sources of income. 29.07.2020 Chargesheet filed against the respondent after obtaining sanction. 28.01.2021 Special Judge, Vigilance, Patna, took cognizance of offences under Section 13(1)(e) read with Section 13(2) of the Prevention of Corruption Act, 1988. 2021 Respondent filed Criminal Miscellaneous Petition No. 56839 of 2021 before the Patna High Court challenging the order of cognizance. 14.05.2026 Patna High Court quashed the order of cognizance. 02.11.2026 Supreme Court allowed the appeal filed by the State of Bihar and restored the order of cognizance. 2. Legal Issue Before the Supreme Court The principal issue before the Supreme Court was: Whether the permissible quantity of gold jewellery prescribed under CBDT Instruction No. 1916 dated 11.05.1994 and the CBDT Press Release dated 01.12.2016 can, by itself, be treated as sufficient justification or explanation for possession of assets in a disproportionate-assets prosecution under the Prevention of Corruption Act, 1988. The respondent contended that, being a married woman, she was entitled to possess 500 grams of gold and that the possession of gold within the prescribed permissible limits could not constitute disproportionate assets. The State of Bihar opposed this contention, arguing that the guidelines concerning seizure of jewellery were not applicable for determining whether assets were disproportionate to known sources of income. 3. Relevant CBDT Instruction and Permissible Gold Limits CBDT Instruction No. 1916 dated 11.05.1994 prescribes guidelines concerning seizure of jewellery during income-tax searches. The permissible quantities referred to in the proceedings were: Category Jewellery ordinarily not seized Married woman 500 grams Unmarried woman 250 grams Male member 100 grams The respondent relied upon these limits to contend that her possession of gold jewellery was permissible and could not justify prosecution. The Patna High Court accepted this argument and observed that the respondent did not possess more than 750 grams of gold, which it considered permissible in her case. On this basis, the High Court concluded that the Vigilance Department had erroneously seized the jewellery and incorrectly calculated the disproportionate assets. The Supreme Court rejected the above reasoning. Important distinction: The Supreme Court did not abolish or invalidate CBDT Instruction No. 1916. It rejected the use of that Instruction as a substitute for explaining the possession of assets in a disproportionate-assets prosecution. 4. Findings and Reasoning of the Supreme Court 4.1 Possession of Gold and Accounting for Its Source Are Two Different Matters The Supreme Court accepted the State’s submission that entitlement to possess gold and the obligation to explain its possession when called upon in a prosecution are distinct legal matters. The Court observed that in proceedings under Section 13(1)(e) read with Section 13(2) of the Prevention of Corruption Act, the accused is required to explain allegations relating to assets disproportionate to known sources of income. Thus, merely demonstrating that a person possesses gold within the permissible quantity mentioned in CBDT guidelines does not automatically explain the source of acquisition of that gold. 4.2 CBDT Circulars Cannot Constitute an Explanation of Assets The Court held that the press release and circulars permitting the holding of gold up to specified limits cannot, by themselves, amount to accounting for possession of the assets. This is the principal legal proposition emerging from the judgment. 4.3 The High Court’s Reasoning Was Untenable The Supreme Court expressly held that the reasoning adopted by the Patna High Court was totally untenable. The High Court had treated permissible gold limits as sufficient to exclude the jewellery from the disproportionate-assets calculation and had consequently quashed the criminal proceedings. The Supreme Court found that this approach was legally incorrect. 5. Exact Operative Observation of the Supreme Court The following is the most important paragraph of the judgment: 9. Having heard learned counsel for the parties and perused the records, we find that the reasoning of the High Court is totally untenable. In a prosecution under Section 13(1)(e) read with Section 13(2) of the Act, the accused is called upon to explain allegations with regard to owning assets disproportionate to known sources of income. Merely showing certain press release and circulars permitting the holding of gold to a certain extent will not amount to accounting for the possession of the said assets. 6. Final Decision of the Supreme Court The Supreme Court passed the following directions: 1. The impugned order dated 14.05.2026 passed by the Patna High Court was set aside. 2. The order of cognizance dated 28.01.2021 passed by the Special Judge, Vigilance, Patna, in Special Case No. 20 of 2015 was restored. 3. The criminal proceedings were directed to proceed from the stage at which cognizance had been taken. 4. The respondent was expressly permitted to initiate such proceedings as may be available in law before the Trial Court. 5. Any such proceedings initiated by the respondent were to be decided uninfluenced by the observations made either by the High Court or by the Supreme Court. The appeal was accordingly allowed. 7. Legal Ratio Decidendi The ratio decidendi of the judgment can be stated as follows: CBDT Instruction No. 1916 dated 11.05.1994 and the CBDT Press Release dated 01.12.2016, which prescribe permissible quantities of jewellery ordinarily not to be seized during income-tax searches, cannot independently constitute an explanation or accounting of assets in a prosecution alleging possession of assets disproportionate to known sources of income under Section 13(1)(e) read with Section 13(2) of the Prevention of Corruption Act, 1988. The permissible quantity of jewellery and the explanation of its source of acquisition are legally distinct considerations. 8. Practical Legal Implications Issue Legal implication Possession of 500 grams of gold by a married woman Does not automatically establish the source of acquisition. CBDT Instruction No. 1916 Cannot independently be relied upon as an explanation of assets in a disproportionate-assets prosecution. Jewellery received during marriage Its source and circumstances may require separate explanation where legally relevant. Ancestral jewellery Ancestral origin and supporting evidence may be relevant to explaining the source. Gift of jewellery Gift documentation and circumstances of acquisition may be relevant. Income-tax search The judgment does not invalidate CBDT jewellery seizure guidelines. Disproportionate-assets prosecution The accused cannot obtain automatic immunity merely by demonstrating jewellery within the prescribed limits. The above practical implications are analytical conclusions drawn from the judgment and are not separate directions issued by the Supreme Court. 9. What This Judgment Does NOT Decide It is equally important to understand the limitations of this judgment: The Supreme Court did not hold that possession of gold within CBDT limits is unlawful. The Court did not abolish the permissible jewellery limits under CBDT Instruction No. 1916. The Court did not hold that every person possessing gold must necessarily produce purchase invoices. The Court did not determine the respondent’s guilt or whether her assets were actually disproportionate. The Court did not decide any independent income-tax assessment or liability under Section 56(2)(x) of the Income-tax Act. The Court restored the criminal proceedings and left other legal defences open to the respondent. These limitations follow from the actual scope and operative directions of the judgment. 10. Conclusion The judgment in State of Bihar v. Phulpari Kumari establishes an important distinction between the permissible possession of gold jewellery and the explanation of its acquisition. The CBDT guidelines prescribing permissible quantities of jewellery ordinarily not to be seized during income-tax searches are not a declaration that such jewellery has been acquired from explained or lawful sources. In a disproportionate-assets prosecution, the accused may still be required to explain the possession of assets in accordance with the applicable law.

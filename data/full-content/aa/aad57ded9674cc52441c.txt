Summary: The Supreme Court’s decision in State of Karnataka v. Ecom Gill Coffee Trading Private Limited reinforces the burden on a purchaser claiming input tax credit to establish the genuineness of purchases and the physical movement of goods, rather than relying solely on invoices and bank payments. The ruling arose under Section 70 of the Karnataka VAT Act, but its evidentiary reasoning is relevant to GST disputes where a supplier is alleged to be non-existent or its registration is cancelled retrospectively. Under GST, entitlement must nevertheless be independently tested against Section 16 and the burden of proof under Section 155 of the CGST Act. The article examines a December 2022 purchase of goods valued at ₹5,60,000 plus ₹1,00,800 GST, supported by e-way bill, transport documents, weighment slips, bank payment and potentially stock and onward-sale records. A supplier’s premises being found locked in May 2026 does not, by itself, prove that goods were not supplied in December 2022; however, the recipient must produce credible transaction-specific evidence and establish the remaining statutory ITC conditions. The discussion compares Ecom Gill Coffee with Gargo Traders and the reported Shaurya Alloys ruling, examines the effect of retrospective cancellation, clarifies record-preservation obligations under Section 36, and provides an evidence checklist, procedural response steps and a draft reply paragraph. It emphasises that genuine purchases and statutory compliance must be assessed on the complete documentary record, while fraud allegations, limitation and hearing rights require separate scrutiny. However, that judgment does not dispense with examination of the purchaser’s evidence or authorise automatic reversal of ITC merely because the supplier’s registration was subsequently cancelled retrospectively. The judgment arose under the Karnataka VAT Act; entitlement under GST must be decided independently under the applicable GST provisions, particularly section 16 read with section 155. For example, illustration, the purchaser has considerably more than an invoice and a bank payment: you mention an e-way bill, transport documents and weighment slip for movement from Mysuru to Bengaluru. Those documents must be examined together with the inward-stock records, subsequent sale or consumption records and the supplier’s relevant GST compliance. The proper answer is neither “ITC must be reversed because registration was cancelled” nor “ITC must be allowed because an invoice exists.” The issue is whether the particular December 2022 supply was genuine and the statutory conditions were fulfilled. One point must also be stated candidly: the plea that records cannot be expected after three years and five months is not supported by section 36. GST records ordinarily have to be retained for 72 months from the due date of the relevant annual return—not merely for six years from the purchase date. The following is a professional article-style draft, followed by a suggested submission for the purchaser’s reply. 1. The judgment: particulars and background Case: State of Karnataka v. M/s. Ecom Gill Coffee Trading Private Limited, Civil Appeal No. 230 of 2023 and connected appeals. Decision: 13 March 2023. Bench: Justice M.R. Shah and Justice C.T. Ravikumar. The judgment was delivered by Justice M.R. Shah. The common judgment concerned section 70 of the Karnataka Value Added Tax Act, 2003. Although Civil Appeal No. 230 of 2023 bears the name of Ecom Gill Coffee, the Court treated Civil Appeal No. 231 of 2023, concerning Tallam Apparels, as the lead matter for convenience. Facts concerning Ecom Gill Coffee The purchaser dealt in green coffee beans, which were purchased for further sale in the export and domestic markets. The dispute related to assessment year 2010–11. The Assessing Officer examined purchases from 27 sellers. The judgment records that: Six sellers were de-registered. Three had effected sales but had not paid the relevant tax. Six had denied the turnover and had not paid tax. ITC of approximately ₹10.52 lakh was disallowed. The first appellate authority confirmed the disallowance. The Tribunal allowed the purchaser’s appeal, principally on the basis of purchases under tax invoices from registered dealers. The High Court declined to interfere. The State carried the matter to the Supreme Court. Facts concerning Tallam Apparels The purchaser claimed ITC of ₹4,18,818 on purchases of readymade garments for assessment year 2012–13. The selling dealers had either had their registration cancelled or filed nil returns. The Tribunal and High Court granted relief, whereas the Assessing Officer and first appellate authority had held that the purchaser had not discharged its burden of proof. These facts matter. The Supreme Court was not considering a case where a purchaser had conclusively established delivery through a complete transport-and-stock trail and the only objection was that the supplier’s premises were found locked several years later. It was considering disputed transactions where the supporting evidence was found insufficient. 2. What the Supreme Court held in paragraphs 9.1–12 and 15 Paragraph 9.1: the purchaser must prove the claim Section 70 of the KVAT Act placed the burden of proving the correctness of an input-tax claim on the dealer claiming it. The Supreme Court held: “Mere production of the invoices or the payment made by cheques is not enough…” The purchaser had to establish the actual transaction and physical movement of goods through supporting particulars, including the selling dealer’s name and address, vehicle details, freight-payment details, acknowledgement of delivery, tax invoices and payment particulars. Merely describing oneself as a bona fide purchaser did not discharge the statutory burden. Paragraph 10: credit must arise from a genuine transaction The Court held that genuineness of the purchase and actual physical movement of the goods were essential. If the purchaser failed to establish those matters, the Assessing Officer was justified in rejecting the ITC claim. Paragraph 11: the finding was based on the evidentiary failure in those cases The Court noted that the Assessing Officer had given cogent reasons for doubting the transactions. In some cases, the seller’s registration stood cancelled; in others, the seller disputed or denied the sale. The purchasers had not produced the further supporting material necessary to establish the transactions. Paragraph 12: invoices and cheque payments were not conclusive The Tribunal and High Court had erred in overturning the concurrent findings of the Assessing Officer and first appellate authority on the footing that invoices or cheque payments were sufficient. The Court required supporting evidence of the underlying supply and movement of goods. Paragraph 15: the operative conclusion The Supreme Court: 1. Allowed the State’s appeals. Set aside the orders of the Tribunal and High Court allowing ITC. Restored the Assessing Officers’ orders denying ITC, as confirmed by the first appellate authorities. The operative reason was the purchasers’ failure to establish genuine transactions and actual movement of goods through cogent material. The principle in practical terms An invoice proves that a document was issued. A bank statement proves that money moved. Neither, by itself, necessarily proves that the goods described in that invoice were supplied by that seller and received by that purchaser. That is the evidentiary difficulty addressed by the judgment. 3. How far does this judgment apply under GST? It would be unsafe to argue that Ecom Gill Coffee has no relevance merely because it arose under VAT. Its reasoning on proof of genuine purchases is relevant where GST credit is disputed on the ground of non-receipt or fictitious supply. At the same time, the officer must identify and apply the GST provision allegedly violated. Section 70 of the KVAT Act cannot itself become the charging or recovery provision for a GST demand. The GST inquiry must address section 16, section 155 and the applicable procedural provisions. The relevant GST requirements for December 2022 Provision Matter requiring verification Section 16(1) Whether the inward supply was used or intended to be used in the course or furtherance of business. Section 16(2)(a) Possession of the prescribed tax invoice or other eligible document issued by a registered supplier. Section 16(2) (aa) Supplier’s furnishing of invoice details and communication of those details to the recipient in the prescribed manner. Section 16(2)(b) Receipt of the goods, including deemed receipt where the statutory explanation applies. Section 16(2)(ba) Whether the communicated ITC was restricted under section 38. Section 16(2)(c) Actual payment of the tax charged on the supply to the Government, subject to the statutory scheme. Section 16(2)(d) Furnishing of the recipient’s return under section 39. Section 155 The burden of proving ITC eligibility rests on the claimant. The applicable payment, time-limit and other eligibility conditions must also be checked. In particular, reflection in GSTR-2B is important but does not, by itself, establish physical delivery or conclusively prove payment of the corresponding tax to the Government. There are therefore three distinct questions: Did the goods actually reach the purchaser? Was the supply genuinely made by the invoicing supplier? Were the remaining statutory conditions for ITC satisfied? Proof of receipt is essential, but receipt alone does not answer every condition of section 16. 4. Is the judgment prospective or retrospective? The judgment contains no direction restricting its operation to transactions taking place after 13 March 2023. In fact, the Supreme Court itself applied its reasoning to transactions relating to assessment years 2010–11 and 2012–13. Accordingly, the argument that it cannot be considered for a December 2022 purchase merely because the purchase preceded the judgment is not sustainable. In M.A. Murthy v. State of Karnataka, (2003) 7 SCC 517, the Supreme Court explained that a judicial declaration of law ordinarily applies to pending cases, unless the Court expressly limits its operation prospectively. However, three different matters should not be confused: Matter Position Application of a judicial interpretation to earlier transactions Not excluded merely because the transaction predates the judgment. Retrospective cancellation of a supplier’s registration A separate administrative action requiring statutory grounds and justification. Reopening or demanding tax for an earlier period Must independently satisfy the applicable jurisdictional, limitation and procedural requirements. Thus, Ecom Gill Coffee is not confined to post-13 March 2023 transactions, but it does not create an unlimited power to reopen concluded matters or override GST limitation provisions. Its application remains confined to its legal principle and the statutory framework governing the dispute. 5. Your illustration: December 2022 purchase and cancellation in May 2026 Corrected statement of facts For the article, the facts may be stated as follows: A registered purchaser at Bengaluru purchased goods from a supplier at Mysuru in December 2022 for a taxable value of ₹5,60,000, together with GST of ₹1,00,800. The total invoice value was ₹6,60,800. The goods were transported from Mysuru to Bengaluru under a tax invoice, e-way bill and transport documents. Weighment slips were also available. Payment was made through banking channels, and the purchase was recorded in the purchaser’s books. In May 2026, approximately three years and five months after the purchase, the Department cancelled the supplier’s registration with retrospective effect, relying on an enforcement report that the supplier was not available and the premises were locked on the date of inspection. The purchaser states that the supplier was temporarily away procuring goods and had actually carried on business during the relevant period. The purchaser is now called upon either to reverse ITC of ₹1,00,800 or to establish receipt of the goods. Two factual points require correction before this is used in an actual reply: The cancellation order’s precise effective date must be stated. “Cancelled retrospectively” is insufficient; it must be seen whether the date covers December 2022. Your reference to business continuing until June 2026, despite cancellation in May 2026, needs explanation. Actual business activity and an active GST registration are different matters. Do not state that the registration remained valid until June 2026 unless the portal history or an order supports it. Can the officer ask for proof of receipt? Yes. Section 16(2)(b) requires receipt of goods, and section 155 places the burden of proving eligibility on the purchaser. The officer can examine whether the transport documents and other records establish actual delivery. But the inquiry must address the evidence actually produced. Where the purchaser submits an invoice, completed e-way bill, lorry receipt, weighment slips, inward-stock entries and corresponding sales or consumption records, the officer cannot dispose of the matter by merely repeating that invoices and bank payments are insufficient. Those additional documents are precisely the kind of evidence relevant to the concern expressed in Ecom Gill Coffee. Does a locked premises in May 2026 establish non-existence in December 2022? Not by itself. A later inspection is relevant evidence, but its connection with the earlier transaction must be examined. The officer must distinguish between: 1. A supplier who never conducted business and issued invoices without supplying goods. A supplier who genuinely traded in December 2022 but later closed, shifted, defaulted or became untraceable. A supplier whose premises were temporarily locked on the inspection date. A conclusion about the December 2022 supply requires examination of that period and that transaction. A subsequent cancellation is a reason for inquiry; it is not a substitute for that inquiry. Equally, the purchaser’s explanation that the supplier was away procuring goods must be supported where possible. A bare explanation should not be presented as an established fact. 6. Effect of retrospective cancellation: what the courts have recognised Gargo Traders In Gargo Traders v. Joint Commissioner, Commercial Taxes, WPA No. 1009 of 2022, decided on 12 June 2023, the Calcutta High Court dealt with a purchaser whose supplier’s registration was subsequently cancelled retrospectively. The Court set aside the adverse order and required reconsideration of the documents supporting the genuineness of the transactions. It was not an unconditional direction that every purchase from a retrospectively cancelled supplier must receive ITC. The useful proposition is that the authority must examine genuine-transaction evidence rather than treat subsequent retrospective cancellation as the complete answer. A directly relevant recent development A report published on 11 November 2026 discusses Shaurya Alloys (P) Ltd. v. State of Punjab, CWP No. 34296 of 2024 (O&M), decided on 1 October 2026, by the Punjab and Haryana High Court. According to that report, the Court upheld section 16(2)(c), while laying down safeguards against mechanical denial of ITC. Among the reported directions: Retrospective cancellation may justify inquiry but should not, by itself, determine reversal. The officer must examine whether the grounds and effective date of cancellation bear upon the particular supply. The purchaser’s invoices, e-way bills, transport receipts, weighbridge slips and stock or consumption records must be considered. The purchaser retains the burden under section 155. Where fraud provisions are invoked, the notice must disclose the facts connecting the purchaser with the alleged fraud. This is particularly relevant to my question. However, this discussion is based on the published report; the authenticated judgment and its current appellate status should be checked before quoting its precise directions in a pleading. A Punjab and Haryana or Calcutta High Court decision should also be presented as persuasive authority in Karnataka, not as a Karnataka High Court ruling. 7. What evidence should the purchaser produce? The reply should establish a connected transaction trail, not merely attach a bundle of documents. Stage Evidence What it helps establish Placement of order Purchase order, quotation, correspondence Commercial origin of the purchase. Identity of supplier Invoice, GSTIN particulars, available registration history Identity of the invoicing seller and its status at the relevant time. Dispatch E-way bill, lorry receipt, dispatch record Particulars of the goods, vehicle, origin and destination. Movement Vehicle particulars, transporter confirmation, available journey records Corroboration of the stated transport. Receipt Delivery acknowledgement, inward register, goods-receipt note, weighment slip Arrival and acceptance of the goods. Stock accounting Quantity-wise stock ledger Inclusion of the goods in business inventory. Subsequent use Sales invoices or production/consumption records Commercial use or onward disposal of the goods. Payment Bank statement, UTR, supplier ledger Payment of invoice consideration. GST compliance GSTR-2B, purchase reconciliation, recipient’s returns and available supplier confirmation Compliance with the relevant GST conditions. These are evidentiary categories, not a statement that every business must possess every document in the table. The material must be appropriate to the goods, transport arrangement and manner of delivery. Nevertheless, a claimant must produce enough reliable evidence to establish the transaction and cannot rely on the invoice alone. Freight paid by the supplier Suppose the purchase was on a delivered-price basis and the supplier engaged and paid the transporter. The purchaser may not possess a freight-payment voucher in its own name. The reply should explain that arrangement and support it with the purchase terms, invoice, lorry receipt or transporter confirmation. The absence of a purchaser’s freight voucher must be considered in that context. It should not be concealed, nor should a voucher be created merely to fill a perceived gap in the record. The object of the inquiry is proof of the real movement, not production of a document inconsistent with the actual arrangement. Direct delivery to another person Where goods were delivered to a customer or another person on the purchaser’s direction, the explanation to section 16(2)(b) concerning deemed receipt must be examined. In such a case, absence of an inward entry at the purchaser’s own godown is not necessarily proof of non-receipt. The bill-to/ship-to documents and the actual delivery trail become important. 8. How long must the records be preserved? This is the point on which the proposed defence needs reconsideration. Section 36 requires the relevant accounts and records to be retained for 72 months from the due date of furnishing the annual return for the financial year concerned. Rules governing accounts also require preservation of the relevant invoices, delivery challans and supporting business records. Application to the December 2022 purchase Particular Position Financial year 2022–23 Ordinary annual-return due date 31 December 2023, subject to any applicable extension Ordinary retention endpoint on that basis 31 December 2029 Departmental inquiry in May 2026 Within the ordinary retention period The above date is illustrative on the stated annual-return due-date basis. The statutory calculation must use the applicable due date, including any valid extension. Where the proviso to section 36 applies—for example, relevant appellate proceedings or an offence investigation—the records must be retained for one year after final disposal or the ordinary retention period, whichever is later. Accordingly, it would be incorrect to submit: “The transaction is more than three years old; therefore, the purchaser was not required to preserve delivery and supporting records.” A more sustainable submission would be: “The purchaser is producing the statutory and contemporaneous records available in its custody. Certain additional documents now called for were not generated by the purchaser or were maintained by the supplier or transporter. The purchaser requests that receipt be determined on the cumulative evidence, with reasonable opportunity to obtain third-party confirmations.” That submission must be adapted to the actual facts. Can records be maintained electronically? The GST rules recognise electronic maintenance of records, subject to the applicable safeguards, accessibility and backup requirements. A business should therefore preserve organised digital copies of invoices, transport records, delivery acknowledgements and other relevant documents, together with reliable accounting data and backups. A scanned copy does not cure a fictitious transaction. Its practical value is preservation of genuine contemporaneous evidence. 9. What if some old documents are missing? Missing documents should be addressed openly. The purchaser may seek: Duplicate lorry receipts or proof of delivery from the transporter. Duplicate weighment slips from the weighbridge operator. Confirmation from the supplier identifying the invoice, goods, quantity and vehicle. Relevant bank records. Available inward-register and stock-ledger extracts. Corresponding sales or production records. Contemporary correspondence concerning dispatch or delivery. These materials should be reconciled with the original transaction records. A confirmation obtained in 2026 should be described as a subsequent confirmation of a December 2022 transaction—not as a document created at the time of delivery. The officer must assess its reliability with the other evidence. Two illustrations show the difference. Illustration A: one delivery document is unavailable The purchaser has the invoice, e-way bill, lorry receipt, weighment slip, bank payment and stock entries. The separate signed goods-receipt note cannot be located. This is not the same evidentiary position as having only an invoice and bank payment. The purchaser should explain the missing document and show how the remaining independent records establish the delivery. The officer must examine their cumulative effect. Illustration B: no movement or stock evidence exists The purchaser has only an invoice, bank payment and a purchase-ledger entry. There is no reliable transport record, delivery evidence, quantity-wise stock trail or corresponding consumption or sale evidence. Here, Ecom Gill Coffee is a serious adverse authority. The purchaser cannot safely insist that registration and banking payment alone establish entitlement. Further credible evidence is required. 10. Procedure for answering the notice Step 1: identify the precise allegation The reply should ascertain whether the Department alleges: Non-receipt of goods. Invoice issuance by a fictitious supplier. Non-payment of tax by the supplier. Absence of the invoice from the prescribed communicated statement. Retrospective cancellation alone. Fraud or collusion involving the purchaser. These allegations are not interchangeable. The notice and final order must identify the condition allegedly violated and deal with the evidence relevant to it. Step 2: obtain the relied-upon material Request the supplier’s cancellation order, its effective date, relevant enforcement report, statements and other material relied upon against the purchaser. Where the report concerns only a 2026 visit, ask what material establishes non-existence or fictitious supply in December 2022. Step 3: submit an invoice-wise reconciliation For the disputed invoice, give: Invoice number and date. Description and quantity of goods. Taxable value and tax. E-way bill and vehicle details. Transport document reference. Weighment and receipt references. Stock-ledger reference. Subsequent sale or consumption reference. Payment date and bank reference. GSTR-2Band return references. This directly addresses the evidentiary burden rather than leaving the officer to reconstruct the transaction from unrelated annexures. Step 4: request a hearing and findings on the documents Request a personal hearing under section 75(4) and a reasoned order dealing with the reply and documentary evidence. If a third-party statement is relied upon, seek its disclosure and, where material to the defence, request cross-examination with reasons. Step 5: examine limitation and the demand provision separately The transaction belongs to FY 2022–23. Do not accept a demand provision or limitation calculation merely because the notice was issued in 2026. Where section 74 is invoked, examine whether the notice states the facts constituting fraud, wilful misstatement or suppression attributable to the purchaser. A supplier’s alleged wrongdoing cannot simply be reproduced as a finding of fraud against the recipient without the connecting facts. The applicable period, notice date and statutory requirements must be checked from the actual notice. 11. Suggested substantive paragraph for the purchaser’s reply Use the following only after inserting the correct references and deleting any statement not supported by records: The notice relies upon the judgment of the Hon’ble Supreme Court in State of Karnataka v. M/s. Ecom Gill Coffee Trading Private Limited, decided on 13.03.2023. The noticee respectfully accepts the principle that a claim of input tax credit must arise from a genuine transaction and that a tax invoice and banking payment, by themselves, may not discharge the evidentiary burden. The present claim, however, does not rest on those two documents alone. The goods covered by Invoice No. _ dated _ were transported from Mysuru to Bengaluru under E-way Bill No. _ and Transport Document No.. The corresponding weighment slips, inward/stock records, payment particulars and subsequent sale/consumption records are enclosed and reconciled in Annexure. These records may kindly be examined together for determining actual receipt and genuineness of the supply. The subsequent cancellation of the supplier’s registration, pursuant to an inspection conducted in 2026, does not by itself answer whether the particular supply took place in December 2022. The noticee therefore requests disclosure of the cancellation order, its effective date, the relevant enforcement report and the material, if any, linking the later inspection finding with the disputed invoice. The noticee is not claiming that possession of an invoice, portal reflection or payment through a bank creates an unconditional entitlement to credit. The submission is that eligibility must be determined under the applicable provisions of section 16 read with section 155, on consideration of the evidence produced, and not solely from the subsequent registration status of the supplier. A personal hearing may kindly be granted. Before any adverse conclusion is drawn, the noticee may also be informed of any specific discrepancy in the enclosed transport, receipt, stock or payment records and afforded an opportunity to explain the same. This approach acknowledges the adverse authority and distinguishes the facts. It is stronger than an argument that the Supreme Court judgment is irrelevant or operates only prospectively. 12. Conclusion The conclusion of Ecom Gill Coffee is clear: a purchaser claiming ITC must prove the genuine transaction and actual movement of goods; invoices and cheque payments alone were insufficient on the facts before the Court. The Supreme Court therefore restored the disallowance. Its application to a later “non-existent supplier” case requires equal care. A supplier who never existed and issued accommodation invoices is materially different from a genuine supplier whose premises were found locked, or whose registration was cancelled, years after the supply. The purchaser must still discharge the GST burden, but the Department must examine the particular transaction and the evidence—not use the later cancellation as a substitute for findings on receipt and eligibility. For your December 2022 illustration, the proper defence is to establish the complete transaction trail for the ₹1,00,800 credit, verify the other section 16 conditions, and challenge the unsupported inference that a 2026 inspection proves non-existence in 2022. The defence should not rest on prospective application of the judgment or on an assertion that three-year-old records need not be retained.

One GST Notice for Four Financial Years: What the Kerala High Court Decided in Jovin George—and What Remains Open – A practitioner’s analysis of composite show cause notices, year-wise limitation and the limits of procedural relief Summary:In Jovin George v. Assistant Commissioner and Others (Kerala High Court), W.P.(C) No. 33147 of 2026, decided on 28 September 2026, HC quashed a consolidated GST show cause notice dated 27 August 2026 covering financial years 2020–21, 2021–22, 2022–23 and 2023–24. The Court followed its earlier decisions concerning composite proceedings covering multiple assessment years. However, the judgment did not determine the underlying tax liability or permanently prohibit further proceedings. The Department was expressly permitted to issue separate notices for the relevant years. The Court also directed that the period beginning on the date of the original notice and ending on receipt of the certified copy of the judgment be excluded while computing limitation for initiating fresh proceedings. This direction is significant because the statutory limitation periods applicable to different financial years may vary. The judgment does not identify whether the challenged notice was issued under Section 73 or Section 74, nor does it determine whether any particular year was already time-barred. All other contentions of the parties were left open. Table of Contents▸ 1. The Case at a Glance 2. What Exactly Was Challenged? 3. The Operative Order Has Three Separate Consequences 4. Assistant Commissioner and Others Court Kerala High Court Case number W.P.(C) No. 33147 of 2026 Neutral citation 2026: KER:74875 Date of judgment 28 September 2026 Judge Justice Ziyad Rahman A.A. Notice challenged Exhibit P1, dated 27 August 2026 Financial years covered 2020–21, 2021–22, 2022–23 and 2023–24 Result Consolidated notice quashed; separate notices permitted, with a specific limitation exclusion These particulars appear in the judgment and its appendix. There is one material drafting caution at the outset. The uploaded judgment does not identify whether Exhibit P1 was issued under Section 73 or Section 74. It also does not reproduce the allegations, the demand amount or the year-wise computation. An article on this case should not fill those gaps by assumption. 2. What Exactly Was Challenged? The petitioner challenged one consolidated show cause notice covering four financial years. His principal objection was that a composite notice for multiple assessment years was legally unsustainable in view of the Kerala High Court decisions in: Joint Commissioner (Intelligence & Enforcement) v. M/s. Lakshmi Mobiles Accessories, 2025 KHC OnLine 149; and Tharayil Medicals (M/s.), Thrissur v. Deputy Commissioner, Thrissur, 2025 KHC OnLine 467. The Court accepted that submission and followed the earlier decisions. This was a short precedent-following judgment, not a fresh, detailed examination of every expression in Sections 73 and 74. That matters when identifying its ratio: the Court applied the existing Kerala position to the consolidated notice before it. 3. The Operative Order Has Three Separate Consequences The relief should be read in three parts, rather than reduced to the statement “the notice was quashed.” First: the existing notice was set aside Exhibit P1 could no longer serve as the operative notice for the proposed adjudication. The Court expressly quashed it. Second: fresh year-wise notices were permitted The Department retained liberty to issue separate notices for the relevant assessment years. The judgment therefore did not grant the petitioner immunity from proceedings concerning those years. Third: the Court protected a defined period from limitation The Court directed exclusion of the period beginning with the date of Exhibit P1 and ending with receipt of the certified copy of the judgment, while computing limitation for fresh proceedings. All other contentions were left open. For the taxpayer, this is relief against the consolidated proceeding. For the Department, it is an opportunity to recommence through separate notices, subject to the judgment and the remaining legal objections. 4. Why Does Combining Financial Years Create a Legal Difficulty? A multi-year investigation and a multi-year adjudication are not necessarily the same thing. An investigation may uncover a common practice over several years. The question is whether one notice and one adjudicatory timetable can deal with those years without disturbing the statutory safeguards attached to each period. Under Section 74(10), the ordinary outer limit for issuing an order is five years from the due date for furnishing the annual return for the financial year concerned. In erroneous-refund cases, the provision uses the date of erroneous refund. Section 74(2) separately requires the notice to be issued at least six months before the applicable order deadline. Section 73 follows a different ordinary timetable: three years for the order, with the notice required at least three months before that deadline. The applicable annual-return due date, statutory extensions, exclusions and court orders must still be examined before fixing an actual calendar deadline. The point is not simply that different years have different numbers. Each year can have its own limitation position, records, factual explanation and legal defence. An illustration Suppose one notice includes: Financial year Dispute raised Year 1 Difference between turnover in returns and accounts Year 2 ITC disputed on particular supplier invoices Year 3 Classification and rate dispute Year 4 Alleged non-payment under reverse charge This is an illustration, not the factual record in Jovin George. Putting these issues in one document does not make the evidence interchangeable. A satisfactory explanation for the turnover difference will not answer the supplier-invoice allegation. Nor does an allegation concerning one year establish fraud in every other year. The practical concern is whether consolidation conceals those differences or compresses the opportunity to deal with them. 5. The Earlier Lakshmi Mobile Accessories Ruling Needs Careful Reading The reported reasoning in Joint Commissioner v. Lakshmi Mobile Accessories focuses on the prejudice caused when the earliest year’s approaching limitation date controls adjudication for later years. The Division Bench explained that the taxpayer’s opportunity to produce evidence for later years should not become illusory merely because the oldest year is nearing its statutory deadline. It also recognised the significance of whether there is a common period for initiation and completion of adjudication. That reasoning should not be rewritten as though the taxpayer has an absolute right to postpone adjudication until the final day of limitation. The concern is meaningful opportunity—not a right to consume the entire statutory period. The practical objection is therefore stronger when it identifies what consolidation has actually done: Which later-year documents could not be collected? Which distinct allegation requires a separate explanation? Which year is independently outside limitation? Has the oldest year’s deadline been used to justify an unduly compressed hearing? These questions connect the procedural objection to the prejudice discussed in the Kerala precedent. 6. The Judgment Does Not Decide Whether Tax Is Payable The uploaded decision contains no finding that: the petitioner’s turnover was correctly declared; the disputed ITC, if any, was eligible; fraud or suppression was absent; the proposed computation was correct or incorrect; or any particular financial year was already time-barred. Instead, the Court expressly left the parties’ other contentions open. Consequently, describing the judgment as “GST demand cancelled permanently” would be inaccurate. Equally, describing it as “all four years held time-barred” would go beyond the text. The accurate formulation is narrower: the consolidated notice was quashed, with liberty to issue separate notices and with a court-directed exclusion for limitation. 7. The Limitation-Exclusion Clause Deserves as Much Attention as the Quashing The Court’s exclusion begins on 27 August 2026—the date of the original notice—and ends on receipt of the certified copy. It does not end automatically on the judgment date of 28 September 2026. The judgment supplied does not state the actual date of receipt. A fresh-notice review should therefore distinguish the following: Question Significance What was the original statutory deadline for that year? Establishes the starting limitation position Was the original notice within time? A separate question not answered in this judgment What exact period did the Court exclude? Determines the effect of the operative direction When was the certified copy received? Establishes the end of the excluded interval When was the fresh notice issued? Allows the fresh proceeding to be tested The judgment does not expressly pronounce upon the revival of a demand already barred before the original notice. Since other contentions were reserved, it should not be presented as having conclusively resolved that question either way. There is also a distinction between this express judicial direction and Section 75(1). That provision excludes a period during which service of notice or issuance of an order is stayed by a court or tribunal. Merely filing a writ petition should not be confused with obtaining such a stay. Section 75(3), concerning orders required pursuant to appellate or court directions, is another provision requiring separate examination; the short Jovin George judgment does not discuss it. 8. Why the Karnataka Position Cannot Be Ignored For a practitioner advising businesses in Karnataka, this is the most important qualification. The reported Division Bench decision in Commissioner of Central Tax v. Chimney Hills Education Society, W.A. No. 1751/2024 and connected matters, takes a different approach. The published reports describe the Court as permitting consolidated notices under Sections 73 and 74, while retaining the requirement that limitation be tested independently for each period. The Revenue’s appeals arose from Single Judge orders quashing consolidated notices. Accordingly, an article should not tell Karnataka taxpayers that the Kerala ruling automatically invalidates their notices. Nor is it safe to rely only on the earlier Karnataka Single Judge line associated with Veremax without addressing the subsequent reported Division Bench position. The difference between the two approaches can be stated plainly: Approach Central concern Kerala line applied in Jovin George Separate year-wise treatment to preserve limitation and fair adjudication Reported Karnataka Division Bench approach No general statutory prohibition on consolidation, provided period-wise safeguards remain intact The Karnataka position stated here is drawn from published reports; the complete signed Division Bench judgment should be checked before quoting particular paragraphs or framing a pleading. 9. Even Where Consolidation Is Permitted, the Notice Must Withstand Substantive Scrutiny Permission to issue one notice is not permission to make an unexplained aggregate demand. Year-wise allegations and computation The taxpayer must be able to identify the transactions, tax period, amount and factual basis being disputed. A common conclusion cannot substitute for examination of materially different records. The reported decisions permitting consolidation themselves retain safeguards concerning limitation and adequate opportunity. Foundation for Section 74 Section 74 requires the alleged non-payment, short payment, erroneous refund or wrongful ITC to arise by reason of fraud, wilful misstatement or suppression of facts to evade tax. The notice must be examined against those statutory ingredients, rather than merely its heading or its use of the word “suppression.” Hearing and reasoned determination Section 75(4) requires an opportunity of hearing where requested in writing or where an adverse decision is contemplated. Section 75(6) requires the order to set out the relevant facts and the basis of the decision. Section 75(7) prevents confirmation beyond the amount specified in the notice or on grounds not specified in it. These safeguards remain relevant whether the Department issues one notice or four. 10. A Defensible Response Should Have More Than One Ground A taxpayer who receives a multi-year notice should not leave the substantive allegations unanswered merely because a consolidation objection is available. A practical response can be organised in four parts: Preliminary objections: the applicable jurisdictional precedent, limitation and defects in the proceeding. Year-wise factual reply: reconciliation, transaction records and explanation for each allegation. Statutory objections: the basis for invoking Section 73 or Section 74, proposed interest and penalty. Procedural requests: relied-upon documents, adequate time and a personal hearing. This structure reflects the two distinct questions emerging from the authorities: whether consolidation itself is permissible, and whether the particular proceeding respects limitation and fair opportunity. A focused objection might read: “The notice covers several financial years having separate statutory limitation dates and distinct factual records. The notice objects to consolidation to the extent that it merges those limitation periods, obscures year-wise allegations or restricts an effective opportunity of defence. Without prejudice to this objection, the notice submits the following financial-year-wise reply on merits.” That paragraph is a drafting starting point—not a substitute for identifying the specific defect in the client’s notice. 11. Section 74A Must Be Treated Separately The years in Jovin George end with FY 2023–24. Section 74A applies to determination of tax pertaining to FY 2024–25 onwards; Sections 73 and 74 continue to govern their specified earlier periods. It would therefore be unsafe to state that this judgment has already decided the validity of multi-year notices under Section 74A. Its text and limitation framework require independent analysis. The broader concerns remain relevant—clear allegations, identifiable periods, proper computation and fair opportunity—but the statutory reasoning cannot simply be transferred from one provision to another. 12. A Publication-Ready Statement of the Holding The decision can accurately be summarised as follows: “In Jovin George v. Assistant Commissioner, 2026: KER:74875, the Kerala High Court quashed a consolidated show cause notice dated 27 August 2026 covering FY 2020–21 to FY 2023–24, following its earlier decisions concerning composite proceedings. The Court permitted separate notices for the relevant years and excluded the period from the original notice date until receipt of the certified copy of the judgment while computing limitation for fresh proceedings. All other contentions were left open.” The case illustrates a precise form of procedural relief: the Department must restart through the permitted route, but the taxpayer must still examine and contest the fresh proceedings on limitation, facts and law. Its value lies in that disciplined distinction—not in a sweeping claim that every multi-year GST notice is void throughout India.

Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); A Sarraf and Om Group Developers Vs Central Board of Direct Taxes (Rajasthan High Court) Summary: The Rajasthan High Court examined the CBDT’s refusal to condone a 53-day delay in filing the income-tax return for assessment year 2022-23. A Sarraf and Om Group Developers, engaged in affordable housing development, had uploaded its audit report within time but filed its return on 30 December 2022. Its Section 80-IBA deduction was consequently denied and a demand of ₹77,11,470 was raised. The petitioner attributed the delay to professional negligence, pleaded genuine hardship and liquidity constraints, and challenged the reliance on undisclosed departmental reports. The Revenue argued that Sections 80AC and 139(1) made timely filing mandatory and that the explanation did not warrant discretionary condonation under Section 119(2)(b). The Court held that Sections 80AC and 119(2)(b) operate harmoniously: the former prescribes the ordinary filing condition, while the latter allows relief from genuine hardship in deserving cases. It emphasised the timely audit report, supporting affidavits, professional advice, and the distinction between balance-sheet assets and actual liquidity. It also found that reliance on adverse reports without supplying the underlying material impaired an effective opportunity of hearing. The High Court concluded that the petitioner had made out a case for condonation, quashed the CBDT order dated 30 April 2024 and allowed the writ petition without costs. It clarified that condonation did not establish substantive entitlement to the deduction, which remained subject to examination on merits. Cases Discussed B.M. Malani v. DISCUSSION AND FINDING : 27. Having heard learned counsel for the parties, examined the pleadings and the material available on record, this Court finds that the controversy involved in the present writ petition primarily concerns the manner in which the respondent-authority exercised its discretion under Section 119(2)(b) of the Income-tax Act, 1961, while considering the petitioner’s application seeking condonation of delay in furnishing its return of income for the assessment year 2022-23. 28. The question which arises for consideration is whether the respondent-authority was justified in rejecting the petitioner’s application seeking condonation of delay of 53 days, particularly when the petitioner had furnished its audit report within the prescribed period, had explained the circumstances leading to the delayed filing of the return, had pleaded financial hardship and had placed supporting material on record. 29. At the outset, it is pertinent to observe that the power conferred under Section 119(2)(b) of the Act is not an empty formality or a mere enabling provision without substantive purpose. The legislature, while enacting the aforesaid provision, consciously vested the Board with the authority to relax the rigours of the prescribed timelines in appropriate cases so as to prevent genuine hardship to an assessee. The provision empowers the Board, where it considers it desirable or expedient for avoiding genuine hardship, to authorize an income-tax authority to admit an application or claim for exemption, deduction, refund or other relief after the expiry of the period specified under the Act and to deal with the same on merits in accordance with law. Thus, the very object underlying the provision is to ensure that procedural requirements, though ordinarily required to be complied with, do not operate as an insurmountable obstacle in cases where genuine hardship is demonstrated and the circumstances justify exercise of the discretionary power. 30. It is true that Section 80AC of the Act prescribes the requirement of furnishing the return of income within the due date specified under Section 139(1) as a condition for claiming the deduction contemplated therein. It is equally true that Section 80IBA falls within the category of deductions to which the aforesaid statutory restriction applies. Consequently, the petitioner could not have claimed the deduction as an automatic entitlement notwithstanding the delayed filing of the return. However, the matter does not end there. Section 119(2)(b) of the Act itself provides a statutory mechanism for entertaining an application or claim beyond the prescribed period in order to avoid genuine hardship. Therefore, the provisions of Section 80AC cannot be read in isolation so as to render the remedial jurisdiction conferred under Section 119(2)(b) wholly ineffective. 31. The two provisions operate in their respective fields.Section 80AC prescribes the ordinary statutory requirement, whereas Section 119(2)(b) provides an enabling mechanism for relaxation in deserving cases. A harmonious interpretation of the two provisions is, therefore, necessary. If the mere existence of the statutory deadline under Section 80AC were sufficient to defeat every application seeking condonation, the very purpose of conferring the power under Section 119(2)(b) would stand frustrated. 32. The respondents were, therefore, required to examine the petitioner’s application in the light of the object and purpose of Section 119(2)(b), rather than reject the same merely by reiterating the statutory consequence of belated filing. In this regard, the observations made by the Hon’ble Supreme Court in B.M. Malani v. Commissioner of Income Tax & Anr. 24, assume considerable significance. The Hon’ble Supreme Court, while interpreting the expression ‘genuine hardship’, observed that the expression was required to be understood purposively and that genuine hardship would, inter alia, mean a genuine difficulty. It was further observed that the circumstances constituting genuine hardship were required to be determined by keeping in view the dictionary meaning of the expression as well as the legal conspectus attending thereto. Major General Madan Lal Yadav (Retd.), (1996) 4 SCC 127 at 142, paragraphs 28 and 29, Ashok Kapil v. Sana Ullah, (1996) 6 SCC 342 at 345, paragraph 7, Sushil Kumar v. Rakesh Kumar, (2003) 8 SCC 673 at 692, paragraph 65, first sentence, Kusheshwar Prasad Singh v. State of Bihar, (2007) 11 SCC 447, paragraphs 13, 14 and 16). Thus, the said principle, in our opinion, should be applied even in a case of this nature. A statutory authority despite receipt of such a request could not have kept mum. It should have taken some action. It should have responded to the prayer of the appellant.” 33. The Hon’ble Supreme Court observed that “A genuine hardship would, inter alia, mean a genuine difficulty.” The Court further emphasised that the expression ‘genuine’ meant something real and not fictitious, bogus or merely a pretence. The aforesaid judgment clearly demonstrates that the authority exercising discretion under a provision intended to alleviate hardship is required to examine the actual circumstances of the assessee and cannot adopt a narrow or mechanical interpretation of the expression ‘genuine hardship’. Applying the aforesaid principle to the facts of the present case, this Court finds that the petitioner’s explanation was required to be examined in its entirety, including the circumstances surrounding the engagement of its Chartered Accountant, the timely uploading of the audit report, the subsequent delay in furnishing the return, the financial difficulties pleaded by the petitioner and the supporting affidavits furnished along with its application. A mere conclusion that the petitioner had failed to establish circumstances which prevented timely filing, without undertaking a holistic examination of the material placed on record, would not constitute a proper exercise of the jurisdiction vested under Section 119(2)(b) of the Act. 34. Another significant aspect which cannot be overlooked is that the petitioner had not remained completely non-compliant with its statutory obligations. The audit report for the assessment year 2022-23 had admittedly been uploaded on 30.09.2022, within the prescribed period. The return of income, however, came to be furnished on 30.12.2022, with a delay of 53 days. The aforesaid circumstance assumes relevance because it demonstrates that the petitioner had undertaken the requisite audit exercise within time and that the delay was confined to the subsequent filing of the return. Though timely uploading of the audit report, by itself, did not dispense with the statutory requirement of filing the return within the prescribed period, it was certainly a relevant circumstance while examining the bona fides of the petitioner and the explanation furnished for the delay. The respondents were, therefore, required to consider whether the petitioner’s conduct, viewed in its entirety, indicated a deliberate attempt to evade statutory compliance or whether the delay had occurred on account of circumstances which could reasonably be accepted as genuine. 35. The explanation furnished by the petitioner that the delay had occurred on account of negligence and incorrect professional advice of its Chartered Accountant also required consideration in the proper perspective. There can be no quarrel with the proposition that an assessee cannot, in every circumstance, escape statutory consequences merely by attributing its default to a professional consultant. The responsibility of complying with statutory obligations ordinarily rests upon the assessee. However, the aforesaid principle cannot be applied as an inflexible rule so as to exclude consideration of professional negligence altogether while examining an application seeking condonation on the ground of genuine hardship. 36. The authority was required to examine whether the explanation was bona fide, whether the petitioner had taken reasonable steps towards compliance, whether the audit report had been uploaded within time, and whether any material suggested that the petitioner had deliberately withheld the return or had derived any undue advantage from the delay. The fact that the petitioner had subsequently discontinued the services of the Chartered Accountant and had furnished affidavits explaining the circumstances was also a relevant circumstance which ought to have received due consideration. It was, therefore, not appropriate for the respondents to reject the explanation solely on the ground that the petitioner was ultimately responsible for filing its return. 37. This Court also finds substance in the petitioner’s contention that the authority failed to properly appreciate the financial circumstances pleaded by it. The petitioner had specifically contended that it was undergoing acute liquidity constraints at the relevant point of time and had furnished copies of its bank statements in support of such contention. The respondents, however, appear to have proceeded on the basis of the overall financial statements of the petitioner and had drawn conclusions regarding its financial capacity without adequately distinguishing between the overall financial position of a business entity and the actual availability of liquid funds at a particular point of time. 38. It is a settled proposition that the existence of assets or a substantial balance sheet does not necessarily establish the immediate availability of liquid funds. Financial hardship cannot be examined merely by referring to the gross financial position of an assessee without considering the nature of its liabilities, cash flows, fund commitments and actual liquidity at the relevant time. 39. In B.M. Malani (supra), the Hon’ble Supreme Court specifically cautioned against drawing an automatic inference that a person having substantial assets could never suffer genuine hardship merely because such assets could theoretically be sold to discharge an obligation. The aforesaid principle was directly relevant to the petitioner’s explanation. The authority was required to examine the liquidity position of the petitioner at the relevant time instead of treating its financial statements as conclusive proof of its capacity to comply with the statutory timeline. 40. Equally significant is the objection regarding the reliance placed upon the departmental reports obtained from the DGIT (Investigation), Jaipur and the PCCIT, Jaipur. The petitioner had specifically contended that the CBDT, in its communication dated 29.11.2023, had referred to adverse reports received from the departmental authorities and had indicated that the recommendation was against grant of condonation. However, copies of the underlying reports were not supplied to the petitioner, despite the same having been relied upon while passing the impugned order. 41. The respondents, on the other hand, submitted that the reports had been communicated to the petitioner and that an opportunity to submit a response had duly been afforded. This Court is of the considered opinion that there exists a material distinction between informing an assessee about the existence or conclusion of an adverse departmental report and actually furnishing the report or the relevant material forming its basis. An opportunity of hearing cannot be reduced to a mere formality. Where an authority proposes to rely upon adverse material collected from another departmental authority, fairness requires that the affected party be furnished with such material, at least to the extent that it forms the basis of the proposed adverse decision, so that an effective response can be submitted. 42. Similarly, in Kishinchand Chellaram v. Commissioner of Income Tax25, the Hon’ble Supreme Court emphasised that material collected behind the back of an assessee could not be relied upon to his prejudice without affording him an adequate opportunity to controvert the same. The aforesaid principle assumes greater significance in the present matter because the departmental reports were apparently treated as an important basis for rejecting the petitioner’s request for condonation. 43. Merely mentioning in the show-cause communication that adverse reports had been received could not be treated as sufficient compliance with the requirement of natural justice, particularly when the petitioner was not furnished with the reports themselves or the material forming the basis of the adverse conclusions. The requirement of fairness is not satisfied by providing an opportunity to make a representation in the abstract. Such opportunity must be real, meaningful and effective. Consequently, this Court finds that the failure to furnish the material relied upon by the respondents materially impaired the petitioner’s opportunity to effectively defend its application. 44. The respondents had also relied upon the report dated 06.11.2023 submitted by the Deputy Commissioner of Income Tax, Central Circle-1, Jaipur, which contained details of certain earlier instances of delayed filing of returns by the petitioner. This Court is conscious of the fact that the past compliance history of an assessee may constitute a relevant circumstance while considering an application for condonation of delay. However, such 25(1980) 4 SCC 660 history cannot be treated as an independent or conclusive ground for rejecting an application without examining the explanation furnished in respect of the particular assessment year under consideration. 45. Every application seeking condonation has to be examined on its own facts and circumstances. The existence of previous instances of delayed compliance may invite closer scrutiny of the explanation furnished by the assessee, but it cannot lead to an automatic presumption that the subsequent delay was also deliberate or without sufficient cause. The respondents were, therefore, required to examine whether the earlier defaults had any bearing upon the delay in question and whether the circumstances leading to the present delay were genuine. A mere reference to the petitioner’s past compliance history, without examining the explanation furnished for the delay of 53 days in the assessment year under consideration, could not have constituted a sufficient basis for rejection. The authority was required to undertake an objective assessment of the petitioner’s conduct instead of allowing one adverse circumstance to eclipse all other relevant material. 46. Another aspect which deserves consideration is the reliance placed by the respondents upon the alleged failure of the petitioner to furnish complete bank statements, narrations of transactions and an affidavit disclosing the number of bank accounts maintained by it. The petitioner had specifically contended that no such particulars had been demanded from it during the proceedings. In the absence of any specific requisition or opportunity to furnish additional documents, the authority could not have drawn an adverse inference against the petitioner merely because such particulars had not been furnished. 47. It is undoubtedly open to an authority exercising statutory discretion to seek additional information which may be necessary for arriving at a just conclusion. However, where the authority considers certain particulars essential for determining the genuineness of the hardship pleaded by an assessee, fairness demands that the assessee be specifically called upon to furnish such particulars before an adverse inference is drawn. The respondents could not have expected the petitioner to anticipate every document or clarification which might subsequently be considered relevant by the authority. 48. The same principle applies to the observations made in respect of fund management, loan arrangements and non-deposit of advance tax. These circumstances could certainly have been examined if they had a demonstrable nexus with the petitioner’s liquidity position and the reasons for delayed filing. However, they could not have been treated as independent disqualifications without establishing their relevance to the question of genuine hardship. The exercise of discretion under Section 119(2)(b) required consideration of relevant material and exclusion of considerations which were extraneous to the issue involved. The relevant provisions i.e. Section 119(2)(b) is reproduced hereinbelow: “Instructions to subordinate authorities. 119(2) Without prejudice to the generality of the foregoing power,- (a) xx xx xx (b) the Board may, If It considers It desirable or expedient so to do for avoiding genuine hardship in any case or class of cases, by general or special order, authorise any Income-tax authority, not being a Joint Commissioner (Appeals) or a Commissioner (Appeals) to admit an application or claim for any exemption, deduction, refund or any other relief under this Act after the expiry of the period specified by or under this Act for making such application or claim and deal with the same on merits in accordance with law.” 49. Now, it is also necessary to examine the nature and scope of the discretion vested in the respondent-authority under Section 119(2)(b) of the Act. The expression ‘discretionary power’ does not confer an unfettered or arbitrary authority upon the decision- making body. Every statutory discretion is required to be exercised in accordance with the object of the enactment, relevant considerations and settled principles of law. 50. The discretion must be exercised judiciously, reasonably and upon an objective consideration of the material placed before the authority. It cannot be exercised mechanically or with a pre- determined approach. In B.M. Malani (supra), the Hon’ble Supreme Court emphasised the requirement of purposive interpretation while determining genuine hardship. The underlying principle is that the authority must examine the real difficulty faced by the assessee rather than adopt a rigid interpretation which defeats the very purpose for which the power was conferred. 51. Similarly, in Collector, Land Acquisition, Anantnag v. Mst. Katiji26, the Hon’ble Supreme Court held that the expression ‘sufficient cause’ should receive a liberal construction so as to 26(1987) 2 SCC 107 advance substantial justice. The Court observed that ordinarily a litigant does not stand to benefit by lodging a claim late and that refusal to condone delay may result in a meritorious matter being thrown out at the threshold. Though the aforesaid judgment arose in the context of condonation of delay under the law of limitation, the principle underlying the same assumes relevance while examining the exercise of discretion under Section 119(2)(b) of the Act. 52. The Hon’ble Supreme Court further in the case of Mangalore Chemicals and Fertilizers Ltd. (supra) emphasised that when substantial justice and technical considerations are pitted against each other, the cause of substantial justice deserves to be preferred. The aforesaid principle does not mean that every delay, irrespective of its explanation, must necessarily be condoned. It does, however, require the authority to distinguish between a deliberate default and a bona fide lapse and to adopt an approach which advances substantive justice without compromising the statutory framework. 53. In Manav Seva Samiti (Supra) the Hon’ble Supreme Court observed that the length of delay is not material so much as the acceptability of the explanation. The Court held that the primary function of a court while considering condonation is to advance substantial justice and that the acceptability of the explanation, rather than the mere number of days of delay, is the relevant consideration. The aforesaid principle reinforces the requirement that the authority must examine the explanation in its proper perspective. In the present case, the delay was only 53 days. The petitioner had already uploaded its audit report within the prescribed period and had furnished an explanation attributing the subsequent delay to professional negligence and financial constraints. 54. The respondents have not demonstrated that the petitioner had deliberately withheld the return, intended to evade tax or derived any undue advantage by filing the return belatedly. Nor has any material been brought to the notice of this Court to establish that the petitioner had adopted a calculated course of non-compliance for the assessment year in question. The mere fact that the petitioner was a partnership firm engaged in the business of developing affordable housing projects and had claimed a substantial deduction could not, by itself, justify an adverse inference against it. The amount involved in the deduction was a relevant factor for scrutiny, but not a substitute for examination of the explanation regarding delay. 55. The Court is also mindful of the submission advanced by learned counsel for the respondents that the petitioner had not established any genuine hardship and that the delay was attributable to the negligence of its own Chartered Accountant. However, the aforesaid submission does not persuade this Court to sustain the impugned order. The question was not whether the petitioner could be absolved of every responsibility arising from the conduct of its professional consultant. The real question was whether, considering the totality of the circumstances, the delay was sufficiently explained and whether refusal to condone the same would result in disproportionate hardship. 56. The petitioner had specifically pleaded that it had been dependent upon its Chartered Accountant for professional assistance in taxation matters, that the audit report had been uploaded within time, that the return could not be furnished on account of incorrect professional advice and that it had subsequently discontinued the services of the said professional. The explanation was supported by affidavits. Whether such explanation was ultimately sufficient was required to be determined by examining the surrounding circumstances and supporting material. It could not have been rejected merely by invoking the general proposition that the assessee was responsible for statutory compliance. 57. This Court further finds that the impugned order does not demonstrate an adequate consideration of the petitioner’s specific explanation regarding the liquidity constraints prevailing at the relevant point of time. The petitioner had furnished bank statements and had specifically asserted that its financial position during the relevant period did not permit it to arrange the requisite funds and complete the necessary compliance within the prescribed timeline. The authority, instead of examining the availability of liquid funds at the relevant point of time, appears to have drawn conclusions from the overall financial statements and transactions extending over the entire financial year. 58. Coming to the question of availability of an alternative remedy, it is true that the petitioner had preferred an appeal against the intimation dated 03.04.2023 before the Commissioner of Income Tax (Appeals), which was pending consideration, however, the same stands to be infructuous in light of the petitioner’s application for condonation of delay, being denied by the learned CBDT. Nonetheless, the present writ petition primarily challenged the subsequent order dated 30.04.2024 passed by the CBDT under Section 119(2)(b) of the Act. 59. It is clarified that condonation of delay would not, by itself, establish the petitioner’s entitlement to deduction under Section 80IBA of the Act. The petitioner would still be required to satisfy the substantive conditions prescribed under the said provision. Nevertheless, the question of substantive eligibility and the question of condonation of delay operate at different stages and ought not to be conflated. 60. The authority concerned, while examining an application under Section 119(2)(b), is required to determine whether the circumstances warrant relaxation of the prescribed timeline. It is not expected to finally adjudicate the entire claim for deduction as though it were exercising appellate jurisdiction over the assessment. 61. In the present case, the petitioner had not merely pleaded inadvertence in abstract terms. It had relied upon the timely uploading of the audit report, the circumstances concerning its Chartered Accountant, the affidavits furnished in support of its explanation, the liquidity constraints prevailing at the relevant time and the fact that the delay was confined to 53 days, and the fact that in the previous years also such benefit has been granted. Learned counsel for the petitioner, in support of the prayer for condonation of delay, placed reliance upon a catena of judicial pronouncements, including the judgments of the Hon’ble Supreme Court in B.M. Malani (supra) and Mangalore Chemicals and Fertilizers Ltd. (supra), to emphasise that the expression ‘genuine hardship’ was required to be construed purposively and that procedural requirements ought not to be applied in a manner which defeats substantial justice. Reliance was further placed upon inter alia, Maharashtra Seva Samiti (supra), wherein the issue of condonation of delay of 700 days had been considered by this Court. Placing reliance upon the aforesaid decisions, learned counsel submitted that the judicial approach towards applications seeking condonation of delay ought to be pragmatic, equitable and guided by the object of preventing genuine hardship, rather than being governed by a rigid or hypertechnical interpretation of procedural requirements. It was contended that when delays extending to several months and even years had been considered by various High Courts in the context of genuine hardship, the petitioner’s delay of merely 53 days, supported by an explanation regarding professional negligence and financial constraints, deserved consideration on a more liberal and justice-oriented approach. It was, therefore, submitted that denial of the benefit of condonation in the facts of the present case would result in undue prejudice to the petitioner and defeat the very object underlying Section 119(2)(b) of the Act; and thus, we are satisfied with the contentions so put forth. 62. Further, it is noteworthy that the petitioner had approached the CBDT by submitting an application dated 25.07.2023, seeking condonation of the delay and had also responded to the show- cause communication by submitting a detailed reply dated 05.12.2023. There is nothing on record to suggest that the petitioner had sought to obtain any undue advantage by deliberately withholding its return. On the contrary, the audit report had been uploaded within time and the return had subsequently been furnished, though belatedly. It is also relevant that the petitioner had claimed deduction under Section 80IBA for the assessment years 2021-22, 2022-23 and 2023-24. Its claim for the assessment year in question was, therefore, not shown to be an afterthought devised merely to take advantage of the condonation proceedings. The circumstances, therefore, warranted a justice-oriented approach rather than a rigid or hypertechnical consideration. 63. The petitioner was not seeking exemption from the substantive requirements of Section 80IBA Its request was confined to condonation of the delay so that its claim could be considered in accordance with law. The distinction is material. By condoning the delay, the Revenue does not lose its right to examine the genuineness of the deduction claimed, verify the conditions prescribed under Section 80IBA or determine the tax liability in accordance with the applicable provisions. The Revenue merely becomes obligated to examine the claim on its merits rather than reject it at the threshold solely on account of the procedural delay. 64. It is well settled that the existence of discretionary power does not mean that its exercise is immune from judicial scrutiny. Where the authority fails to consider relevant material, relies upon undisclosed adverse material or adopts an approach contrary to the object of the statutory provision, the resulting decision becomes amenable to judicial review under Article 226 of the Constitution of India. 65. The Hon’ble Supreme Court in B.M. Malani (supra) and Mangalore Chemicals and Fertilizers Ltd. (supra) itself recognised that the principle that a person cannot take advantage of his own wrong must be considered in the overall factual and legal context while determining genuine hardship. Thus, the said principle cannot be mechanically employed to defeat an otherwise bona fide claim without examining the explanation furnished by the assessee. 66. The legislative intent underlying Section 119(2)(b) is to enable the competent authority to alleviate genuine hardship and ensure that a procedural default does not, in appropriate cases, result in denial of consideration of a claim on its merits. The authority was, therefore, expected to adopt a balanced approach, as the substantial requirements were already fulfilled by the petitioner; keeping in view the nature of the delay, the explanation furnished, the conduct of the petitioner and the consequences of refusing condonation. 67. This Court is satisfied that the petitioner had made out a case warranting condonation of the delay. Refusal to condone the same, in the circumstances noticed hereinabove, would result in the petitioner’s claim being excluded from consideration without an adequate adjudication of its substantive entitlement. 68. Accordingly, this Court finds that the impugned order dated 30.04.2024 cannot be sustained in the eyes of law and is liable to be quashed and set aside. Consequently, the present writ petition is allowed. The order dated 30.04.2024 passed by the respondent-authority rejecting the petitioner’s application under Section 119(2)(b) of the Income-tax Act, 1961, is hereby quashed and set aside. 69. All consequential proceedings arising solely on account of denial of deduction due to delayed filing shall abide by the decision taken in terms of the aforesaid directions. 70. Ordered accordingly. There shall be no order as to costs. Pending applications, if any, shall stand disposed.

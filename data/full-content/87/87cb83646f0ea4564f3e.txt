Cements Limited (NCLT Guwahati) Summary: Catalyst Trusteeship Limited, acting as Debenture Trustee for the debenture holders, filed an application under Section 7 of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016, seeking initiation of the Corporate Insolvency Resolution Process against J.U.D. Cements Limited for an alleged default in repayment of financial debt arising from 1,550 secured, unlisted, redeemable non-convertible debentures of ₹10 lakh each, aggregating ₹155 crore. The debentures were subscribed on 2 January 2025, and the amount was credited to an escrow account maintained in the Corporate Debtor’s name. The Financial Creditor alleged that the Scheduled Coupon and Upfront Coupon remained unpaid despite subsequent acknowledgments of default by the Corporate Debtor through its letter dated 21 August 2025 and Deed of Undertaking dated 28 October 2025. Following an Event of Default-cum-Recall Notice dated 5 March 2026, the Financial Creditor claimed an outstanding amount of ₹1,96,69,05,415 as on 30 April 2026. ordersview(2).pdf The Corporate Debtor challenged the maintainability of the petition, contending that the individual debenture holders were necessary parties and that the Financial Creditor, as Debenture Trustee, lacked independent locus. It further argued that the ₹155 crore had not been effectively disbursed because the escrow account and the fixed deposits created from its proceeds remained under the exclusive control of the Trustee and the financer’s representatives. According to the Corporate Debtor, restrictions on withdrawals and delays in releasing funds impaired its operations and prevented it from making payments, including the coupon instalments. It also disputed the validity of the acceleration of the principal, the Information Utility record of default and the computation of the claim, and alleged that the proceedings were being used as a recovery mechanism under the guise of insolvency proceedings. ordersview(2).pdf The Tribunal held that the Financial Creditor, acting under the Debenture Trustee Agreement, Debenture Trust Deed and authorisations issued by the debenture holders, had locus to maintain the application; the individual debenture holders were not necessary parties. It found that the ₹155 crore constituted financial debt under Section 5(8)(c) of the Code, since the amount was raised through non-convertible debentures carrying coupon and redemption obligations and was credited to an account in the Corporate Debtor’s own name for its stipulated purposes. The Tribunal further held that the escrow arrangement regulated the custody and utilisation of funds but did not transfer or suspend the Corporate Debtor’s primary contractual obligation to pay amounts on their respective due dates. As the Scheduled Coupon due on 31 January 2025 remained unpaid, default was established; subsequent appropriations from the escrow account were required to be credited against the outstanding liability but did not retrospectively extinguish the default. The letter and Deed of Undertaking also corroborated the subsisting liability, as the Corporate Debtor had not pleaded coercion, undue influence or duress or taken steps to avoid those documents. ordersview(2).pdf The Tribunal clarified that while the default date for the Scheduled Coupon was 31 January 2025, the principal amount became payable only upon maturity or valid acceleration, with the relevant default date for the principal being 12 March 2026. This distinction did not affect maintainability because the coupon default itself exceeded the statutory threshold under Section 4. It also held that a disputed or incomplete Information Utility record was not, by itself, a ground to reject the petition where other evidence, including the transaction documents, acknowledgments, Recall Notice, payment history and the Corporate Debtor’s own pleadings, established the debt and default. Discrepancies in the computation of the claim and the Corporate Debtor’s solvency, security cover or going-concern status were not sufficient to defeat admission of the application. Finding no adequate basis to treat the proceedings as malicious or as a mere recovery mechanism under Section 65, the Tribunal admitted the Section 7 application, initiated CIRP against J.U.D. Cements Limited, ordered the consequential moratorium under Section 14 and appointed Mr. Avishek Gupta as the Interim Resolution Professional. Cases Discussed 1. Vistra ITCL (India) Ltd. v. FINDINGS AND ANALYSIS Issue No. 1: Whether the Petitioner has locus to maintain the present application and whether the Debenture Holders are necessary parties? 12. The present application has been filed by the Petitioner in its capacity as the Debenture Trustee for and on behalf of the Debenture Holders. The Petitioner has placed on record the written authorisations dated 14.05.2026 issued by the Debenture Holders authorising it to take appropriate proceedings against the Corporate Debtor. The Petitioner also relies upon the Debenture Trustee Agreement and the Debenture Trust Deed (“DTD”), under which it is entrusted with the rights and obligations of the Debenture Trustee, including taking necessary steps for protection and enforcement of the interests of the Debenture Holders. 13. The Corporate Debtor does not dispute that the Petitioner is the Debenture Trustee representing the Debenture Holders. In fact, the Corporate Debtor itself refers to the Petitioner acting on behalf of the Debenture Holders under the DTD. The objection is essentially that the individual Debenture Holders have not been impleaded as parties to the present proceedings. 14. The objection, in our considered view, is without merit. A necessary party is one whose presence is indispensable for the Court or Tribunal to effectively and completely adjudicate the matter. In the present case, the Petitioner is acting in its representative capacity as Debenture Trustee. The relief sought is initiation of the Corporate Insolvency Resolution Process against the Corporate Debtor on account of the alleged default in payment of the financial debt. No separate or personal relief is sought against any of the Debenture Holders. 15. The fact that the Corporate Debtor has raised allegations concerning the instructions or actions of the Debenture Holders in relation to the Escrow Account does not make them necessary parties. Those allegations form part of the defence of the Corporate Debtor and can be considered while examining the existence of debt and default. Their individual presence is not necessary for determination of the present application. 16. The objection based on the Petitioner’s control over the Escrow Account also does not affect its locus. Such arrangement arises from the contractual documentsexecuted by the parties themselves. The fact that the Petitioner, in its capacity as Common Security Trustee, had certain rights and responsibilities in relation to the Escrow Account does not take away its separate authority to represent and protect the interests of the Debenture Holders in accordance with the DTD. 17. Accordingly, we find that the Petitioner, acting in its capacity as Debenture Trustee and pursuant to the authority vested in it under the relevant transaction documents and the authorisations placed on record, has locus to maintain the present application. The objection regarding non-joinder of the individual Debenture Holders is therefore rejected. Issue No. 2: Whether the amount of Rs. 155 Crores constitutes a “financial debt” under Section 5(8) of the Code? 18. Section 5(8)(c) expressly includes amounts raised through the issue of debentures. The Rs 155 Crores was raised through Non-Convertible Debentures carrying Scheduled Coupon, Upfront Coupon and Redemption Premium. The transaction is therefore against consideration for the time value of money. 19. The Corporate Debtor contends that there was no disbursement to it, since the amount was credited to an Escrow Account controlled by the Petitioner. 20. The amount was credited on 02.01.2025 to an account in the Corporate Debtor’s own name, “J.U.D. Cements Limited Escrow Account”. Clause 3 of the Escrow Agreement, as quoted in the affidavit, records that “the Company has duly opened” the account. The DTD earmarked the proceeds for the Corporate Debtor’s own Purposes including repayment of its pressing liabilities, working capital, capital expenditure, and an inter-corporate deposit to its associate JSB. 21. The Corporate Debtor itself states that the fixed deposits created from the account “stood in the name of JUD Cements”, and it claims credit for the interest earned on them and for sums appropriated by the Petitioner towards the debenture liability. It also admits payment of Rs. 5,93,60,458/- towards coupon, which presupposes an advance on which the coupon runs. The Petitioner further relies on the Corporate Debtor’s Annual Report for FY 2024-25 recording the NCD liability. The Balance Sheets acknowledged by the CD (Page 634 to the petition) constitutes ipso facto acknowledgement of debt. 22. A restriction on when and how borrowed funds may be used is a common feature of structured lending. It regulates utilization and does not by itself negate the borrowing. Clause 11.5.4 of the DTD, quoted in the affidavit, provides that the Initial Subscription Amount is to be utilized on fulfilment of the Conditions Precedent in Part A of Schedule IV. 23. Treated as a whole, the subscription against the instrument, the credit to an account in the borrower’s own name under an arrangement the borrower signed, the utilization of such proceeds and the earmarking for its own purposes have the commercial effect of borrowing. 24. Reliance is also placed upon the judgment of the Hon’ble NCLAT in Vistra ITCL (India) Ltd. v. Vithal Madhukar Dahake (RP) and Ors.1, wherein it was held that -: “…53. Thus, we need to deliberate the issue whether the direct disbursement to the Corporate Debtor is sine qua non under section 5 (8) of the Code. We find that Disbursement refers to the actual release or payment of money pursuant to a financial arrangement. Disbursement indicate the outflow of funds by a financial creditor as part of financial arrangements. It implies that money has been advanced or made available with an expectation of repayment. Disbursement therefore is a foundational element in determining whether a transaction qualifies as “financial debt.” However, the pivotal issue is whether such disbursement need to be directly made only to Corporate debtor or it can be otherwise also. Section 5(8) of the Code defines “financial debt” as: “A debt along with interest, if any, which is disbursed against the consideration for the time value of money” We carefully observe that the Section does not use the word “to the Corporate Debtor” after word “disbursed”. From the statutory language, the essential ingredients of financial debt are Existence of a debt, Disbursement of money, Consideration for time value of money and Commercial effect of borrowing… 57 Thus, we hold that direct disbursement of the Corporate Debtor is not sine qua non. We therefore do not agree to the arguments of the Respondent No.1 and Respondent No.3 on this point and also find that Adjudicating Authority erred on this issue in the Impugned Order.” 25. Therefore, we are of the considered opinion that the Rs. 155 Crores constitute a financial debt within the meaning of Section 5(8)(c) of the Code. Issue No. 3: Whether the Corporate Debtor committed a default and whether the Escrow arrangement absolves it of such default? 26. The Corporate Debtor’s principal contention is that sufficient funds were available in the Escrow Account to meet the coupon, but the Petitioner, having control of the account, did not release them. It is not disputed that the Scheduled Coupon falling due on 31.01.2025 was not paid on that date. The Corporate Debtor’s defence is not that the amount was paid, but that sufficient funds were available in the Escrow Account and that the Petitioner, having exclusive control over the account, did not apply those funds towards the coupon. 27. The issue, therefore, is whether such contractual control over the Escrow Account shifted or suspended the Corporate Debtor’s primary obligation to pay the amount on the Due Date. The Corporate Debtor itself states that the monies needed to discharge the instalment were present in the Escrow Account and “were not applied” (para 31 of the reply). 28. Clause 4.1 of the DTD places the contractual obligation to discharge the Outstanding Amounts upon the Corporate Debtor on the respective Due Dates. The Escrow provisions regulate the custody, operation and application of the monies forming part of the Escrow Account; they do not, on the material placed before us, substitute the Trustee as the primary obligor for payment of the amounts contractually payable by the Corporate Debtor. 29. The Corporate Debtor relies upon Clause 14.1(i), under which the Trustee may utilise monies lying in the Escrow Account towards the Outstanding Amounts. However, the said provision is contained in the enforcement mechanism following the occurrence and continuance of an Event of Default. It cannot, therefore, be construed as a condition precedent to the Corporate Debtor’s obligation to make payment on the Due Date. The existence of a contractual power in the Trustee to appropriate monies after an Event of Default does not, by itself, transfer the primary payment obligation from the Corporate Debtor to the Trustee. 30. Even assuming that the Escrow Agreement does not prescribe a detailed waterfall or order of application, the Corporate Debtor has not identified any provision which expressly suspends the obligation to pay the Scheduled Coupon merely because the funds required for such payment were lying in the Escrow Account. The contractual mechanism for operation of the account cannot, without more, be construed as extinguishing or postponing the payment obligation under Clause 4.1. 31. Further, the Corporate Debtor had pleaded that the Escrow Agreement prescribes no waterfall and no order of application (para 14), yet also pleads that the instalment was payable in accordance with the waterfall and the Petitioner’s Written Instructions (para 111). Read together, the grievance is that the Petitioner alone controlled application of the funds. Even so, no provision has been identified that suspends the coupon obligation. 32. On the material cited in the affidavit (Annexures R-5 to R-9), the correspondence concerns salaries, labour, civil and equipment payments, electricity and bags. It does not show that the Corporate Debtor asked for release of escrow funds for the coupon and was refused. The Corporate Debtor’s own message of 17.10.2025 states that it had “to start all payments from sbi” (para 32(d) of the reply). 33. The subsequent appropriations from the Escrow Account on 17.10.2025, 01.12.2025, 31.12.2025 and 03.01.2026, totalling Rs. 5,64,16,179/-, constitute payments towards the outstanding liability. They do not retrospectively extinguish the factum of the default which had already occurred on 31.01.2025, although such payments must necessarily be given due credit while determining the amount outstanding. 34. The Scheduled Coupon fell due on 31.01.2025 and was not paid. On the Corporate Debtor’s own computation, that instalment was of the order of Rs. 1,29,00,000/-(para 30), which exceeds the threshold under Section 4. The Upfront Coupon was due from 31.03.2025 in six monthly instalments of Rs. 60 lakhs each, according to the Petitioner. 35. The allegations of delayed release of operational funds, stoppage of work and business losses may found separate contractual claims. However, they do not, on this record, extinguish the obligation to pay amounts that had fallen due. 36. Accordingly, default in payment of the Scheduled Coupon is established. The Corporate Debtor’s inability to operate the Escrow Account does not, on the contractual terms and material placed before us, absolve it of the payment obligation. Issue No. 4: What is the effect of the letter dated 21.08.2025 and the Deed of Undertaking dated 28.10.2025? 37. The Corporate Debtor does not dispute execution of the letter and the Deed. It says they were executed when operations were paralysed, to obtain release of its own funds (para 34 of the reply). On the Petitioner’s description, the Deed records the occurrence and continuance of an Event of Default and acknowledges the rights of the Secured Parties. 38. The Corporate Debtor has pleaded no coercion, undue influence or duress, and has taken no step to avoid the documents. Commercial pressure arising from a financing arrangement the Corporate Debtor itself agreed to is not, without more, a ground to invalidate an acknowledgment. Its explanation goes to motive of such contracts at most and does not negate the acknowledgment. 39. The documents therefore corroborate the subsisting liability and default. Issue No. 5: Whether the date of default affects maintainability of the application? 40. The Corporate Debtor contends that 31.01.2025 cannot be the date of default for the entire principal. To this extent the contention is correct. 41. Under Clause 13.1.1 of the DTD, an Event of Default arises upon non-payment of amounts falling due on the relevant Due Date. The principal became payable on maturity or valid acceleration under Clause 14. The Petitioner relies on the Recall Notice of 05.03.2026, the seven-day period expiring on 12.03.2026. The date of default for the Scheduled Coupon is 31.01.2025, and for the principal it is 12.03.2026. 42. The distinction does not affect maintainability of the present application, since the default in payment of the Scheduled Coupon itself exceeds the statutory threshold. The fact that the entire principal became payable only upon valid acceleration does not erase the earlier default in respect of the coupon. 43. Section 7 also does not require a demand notice or default notice as a precondition to filing. The Corporate Debtor accepts that the Recall Notice itself invoked Clause 14. Its contention that the notice was timed to coincide with the start of production goes to motive, which is not relevant once default is shown. Issue No. 6: Whether the record of default relied upon by the Petitioner is sufficient? 44. The Petitioner relies on the NeSL record of 27.04.2026 (Form D). The Corporate Debtor disputes its authentication under Regulation 21 and service of Form C. 45. Upon perusal, the status of the record of default highlights “ Deemed to be authenticated” against the total outstanding amount of Rs. 190,86,49,811.00 with the date of default being 31.01.2025 against the Corporate Debtor by the Financial Creditor. 46. The record of default is not the sole means of establishing default, and Section 7(3)(a) permits other evidence. On the Petitioner’s description, The Deed of Undertaking, Recall Notice, payment history and the Corporate Debtor’s own pleadings provide independent material regarding the subsistence of the liability and occurrence of default. The Annual Report additionally corroborates the existence of the NCD liability and the Corporate Debtor’s own statements at paras 31 and 38 are consistent with default. 47. Moreover, the Hon’ble NCLAT in the matter of Vijay Kumar Singhania v. Bank of Baroda2 [vide Judgment dated 13th December, 2023] has held that furnishing the record of default is not mandatory even after amendment of Regulation 20 by insertion of Regulation 20(1A) of the IBBI (Information Utilities) Regulations, 2017 w.e.f. 14.06.2022. 48. The relevant paragraph from the Vijay Kumar Singhania (supra) is reproduced herein below: “29. From the above examination of statutory scheme, Rules and Regulations, it is clear that Regulation 20(1A) cannot be read to mean that after the said amendment brought in regulation w.e.f 14.06.2022 an application filed under Section 7 which is not supported by information of default from an information utility is to be rejected and if the Financial Creditor has filed other evidence to prove default which is contemplated by the Insolvency and Bankruptcy (Application to Adjudicating Authority) Rules, 2016 and the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Board of India (Insolvency Resolution Process for Corporate Persons) Regulations, 2016, the said application has not to be considered. We, thus, are of the considered view that even after amendment of Regulation 20 by insertion of Regulation 20(1A) w.e.f 14.06.2022, Financial Creditor is entitled to file evidence of record of default as contemplated by Regulation 2A of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Board of India (Insolvency Resolution Process for Corporate Persons) Regulations, 2016 r/w Rule 4 of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy (Application to Adjudicating Authority) Rules, 2016. We, thus, do not find any substance in the submission of the Appellant that since Financial Creditor has not filed the record of default from an information utility, Section 7 deserves to be rejected.” 49.Therefore, in view of the aforesaid judgment, we cannot dismiss the instant application u/s 7(5) of the Code on the ground that the record of default from the Information Utility i.e. NeSL, as disputed by the CD as incorrect, especially when the existence of debt and the factum of default committed by the Corporate Debtor in repayment of financial debt to the Financial Creditor have been satisfactorily established from other evidences. 50. Accordingly, the objection to the correctness or completeness of the Information Utility record, by itself, does not warrant rejection of the application. Issue No. 7: Whether the discrepancies in computation of the claim affect admission? 51. The Corporate Debtor points to the following: 1. The Petition claims Rs. 1,96,69,05,415/-, while Form D records Rs. 1,90,86,49,811/-, a difference of Rs. 5,82,55,604/-. 2. Redemption Premium is included in a claim stated as on 30.04.2026, though under the DTD it commenced only on 30.06.2026. 3. No credit appears for Rs. 5,64,16,179/- appropriated from the Escrow Account. 4. No credit appears for Rs. 2,76,27,995.01 earned as interest on the fixed deposits. 52. These discrepancies require reconciliation and verification in accordance with the contractual documents and the applicable provisions of the Code and Regulations. However, they do not warrant rejection of the application where the existence of the financial debt and a default exceeding the statutory threshold are independently established, notwithstanding the disputed components of the computation. Issue No. 8: Whether the application has been filed as a recovery mechanism or attracts Section 65 of the Code? 53. The Corporate Debtor alleges that the proceedings are coercive, having regard to the Petitioner’s control of the Escrow Account, the alleged withholding of operational funds, and acceleration in the month production began. 54. Section 65 requires material showing that the proceedings were initiated fraudulently or maliciously, or for a purpose other than resolution of insolvency. The Code is not a mere recovery mechanism, but a financial creditor that establishes a debt and default is entitled to invoke it . 55. The allegations concerning release of funds, approvals, monitoring arrangements and business losses are contractual disputes. The Corporate Debtor has not placed sufficient material to establish that the present application was initiated fraudulently or maliciously, or for a purpose other than resolution of insolvency or liquidation. 56. Therefore, no case under Section 65 is made out. The Corporate Debtor’s liberty to apply separately is noted, without any expression of view. Issue No. 9: Whether the solvency, security cover and going-concern status of the Corporate Debtor constitute grounds for rejecting the application? 57. The Corporate Debtor has further relied upon its valuation report dated 08.08.2026 to contend that its assets have substantial value and that the security available to the Financial Creditors is more than enough. It has also submitted that the Corporate Debtor continues to operate and has referred to the communication from UltraTech Cement Limited dated 28.07.2026 as indicative marker of its possible revival. 58. These circumstances, even if taken at their highest, do not displace the statutory requirements for admission of an application under Section 7 of the Code. The question at the admission stage is whether a financial debt is due and whether a default in respect thereof has occurred. The existence of valuable assets, security, or a going concern status does not, by itself, extinguish an admitted financial liability or a default in payment. 59. In the present case, as already held above, the Petitioner has established the existence of the financial debt and the occurrence of default in payment of the Scheduled Coupon. The subsequent payments or appropriations made from the escrow account do not retrospectively cure the default which had already occurred on the due date. 60. The valuation report and the alleged future business prospects of the Corporate Debtor are matters which may be relevant in the course of the insolvency resolution process. They cannot, in the absence of any statutory ground for rejection, constitute a defence to an otherwise maintainable application under Section 7. Likewise, the communication from UltraTech Cements as relied upon by the Corporate Debtor does not at all establish a binding arrangement or a certainty of revival which is sufficient to negate the established default. 61. It is also relevant to state that the present application was filed online on 29.05.2026, after the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (Amendment) Act, 20263 had come into force with effect from 26.05.2026. The amended statutory framework therefore governs the present application. Therefore, the admission of the application has to be determined in accordance with the amended Section 7(5) and the satisfaction of its mandated requirements, and not by undertaking a venture assessment of the commercial solvency or viability of the Corporate Debtor. 62. Therefore, in the absence of any finding that the present proceedings have been initiated fraudulently, maliciously or for a purpose other than the resolution of insolvency or liquidation, the existence of the Corporate Debtor’s valuable assets, security cover or continuing business operations cannot warrant rejection of this present application. 63. Accordingly, the objections founded upon the valuation of the assets, security cover, continuing operations and proposed business revival coupled with the solvency, security cover and going-concern status do not constitute a ground to reject the present application. 64. Furthermore, as for the objections regarding the proposed IRP, the consent of the proposed Interim Resolution Professional in the prescribed form, together with the requisite declarations as to eligibility and pending disciplinary proceedings, is annexed with the application. The application is accordingly complete in this regard and any objections in this regard is accordingly held to be devoid of merits. 65. Before proceeding to the final conclusion, we deem it appropriate to consider the judicial authorities relied upon by the Corporate Debtor in support of its objections. Upon careful consideration of the said authorities in the factual and contractual context of the present case, we find that none of them warrants rejection of the present application. 66. The Corporate Debtor has placed reliance upon the judgement of Pioneer Urban Land and Infrastructure Ltd. v. Union of India4, particularly paragraphs 70 and 71, to advance its contention that there can be no financial debt unless there has been a disbursement against the time value of money. There is no question in regard to the proposition laid down therein. However, the facts in the present case are materially different. The sum of 155 Crores was provided by the Debenture Holders pursuant to the transaction documents and was credited on 02.01.2025 to the Escrow Account duly maintained in the name of the Corporate Debtor. The subsequent placement of substantial amounts in fixed deposits pursuant to the agreed escrow mechanism does not, by itself, efface the underlying disbursement. The Corporate Debtor itself relies upon the interest earned on such deposits and has acknowledged payments towards coupon. The requirement of disbursement against consideration for the time value of money, therefore, stands satisfied in the facts of the present case. The reliance on Pioneer Urban is accordingly misplaced. 67. The Corporate Debtor has also relied upon Park Energy Pvt. Ltd. v. Syndicate Bank5, particularly paragraph 29, to contend that no default can be attributed to the Corporate Debtor where funds were available in a Trust Retention Account but their release was regulated by the contractual arrangement. The said decision turned upon the particular facts of that case, where the Corporate Debtor had performed its part of the contract and the failure to release the funds by the Lenders. However, the facts presented before us are materially distinct. In the present case, Clause 4.1 of the Debenture Trust Deed places the obligation to make payment of the amounts due upon the Corporate Debtor on the respective Due Dates. And no material establishes that the Corporate Debtor had itself tendered the Scheduled Coupon due on 31.01.2025 and that such payment was prevented solely on account of any act or omission of the Financial Creditor. The mere existence of funds in the Escrow Account, does not absolve the Corporate Debtor of its primary contractual obligation to discharge the amount falling due on the agreed date. The ratio laid down in Park Energy6 is consequently not attracted to the facts of this present case. 68. Reliance has also been placed upon Dheeraj Wadhawan v. Yes Bank Ltd.7, where the finding of absence of default was founded upon the fact that a specific amount was liable to be reversed or adjusted by the bank and that the alleged default was attributable to the bank’s own conduct. No such factual circumstance has been established in the present case. The subsequent appropriations made from the Escrow Account towards the outstanding liability do not establish that the Scheduled Coupon due on 31.01.2025 had been paid on that date. At the same time, the observation in Dheeraj Wadhawan8 that the record maintained by an Information Utility is not conclusive proof of default is a settled proposition (para 31 of the Judgement). However, the present finding of default is not founded solely upon the NeSL record, but upon the contractual documents, the Scheduled Coupon falling due on 31.01.2025, the subsequent correspondence and the other material placed on record. The disputed entries in the Information Utility record, therefore, do not by themselves displace the finding of default independently established from the record. 69. The Corporate Debtor has further relied upon Siddharth Satish Katariya v. Central Bank of India9, particularly paragraphs 136, 138 and 141 to 145. The said decision concerns a situation where a creditor sought to sustain admission by relying, at a later stage, upon a materially different and previously unpleaded basis or date of default. The principle is not attracted in the present case. The present application itself pleads the default commencing from 31.01.2025, when the Scheduled Coupon fell due and remained unpaid coupled with the fact that the said coupon by itself crosses the threshold amount. The subsequent Recall Notice dated 05.03.2026 and the acceleration of the outstanding amounts constitute contractual consequences of the continuing default and do not substitute the original basis of default. The distinction between the Scheduled Coupon default and the subsequent acceleration of the principal liability has already been considered by us while determining the relevant issues. Accordingly, the judgment in Siddharth Satish Katariya‘0 does not render the present application unsustainable. 70. The placed reliance by the CD upon Vidarbha Industries Power Ltd. v. Axis Bank Ltd”., contending that this Tribunal has discretion to decline admission even on the face of an existing financial debt and default, since the assets of the Corporate Debtor are stated to be substantially higher than the amount claimed by the Petitioner. The said contention cannot be accepted in the facts of the present case. The decision in Vidarbha judgement‘2 was rendered in the context of the statutory language contained in pre-amendment Section 7(5)(a) and the peculiar circumstances before the Hon’ble Supreme Court, that included an existence of a substantial realisable award in favour of the Corporate Debtor. The mere fact that a Corporate Debtor possesses assets or that the value of its assets may exceed the amount of the financial debt does not absolve the debt or negate an established default. The reliance on Vidarbha‘3 is, therefore, distinguishable on facts and does not constitute a ground for rejection of the present application. 71. The Corporate Debtor has further relied upon the order of the Hon’ble NCLT, Kolkata Bench in Central Bank of India v. Simplex Infrastructure Limited‘4, in support of the proposition that admission under Section 7 is not to be treated as an automatic consequence in every case where financial debt and default are alleged. The said decision was rendered in its own factual context and does not lay down a general proposition that the existence of substantial assets or continuing business operations constitutes a valid ground to reject a Section 7 application where financial debt and default are otherwise established. In the present case, having independently considered the existence of the financial debt, the contractual obligations and the occurrence of default, we find no sufficient reason to decline admission on that basis. 72. Thus, the authorities relied upon by the Corporate Debtor, while laying down the respective principles contained therein, are distinguishable on the facts and contractual framework of the present case. None of the said authorities displaces our findings regarding the existence of financial debt and the occurrence of default. The objections founded upon the aforesaid judgments, therefore, do not warrant rejection of the present application. 73. The Petitioner, therefore, has locus in this present petition. The Rs. 155 Crores is a financial debt disbursed against the time-value of money with an arrangement agreed upon by both the Corporate Debtor and the Financial Creditor. The Default in the Scheduled Coupon is established, and the Escrow arrangement does not absolve the Corporate Debtor. Accordingly, this Tribunal is satisfied that the Application is liable to be admitted under Section 7 of the Code. ORDER 74.

Summary : From 1 November 2026, a new GST treatment applies to specified delivery services supplied through an electronic commerce operator. The operative design has two connected parts. First, the notified rate for covered delivery services relating to goods supplied or ordered through an ECO is 5% without input tax credit, subject to the statutory wording and exclusions. Second, section 9(5) shifts the tax liability to the ECO where the delivery service provider is not liable to registration under section 22(1). Where the provider is liable to registration, the provider remains liable to pay GST at the notified rate. Postal and courier services are outside the new section 9(5) delivery-services clause, and goods transport agency services require separate analysis. This guide converts the notification and official FAQs into an operational framework for onboarding, tax determination, invoicing, return mapping and evidence. It emphasises that an app label such as “delivery partner” is not conclusive: the actual service, registration liability, contractual chain and GST status must be verified transaction by transaction. Key Takeaways Show all 20 sections Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); The 1 November 2026 Change Has a Rate Rule and a Liability Rule The Central Government issued Notification No. 09/2026-Central Tax (Rate) to revise the services on which an electronic commerce operator may be required to pay tax under section 9(5) of the Central Goods and Services Tax Act, 2017. The amendment is effective from 1 November 2026. The new clause covers services by way of delivery of goods through an ECO, other than services by the Department of Posts and a courier agency, where the person supplying the delivery service is not liable to registration under section 22(1). The statutory condition is important: the ECO does not become liable merely because a delivery order passes through its platform. Notification No. 06/2026-Central Tax (Rate), read with the official GST reform FAQs, provides the 5% without-ITC rate for the covered delivery services. The official FAQ explains the division of responsibility. If the delivery service provider is not liable to register, the ECO pays GST under section 9(5). If the provider is liable to register, the provider pays GST at 5% without input tax credit. This is a supplier-liability test, not a choice between the platform and the service provider. A local rider transporting a meal, grocery item or retail parcel from the seller to the customer may supply a delivery service contemplated by the new entry. A courier agency ordinarily undertakes carriage of time-sensitive documents, goods or articles under the relevant service description and remains expressly excluded from the new section 9(5) clause. A goods transport agency is identified through the statutory and rate-framework characteristics applicable to GTA services, including issuance of a consignment note. The new rule should not be used to relabel courier or GTA supplies merely to obtain a different liability result. Identify the Service Supplier in the Contract Chain The seller of goods, the ECO, a fleet operator and an individual rider may all appear in one commercial flow. Finance teams must identify who contracts to provide delivery, who receives the consideration, whose invoice or document represents the service, and whether a subcontractor merely performs the physical movement. The registration test applies to the supplier of the notified delivery service. If a registered fleet company supplies delivery to the ECO and employs or engages riders, the riders’ personal registration position does not by itself transfer the fleet company’s liability to the ECO. Build a Defensible Section 22 Registration Test The ECO needs a repeatable method to determine whether the delivery service provider is liable to registration under section 22(1). That inquiry normally considers aggregate turnover, the State or Union territory from which the supply is made, the applicable threshold, and whether another compulsory-registration provision affects the supplier. A declaration from the provider is useful but should not be the only control where platform data shows turnover or status inconsistent with the declaration. GSTIN validation, PAN-level aggregation within platform records and periodic refreshes can reduce misclassification. The phrase “not liable for registration” is different from “not registered.” A person may hold a voluntary registration despite turnover below the section 22 threshold, or may be liable under another provision. Conversely, a provider without a GSTIN may already have crossed the threshold. The tax engine should not treat a blank GSTIN field as conclusive. It should retain the provider’s declaration, registration search result, turnover data available to the ECO, effective dates and the reason for the selected liability path. Customer-facing documentation should not obscure the supplier or tax liability for each component. The system should separately identify the goods value, delivery consideration, convenience or platform fee, discounts and taxes. A single commercial receipt may display several components, but the accounting and GST ledgers must preserve the legal nature and responsible supplier for each amount. Map Section 9(5) Tax to the ECO’s Return Process Where the ECO is liable under section 9(5), the tax should flow to the ECO’s section 9(5) liability records and return controls, not to the seller’s output tax merely because the original goods order belongs to that seller. Reconcile the order database, completed-delivery events, cancellations, refunds, promotional subsidies and accounting entries to the GST return data. The control should identify supplies crossing a tax period, failed deliveries and post-period credit adjustments. The 5% without-ITC condition also requires the appropriate restriction of credit attributable to the notified supply; general overhead and common-credit consequences should be analysed under the applicable credit rules rather than assumed away. Where the provider is liable to registration, the provider is responsible for its tax invoice and payment. The ECO should validate the GSTIN, retain invoice data and prevent the same delivery from also being included in the ECO-paid section 9(5) population. A change in status during a month should be applied from the legally supportable effective date, with a documented cutover and exception report. Month-End Reconciliation for Delivery Transactions Reconcile accepted orders to completed, cancelled, returned and failed deliveries by tax period. Match delivery consideration in customer records, provider settlements and the general ledger. Split ECO-liable and provider-liable populations using effective-dated registration evidence. Review courier, postal and GTA exclusions through a classification exception report. Test refunds and incentives to determine whether they alter consideration or create a separate supply. Tie section 9(5) liability to return workings, payment records and retained transaction-level evidence. Common Failure Modes After the Effective Date The most serious error is applying one rule to every delivery arranged through a platform. Other risks include treating every unregistered profile as section 22-exempt, overlooking turnover across multiple platform accounts, charging the ECO and provider for the same service, applying the delivery entry to courier or GTA services, and claiming credit despite the without-ITC condition. Another recurring weakness is leaving status changes to a manual spreadsheet after the tax engine has already finalised the period. An effective control environment keeps classification and registration data effective-dated and links each override to reviewer approval. Frequently Asked Questions When did the new ECO delivery-service treatment start? The notified changes are effective from 1 November 2026. Transactions around the cutover should be mapped using the applicable time-of-supply and completion facts. Does the ECO pay GST for every delivery arranged on its platform? No. Under the new section 9(5) clause, the ECO is liable for the covered delivery service when the service provider is not liable to registration under section 22(1), subject to the notification wording. What if the delivery provider is liable to GST registration? The official FAQ states that the provider pays GST at 5% without ITC for the covered service. The ECO should prevent duplicate section 9(5) reporting. Are courier services covered by the new section 9(5) clause? No. Services by the Department of Posts and a courier agency are expressly excluded from the new delivery-services clause. Does the rule cover goods transport agency services? The official FAQ distinguishes GTA services and states that the ECO is not liable under the new delivery clause for delivery services supplied by a GTA. The GTA framework must be applied separately. Is a missing GSTIN enough to treat a provider as not liable to register? No. Registration liability depends on the law and facts. The ECO should retain a declaration, validation and available turnover or status evidence. Can the ECO claim input tax credit against the 5% delivery-service output? The notified rate is described as 5% without ITC for the covered service. Credit attribution and reversal should be analysed and documented under the applicable GST credit rules. What is the most important month-end control? Reconcile every completed delivery to the supplier identity, effective registration status, classification, liability owner, consideration and GST return treatment. Key Takeaways The operative change applies from 1 November 2026 and combines a 5% without-ITC rate with a conditional section 9(5) liability shift. The ECO pays only where the covered delivery service provider is not liable under section 22(1). Postal, courier and GTA services require separate classification and are not absorbed into the new clause. Effective-dated supplier status, invoice separation and transaction-level reconciliation are essential.

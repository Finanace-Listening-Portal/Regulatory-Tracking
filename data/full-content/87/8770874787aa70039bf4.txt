Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); In Re Orient Cement Limited (GST AAAR Telangana) Summary: The Telangana State Appellate Authority for Advance Ruling (AAAR), in the case of Orient Cement Limited, upheld the advance ruling that distribution of gold coins and white goods to cement dealers under promotional incentive schemes constitutes a taxable supply under the GST law. The appellate authority rejected the company’s contention that these goods were merely discounts or incentives connected with cement sales and therefore did not constitute independent supplies. It further held that the value of the promotional goods must be determined separately under the applicable valuation provisions and that input tax credit (ITC) on their procurement was blocked under Section 17(5)(h) of the Central Goods and Services Tax Act, 2017. The appeal was rejected by order dated 10 November 2026. Orient Cement Limited manufactures and sells Ordinary Portland Cement (OPC) and Pozzolana Portland Cement (PPC). Its business model involves marketing and distributing cement through a dealer network. To encourage dealers to achieve higher sales targets, the company operates promotional schemes, including the Dealer White Goods Scheme and the Monthly/Quarterly Quantity Discount Scheme. Under these arrangements, dealers who achieve prescribed purchase targets become eligible for specified rewards, including gold coins and white goods. The company maintained that these schemes were intended to promote cement sales, strengthen dealer relationships and increase its market share. Under the Monthly/Quarterly Quantity Discount Scheme, eligible dealers were entitled to receive benefits linked to the quantity of cement purchased during the relevant period. The company explained that certain benefits were credited to dealers through credit notes, while gold coins were distributed to qualifying dealers under the promotional arrangements. According to the applicant, the gold coins and white goods represented commercial incentives or discounts associated with cement purchases and were not independent transactions involving consideration for those goods. The company originally approached the Telangana State Authority for Advance Ruling seeking clarification on three questions. First, whether distribution of gold coins and white goods to dealers achieving stipulated purchase targets would constitute goods disposed of by way of gift, thereby attracting the restriction on ITC under Section 17(5)(h). Second, whether the distribution constituted a permanent transfer or disposal of business assets where ITC had been availed, attracting Schedule I of the CGST Act even without consideration. Third, whether the distribution would constitute a supply under Section 7 of the CGST Act. The original Authority for Advance Ruling, by Order No. 20/2023 dated 30 September 2023, held that the distribution of gold coins and white goods was taxable as a supply of goods and that ITC was restricted under Section 17(5)(h). It also held that the transaction constituted a supply of goods rather than a permanent transfer or disposal of business assets. Aggrieved by these findings, Orient Cement filed an appeal under Section 100 of the CGST Act and the corresponding Telangana GST provisions. Before the appellate authority, Orient Cement argued that the gold coins and white goods were provided as part of a contractual promotional arrangement and were linked to purchases of cement. It submitted that dealers were required to achieve specified purchase targets and that the incentive goods formed part of the commercial bargain. According to the company, there was no separate consideration for the promotional goods and therefore no independent supply. The company further contended that the incentives could be regarded as discounts associated with cement sales rather than gifts. On the issue of input tax credit, the appellant submitted that the promotional goods were procured for use in the course or furtherance of business and that the general entitlement under Section 16(1) of the CGST Act should apply. It argued that the restriction under Section 17(5)(h), relating to goods disposed of by way of gift or free samples, was inapplicable because the goods were distributed pursuant to commercial arrangements intended to promote sales. The appellant also maintained that these goods did not constitute business assets permanently transferred or disposed of within the meaning of Schedule I. The appellate authority examined the substance of the promotional schemes and observed that dealers paid consideration for cement, whereas the entitlement to gold coins and white goods arose only upon achieving specified targets. The goods were distributed under a separate incentive arrangement. The authority concluded that the existence of commercial incentives and target-based eligibility did not prevent the distribution from constituting a supply under Section 7(1)(a) read with Section 2(31) of the CGST Act. It held that the promotional goods were supplied for consideration in the course or furtherance of business. On valuation, the appellate authority rejected the contention that the value of gold coins and white goods was already included in the price of cement or represented a permissible discount under Section 15(3). It observed that the statutory requirements for excluding discounts from the value of supply had not been satisfied. In particular, the promotional arrangements did not establish the necessary invoice-linked discount treatment. The authority further noted that gold coins and white goods had no separately ascertainable selling price charged to dealers. Accordingly, their value was required to be determined under Section 15(4) read with Rule 30 of the CGST Rules, 2017, on the basis of 110% of the cost of production, manufacture or acquisition, as applicable. Addressing Schedule I, the appellate authority held that the promotional goods did not constitute a permanent transfer or disposal of business assets for the purposes of Entry 1 of that Schedule. It distinguished between assets of the business and goods procured specifically for distribution under promotional schemes. Nevertheless, the authority held that the transaction remained taxable under Section 7(1)(a), because the goods were supplied for consideration in the course or furtherance of business. Thus, rejection of the Schedule I classification did not remove the independent GST liability arising from the transaction. Regarding ITC, the appellate authority examined Section 17(5)(h), which restricts credit on goods lost, stolen, destroyed, written off or disposed of by way of gift or free samples. It concluded that the gold coins and white goods were distributed as gifts or promotional incentives under the relevant schemes and that the statutory restriction applied. The authority rejected the appellant’s attempt to claim ITC on the basis that the expenditure promoted cement sales. It also distinguished the eligibility of input tax credit on procurement from the taxability of the outward supply, observing that the two questions were governed by separate statutory provisions. The appellate authority finally recorded that the gold coins and white goods distributed to dealers achieving stipulated purchase targets constituted taxable supplies under Section 7(1)(a) read with Section 2(31) of the CGST Act. Their value was required to be determined under Section 15(4) read with Rule 30, and the transaction could not be treated as a permissible discount under Section 15(3). The authority further held that the distribution did not amount to a permanent transfer or disposal of business assets under Schedule I, but that ITC on procurement of the promotional goods was blocked under Section 17(5)(h). Finding no reason to interfere with the original advance ruling, the Telangana AAAR rejected the appeal and upheld the ruling of the lower authority. That it is evident that the legislature has deliberately used the term “goods” under entry 2 & 3 of the schedule- I with the intent to tax all categories of goods. That entry 1 of Schedule I is applicable on “permanent transfer or disposal of business assets”. If the intention of the legislature was to tax all types of goods including the capital goods under the aforesaid entry 1, then the legislature would have used the “goods” which are available under entry 2 and 3. However, the legislature intentionally used the term “business asset” and therefore, it is evident that the intention of the legislature was to restrict the scope of said entry to the business asset which is an item of balance sheet. Therefore, it is abundantly clear that the intention of the legislature is to apply the entry 1 of the schedule I only to the business assets which are item of the balance sheet and not to all the goods. xvi. GROUNDS OF APPEAL A. There is no consideration in return for distribution of gold coins and white goods 1. The Authority has held that distribution of gold coins and white goods is a supply under Section 7(1) of the CGST Act, 2017, on the footing that achievement of marketing targets is an inducement from the dealers, or in other words non-monetary consideration for those goods. 2. The Authority has ignored that the dealers purchase cement from the appellant and that the consideration paid by them is strictly towards those purchases in the ordinary course of business. No additional effort is made by the dealer. The act of achieving a particular level of business is specifically towards purchase of cement. Gold coins and white goods are received in place of a monetary reduction in the value of that supply. The monetary value of such a discount cannot be added to the value of supply in addition to the cement already supplied. 3. Distribution of gold coins and white goods is only a discount on those purchases. The appellant receives nothing in return for those goods, neither in money nor otherwise. The concept of non-monetary consideration therefore does not apply. Section 7 cannot be invoked. 4. The Authority has directed valuation under Section 15 read with Rule 30 of the CGST Rules, 2017. Since the distribution is itself a simple discount, a value of 110% of the cost of procurement cannot be adopted as the value of supply of the gold coins and white goods. B. Any value of supply between unrelated parties is the sole consideration for such supply 1. The relationship with dealers is one of outright sale and purchase. The dealers are not agents. The appellant does not control or advise the dealers on target markets, timing of sales or pricing. The parties are not related persons. Even assuming, without admitting, that distribution of gold coins and white goods is a supply, since the parties are unrelated, the value shall be the transaction value under Section 15(1), namely the price actually paid or payable where the price is the sole consideration. 2. There is nil consideration from the dealers for the gold coins and white goods, and no other consideration, monetary or otherwise. Tax under Section 9(1) is levied only on the value determined under Section 15. That value being nil, tax is nil. 3. The appellant contends that the issue of gold coins and white goods is only a bundled supply along with the cement, and that the cost of those goods is already taken into account while fixing the price of cement, on which tax has been paid, so that a separate levy would amount to double taxation. The contention is not accepted. A composite supply, as defined in Section 2(30), is a supply of two or more taxable supplies which are naturally bundled and supplied in conjunction with each other in the ordinary course of business, one of which is a principal supply. Section 8 treats such a supply as a supply of the principal supply. In the present case the cement is invoiced and delivered first, at its full value. The gold coins or white goods are purchased thereafter and are delivered only if the stipulated lifting is achieved. They are neither supplied in conjunction with the cement nor naturally bundled with it. Section 8 therefore does not apply. The further plea of double taxation is also not a ground known to the Act. Section 9 levies tax on every supply, on the value of that supply determined under Section 15. Cement and gold coins are different goods. Tax discharged on the cement price is tax on the cement. Inclusion of the estimated scheme cost in that price is a matter of pricing. It does not extinguish the subsequent supply of gold coins or white goods, and it does not operate as a set-off against the tax payable on that supply. 4. Rule 30 cannot be applied. Any value adopted between the dealers and the appellant is the value of the supply. 5. The dealers are registered persons. Supplies to them are on a principal-to-principal basis. The transaction concludes when cement is supplied, accepted and paid for. There is no mechanism for return of cement once accepted. Risk and reward pass on delivery. The supply of cement is not merely towards achievement of marketing targets. Applicable GST on that consideration has already been discharged. 6. Consideration for distribution of gold coins and white goods is therefore nil, and tax thereon is nil. Even if the distribution constitutes a supply, no tax is payable. 7. In the absence of consideration, issue of gold coins and white goods fails the test of “supply” and does not qualify as supply under the Act. C. Input tax credit on promotional products should be allowed 1. The query raised was whether issue of gold coins and white goods on achieving the stipulated lifting would be regarded as goods disposed of by way of gift so that credit is restricted under Section 17(5)(h). The Authority has not discussed that query on merits and has merely linked availability of credit with taxability of the outward supply. 2. The submissions were directed to showing why the goods are not gifts. The Authority has instead answered by reference to Question 3 — that the issue is a supply and therefore credit is not restricted. 3. Since the merits of the gift question were not discussed, the appellant reiterates why the issue of gold coins and white goods is not disposal by way of gift. 4. Section 17(5)(h) provides that input tax credit shall not be available in respect of goods lost, stolen, destroyed, written off or disposed of by way of gift or free samples. Credit is not available on goods given as gifts and free samples. 5. Gold coins and white goods are distributed only to the extent of the quantity lifted and the discount the dealer is eligible for. Dealers are thereby motivated to purchase higher quantity. Sales of the company are enhanced. 6. On a plain reading, credit has to be reversed only where goods are disposed of by way of gift or free samples. The ambit of “gift” must therefore be correctly understood. 7. “Gift” is not defined in the Act. Under the Gift-tax Act, 1958, gift means transfer of existing movable or immovable property voluntarily and without consideration in money or money’s worth. The Supreme Court in Sonia Bhatia v. State of U.P. has held that a gift is a voluntary transfer without consideration or compensation; if there is consideration, it is not a gift. In Shakuntala v. State of Haryana it was held that it is of the essence of a gift that it should be without consideration of the nature defined in Section 2(d) of the Contract Act. 8. The Australian High Court in Commissioner of Taxation (Cth) v. McPhail held that to constitute a gift the property must be transferred voluntarily and not as a result of a contractual obligation. 9. To constitute a gift, therefore: (a) the supply must be without contractual obligation; (b) the supply must be without consideration in money or money’s worth. 10. In the present case both conditions fail. The goods are issued only on fulfilment of stipulated lifting under the scheme. The transfer is not voluntary and is not without consideration in the commercial sense of the scheme. Hence the goods are not gifts and credit cannot be restricted under Section 17(5)(h). Prayer of the appellant That the Appellate Authority set aside the impugned ruling to the extent it is adverse to the appellant, hold that distribution of gold coins and white goods is not a taxable supply under Section 7, and allow input tax credit on procurement of those goods. DISCUSSIONS & FINDINGS: This appeal is against TSAAR Order No. 20/2023 dated 30.09.2023 passed under Section 98(4) of the Central Goods and Services Tax Act, 2017 and the corresponding provision of the Telangana Goods and Services Tax Act, 2017. The ruling was communicated to the applicant on 12.10.2023. The appeal was filed on 09.11.2023 in Form GST ARA-02 under Rule 106(1) of the CGST Rules, 2017. 1. The appeal is filed within the prescribed time. The appellant requested a personal hearing which was given on 21.2.2024 which was postponed on the request of the appellant at various times and was heard on 30.09.2026. We have considered the application originally made to the Advance Ruling Authority, the impugned ruling, Annexure A (statement of facts), Annexure B (grounds), Circular No. 92/11/2019-GST dated 07.03.2019, and the findings below are recorded after hearing the appellant. 2. The appellant manufactures Ordinary Portland Cement and Pozzolana Portland Cement and sells the same through dealers. In the normal course of business, the appellant operates a Monthly/Quarterly Quantity Discount Scheme and a Dealer White Goods Scheme. Different quantity slabs are fixed for different markets. When a dealer achieves the prescribed quantity, a credit is accumulated in his account at a specified rate per bag. However, this amount is not deducted from the cement invoice and is not given through a tax invoice or tax credit note relating to the sale of cement. After completion of the scheme period, the appellant purchases gold coins or white goods, such as air-conditioners, microwave ovens, vehicles and similar items, in its own name and avails input tax credit on such purchases. These goods are thereafter handed over to the eligible dealers. The gold coins carry the appellant’s brand. Higher purchases result in higher eligibility under the scheme. Growth in purchases increases the points, whereas de-growth reduces the points. Dealers having outstanding dues for more than ninety days are not eligible under the scheme. The appellant also obtains a declaration from the dealers stating that the gold is offered in proportion to the sales achieved. The appellant has itself stated that it follows this method “instead of adjusting it against the payment to be received from such dealers or issuing them credit notes.” 3. The three questions before the Advance Ruling Authority were: (i) whether issue of gold coins and white goods on achieving the stipulated lifting is “goods disposed of by way of gift” so that input tax credit is restricted under Section 17(5)(h) of the CGST Act, 2017; (ii) whether it is a “permanent transfer or disposal of business assets where ITC has been availed on such assets” and is therefore a supply even without consideration under Sl. No. 1 of Schedule I of the CGST Act, 2017; and (iii) whether it is a supply under Section 7 of the CGST Act, 2017. The Advance Ruling Authority held that the transaction constitutes a supply of goods under Section 7 of the CGST Act, 2017, and that the consideration for such supply is the monetary value attributable to the dealer’s act of attaining the prescribed threshold. It further held that the value of the supply is to be determined in accordance with Section 15 of the CGST Act, 2017 read with Rule 30 of the CGST Rules, 2017, and that the goods supplied cannot be treated as gifts. The Authority also held that input tax credit on the procurement of such goods is admissible. It was further held that the transaction constitutes a supply of goods and cannot be treated merely as a disposal covered under Schedule I of the CGST Act, 2017, since consideration already exists in respect of the transaction. 4. The appellant does not, in substance, contest eligibility of credit per se as the credit was held to be eligible as the outward transaction treated as taxable supply, what it contests is the finding of outward supply. Its grounds, in short, are that the dealer pays only for cement, that attainment of the target is not a separate act given in return for gold, that gold is only the shape of a discount already loaded into the cement price, that consideration for gold is nil, that Section 15(1) therefore yields a nil value because the parties are unrelated, that Rule 30 cannot be used, that Section 7 is not attracted, and that the Authority did not decide the “gift” question on merits and merely linked credit to outward tax. 5. The starting point of the issue is the charging section and the definition of supply. Section 9(1) of the CGST Act, 2017 provides as follows: “Section 9(1) of the CGST Act, 2017 Subject to the provisions of sub-section (2), there shall be levied a tax called the central goods and services tax on all intra-State supplies of goods or services or both, except on the supply of alcoholic liquor for human consumption, on the value determined under section 15 and at such rates, not exceeding twenty per cent., as may be notified by the Government on the recommendations of the Council and collected in such manner as may be prescribed and shall be paid by the taxable person.” Tax is therefore on “supply”, on the value determined under Section 15. Section 7, so far as is relevant, provides as follows: “Section 7 (1) of the CGST Act, 2017 For the purposes of this Act, the expression “supply” includes–– (a) all forms of supply of goods or services or both such as sale, transfer, barter, exchange, licence, rental, lease or disposal made or agreed to be made for a consideration by a person in the course or furtherance of business; (b) import of services for a consideration whether or not in the course or furtherance of business; (c) the activities specified in Schedule I, made or agreed to be made without a consideration; and (d) the activities to be treated as supply of goods or supply of services as referred to in Schedule II. …… (1A) where certain activities or transactions constitute a supply in accordance with the provisions of sub-section (1), they shall be treated either as supply of goods or supply of services as referred to in Schedule II.” Section 7(1)(a) and Section 7(1)(c) operate independently. Clause (a) covers a disposal made for a consideration in the course or furtherance of business. Clause (c) covers an activity specified in Schedule I, even where it is made without consideration. A finding under one clause is not a condition for the other. “Consideration” is defined in Section 2(31) of the CGST Act, 2017 as follows: “Section 2 (31) “consideration” in relation to the supply of goods or services or both includes–– (a) any payment made or to be made, whether in money or otherwise, in respect of, in response to, or for the inducement of, the supply of goods or services or both, whether by the recipient or by any other person but shall not include any subsidy given by the Central Government or a State Government; (b) the monetary value of any act or forbearance, in respect of, in response to, or for the inducement of, the supply of goods or services or both, whether by the recipient or by any other person but shall not include any subsidy given by the Central Government or a State Government; Provided that a deposit given in respect of the supply of goods or services or both shall not be considered as payment made for such supply unless the supplier applies such deposit as consideration for the said supply.” Clause (b) of Section 2(31) is relevant in the present case. It provides that consideration includes the monetary value of any act or forbearance in respect of, in response to, or for the inducement of, the supply of goods or services. Therefore, consideration is not limited to an amount paid in cash for the goods supplied. It can also include the monetary value of an act performed by the recipient in connection with or as an inducement for the supply. In the present case, the scheme is announced before the dealer makes the purchases. The eligibility for the gold coins and white goods is determined on the basis of the quantity purchased, grade and growth achieved by the dealer. A dealer who does not achieve the prescribed target is not eligible for the goods, whereas a dealer who achieves a higher target receives a higher benefit. The declaration obtained from the dealers also records that the gold is offered in proportion to the sales achieved. Thus, the scheme is designed to induce the dealers to attain the prescribed sales threshold. The act of attaining such threshold has a monetary value and constitutes consideration within the meaning of Section 2(31)(b) of the CGST Act, 2017. Once the act of achieving the prescribed target is treated as consideration, the conditions of Section 7(1)(a) of the CGST Act, 2017 are satisfied. The goods are supplied for consideration in the course or furtherance of business. Accordingly, the distribution of gold coins and white goods constitutes a supply of goods. 6. The appellant’s plea is that the dealer pays only for cement and that achieving the target is merely part of that purchase of cement, not a separate act performed in return for the gold coins or white goods. That submission ignores the manner in which the appellant itself structured the transaction. The cement invoice is raised and paid for at its full value and GST is discharged on such value. The appellant does not issue any credit note under Section 34 of the CGST Act, 2017 in respect of the cement invoice. Section 34(1) and (2), so far as relevant, provides as follows:- “Section 34 of the CGST Act, 2017 (credit and debit notes):- (1) Where one or more tax invoices have been issued for supply of any goods or services or both and the taxable value or tax charged in that tax invoice is found to exceed the taxable value or tax payable in respect of such supply, or where the goods supplied are returned by the recipient, or where goods or services or both supplied are found to be deficient, the registered person, who has supplied such goods or services or both, may issue to the recipient one or more credit notes for supplies made in a financial year containing such particulars as may be prescribed. (2) Any registered person who issues a credit note in relation to a supply of goods or services or both shall declare the details of such credit note in the return for the month during which such credit note has been issued but not later than the thirtieth day of November following the end of the financial year in which such supply was made, or the date of furnishing of the relevant annual return, whichever is earlier, and the tax liability shall be adjusted in such manner as may be prescribed: Provided that no reduction in output tax liability of the supplier shall be permitted, if the incidence of tax and interest on such supply has been passed on to any other person.” In the present case, the appellant has consciously not adopted the credit-note mechanism. Instead, after the cement supply is completed, it purchases gold coins and other goods falling under different tariff headings and supplies them to the eligible dealers. Therefore, the transaction involving the gold coins and white goods is a subsequent supply of different goods. It cannot be treated as a revision of the value of the earlier supply of cement. 7. Section 15 of CGST Act, 2017, provides the mechanism for determining the value of a supply reproduced as follows: “Section 15. Value of taxable supply:- (1) The value of a supply of goods or services or both shall be the transaction value, which is the price actually paid or payable for the said supply of goods or services or both where the supplier and the recipient of the supply are not related and the price is the sole consideration for the supply. (2) The value of supply shall include–– (a) any taxes, duties, cesses, fees and charges levied under any law for the time being in force other than this Act, the State Goods and Services Tax Act, the Union Territory Goods and Services Tax Act and the Goods and Services Tax (Compensation to States) Act, if charged separately by the supplier; (b) any amount that the supplier is liable to pay in relation to such supply but which has been incurred by the recipient of the supply and not included in the price actually paid or payable for the goods or services or both; (c) incidental expenses, including commission and packing, charged by the supplier to the recipient of a supply and any amount charged for anything done by the supplier in respect of the supply of goods or services or both at the time of, or before delivery of goods or supply of services; (d) interest or late fee or penalty for delayed payment of any consideration for any supply; and (e) subsidies directly linked to the price excluding subsidies provided by the Central Government and State Governments. Explanation:– For the purposes of this sub-section, the amount of subsidy shall be included in the value of supply of the supplier who receives the subsidy. (3) The value of the supply shall not include any discount which is given–– (a) before or at the time of the supply if such discount has been duly recorded in the invoice issued in respect of such supply; and (b) after the supply has been effected, if— (i) such discount is established in terms of an agreement entered into at or before the time of such supply and specifically linked to relevant invoices; and (ii) input tax credit as is attributable to the discount on the basis of document issued by the supplier has been reversed by the recipient of the supply. (4) Where the value of the supply of goods or services or both cannot be determined under sub-section (1), the same shall be determined in such manner as may be prescribed. (5) Notwithstanding anything contained in sub-section (1) or sub-section (4), the value of such supplies as may be notified by the Government on the recommendations of the Council shall be determined in such manner as may be prescribed.” In the present case, the appellant’s contention that the gold coins and white goods represent a discount on the sale of cement cannot be accepted for the following reasons: First, where a discount is given after the supply, it can be excluded from the value of the original supply only when the conditions prescribed under Section 15(3)(b) of the CGST Act, 2017 are satisfied. In particular, the discount must be linked to the relevant invoices and the recipient must reverse the corresponding input tax credit. There is no evidence of such linkage or reversal in the present case. Second, Section 15(1) of the CGST Act, 2017 applies where the price actually paid or payable is the sole consideration for the supply. In the present case, once the act of achieving the prescribed target is treated as consideration under Section 2(31)(b), the price actually paid for the goods is not the sole consideration. Therefore, the fact that the parties are unrelated does not by itself result in the value of the supply being nil. The appellant’s contention that Section 15(1) of the CGST Act, 2017 results in a nil value is, therefore, not acceptable. 8. Where the value of a supply cannot be determined under Section 15(1), Section 15(4) of the CGST Act, 2017 provides that the value shall be determined in the manner prescribed by the Rules. Rule 27 of CGST Rules, 2017. Value of supply of goods or services where the consideration is not wholly in money. – Where the supply of goods or services is for a consideration not wholly in money, the value of the supply shall, – (a) be the open market value of such supply; (b) if the open market value is not available under clause (a), be the sum total of consideration in money and any such further amount in money as is equivalent to the consideration not in money, if such amount is known at the time of supply; … (d) if the value is not determinable under clause (a) or clause (b) or clause (c), be the sum total of consideration in money and such further amount in money that is equivalent to consideration not in money as determined by the application of rule 30 or rule 31 in that order. Rule 30 of CGST Rules, 2017. Value of supply of goods or services or both based on cost. – Where the value of a supply of goods or services or both is not determinable by any of the preceding rules of this Chapter, the value shall be one hundred and ten percent of the cost of production or manufacture or the cost of acquisition of such goods or the cost of provision of such services. 31.Residual method for determination of value of supply of goods or services or both.- Where the value of supply of goods or services or both cannot be determined under rules 27 to 30, the same shall be determined using reasonable means consistent with the principles and the general provisions of section 15 and the provisions of this Chapter Rule 27 of the CGST Rules, 2017 deals with cases where consideration is not wholly in money and Rule 30 of the CGST Rules, 2017 provides for valuation based on cost. Rule 30 provides that where the value of a supply cannot be determined under the preceding valuation rules, the value shall be 110% of the cost of production or manufacture or the cost of acquisition of the goods or the cost of provision of services, as applicable. In the present case, the gold coins and white goods do not have a separate selling price charged to the dealer. However, their cost of acquisition is ascertainable from the purchase invoices issued in the name of the appellant. Accordingly, where the value cannot be determined under the preceding rules, Rule 30 of the CGST Rules, 2017, is applicable. The Advance Ruling Authority was therefore justified in directing that the value be determined under Section 15 read with Rule 30 of the CGST Rules. 9. The appellant has further contended that the cost of the scheme is already included in the price of cement and that levy of GST again on the gold coins and white goods would amount to double taxation. This contention is also not acceptable. GST is levied on each taxable supply in accordance with Section 9 of the CGST Act. The cement and the gold coins or white goods are separate goods and constitute separate supplies when the statutory conditions are satisfied. The appellant’s plea that they have factored the cost of the scheme into the commercial price of cement, for which they have not submitted any evidence, does not alter the nature of the subsequent supply of gold coins or white goods. Such commercial pricing does not create any statutory exemption or set-off against the GST payable on a separate supply. Accordingly, the levy of GST on the supply of gold coins and white goods does not amount to double taxation. 10. With reference to appellant’s question no. 2 that the scheme would be treated as a “permanent transfer or disposal of business assets where ITC has been availed on such assets” and would be treated as a supply even if made without consideration and be subjected to GST under Sl.No. 1 of the Schedule I to the CGST Act 2017. Even if it is assumed, for the sake of argument, that there is no consideration, which is not the finding in the present case, the transaction would still fall within Section 7(1)(c) read with Entry 1 of Schedule I of CGST Act, 2017. Section 7. Scope of supply.- (1) For the purposes of this Act, the expression – “supply” includes- (a) all forms of supply of goods or services or both such as sale, transfer, barter, exchange, licence, rental, lease or disposal made or agreed to be made for a consideration by a person in the course or furtherance of business; ¹[(aa) the activities or transactions, by a person, other than an individual, to its members or constituents or vice-versa, for cash, deferred payment or other valuable consideration. [ See section 7 ] ACTIVITIES TO BE TREATED AS SUPPLY EVEN IF MADE WITHOUT CONSIDERATION (1) Permanent transfer or disposal of business assets where input tax credit has been availed on such assets. The essential requirements of the above provisions of law are that the goods are business assets, input tax credit has been availed on them, and they are permanently transferred or disposed of. In the present case, the appellant purchased the gold coins and white goods for business purposes and availed input tax credit on such purchases under Section 16(1) of the CGST Act, 2017. The goods were thereafter permanently handed over to the eligible dealers. The requirements of Entry 1 of Schedule I of the CGST Act, 2017, are therefore satisfied. Section 16(1) of the CGST Act, 2017 reproduced below: “section 16 (1) Every registered person shall, subject to such conditions and restrictions as may be prescribed and in the manner specified in section 49, be entitled to take credit of input tax charged on any supply of goods or services or both to him which are used or intended to be used in the course or furtherance of his business and the said amount shall be credited to the electronic credit ledger of such person.” The appellant availed that credit. It then disposed of the same goods. Schedule I, Sl. No. 1 therefore treats the disposal as a supply even without consideration. The Telangana Advance ruling Authority found consideration under Section 7(1)(a) of the CGST Act, 2017 and the TGST Act, 2017, read with Section 2(31)(b), and therefore did not rest its conclusion on Schedule I. Schedule I is held only in the alternative. Even if the consideration were to be disputed, the disposal would still be a supply under Section 7(1)(c) read with Sl. No. 1 of Schedule I. 11. We now turn to gifts and blocked credit. Section 17(5), so far as relevant, provides as follows: “Section 17(5) Notwithstanding anything contained in sub-section (1) of section 16 and sub-section (1) of section 18, input tax credit shall not be available in respect of the following, namely:— …… (h) goods lost, stolen, destroyed, written off or disposed of by way of gift or free samples; ……” The non-obstante clause shows that even credit otherwise available under Section 16(1) is barred if the goods are disposed of by way of gift or free samples. Circular No. 92/11/2019-GST dated 07.03.2019 clarifies the Board’s understanding of sales promotion schemes. On free samples and gifts, it states that goods supplied free of cost without consideration do not qualify as supply except where the activity falls within Schedule I, and that input tax credit shall not be available on inputs, input services and capital goods to the extent they are used in relation to such gifts or free samples; but that where the activity falls within Schedule I, the supplier would be eligible to avail the credit. On “buy one get one free” it states that the transaction is not a free supply but two or more supplies for a single price, and that credit remains available. On volume discounts it states that discounts which satisfy Section 15(3) are excluded from value. The structure of the Circular is therefore this: If the giveaway remains a free gift without consideration and outside Schedule I, there is no outward tax and credit is blocked. If the giveaway is brought within supply — either because there is consideration, or because Schedule I applies — outward tax is payable and credit is allowed. The appellant wants the second half of that structure without paying the outward tax which is not permissible under the Act. 12. The word gift is not defined in the Act. A gift, as explained by the appellant with reference to the Gift-tax Act and the principle in Km. Sonia Bhatia v. State of U.P., is a voluntary transfer without consideration or compensation therefor. The same test requires that the transfer not be under a prior obligation. Here the goods are issued only if the published target is met, and only to the extent of the credit earned. The transfer is conditional. It is not a voluntary gratuity, and hence cannot be treated as “gift” as mentioned under 17 (5)(h) of the Act. The Advance ruling Authority was not in error in linking credit to the finding of supply. The gift question is decided by this finding. Credit on procurement is admissible under Section 16(1), subject to the other conditions of Sections 16 and 17, because the outward supply is taxable. It is not admissible as a stand-alone relief if that supply itself is denied. 13. We therefore find as follows. First, the issue of gold coins and white goods to dealers who achieve the stipulated lifting during the scheme period is a supply of goods under Section 7(1)(a) of the CGST Act, 2017, made for a consideration as defined in Section 2(31)(b), in the course or furtherance of business. Second, the value of that supply shall be determined under Section 15(4) of the Act read with Rule 30 of the CGST Rules, 2017, being one hundred and ten per cent of the cost of acquisition of such goods, there being no price actually paid by the dealer which is the sole consideration. Third, the same disposal is, in the alternative, a supply under Section 7(1)(c) read with Sl. No. 1 of Schedule I, being a permanent transfer or disposal of business assets on which input tax credit has been availed. Fourth, the goods are not disposed of by way of gift or free samples. Section 17(5)(h) does not block credit. Fifth, input tax credit of the tax charged on procurement of those gold coins and white goods is admissible under Section 16(1), subject to the other conditions of Sections 16 and 17, because the outward supply is taxable. Credit is not admissible as a stand-alone relief if the outward supply is denied. The charging section, the definition of supply, Schedule I and Section 17(5)(h) do not permit input tax credit on goods that are given away without being brought to tax either as a supply for consideration or as a Schedule I disposal. Sixth, the transaction is not a discount on cement within Section 15(3), because no credit note linked to the cement invoices has been issued and the statutory conditions of that sub-section are not met. In view of the above discussions and findings we pass the following order ORDER We find no reason to interfere with the conclusion of the lower authority. The appeal is rejected and the ruling of the lower authority is upheld.

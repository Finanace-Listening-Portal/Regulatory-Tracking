Articles 5 min read RBI's FCNR liquidity problem is becoming a treasury problem for banks The Reserve Bank of India's struggle to absorb a record liquidity surplus is increasingly becoming a treasury-management challenge for banks, which must decide how much excess cash to park with the central bank, deploy into loans or securities, or keep readily available. Economists estimate that the core liquidity surplus has now reached around Rs 14-15 lakh crore, with the RBI looking to drain at least Rs 7 lakh crore through a combination of liquidity-management instruments.The scale of the surplus is changing the problem for banks. It is no longer simply a question of having sufficient funds to meet credit demand; treasury desks must decide where to deploy money that has arrived faster than the banking system can immediately absorb it through loans or other assets without compromising returns or balance-sheet flexibility. Advt The RBI's recent operations have offered a glimpse of that dilemma. On September 7, the central bank offered to absorb Rs 7 lakh crore through a 30-day variable rate reverse repo (VRRR), but banks submitted only Rs 2.59 lakh crore of bids, all of which was accepted. The RBI also conducted an overnight VRRR operation, taking the total liquidity absorbed through the two operations to more than Rs 6 lakh crore.Banks are valuing flexibility over locking in cashThe muted participation in the 30-day VRRR is significant because it shows that having excess liquidity does not automatically mean banks are willing to lock it away for longer periods.Treasury desks have to weigh the return available from placing funds with the RBI against the opportunity cost of losing access to that cash for several weeks. With liquidity conditions changing rapidly and banks still assessing how much of the surplus can be deployed into credit and investments, keeping funds available can itself have value.The September 7 auction offered a clear illustration. Despite system liquidity being more than Rs 11 lakh crore around the time of the operation, banks offered only Rs 2.59 lakh crore against the Rs 7 lakh crore notified amount for the 30-day VRRR.That behaviour suggests treasury managers are not treating the liquidity as money that can simply be parked indefinitely. They are retaining optionality over where and when it can be deployed. Advt Lending cannot absorb the surplus overnightOne destination for the excess funds is credit. The FCNR(B) inflows have strengthened banks' liquidity and funding capacity, and Moody's has said the deposits should support the funding position of Indian banks.But the banking system cannot automatically convert every additional rupee into loans. Credit demand, underwriting standards, capital requirements and asset-quality considerations determine how quickly banks can expand their loan books.This creates a treasury trade-off. Keeping money in short-term instruments preserves liquidity but can limit returns. Moving into loans can improve yields but requires suitable demand and acceptable credit risk. Buying longer-duration government securities can provide additional returns but exposes banks to mark-to-market and interest-rate risk. Advt The excess liquidity therefore creates a deployment problem as much as a funding benefit.The bond market presents another trade-offThe RBI also has to choose how much of the surplus should be removed temporarily and how much should be absorbed more durably.The central bank has several options, including VRRR operations, foreign-exchange swaps, government bond sales and changes in reserve requirements. Each has different implications for banks and financial markets.Foreign-exchange sell-buy swaps are emerging as one of the preferred options among market participants because they can absorb rupee liquidity without directly forcing large transactions in the government securities market. Bank executives had suggested the approach to the RBI earlier this month, arguing that it could remove liquidity without major disruption to other asset classes. Advt Reuters reported on Wednesday that the RBI was likely conducting near-maturity dollar-rupee sell-buy swaps for September and possibly October, based on information from bankers and foreign-exchange brokers. The sources estimated that at least about $1 billion of such swaps may have been conducted across the two maturities. The RBI has not officially confirmed these transactions.For banks, the use of swaps matters because it changes the way the liquidity is removed. If the RBI were instead to rely heavily on government bond sales, banks could face greater volatility in bond prices and yields, affecting their investment portfolios.The FCNR(B) windfall comes with an FX liabilityThe treasury challenge does not end with the rupees created by the currency swaps. The underlying FCNR(B) deposits are dollar-denominated liabilities, meaning banks must also manage their foreign-currency obligations over the life of those deposits.The RBI's swap arrangement effectively addresses the principal foreign-exchange exposure associated with the deposits, but banks still have to manage the dollar interest payments due on those liabilities. Reuters reported that Indian banks have left a sizeable portion of this future interest exposure unhedged, partly because hedging costs are relatively high.This creates another calculation for treasury desks. A bank receiving a large FCNR(B) inflow has to determine not only how to deploy the resulting rupee liquidity, but also how and when to manage its future dollar interest obligations.The issue becomes more relevant when the rupee is under pressure. The currency fell beyond Rs 95 to the dollar on Wednesday as oil prices climbed above $100 a barrel amid heightened geopolitical tensions.A weaker rupee can increase the domestic-currency cost of servicing unhedged dollar interest liabilities, adding another layer to the treasury decisions created by the FCNR(B) inflows.

Summary: The Allahabad High Court, Lucknow Bench, in Anupam Yadav v. State of U.P. & 3 Others, Habeas Corpus Writ Petition No. 235 of 2026, decided on 6 July 2026, examined whether a husband could invoke the extraordinary remedy of habeas corpus under Article 226 of the Constitution to secure production of his wife, whom he alleged was being illegally detained by her father. The parties had married on 28 April 2026, but their matrimonial relationship deteriorated shortly thereafter. The wife left the matrimonial home on 25 May 2026 and subsequently submitted a police complaint alleging dowry demands and cruelty. The petitioner relied on the Supreme Court’s decision in Devu G. Nair v. State of Kerala, 2024 INSC 228, concerning procedural safeguards for persons allegedly detained by family members. He also invoked the Explanation to Section 9 of the Hindu Marriage Act, 1955, concerning the burden of establishing reasonable excuse for withdrawal from matrimonial society. Justice Subhash Vidyarthi found that the petitioner’s own pleadings, together with the material placed before the Court, did not establish a prima facie case of forcible detention. The Court distinguished the Supreme Court’s protective guidelines from a situation involving apparent voluntary departure following marital discord. It further explained that the statutory questions arising under Section 9 of the Hindu Marriage Act must be determined through appropriate matrimonial proceedings rather than through habeas corpus jurisdiction. The writ petition was accordingly dismissed at the admission stage. The decision illustrates the distinction between constitutional protection against unlawful confinement and statutory remedies arising from matrimonial disputes. State of U.P. (2026) Abstract The writ of Habeas Corpus under Article 226 of the Constitution of India is designed to protect personal liberty against unlawful confinement. However, courts across India frequently observe instances where litigating spouses invoke this extraordinary constitutional remedy to resolve domestic disputes, locate spouses who left voluntarily, or bypass civil family court procedures. This paper analyses the decision of the High Court of Judicature at Allahabad (Lucknow Bench) in Anupam Yadav v. State of U.P. & 3 Others (Habeas Corpus Writ Petition No. 235 of 2026, decided July 6, 2026). By examining the interplay between Anupam Yadav and the landmark Supreme Court ruling in Devu G Nair v. State of Kerala (2024 INSC 228), this paper addresses why voluntary departure due to marital breakdown cannot be treated as illegal detention, and why constitutional writs cannot replace statutory civil remedies under Section 9 of the Hindu Marriage Act, 1955. I. Introduction The writ of Habeas Corpus is a primary constitutional safeguard under Article 21 of the Indian Constitution, designed to deliver immediate relief against illegal restraint. While historically directed at state actions, Indian courts permit its issuance against private parties when actual or constructive illegal detention occurs. In recent years, a pattern has emerged in family law litigation where spouses—most commonly husbands—file writ petitions claiming their partner is held in “illegal custody” by her natal family. These petitions are often initiated when phone communication is blocked or when a spouse returns to her parental home following domestic disagreements. This paper reviews the judgment of the Allahabad High Court in Anupam Yadav v. State of U.P. (2026), examining the evidentiary threshold required to entertain a spousal Habeas Corpus petition and how courts differentiate between unlawful restraint and voluntary departure. II. Statement of Facts The petitioner, Anupam Yadav, filed Habeas Corpus Writ Petition No. 235 of 2026 before the Lucknow Bench of the Allahabad High Court. He alleged that his wife, Smt. Anamika Yadav, was illegally detained by her father, that he was being prevented from contacting or meeting her. Marriage and Departure Marriage and Departure: The parties were married on April 28, 2026, according to social customs in Lucknow. Following brief visits between parental and matrimonial homes, the wife returned to her matrimonial home on May 17, 2026. On May 25, 2026, she left the home accompanied by her brother and an acquaintance. Loss of Contact Loss of Contact: On May 28, 2026, the petitioner discovered his wife had blocked his phone number. Petition Filing Petition Filing: After submitting police complaints and filing a grievance on the State IGRS portal, the petitioner approached the High Court seeking an order for his wife’s physical production. III. Issues Raised Whether a spousal Habeas Corpus petition is maintainable when the petitioner’s own pleadings and verified police records show voluntary departure due to marital discord. Whether the procedural guidelines issued by the Supreme Court in Devu G Nair v. State of Kerala (2024) mandate the physical production of a spouse in every matrimonial dispute regardless of threshold evidentiary defects. Whether invoking Article 226 is permissible to bypass statutory remedies available under Section 9 of the Hindu Marriage Act, 1955 (Restitution of Conjugal Rights). IV. Arguments of the Parties A. Arguments for the Petitioner Allegation of Unlawful Custody: Counsel argued that the wife was under the illegal custody of her father, who blocked all communication and refused to let her return. Removal of Belongings: The petitioner pointed out that his wife had taken jewelry worth approximately ₹15 Lakh, claiming this created genuine concern for her safety. Reliance on Devu G Nair: The petitioner relied on the Supreme Court ruling in Devu G Nair v. State of Kerala (2024 INSC 228), arguing that the High Court was required to prioritize listing, order chamber proceedings, and record the corpus’s free will away from parental influence. Statutory Presumption under Section 9 HMA: Citing the Explanation to Section 9 of the Hindu Marriage Act, 1955, counsel argued that the legal burden of showing a “reasonable excuse” for leaving the marriage lies on the spouse who withdrew. B. Arguments for the State / Respondents Police Records and Wife’s Statement: The Additional Government Advocate (AGA-I) presented written instructions and a formal complaint submitted by the wife to the police dated June 15, 2026. Allegations of Cruelty: The wife’s statement described demands for dowry (including cash, a vehicle, and household appliances) made during marital events. She alleged mental and physical abuse by her husband and his family, which forced her to leave for her parental home on May 25, 2026. No Restraint: The State submitted that the wife was residing with her father out of her own free will to escape domestic abuse, and no illegal detention existed. V. Judicial Reasoning and Findings The High Court, presided over by Hon’ble Justice Subhash Vidyarthi, J., dismissed the petition at the fresh admission stage: 1. Self-Contradictions in Pleadings The Court found that the petitioner’s own pleadings disproved his claim of forcible detention: The petitioner admitted that his wife refused consent for physical intimacy throughout her stay at the matrimonial home. He acknowledged that she left with her personal articles and jewelry valued at ₹15 Lakh. The Court observed that taking personal property and withholding consent for intimacy indicated a voluntary departure to end cohabitation, rather than an abduction or forced confinement. 2. Contextual Distinction of Devu G Nair Guidelines The Court addressed the petitioner’s reliance on Devu G Nair (2024), ruling that legal guidelines cannot be applied in abstract isolation from facts. Devu G Nair was framed to protect partners in same-sex, inter-caste, or non-conforming relationships facing natal family coercion. In contrast, Anupam Yadav involved a standard marital dispute where police records established that the wife departed voluntarily due to cruelty allegations. The Court noted that while procedural guidelines like priority listing were followed, mandatory physical production was unnecessary when no prima facie case of illegal restraint existed. 3. Separation of Writ Jurisdiction and Civil Remedies Addressing the Explanation to Section 9 of the Hindu Marriage Act, 1955, the Court held that the statutory burden of showing “reasonable excuse” for withdrawal applies exclusively within a civil proceeding in Family Court after summons are served and contested. Civil evidentiary rules under family law cannot justify replacing statutory procedures with a Habeas Corpus writ under Article 226. VI. Conclusion The judgment in Anupam Yadav v. State of U.P. (2026) reinforces clear boundaries between constitutional writ remedies and statutory family law frameworks. By dismissing the petition at the fresh stage based on internal pleading contradictions and verified statements of voluntary departure, the Allahabad High Court affirmed that Article 226 cannot be used to trace spouses or resolve marital disputes. The decision preserves the statutory function of Family Courts under Section 9 of the Hindu Marriage Act, 1955, and ensures that progressive procedural safeguards established in cases like Devu G Nair are reserved for genuine instances of unlawful confinement.

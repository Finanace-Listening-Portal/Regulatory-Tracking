Articles 5 min read FCNR windfall may mask underlying deposit mobilisation weakness The end of the RBI’s special FCNR(B) mobilisation window is shifting the focus back to banks’ ability to attract domestic deposits, after the scheme delivered a sharp but exceptional boost to deposit growth and system liquidity. The exceptional inflow has strengthened banks’ funding position and pushed deposit growth sharply higher, but credit is still expanding faster than deposits, leaving lenders dependent on continued mobilisation of domestic savings.Total inflows under the RBI’s special swap facility stood at $136.4 billion as of August 31. The FCNR(B) mobilisation window closed on August 31, although banks can execute the associated swaps with the RBI until September 11. Advt The impact of the inflows was visible in the banking system’s deposit numbers. Bank deposits grew 15.4% year-on-year at the end of July, the fastest pace since December 2016, compared with 12.7% a fortnight earlier and 10% a year earlier, according to CareEdge. The rating agency attributed the sharp acceleration partly to the strong FCNR(B) mobilisation.But deposit growth had moderated to 14.7% by August 15, while credit growth remained considerably higher at 18.3%. The gap of around 360 basis points highlights the funding challenge banks could face once the exceptional FCNR(B) inflows stop adding to their deposit base.The foreign-currency inflows have come at a time when banks are seeing strong demand for loans. Bank credit growth stood at 19.3% at the end of July before moderating to 18.3% by August 15, according to CareEdge.The inflows have also produced a substantial liquidity surplus in the banking system. System liquidity surplus reached around Rs 9.7 lakh crore on September 3 and rose further in subsequent days, prompting the RBI to step up liquidity-absorption operations.For banks, the additional liquidity provides greater flexibility in managing funding requirements and reduces the immediate need to compete aggressively for domestic deposits or rely on short-term wholesale funding. Advt The benefit, however, has not been evenly distributed. Private-sector banks accounted for $61 billion, or 46.9%, of the roughly $130 billion FCNR(B) deposits mobilised by early September. Public-sector banks accounted for about $37 billion and foreign banks around $32 billion, according to Financial Express.The concentration means lenders that attracted a larger share of the FCNR(B) pool have gained a bigger funding cushion than those with lower mobilisation.Domestic deposits face the next testThe bigger question for banks is whether deposit growth can remain in the mid-teens after the special foreign-currency mobilisation has run its course. Advt FCNR(B) deposits have strengthened banks’ funding position, but they do not represent a permanent increase in domestic household or corporate savings. Banks will still need to mobilise rupee deposits to fund loan growth and replace deposits as they mature.The credit-deposit gap has remained a concern for the banking sector. At 18.3% as of August 15, credit growth was almost 360 basis points ahead of deposit growth. If loan growth remains in the high teens, banks could again face greater competition for retail and term deposits once the effect of FCNR(B) mobilisation fades.Conventional deposits are nevertheless showing healthy growth. CareEdge data showed time deposits rising 14.8% year-on-year as of August 15 and accounting for 87.3% of total deposits, while demand deposits grew 16%. Advt This indicates that the improvement in deposit mobilisation is not entirely dependent on FCNR(B) inflows. However, the exceptional scale of the foreign-currency mobilisation makes the months ahead important for determining how much of the recent acceleration can be sustained.Private banks get greater liquidity cushionThe concentration of FCNR(B) deposits among private-sector banks could also influence funding conditions across lenders.With almost half of the deposits mobilised by private banks, these lenders have gained additional room to support loan growth without immediately increasing their dependence on other wholesale funding sources.The surge in foreign-currency inflows has simultaneously pushed system liquidity to unusually high levels, forcing the RBI to absorb surplus funds through variable-rate reverse repo operations.The immediate effect is favourable for banks because excess liquidity reduces short-term funding pressure. But it also means that the funding benefit from FCNR(B) mobilisation could diminish as deposits mature and the one-off inflow drops out of the banking system’s deposit-growth calculations.Banks will then have to rely more heavily on domestic deposits, along with wholesale borrowings and other market instruments, to finance incremental credit.What happens after FCNR?The July deposit-growth number therefore needs to be viewed against the exceptional funding flows that preceded it. The 15.4% growth represents a genuine improvement in banks’ funding position, but the pace was also helped by the rapid build-up of FCNR(B) deposits.The next few months will provide a clearer picture of the underlying strength of banks’ liability franchises. Sustained deposit growth in the mid-teens, even as the FCNR effect fades, would indicate that domestic mobilisation has strengthened. A sharper slowdown while credit remains close to 18% would reopen concerns over the credit-deposit gap.For now, the FCNR(B) scheme has given banks a substantial liquidity cushion and reduced near-term funding pressure. The next challenge will be to sustain deposit growth without the support of another exceptional foreign-currency inflow.

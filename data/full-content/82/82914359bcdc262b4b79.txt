Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); JSW Ispat Special Products Limited Vs Bharat Petroresources Limited (Delhi High Court) Summary: The Delhi High Court, in JSW Ispat Special Products Limited Vs Bharat Petroresources Limited, examined the validity of an arbitral award dated 21 August 2024 arising from disputes under a Joint Operating Agreement (JOA), where the corporate debtor had already undergone a Corporate Insolvency Resolution Process (CIRP) under the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016 (IBC). Justice Jyoti Singh considered a petition filed under Section 34 of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996 by JSW Ispat Special Products Limited, subsequently known as JSW Steel Limited, challenging the award passed in favour of Bharat Petroresources Limited (BPRL). The central question was whether claims arising from contractual obligations, including amounts asserted after the insolvency commencement date, could be adjudicated and enforced through arbitration when the approved resolution plan contained provisions extinguishing operational-creditor claims and liabilities. The dispute also raised a separate challenge concerning the independence and impartiality of the presiding arbitrator, who had previously participated in deciding an appeal involving the same parties and related insolvency issues while serving as Chairperson of the National Company Law Appellate Tribunal (NCLAT). The High Court was therefore required to consider the relationship between the statutory finality of an approved resolution plan, the jurisdiction of an arbitral tribunal, the treatment of pre-insolvency and post-insolvency claims, and the consequences of an arbitrator’s previous involvement in connected proceedings. The case was particularly significant because BPRL had already participated in the insolvency proceedings, submitted its claims, challenged the approval of the resolution plan before NCLAT and subsequently pursued arbitration for additional amounts allegedly payable under the contractual arrangements.The underlying contractual relationship originated from a Production Sharing Contract dated 30 August 2012, executed between the Government of India and a consortium comprising GAIL, Engineers India Limited, BF Infrastructure Limited, Monnet Ispat & Energy Limited (MIEL) and BPRL for petroleum exploration, development and production in Contract Area Block CB-ONN-2010/8. The consortium members subsequently executed a Joint Operating Agreement on 5 April 2013, establishing their respective participating interests, financial contributions and obligations concerning petroleum operations. BPRL functioned as the lead operator and issued cash calls requiring consortium members to contribute towards exploration and operational expenditure. According to the record, MIEL defaulted in making payments under Cash Call No. 12 dated 7 March 2016, resulting in a default notice dated 13 April 2016. Further cash calls were issued, and disputes developed regarding MIEL’s outstanding contributions, the consequences of its default and the status of its participating interest. The petitioner maintained that its participating interest had been forfeited and that subsequent cash calls reflected NIL amounts payable by it. On 18 July 2017, the National Company Law Tribunal, Mumbai, admitted an insolvency petition filed by State Bank of India under Section 7 of the IBC against MIEL and appointed an Interim Resolution Professional. BPRL subsequently submitted its proof of claim on 5 January 2018 for an aggregate amount of ₹19,51,75,868, comprising ₹9,58,88,886 allegedly payable up to the insolvency commencement date and ₹9,92,86,982 representing estimated expenditure attributable to the subsequent period. The Resolution Professional admitted the claim relating to the period up to 18 July 2017 but declined to admit the amount claimed for the period after commencement of insolvency proceedings. This distinction between admitted pre-insolvency liabilities and excluded future claims became the principal foundation of the subsequent arbitration dispute. The insolvency proceedings culminated in the approval of a resolution plan submitted by a consortium comprising Aion Investments Private Limited and JSW Steel Limited. The Committee of Creditors approved the plan with a majority voting share of 98.97%, and the NCLT sanctioned it on 24 July 2018. The approved plan provided that the liquidation value available to operational creditors was NIL and contemplated the extinguishment of operational debts, liabilities and claims in accordance with its terms. Nevertheless, pursuant to an undertaking made by the resolution applicant, the NCLT directed distribution of ₹25 crore among eligible operational creditors on a proportionate basis. BPRL ultimately received ₹2,08,79,405 on 22 July 2019 against its admitted claim of ₹9,58,88,886. Meanwhile, BPRL challenged the NCLT’s approval order before NCLAT, principally questioning the exclusion of claims arising after the insolvency commencement date. By its judgment dated 19 August 2019, NCLAT dismissed the appeal, observing that the Resolution Professional was required to collate claims within the statutory insolvency framework and could not include future claims accruing after the insolvency commencement date under Section 18(1)(b) of the IBC. Thereafter, disputes continued between the parties regarding outstanding cash calls, contractual interest, plugging and abandonment expenditure, and other alleged financial obligations under the JOA. BPRL issued an amicable settlement communication dated 10 December 2019 asserting outstanding dues of approximately ₹5.39 crore as of 30 September 2019. When negotiations failed, it invoked the arbitration clause contained in Article 19.3 of the JOA and sought constitution of an arbitral tribunal. The Delhi High Court appointed the petitioner’s nominee arbitrator, and the tribunal was subsequently constituted. The petitioner challenged the appointment process and the tribunal’s jurisdiction, but arbitration continued and culminated in the disputed award dated 21 August 2024. The three-member Arbitral Tribunal awarded claims which the High Court recorded as totalling ₹12,90,83,199, calculated up to 10 January 2025 and including applicable GST on interest. The components identified in the judgment included ₹4,84,61,358 towards outstanding cash calls under the JOA, ₹40,14,126.45 towards plugging and abandonment expenditure, ₹1,75,94,973.04 towards default interest, ₹1,99,95,206 towards interest under the JOA, ₹3,18,59,787 towards arbitration costs and ₹3,31,794 towards additional interest. JSW challenged the award primarily on two grounds. First, it contended that the claims were legally incapable of arbitration because the approved resolution plan had extinguished all operational-creditor liabilities falling within its provisions, including contingent, disputed, uncrystallised, known, unknown, present and future claims. The petitioner argued that the arbitral tribunal could not revive liabilities which had ceased to exist by operation of the IBC and the binding resolution plan. It relied upon Sections 31 and 238 of the IBC, contending that the approved plan bound all stakeholders and prevailed over inconsistent contractual rights and remedies. Second, JSW questioned the eligibility of the presiding arbitrator under Section 12 of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996, read with Entry 16 of the Seventh Schedule. The petitioner pointed out that the presiding arbitrator had previously served as Chairperson of NCLAT and participated in deciding BPRL’s appeal concerning the same insolvency proceedings and related claims. According to JSW, this amounted to previous involvement in the case and created justifiable doubts regarding independence and impartiality. It further alleged that the tribunal members had not furnished the individual disclosures required under Section 12 and the Sixth Schedule, and that a composite procedural order could not substitute for the prescribed statutory disclosures. Bharat Petroresources Limited opposed the challenge and maintained that the arbitral award was legally sustainable. Its principal submission was that the claims adjudicated in arbitration arose after the insolvency commencement date and therefore could not have been collated or determined by the Resolution Professional during CIRP. BPRL argued that the approved resolution plan extinguished only those liabilities covered by the insolvency proceedings and did not automatically discharge obligations arising subsequently under the continuing contractual relationship. It relied on the distinction recognised by NCLAT between claims existing as of the insolvency commencement date and claims arising thereafter. According to BPRL, the Resolution Professional’s inability to admit future claims did not mean that those claims had been adjudicated against the creditor or permanently extinguished. The respondent further contended that the arbitral tribunal had jurisdiction to determine disputes arising under the JOA and that the petitioner had failed to establish patent illegality warranting interference under Section 34 of the Arbitration Act. On the issue of arbitrator independence, BPRL submitted that the earlier NCLAT proceedings concerned a distinct legal question relating to the Resolution Professional’s authority to collate claims, whereas the arbitration involved adjudication of substantive contractual liabilities. It argued that previous service as an independent judicial adjudicator did not, by itself, establish bias or disqualification. Reliance was placed on HRD Corporation (Marcus Oil and Chemical Division) v. GAIL (India) Limited and other authorities distinguishing prior involvement in the same dispute from an independent adjudicator’s earlier participation in related proceedings. BPRL therefore maintained that the arbitrator’s appointment was valid and that the award should not be disturbed merely because the presiding arbitrator had earlier decided a connected insolvency appeal. In examining the controversy, the Delhi High Court placed substantial emphasis on the language and binding effect of the approved resolution plan. The Court considered the relevant provisions of the IBC, particularly Section 31(1), which makes an approved resolution plan binding on the corporate debtor and its stakeholders, and Section 238, which gives the IBC overriding effect in the event of inconsistency with other laws. It also considered Section 2(3) of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996, in the context of disputes rendered non-arbitrable by operation of another statute. The Court examined the resolution plan‘s express provisions dealing with operational creditors and observed that the plan contemplated extinguishment of claims and liabilities whether admitted or unadmitted, asserted or unasserted, crystallised or uncrystallised, disputed or undisputed, and present or future, in relation to the specified period and acquisition. It rejected the proposition that the arbitral tribunal could disregard the legal consequences of the approved plan merely because the Resolution Professional had not collated the respondent’s post-insolvency claims. The Court drew support from the Supreme Court’s judgments in Committee of Creditors of Essar Steel India Limited v. Satish Kumar Gupta, Ghanashyam Mishra and Sons Private Limited v. Edelweiss Asset Reconstruction Company Limited, Ruchi Soya Industries Limited v. Union of India, RPS Infrastructure Limited v. Mukul Kumar and Electrosteel Steel Limited v. Ispat Carrier Private Limited. These authorities establish that a successful resolution applicant must be able to take over the corporate debtor on a clean slate, without being confronted by unexpected historical claims after approval of the plan. The Court particularly relied upon the principle that claims not forming part of an approved resolution plan stand extinguished and that creditors cannot subsequently initiate or continue proceedings to recover such amounts. It concluded that the claims referred to arbitration in the present case were outside the approved resolution plan, had stood extinguished and were consequently non-arbitrable. The High Court’s ultimate determination rested on the conclusion that the arbitral tribunal could not adjudicate or award amounts in respect of claims already extinguished under the approved resolution plan. The Court referred particularly to the Supreme Court’s decision in Electrosteel Steel Limited v. Ispat Carrier Private Limited, where an arbitral award concerning claims excluded from an approved resolution plan was held unenforceable because the underlying claims had ceased to survive and the adjudicating forum lacked jurisdiction to entertain them. Applying that reasoning, Justice Jyoti Singh held that the tribunal’s award in favour of BPRL could not be sustained. The judgment reinforces the distinction between the existence of an arbitration agreement and the legal arbitrability of a particular claim: even where parties have agreed to resolve contractual disputes through arbitration, the tribunal cannot grant relief concerning liabilities which have been extinguished by a binding statutory insolvency resolution process. The Court also considered the objections regarding the presiding arbitrator’s earlier participation in connected proceedings, the statutory disclosure requirements and the authorities dealing with previous involvement and impartiality. However, the decisive conclusion for setting aside the award was that the disputed claims were not part of the approved resolution plan and could not lawfully be pursued through arbitration. Accordingly, by its judgment dated 11 September 2025, the Delhi High Court allowed O.M.P. (COMM) 533/2024 and quashed and set aside the arbitral award dated 21 August 2024. All pending applications in the Section 34 proceedings were disposed of. The connected enforcement petition, OMP (ENF.) (COMM.) 28/2025, was separately listed for consideration on 26 November 2025. The ruling underscores the importance of examining the precise language of an approved resolution plan before initiating or continuing arbitration against a resolved corporate debtor and affirms that the statutory finality of insolvency resolution cannot be circumvented through subsequent contractual recovery proceedings. The arbitral award was passed by the Tribunal on 21.08.2024, which is challenged in the present petition. SUBMISSIONS ON BEHALF OF THE PETITIONER: 4. Members of the Tribunal failed to give individual disclosures in writing as mandated under Section 12(1) and (2) read with Sixth Schedule of 1996 Act. Contrary to any known procedure or law, Tribunal passed a composite Procedural Order on 25.03.2022, stating therein that none of the members had any interest in any of the parties or in the subject matter of the disputes; there were no circumstances likely to give rise to justifiable doubts as to impartiality or independence of any one; and there existed no circumstance which was likely to affect their ability to devote sufficient time to arbitration and complete the same within the stipulated time. This was contrary to the Sixth Schedule which prescribes a format in which several details have to be furnished by the Arbitrator, by way of disclosure, so that parties are not at a disadvantage. Additionally, the Presiding Arbitrator failed to disclose that on 19.08.2019 he had decided an appeal filed by the Respondent against the order of NCLT being Company Appeal (AT) Insolvency No. 550/2018 titled ‘Bharat Petroresources Ltd v. Monnet Ispat & Energy Ltd.’, in his capacity as Chairperson of NCLAT and was thus ‘involved in the case’. The subject matter of the appeal and subject matter of the arbitration were the same and consequently, even the legal issues were common. 5. In Ram Kumar and Another v. Shriram Transport Finance Co. Ltd., 2022 SCC OnLine Del 4268, Division Bench of this Court held that it is mandatory for a person to make disclosure under Section 12(1) of the 1996 Act of all circumstances that may give rise to justifiable doubts as to his independence or impartiality, when approached in connection with his appointment as an Arbitrator. This is a necessary safeguard for ensuring and preserving the integrity and efficacy of arbitration as an alternate dispute resolution mechanism. In Shriram Transport Finance Company Limited v. Narender Singh, 2022 SCC OnLine Del 3412, another Division Bench of this Court held that disclosure under Section 12(1) and (2) is mandatory, in the absence of which an award is liable to be set aside. Applying the said observations to the instant case, appointment of the Presiding Arbitrator was untenable in law, in light of Entry 16 of Seventh Schedule read with Section 12(5) of 1996 Act, which read as follows:- “12. Grounds for challenge. … [(5) Notwithstanding any prior agreement to the contrary, any person whose relationship, with the parties or counsel or the subject-matter of the dispute, falls under any of the categories specified in the Seventh Schedule shall be ineligible to be appointed as an arbitrator: Provided that parties may, subsequent to disputes having arisen between them, waive the applicability of this sub-section by an express agreement in writing.] THE SEVENTH SCHEDULE …Relationship of the arbitrator to the dispute … 16. The arbitrator has previous involvement in the case.” [emphasis supplied]” 6. Comparison of the observations made in the NCLAT order and the arbitral award reflects a conflict of interest and previous involvement of the Presiding Arbitrator in the case. The issue in Respondent’s appeal before NCLAT was whether its claims arising after the ICD were rightly excluded from the Resolution Plan. In other words, Respondent was seeking enforcement of post-ICD claims before NCLAT and thereafter in arbitration and Petitioner was objecting to the arbitrability of these very claims before the Arbitral Tribunal, alleging that being outside the approved Resolution Plan, they were extinguished. It was only natural that having taken a decision as the Chairperson, NCLAT that IRP/RP had rightly only collated the claims arising upto the ICD, meaning thereby that future claims were open for adjudication in another forum, the Presiding Arbitrator would not take a contrary decision in arbitration and this apprehension was proved right when the Tribunal actually entertained and allowed the claims of the Respondent, even though they stood extinguished. In fact, the fulcrum of the award is the observation made in the NCLAT order, which is demonstrated from a comparative analysis as follows:- NCLAT Order dated 19.08.2019 in Company Appeal (AT) Insolvency No.550 of 2018 Final Arbitral Award Dated 21.08.2024 “32. ‘Bharat Petro resources Limited’ submitted its claim on 5th January, 2018 towards the ‘operational debt’ amounting to Rs.9,58,88,886/- as on the Insolvency Commencement Date, which has been admitted by the ‘Resolution Professional’. Therefore, any claim of the Appellant towards future claim accrued after the Insolvency Commencement Date, cannot be considered under Section 18(1) (b) by the ‘Resolution Professional’. 33. If any cost incurred during the ‘Corporate Insolvency Resolution Process’ that cannot be treated to be the claim of an ‘Operational Creditor’ and therefore, further claim amounting to Rs.9,92,86,892/- towards future claim made by ‘Bharat Petroresources Limited’ was rightly not collated by the ‘Interim Resolution Professional’/‘Resolution Professional’. 34. For the reason aforesaid merely on the ground that the future claim has not been collated by the ‘Resolution Professional’, the Appellant- ‘Bharat Petroresources Limited’ cannot assail the order of approval of plan (dated 25th July, 2018) passed under Section 31 of the ‘I&B Code’.” 48. It is not in dispute that the application under Section 7 of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016 (‘IBC’) was admitted on 18.07.2017. Thus, 18.07.2017 is the insolvency commencement date (ICD). At first instance it was held that his second adjudication should be set aside for apparent bias because, amongst other things, he had already decided the same issue. The Court of Appeal reversed the decision. Dyson, L.J. said: (All ER p. 732, paras 20-21) ‘20. In my judgment, the mere fact that the tribunal has previously decided the issue is not of itself sufficient to justify a conclusion of apparent bias. Something more is required. Judges are assumed to be trustworthy and to understand that they should approach every case with an open mind. The same applies to adjudicators, who are almost always professional persons. That is not to say that, if it is asked to redetermine an issue and the evidence and arguments are merely a repeat of what went before, the tribunal will not be likely to reach the same conclusion as before. It would be unrealistic, indeed absurd, to expect the tribunal in such circumstances to ignore its earlier decision and not to be inclined to come to the same conclusion as before, particularly if the previous decision was carefully reasoned. The vice which the law must guard against is that the tribunal may approach the rehearing with a closed mind. If a Judge has considered an issue carefully before reaching a decision on the first occasion, it cannot sensibly be said that he has a closed mind if, the evidence and arguments being the same as before, he does not give as careful a consideration on the second occasion as on the first. He will, however, be expected to give such reconsideration of the matter as is reasonably necessary for him to be satisfied that his first decision was correct. As I have said, it will be a most unusual case where the second hearing is for practical purposes an exact rerun of the first. 21. The mere fact that the tribunal has decided the issue before is therefore not enough for apparent bias. There needs to be something of substance to lead the fair-minded and informed observer to conclude that there is a real possibility that the tribunal will not bring an open mind and objective judgment to bear.’ 27. Those comments apply with as much force to arbitrators in international reinsurance arbitration as they do to adjudicators in building disputes. Just as an arbitrator or adjudicator can be expected to bring an open mind and objective judgment to bear when redetermining the same question on the same evidence between the same parties, it is all the more so where the evidence is different and heard in a reference between different parties. 28. It could thus be seen that one of the dominant objects of the I&B Code is to see to it that an attempt has to be made to revive the corporate debtor and make it a running concern. For that, a resolution applicant has to prepare a resolution plan on the basis of the information memorandum. The view taken by the High Court that because the appellant did not challenge the award under section 34 of the 1996 Act, therefore, it was precluded from objecting to execution of the award at the stage of section 47 of the Code of Civil Procedure is wholly unsustainable. 51. Consequently, the view taken by the High Court that notwithstanding approval of the resolution plan by the National Company Law Tribunal, the Facilitation Council did not lose jurisdiction to proceed and pronounce the arbitral award, is erroneous and contrary to the law laid down by this court. 52. In that view of the matter, we have no hesitation to hold that upon approval of the resolution plan by the National Company Law Tribunal, the claim of the respondent being outside the purview of the resolution plan stood extinguished. Therefore, the award dated July 6, 2018 is incapable of being executed. Consequently, the order dated March 3, 2023 passed by the Presiding Officer, Commercial Court/District Judge-1, Bokaro in Commercial Execution Case No. 21 of 2022 (Execution Case No. 77 of 2018) is hereby set aside. Execution proceedings in Commercial Execution Case No. 21 of 2022 (Execution Case No. 77 of 2018) pending in the Court of Presiding Officer, Commercial Court/District Judge-1, Bokaro, are hereby quashed. Resultantly, impugned order of the High Court dated July 17, 2023 is also set aside.” 39. It is thus clear from a conspectus of the aforementioned judgments that all such claims, which are not a part of the Resolution Plan on the date of approval, shall stand extinguished and no person will be entitled to initiate or continue any proceedings in respect of claims, which are not part of the Resolution Plan and so much so this would apply to the statutory dues owed to Central/State Governments, local bodies etc. In the instant case, the claims which were referred to arbitration were not part of the approved Resolution Plan and stood extinguished and were thus not arbitrable. 40. In Electrosteel (supra), the Supreme Court was examining the validity of the arbitral award in respect of claims of the Creditor, which were outside the approved Resolution Plan. After a detailed and extensive discussion on the provisions of IBC and referring to all the earlier judgements of the Supreme Court on this aspect, it was held that the Facilitation Council under the MSME Act, did not have the jurisdiction to arbitrate on the claims which stood extinguished on approval of the Resolution Plan and therefore, the arbitral award was without jurisdiction. Pertinently, in this case it was mentioned in the Resolution Plan that any and all claims or demands, admitted or not, due or contingent, asserted or unasserted, known or unknown, present or future etc., would be written off in full and deemed to be permanently extinguished by virtue of order of NCLT approving the Resolution Plan with a special emphasis that the Consortium or Company, will not be held responsible or liable, at any point of time, directly or indirectly. 41. For all the aforesaid reasons, the impugned arbitral award dated 21.08.2024 is quashed and set aside and this petition is allowed and disposed of. 42. All pending applications stand disposed of.

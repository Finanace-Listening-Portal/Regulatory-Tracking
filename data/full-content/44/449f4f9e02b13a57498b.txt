Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); Shekhar Basappa Wali Vs Shrinivasa Traders (GSTAT Bengaluru) Summary: The GST Appellate Tribunal, State Bench Bengaluru, allowed the Revenue’s appeal against the Commissioner (Appeals) order that had set aside penalties imposed on Shrinivasa Traders under Sections 122(1)(vii) and 122(1)(x) of the Central Goods and Services Tax Act, 2017. The case arose out of an investigation into alleged fake invoicing and arecanut trading. The original adjudicating authority had dropped the proposed tax and interest demands but imposed two penalties of ₹4,28,42,308 each, totalling ₹8,56,84,616. The Commissioner (Appeals) deleted both penalties, questioning the evidence and statutory basis. The Tribunal examined statements recorded under Section 70, searches of premises, cancellation of suppliers’ and recipients’ GST registrations before invoice dates, and vehicle numbers in e-way bills that corresponded to unsuitable vehicles or could not be found in official records. It considered the taxpayer’s admissions about fake invoices and reuse of documents for multiple consignments, which had not been retracted at the earliest opportunity. On Section 122(1)(x), the Tribunal held that Circular No. 171/03/2022-GST did not bar penalties under other applicable statutory clauses merely because it identified Sections 122(1)(ii) and 122(1)(vii) in fake-invoice scenarios. The findings concerning false accounts, invoices and GST returns sufficiently specified the alleged offence. As to Section 122(1)(vii), statements recorded by proper officers could be relied upon in the circumstances; the Tribunal distinguished the precedents relied on by the Commissioner (Appeals). It also held that the taxpayer had not properly substantiated additional documentary evidence relied upon in the first appeal under Rule 112. Sections 16(2)(b), 16(2)(c) and 155 required the taxpayer claiming ITC to demonstrate actual receipt of goods and relevant supplier tax payment. The taxpayer had not supplied sufficient evidence of real transactions or goods movement. The Tribunal further distinguished Circular No. 64/38/2018-GST on minor vehicle-number errors because the very existence or suitability of the vehicles was disputed. It set aside the order-in-appeal dated 03.05.2024 and restored the two penalties under the order-in-original dated 25.01.2023, together with the stated appropriation of ₹22,00,400 and attached bank balances. Order-in-appeal (19) The Appellate Authority held that the adjudicating authority had dropped the demand of GST and the fraudulent ITC availed by the appellant; however, it confirmed the penalties under Section 122(1) (vii) and Section 122(1) (x) of the CGST Act, 2017. The appellate authority set-aside the penalty levied under Section 122(1)(x) of the Act, accepting the contention of the taxpayer that the levy of penalty was not only contrary to the circular of the Board in Circular No. 171/3/2022-GST dated 6.7.2022 but was also imposed casually, without specifying the nature of the offence. (20) Regarding the penalty imposed under Section 122(1)(vii), the Appellate Authority had deleted the penalty for the following reasons: Shri Shridhar Hegde’s statement contained no admission of the offence. Relying on the Supreme Court’s decision in Commissioner of Central Excise vs. CERA Boards and Doors, Kannur, it was held that each transaction must be viewed independently, and applying evidence from one transaction horizontally to all others was not acceptable under law. None of the inward suppliers or recipients was confronted or investigated. Neither the taxpayer’s statements nor Mr Shridhar Hedge’s statements led to an admission that no underlying supply existed, either inward or outward. Referring to paras 34 and 46 of the OIO, the Adjudicating Authority was held to be contradicting itself. The adjudicating authority failed to conclude that there were no transactions at all. Even if some statements indicate the creation of fake documents for Bill trading, it is unclear under which GSTIN those transactions occurred. The bank statements and the Purchase account clearly indicate an underlying supply of goods. The order indicated that some transactions were made without paying taxes. The order’s findings were based on mere presumption. The suo motu cancellation of registrations was done with retrospective effect, i.e. from the date of transaction. The Appellate Authority relied on the Kolkata High Court’s decision in Gargo Traders Vs. The Joint Commissioner of Commercial Taxes (State Tax) [(2023) 7 Centax 136] to contend that retrospective cancellation will not deprive the benefit of ITC. The investigating officers found discrepancies in the vehicle numbers carrying goods in a few countable transactions. CBIC, vide circular No. 64/38/2018-GST dated 14.09.2018, clarified that an error in one or two digits/characters of the vehicle number is a minor error and is liable for a minor penalty of Rs. 500/- each under CGST and SGST, and it does not invite action under Section 129 of the Act. Because of an error in the vehicle number in the E-way bills, not all transactions can be treated as fake. Without corroborative evidence, the statements cannot form the basis for an allegation of availment and utilisation of entire ITC without the underlying supply of goods, whether inward or outward. The taxpayer had not admitted that he had issued bills without underlying supply of goods. The Appellate Authority relied on the Tribunal’s decision in (376) ELT 666 (Habib Uz Zaman v. Commissioner of Customs, New Delhi) and 2022 (63) GSTL 64 (J.P. Iscon Private Limited vs. Commissioner of Central Excise, Ahmedabad). Penalty under Section 122(1)(vii) of the GST Act cannot be imposed in the absence of clear grounds/corroborative evidence. (21) The revenue has filed the present appeal before this Tribunal, assailing the Appellate Authority’s order. As per the above Circular, the taxpayer is liable for penalty under Section 122(1)(vii) of the Act, 2017. As the appellate authority is part of the Department, the CBIC Circular binds it. Findings (24) The respondent being absent, we heard the appellant’s submissions and perused the connected records. The following issues arise for our consideration: (a) Whether the deletion of the penalty imposed under Section 122(1)(x) of the Central Goods and Services Tax Act, 2017, was just and valid? (b) Whether the deletion of the penalty imposed under Section 122(1)(vii) of the Central Goods and Services Tax Act, 2017, was just and valid? (a) Whether the deletion of the penalty imposed under Section 122(1)(x) of the Central Goods and Services Tax Act, 2017, was just and valid? (25) Section 122(1)(x) of the Act is applicable where a taxable person falsifies or substitutes financial records or produces fake accounts or documents or furnishes any false information or return with an intention to evade payment of tax under this Act, then the taxable person shall be liable to pay a penalty of Rs. 10000/- or the amount of tax evaded, whichever is higher. (26) The Appellate Authority would state that, as per serial no. 3 of the Board’s circular in 171/3/2022-GST dated 6.7.2022, penalty can be levied only under section 122(1)(ii) and Section 122(1)(vii) of the Act but penalty was levied under Section 122(1)(vii) and Section 122(1)(x) of the Act. The appellate authority accepted the contention of the taxpayer that the levy of penalty under Section 122(1)(x) of the Act was contrary to the Board’s circular and also was imposed without specifying the nature of the offence. (27) Circular No. 171/03/2022-GST dated 6.7.2022 was issued in respect of transactions involving fake invoices. Serial No. 3 of the circular states that where a taxpayer claims ITC on the basis of invoices without actual receipt of goods or services or both and issues invoices without actual supply of goods or services or both, then no demand can be made from the taxpayer under Section 73 or Section 74, as the case may be. However, the taxpayer shall be liable for penal action under Section 122(1)(ii) and Section 122(1)(vii) of the Act. The circular also states that Actual action to be taken against a person will depend upon the specific facts and circumstances of the case which may involve complex mixture of above scenarios or even may not be covered by any of the above scenarios. Any person who has retained the benefit of transactions specified under sub-section (1A) of section 122 of CGST Act, and at whose instance such transactions are conducted, shall also be liable for penal action under the provisions of the said sub-Section. The Adjudicating Authority has acted in accordance with the circular when he dropped the proceedings to impose tax under Section 74. The Appellate Authority ought to have considered the circular in its entirety before accepting the taxpayer’s submissions. A Board circular cannot be interpreted contrary to a statutory provision. Serial no. 3 of Circular No. 171/03/2022-GST dated 6.7.2022 does not state that penalty can be levied only under Section 122(1)(ii) and Section 122(1)(vii) of the Act. A restrictive interpretation of the circular that curtails the proper officer’s power to invoke the various subclauses in Section 122(1) is not correct. (28) Section 122(1)(x) can be invoked if the taxpayer falsifies or substitutes the financial records or produces fake accounts or documents or furnishes any false information or return with an intention to evade payment of tax. The taxpayer gave contradictory statements during the investigation. Initially, he denied any connection with Shrinivasa Traders, but later admitted he was its proprietor. Again, he initially stated that he purchased arecanut for cash from farmers, but later stated that he purchased it from traders. The taxpayer admitted to issuing and receiving bills without supplying goods. The invoices reflected in the returns were dated after the supplier’s and the recipient’s registration certificates were cancelled. The ITC was claimed and utilised on the basis of invoices without underlying receipt or supply of goods; the vehicle numbers found in the invoices and e-way bills pertained to tankers, 3-wheeler passenger vans, and delivery vans; and, in many cases, the ‘vaahan.nic.in’ website showed the registration numbers as ‘vehicle detail not found’. The Adjudicating Authority stated in para 46 that the taxpayer created fake accounts and documents without any underlying supply of goods and filed GST returns without any underlying supply of goods. (29) Therefore, the reasoning of the Appellate Authority that penalty under section 122(1)(x) was levied without specifying the nature of offence is not correct. The order under Section 74 was not passed against the taxpayer in view of the Board’s circular (cited supra). We hold that the deletion of penalty under section 122(1)(x) of the Act is improper and unjustified. (b) Whether the deletion of the penalty imposed under Section 122(1)(vii) of the Central Goods and Services Tax Act, 2017, was just and valid? (30) Section 122(1)(vii) of the Act states that where a taxable person takes or utilises input tax credit without actual receipt of goods or services or both either fully or partially, in contravention of the provisions of the Act or the rules made there under, then the taxable person shall be liable to a penalty of Rs.10000/- or the tax evaded, whichever is higher. (31) The taxpayer gave statements to the authorities under section 70 of the Act, which empowers a proper officer to summon persons to give evidence and produce documents. The Section states that a proper officer may summon a person whose attendance he considers necessary to give evidence or produce a document or any other thing in any inquiry. The persons summoned shall be bound to attend in person or by an authorised representative and the person shall state the truth during examination or make statements or produce such documents or other things as may be required. Every such inquiry shall be deemed to be a “judicial proceeding”. (32) Pursuant to the proper officer’s summons, the taxpayer appeared before the authorities on 24.11.20, 18.12.20, 19.12.20, 22.12.20, 23.12.20, and 17.3.21 and gave statements relating to their business transactions. The statements given by the appellant on various dates, under Section 70 of the Act, are binding on the taxpayer as per the principles stated by the Supreme Court in (2023) 11 Centax 101 (Commissioner of Customs [Imports], Mumbai Vs. Ganpati Overseas), wherein the Supreme Court interpreted Section 108 of the Customs Act. The Court held that a customs officer is not a police officer and that a statement made before a customs officer is admissible in evidence and can be used against the person. Section 108 aims to elicit the truth from the person being examined regarding the customs infringement. Since the objective is to ascertain the truth, the customs officer must ensure the truthfulness of the statement so recorded. The Court further held that the adjudicating authority must determine whether it used any duress or coercion in recording such a statement, since it exercises quasi-judicial powers. (33) The Karnataka High Court, in (2023) 8 Centax 2 (Commissioner of Customs, Mangaluru Vs. Imtiaz Ahmed), held that a statement made before a customs officer under Section 70 of the Customs Act is not barred by Section 25 of the Evidence Act. It further held that statements of third parties cannot be treated as statements of co-accused but as independent evidence, for which no other independent evidence was required. The decisions of the Tribunal in 2012 (12) TMI 706 (Windson Chem Industries Vs. Commissioner of Central Excise, Daman), and 2014 (11) TMI 203 (Commissioner of central excise & ST, Ahmedabad Vs. M/S. Paras Steel Industries), and of the Gujarat High Court in 2014 (8) TMI 86 (Tulip Lamcraft Pvt Ltd Vs. C.C.E. & St., Ahmedabad III) are also authorities for the view that further corroboration of clandestine removal was not necessary if the proprietor admits clandestine removal in his statement. (34) Section 70 of the Act is similar to Section 108 of the Customs Act; hence, the principles stated by the Supreme Court (cited supra) and the Karnataka High Court (cited supra) apply to the case at hand. The taxpayer’s statement under Section 70 before a proper officer is binding on him, as the proper officer was not a police officer. The taxpayer has full and complete knowledge of his business transactions and can be relied upon by the proper officer. The Appellate Authority did not find that the statement was obtained by duress or coercion; it only stated that it required further corroborative evidence. The Appellate Authority’s reasoning that it required further corroborative evidence before fastening liability on the taxpayer must be rejected in light of the principles laid down in the aforesaid cases. Courts and tribunals have categorically held that corroborative evidence is not necessary when liability is fixed based on an admission statement made before the proper officer under the Act. Hence, the statement can be relied on for determining the validity of the penalty imposed under Section 122(1)(x) and Section 122(1)(vii) of the Act. (35) The Tribunal’s decision in 2021 (376) ELT 666 (Habib Uz Zaman Vs. Commissioner of Customs, New Delhi), relied upon by the Appellate Authority, is not relevant, as in that case third parties gave statements against the appellant, which the Tribunal rejected on the ground of lack of corroborative evidence. In the present case, the appellant gave a statement under Section 70, admitting that it carried on business without actually receiving and supplying goods. (36) The decision of the Tribunal in 2022 (63) GSTL 64 (J.P. Iscon Private Limited vs. Commissioner of Central Excise, Ahmedabad), relied upon by the appellate authority, is not relevant to the case, as in the case before the Tribunal, the department had relied only on Excel sheets furnished by an employee, and hence the Tribunal held that the same cannot be accepted in the absence of corroborative evidence with any other evidence. The decision was not rendered in the context of section 14 of the Central Excise Act and hence not relevant. Section 14 of the Central Excise Act is similar to Section 108 of the Customs Act. (37) The Appellate Authority relied on the Kolkata High Court decision reported in (2023) 7 Centax 136 (Gargo Traders Vs. The Joint Commissioner of Commercial Taxes (State Tax)) to contend that retrospective cancellation will not deprive the benefit of ITC. In the case before the Court, it was found that the supplier was a registered taxable person and the petitioner had paid the sale price, including tax, through the bank. The Court noted that the petitioner did not allege collusion with the supplier. The Court set-aside the order and directed the proper officer to pass a fresh order after considering the documents filed by the petitioner. The Appellate Authority’s finding that the registration certificates of the suppliers and the recipients were cancelled retrospectively is not supported by the materials on record. The adjudicating authority had held that the registration certificates were cancelled suo motu before the invoice dates. The Kolkata High Court’s decision (supra) does not support the taxpayer’s case. In the case under appeal, the proper officer passed the order after considering the taxpayer’s objections. (38) One other reason the Appellate Authority gave for deleting the penalty was that Shri Shridhar Hegde had not given any statement admitting to any offence. Shri Shridhar Hegde is the proprietor of M/S Shyam Traders, Sirsi, and he gave statements on issues relating to his business. He would state that he had issued e- way bills for many suppliers, including the taxpayer; that he had issued invoices for the taxpayer; that this business of generating e-way bills and invoices was generating income for him. Shri Shridhar Hegde categorically stated that he issued e-way bills and invoices for the taxpayer. The taxpayer failed to explain why he received e-way bills and invoices from a third person instead of generating them himself, given his huge business volume. (39) The Appellate Authority had relied on the decision of the Supreme Court in Commissioner of Central Excise vs. CERA Boards and Doors, Kannur [2020 (373) ELT 794] in support of his reasoning that every transaction has to be viewed independently and any horizontal application of any evidence of a transaction to all other transactions was not acceptable under law. The Supreme Court had not laid down such a proposition. The question before the Supreme Court concerned the valuation of excisable goods only for the purpose of charging duty, as the assessees had not challenged the Tribunal’s order on the existence of undervaluation and consequent evasion of duty. The Court found that, for that purpose, it was necessary to consider the period of assessment, as the method of determining value before 1.7.2000 differed from the method of valuation after 1.7.2000, since Section 4 of the Central Excise Act was amended with effect from 1.7.2000. The Court directed the authorities to follow the prescribed procedure for ascertaining value before and after 1.7.2000. The decision contains nothing to support the Appellate Authority’s finding that the Supreme Court’s directions apply universally, irrespective of the facts and circumstances of each case. Reliance placed on the decision is therefore misplaced. (40) The Appellate Authority would state that the taxpayer did not admit that no underlying supply of goods, either inward or outward, existed. This is not correct. The taxpayer deposed on 17.3.2021 that he was using the premises for fake transactions; that goods were not stored in his premises; that the name and GSTIN was not displayed in the premises; that he had not maintained any accounts in the premises; that he had been providing fake invoices to various traders who used the fake invoices for payment of applicable GST on the supplies made by them; that goods were sent to Solapur under the cover of invoices issued in respect of vehicles which had already passed; details of another vehicle was entered in part B of the e-way bill; that he was working with Shridhar Hegde and others who were using the same set of invoices and e-way bills for transportation of two consignments cleared without payment of GST. The taxpayer had not furnished details of the supplies on which he had paid tax and on which his suppliers had paid tax. (41) The Appellate Authority would rely on bank statements and the Purchase account to show an underlying supply of goods and conclude that the order-in-original’s findings were based on presumptions only. We find that the Adjudicating Authority was not presented with the bank statements. This finding is present on pages 210 and 211 of the order-in-original. Rule 112 of the CGST Rules states that the taxpayer shall not be allowed to produce before the Appellate Authority any evidence other than the evidence produced before the Adjudicating Authority. On page 15 of the order-in-appeal, the Commissioner, Mangaluru, while commenting on the appeal filed by the taxpayer, stated that the taxpayer had not submitted documents, such as the weighment slip and toll receipt, indicating the actual movement of goods. The taxpayer had not submitted a bank statement to support its claim that it transacted only through banks. In fact, the document submitted before the appellate authority is not a bank statement but a ledger copy. The taxpayer has not furnished any reasons for non-production of the records before the Adjudicating Authority. The Appellate Authority ought not to have accepted the bank statements, given the embargo imposed by Rule 112 of the CGST Rules. If the Appellate Authority had decided to accept the documents, he ought to have given reasons for accepting documents not furnished to the Adjudicating Authority. (42) The Adjudicating Authority did not base his order solely on the statements. The order-in-original would disclose that the registration certificates of the suppliers and the recipients of the taxpayer were cancelled prior to the date of the invoice issued to and by the taxpayer. The show cause notice contained the allegation. The taxpayer had not furnished any material to disprove the allegation. Instead, the appellate authority would state that the authorities should have conducted further investigation before fastening liability on the taxpayer. This is incorrect. Further investigation would have been required only if the taxpayer had furnished concrete material to disprove the allegations made in the show cause notice. The burden shifted to the taxpayer to prove the genuineness of his dealings. The taxpayer’s contention was that the supplier and the recipients were genuine and hence the burden was on the taxpayer under Section 155 and Section 16(2)(b) & (c) to prove the genuineness of the transaction. The taxpayer had to prove that he received the goods and that the supplier had paid tax. This is what the taxpayer had to do and failed to do. (43) The order-in-original also disclosed that verification of the vehicle numbers on the online portal “vaahan.nic.in” revealed that the vehicle numbers related to scooters, tankers, tippers, 3-wheelers, etc., and, in many cases, the registration numbers showed “vehicle detail not found”. The adjudicating authority also conducted this verification after the taxpayer received objections. After receiving the objections, the adjudicating authority again verified the online portal and uploaded screenshots of the verification. On page 160 of the order-in-original, the taxpayer stated that, due to a mismatch, the vehicle numbers were not available on the portal. The taxpayer blamed the transporter for the mismatch. The taxpayer furnished no evidence in this regard. (44) The Appellate Authority would reject the findings relating to the vehicle mismatch, relying on the Board’s clarification in Circular No. 64/38/2018-GST dated 14.9.2018. The said circular was issued in the context of detention of conveyance to determine violation of Section 129 and 130 of the Act. Under Sections 129 and 130, the existence of a vehicle for transportation is not disputed; however, in the present case, the existence of the vehicle involved in the transportation of goods is disputed. Reliance placed on the circular is therefore misplaced. (45) The Appellate Authority would allege that the Adjudicating Authority made contrary findings in para 34 and para 46; therefore, the Adjudicating Authority failed to categorically conclude that no transactions occurred. In para 34 of the order-in-original, the Adjudicating Authority states that it is clear that Shri Suresh Bhanushali, Shri Praveen Suvarna, Shri Sachin, Shri Prabhkar Hegde, and many others were members of a racket dealing in fake GST invoices without supply of goods. In many cases, multiple consignments were transported under the strength of one set of invoices and e-way bill where goods moved to one destination and recipient but the documents were used for passing on input tax credit to persons who did not receive the supplies. In para 46 of the order, the Adjudicating Authority states that the Noticee has taken fraudulent ITC and passed on the same without actual supply of goods either on inward end or outward end. The Noticee has created fake books of accounts and documents without underlying supply of goods, has filed GST returns without underlying supply of goods. There is no contradiction in the Adjudicating Authority’s findings when comparing the said 2 paras. In para 34, the Adjudicating Authority referred not only to the taxpayer but also to others, whereas in para 46, the Adjudicating Authority referred only to the taxpayer. (46) Under the Act, the burden is on the taxpayer to prove eligibility for input tax credit. The taxpayer was required to prove that he had received the goods under Section 16(2)(b) and the supplier had paid tax under Section 16(2)(c), to claim the benefit of ITC. The authorities had furnished information in the show cause notice in support of their plea that the taxpayer was claiming the benefit of ITC without actual receipt of goods or services or both. The authorities stated that the suppliers’ and the taxpayer’s recipients’ registration certificates were cancelled before the invoice date. The authorities stated that the vehicle numbers mentioned in the e-way bills were either unavailable or linked to other vehicles that cannot be used to transport goods. The taxpayer did not prove that he received the goods. He also has not proved that the suppliers paid tax to the Government. The taxpayer did not furnish documents to prove the genuineness of his dealings. The taxpayer admitted that he had provided fake invoices to various traders, who used them to pay GST liability. The taxpayer did not retract the statement at the earliest opportunity. We hold that deleting the penalty under Section 122(1)(vii) is invalid and unjustified. Order (1) The appeal is allowed. (2) Order-in-appeal No. MLR-CGST-000-APP-AS-003-2024-25 dated 03.05.2024 is set aside. (3) The penalties under Section 122(1)(vii) and 122(1)(x) imposed in order-in-original No. GEXCOM/GST/46/2022-ADJN dated 25.01.2023 are restored. (4) The appropriation of ?22,00,400 and the attached bank balances stand.

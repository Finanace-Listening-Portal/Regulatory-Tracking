The government has defended the numbers, saying the changes reflect the new 2022-23 base year, improved data sources and methodological changes rather than an attempt to inflate growth.Here is what the controversy is about and how the GDP deflator affects the growth calculation.What exactly is the controversy over the 7.8% GDP growth rate?India’s real GDP grew 7.8% year-on-year in Q1 FY27, compared with 6.9% a year earlier. Nominal GDP grew 10.3%, while real GVA grew 8.2%. The growth was stronger than the Reserve Bank of India’s earlier 7% forecast and market expectations of around 7.1%. Advt The controversy arose because the new GDP series has also significantly revised earlier data. Critics have questioned whether the combination of a new base year, changes in price measures and revisions to previous estimates has resulted in an unusually high real growth rate.The government has rejected that interpretation, saying the new series incorporates improved data sources and methodological changes in line with national accounting practices.What is the GDP deflator?The GDP deflator is the implicit price index obtained by comparing GDP at current prices with GDP at constant prices. It captures the price changes associated with the goods and services included in domestic output.In simple terms, it helps separate the change in the value of economic output into its price and volume components.For Q1 FY27, nominal GDP grew 10.3%, while real GDP grew 7.8%. The relationship between the two implies an increase of roughly 2.3% in the implicit GDP deflator.Why has the GDP deflator become central to the issue?Because the gap between nominal and real GDP growth is relatively small.A 10.3% increase in nominal GDP and 7.8% real growth implies an implicit GDP deflator increase of roughly 2.3%. Critics argue that if the relevant economy-wide price increase were higher, the resulting real GDP growth would be lower. Advt This is why the debate has focused on whether the price measures used in the new GDP series adequately capture price changes across the economy.However, it would be incorrect to conclude that a low GDP deflator automatically means real GDP growth is overstated. The deflator is derived from the prices and weights relevant to different components of national output. It is not simply another measure of CPI or WPI inflation.Why doesn’t the GDP deflator simply use CPI inflation?Because the GDP deflator and consumer price inflation measure different things.CPI measures changes in the prices of a basket of goods and services consumed by households. The GDP deflator captures price changes across the goods and services included in domestic output and also reflects changes in the composition of production. Advt WPI, meanwhile, measures wholesale prices of selected goods. Neither CPI nor WPI can therefore simply be substituted for the GDP deflator.The new national accounts series has introduced more granular price data and expanded the range of deflators used in the national accounts, including the use of producer price data in relevant areas.What has changed in the new GDP series?The latest national accounts series uses 2022-23 as the base year, replacing the earlier base year. MoSPI released the new series in February 2026.The latest estimates incorporate the new Output PPI, a revised Index of Industrial Production series with 2022-23 as its base and updated administrative data. MoSPI has also revised quarterly estimates from Q1 FY23 through Q4 FY26. Advt This means comparisons with older GDP estimates need to be made carefully because the underlying methodology and data sources have changed.What is “double deflation” and why is it being discussed?Double deflation is particularly important for manufacturing.Under the new methodology, MoSPI separately deflates output and intermediate consumption, rather than applying the same deflator to both. Real manufacturing GVA is then calculated as real output minus real intermediate consumption.The objective is to capture changes in both selling prices and input costs more accurately.For example, if manufacturers’ input costs rise faster than their output prices, their real value added can behave differently from their nominal value added. This can result in the implicit manufacturing GVA deflator declining, even when both output and input prices are rising.Such an outcome can reflect changes in relative prices, supply conditions and profit margins rather than necessarily indicating a statistical error.So can a negative manufacturing deflator actually happen?Yes. If input prices rise faster than output prices, the price component of value added can decline. An implicit manufacturing GVA deflator can therefore become negative even when both output and input prices are increasing.That does not mean manufacturing prices are falling across the board. It means that the price movement of value added, which represents output minus intermediate consumption, can be negative.This distinction is critical when interpreting the GDP numbers.Does the 7.8% growth rate therefore depend entirely on a low deflator?No. The deflator is an important part of the conversion from nominal GDP into real GDP, but it is not the only factor determining the growth rate.Q1 FY27 recorded strong real growth across major sectors. Manufacturing grew 9.2%, the secondary sector grew 8.6% and the services sector grew 10%. Financial, real estate, IT and professional services grew 12.1%. On the expenditure side, gross fixed capital formation grew 11.9% and private consumption grew 7.1%.The 7.8% figure therefore reflects both the underlying nominal estimates and the price adjustments used to derive real output.Then why are economists and analysts still questioning the number?The concern is less about one isolated deflator and more about whether the new methodology is producing a real-growth estimate that is consistent with other measures of economic activity.The latest 7.8% growth rate is notably strong despite several headwinds, while the new series has also produced sizeable revisions to historical GDP estimates. This has prompted questions about the comparability of the new data with earlier estimates.At the same time, revisions are a normal part of national accounts as more complete data become available. The key question is whether subsequent revisions and other economic indicators broadly validate the strength of the latest estimate.Does a lower deflator necessarily mean higher “real” growth?Mathematically, yes, for a given nominal GDP figure. If nominal GDP grows 10% and the relevant price index rises 3%, real growth will be roughly 6.8%. If the price index rises only 1%, real growth will be roughly 8.9%.But the important question is whether the price index being used is the appropriate measure of price change for the output being measured.That is why the current debate cannot be settled simply by comparing the GDP deflator with CPI or WPI. The relevant issue is whether the prices and weights used to construct the national accounts deflators appropriately represent the underlying economy.So what should one look at beyond the headline 7.8%?Three things will be particularly important.First, subsequent GDP revisions will show whether the strong Q1 estimate is sustained. MoSPI estimates are subject to revision as more data become available, making future releases important for assessing the durability of the latest growth rate. Second, analysts will need to track the relationship between nominal and real GVA across sectors, rather than relying only on the aggregate GDP deflator.Third, independent indicators such as industrial production, corporate earnings, credit growth, electricity consumption, tax collections and consumption trends can provide a cross-check on the pace of economic activity.What is the bottom line on the GDP-deflator controversy?The GDP deflator is not evidence by itself that India’s 7.8% growth is overstated. It is a necessary part of separating price changes from changes in real economic output. The real debate is whether the new system’s price measures, including the expanded use of producer-price data and double deflation in manufacturing, capture changes in output prices and input costs accurately. For now, the 7.8% Q1 FY27 estimate is the official estimate under the new 2022-23 GDP series. The more meaningful test will be whether subsequent revisions and data across sectors continue to support the strength implied by the headline number.

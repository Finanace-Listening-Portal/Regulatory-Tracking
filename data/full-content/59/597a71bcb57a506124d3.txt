Omission of Rule 96(10): Karnataka High Court Clears the Way for Pending IGST Export Refunds Summary: The Karnataka High Court, in Assistant Commissioner of Central Tax vs. Volvo Group India Pvt. Ltd., W.A. No. 1527/2026, 2026, decided on 13 November 2026, dismissed the Revenue’s appeal concerning an IGST refund dispute arising from exports made before the omission of Rule 96(10) of the CGST Rules, 2017. The dispute concerned the rejection of an IGST refund claim of ₹4,38,95,926 relating to exports made during March 2020. The decision follows the Supreme Court judgment in Goodluck India Ltd. v. Union of India, 2026 INSC 821, which held that the omission of Rule 96(10), without a saving clause, applies to proceedings pending on the date of omission. Rule 96(10) previously restricted IGST export refunds where specified exemptions or concessional procurement benefits had been availed. Notification No. 20/2024-Central Tax omitted the restriction with effect from 8 October 2024. Subsequently, CBIC Circular No. 264/10/2026-GST, dated 26 October 2026, clarified that the omission operates retrospectively from 23 October 2017 and extended similar treatment to Rules 89(4A) and 89(4B). The Division Bench declined to interfere with the Single Judge’s order directing the grant of refund. However, the available records do not reconcile the original refund claim of ₹4,38,95,926 with the amount of ₹3,11,25,210 directed to be refunded by the Single Judge. Exporters facing pending refund disputes should establish the status of their proceedings on 8 October 2024 and identify the department’s specific reliance on Rule 96(10). The omission does not automatically dispense with independent statutory refund conditions or authorise the reopening of concluded proceedings. The rule was intended to prevent a double benefit, but the GST Council’s Law Committee found that its operation was causing unnecessary complications without serving the intended purpose (CBIC Circular No. 264/10/2026-GST, para. 3; Goodluck India, paras. 4 and 7). The Government accordingly omitted the sub-rule with effect from 8 October 2024 through Notification No. 20/2024-Central Tax, without inserting a clause to preserve pending proceedings. The distinction is between the date on which a rule is removed and the authority to continue applying it after that date. In Goodluck India, paras. 5–7, the Supreme Court followed the Constitution Bench decision in Kolhapur Canesugar Works Ltd. v. Union of India, (2000) 2 SCC 536, and held that Section 6 of the General Clauses Act, 1897 did not save proceedings under the omitted rule. The GST Council’s recommendation that the omission be prospective was advisory; it could not take the place of a saving clause in the notification. Retrospective Applicability From 23 October 2017 Following the Supreme Court’s ruling, paragraph 6 of CBIC Circular No. 264/10/2026-GST, dated 26 October 2026, clarified that the omission would take effect retrospectively from 23 October 2017, the date from which Rule 96(10) originally operated. The Board directed field formations to deal with pending proceedings in accordance with Goodluck India and extended the same treatment, with necessary adaptations, to the omission of Rules 89(4A) and 89(4B). The two dates answer different questions. 8 October 2024 is the date on which the rule was omitted and the date against which pendency is tested; 23 October 2017 is the date from which the CBIC says the omission operates retrospectively (Goodluck India, paras. 3 and 7; CBIC circular, paras. 3–6). Thus, a refund for exports made in 2020 is not outside the ruling merely because the exports predate the notification. The relevant questions are whether the matter was pending on 8 October 2024 and whether the department’s objection rests on Rule 96(10). Volvo Group India Case: Refund Amount and Judicial Relief The Volvo Refund Figure: What the Record Supports Taxscan reports, and the Division Bench judgment confirms, that Volvo claimed ₹4,38,95,926 as refund of IGST paid on exports of finished goods during March 2020. The refund application was rejected through an Order-in-Original dated 21 July 2025. The Division Bench judgment identifies the order under appeal as the one passed in W.P. No. 32359/2025. However, the operative portion of the Single Judge’s order, as referred to in the available records, directs a refund of ₹3,11,25,210 with applicable interest. The available records do not reconcile the two figures. Accordingly, ₹4.39 crore should be described as the original refund claim amount rather than the precise amount directed to be refunded by the Division Bench. Precedents Followed by the Karnataka High Court The Single Judge had followed the Karnataka High Court’s earlier decision in M/s Hikal Limited v. Union of India and Others, W.P. No. 15251/2020, decided on 9 December 2025, while directing the grant of refund. The Division Bench subsequently considered the Supreme Court’s ruling in Goodluck India and noted the absence of a saving or sunset clause preserving proceedings under the omitted Rule 96(10). The Court also took note of CBIC Circular No. 264/10/2026-GST and dismissed the Revenue’s appeal. Practical Implications for Exporters With Pending Refund Disputes What to Put on Record in a Pending Case A reply or appeal should begin with the department’s own notice, withholding communication or order. Identify the paragraph that invokes Rule 96(10), record the status of the proceeding on 8 October 2024, and specify the relief sought. Where that rule is the sole ground, request that the refund be processed or the proposed recovery be dropped, as the case may be, relying on Goodluck India and paragraph 6 of the CBIC circular. Independent Refund Conditions and Concluded Proceedings If the department has raised an independent objection, such as a mismatch between the shipping bills and the IGST payment records, answer it separately with the relevant reconciliation. Goodluck India removes the Rule 96(10) restriction from pending proceedings; it does not dispense with other applicable refund conditions or direct the automatic reopening of concluded cases. Conclusion The real value of Volvo for practitioners lies not in the amount reported in the headline, but in the answer to the department’s objection: an earlier export date is not enough to sustain a restriction that was omitted without a saving clause (Volvo case record; Goodluck India, paras. 5–8). In a representation, the strongest submission is therefore a specific one: show that the matter was pending on 8 October 2024, point to the precise reliance on Rule 96(10), and ask for the claim to be decided without that restriction.

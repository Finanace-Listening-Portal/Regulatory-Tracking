Ashwathanarayan Vs Union of India (Karnataka High Court) ED Cannot Continue PMLA Probe After Final Closure of Predicate Case: Karnataka HC The Karnataka High Court has held that the Enforcement Directorate cannot continue a money-laundering investigation where the underlying predicate case has been closed for want of prosecution sanction and that closure has attained finality. The Court further held that ED cannot itself institute a private complaint concerning a scheduled offence and then use that complaint as the foundation for proceedings under the Prevention of Money-Laundering Act, 2002 (PMLA). Allowing five connected writ petitions, it quashed the challenged proceedings concerning the petitioners. Background of the Dispute The proceedings originated from allegations concerning the grant of approximately 80 acres of land to 38 persons. K.Y. Nanje Gowda, then Chairman of the Land Grant Committee at Malur, and other committee members were accused of abusing their official positions. A private complaint alleging cheating, forgery and criminal conspiracy resulted in registration of Crime No. 489/2022. Treating these allegations as the predicate offence, ED registered ECIR/BGZO/41/2023 and commenced investigation. During searches, ED allegedly discovered material concerning irregularities in recruitment to Kolar and Chikkaballapur Milk Union Ltd. (KOMUL). This led to summons and proceedings involving the petitioners. ED also sought retention of seized material before the Adjudicating Authority, which confirmed the seizure on June 6, 2024. Closure of the Predicate Case In the land case, the police filed a closure report. The original complainant filed a protest petition, but prosecution required government sanction because the allegations concerned a public servant. As the necessary sanction was not obtained, the Trial Court dismissed the case for non-prosecution. The High Court recorded that the closure had attained finality. Meanwhile, ED shared information concerning the alleged recruitment irregularities with the competent authorities under Section 66(2) of PMLA. When those authorities did not initiate proceedings, ED filed PCR No. 32/2024, seeking investigation by the Lokayukta Police into alleged corruption and IPC offences. Arguments of the Petitioners and ED The petitioners argued that PMLA proceedings could not survive closure of the predicate case, whether the closure occurred on merits or technical grounds. They also submitted that ED could share information about another suspected scheduled offence with the competent agency, but could not itself create the foundation for a fresh money-laundering investigation through a private complaint. ED contended that closure on technical grounds, rather than merits, did not prevent continuation of its investigation. It further argued that, like any other person, it could initiate criminal proceedings through a complaint under Section 200 CrPC or Section 223 BNSS. Predicate Offence Is Essential Examining Sections 3 and 2(1)(u) of PMLA, the Court emphasised that money laundering must concern property derived or obtained from criminal activity relating to a scheduled offence. Relying on Vijay Madanlal Choudhary v. Union of India and V. Senthil Balaji v. Deputy Director, Directorate of Enforcement, it held that the existence of a scheduled offence is essential to the allegation of proceeds of crime. The Court described ED’s authority as derivative, requiring an existing predicate offence. It could not initiate proceedings merely on an independent suspicion of money laundering. In this case, the land complaint had finally closed for want of sanction. Consequently, continuation of the investigation into alleged proceeds arising from that case could not be sustained. ED Cannot Assume Another Agency’s Role The Court held that Section 66(2) requires ED to forward information concerning suspected violations of other laws to the appropriate agency. The PMLA did not expressly empower ED to file a private complaint concerning a scheduled offence and thereafter investigate money laundering on that foundation. Emphasising statutory limits and institutional checks, the Court rejected the argument that ED could act like an ordinary private complainant. Authorities appointed under PMLA must exercise the powers conferred by that legislation. Permitting ED to initiate the predicate complaint whenever the competent agency declined to proceed would place it above other investigating authorities, contrary to the statutory scheme. Final Decision and Significance The High Court allowed all five petitions. It quashed the seizure-confirmation order concerning Y.B. Ashwathanarayan and directed return of articles seized from him, if any. It also quashed the challenged ECIR, summons and consequential proceedings concerning the relevant petitioners, together with the private complaint proceedings to the extent specified in its operative directions. The ruling turns on the final closure of the predicate case and the limits of ED’s authority under PMLA. It reinforces that information-sharing powers cannot substitute for prosecution of a scheduled offence by the competent agency. FULL TEXT OF THE JUDGMENT/ORDER OF KARNATAKA HIGH COURT One Sri K.Y. Nanje Gowda, the petitioner in W.P. No.4630/2025, was the Chairman of the Land Grant Committee of Malur, Kolar District. There was an allegation made against him that he, in collusion with other Members of the Land Grant Committee, recommended grant of land measuring about 80 acres for about 38 persons and that the same was done by abusing their official position, in an illegal manner (hereinafter referred to as ‘Land Scam Case’). While conducting the search, the ED came across certain materials, which, in its opinion, amounted to a scam pertaining to recruitments of employees to KOMUL (hereinafter referred to as ‘Job Scam Case’). 3. In the aforementioned writ petitions, the petitioners have challenged the actions of the ED in continuing the investigation, in respect of the Land Scam Case, even after the predicate offence being dismissed for non-prosecution and also the action of the ED in filing a private complaint, all by itself, against the petitioners in the Job Scam Case, when the concerned police/authority did not initiate any action based upon the information received under Section 66(2) of the PMLA. 12. The case of the petitioners is that once a predicate offence is closed, either on merits or on technical grounds, the ED cannot continue investigation under the provisions of PMLA and when ED, while investigation into proceeds of crime under one offence, comes across any material which in its opinion, amounts to commission of an altogether different scheduled offence (predicate offence), the ED is only empowered to share the information with the relevant police/authority and it is not empowered to present a private complaint and take up the matter for investigation alleging that the accused therein are benefitted by the proceeds of crime. 13. The case of the ED is that once the investigation is taken up based on the existence of a predicate offence and the same gets closed on technical grounds and not on merits, still the ED is empowered to investigate into the offence of money laundering under the provisions of PMLA. It is also contended that when the concerned authority does not act upon the information received under Section 66(2) of the PMLA, then in that event, as any person is empowered to set into motion the criminal proceedings, the ED is empowered to maintain a private complaint under Section 200 of the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973/Section 223 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023. On the said grounds, it is prayed that the writ petitions be dismissed. 14. The questions that arise under the instant petitions are: i. Can the Enforcement Directorate continue investigation into proceeds of crime under the provisions of the PMLA when the predicate offence is closed on technical ground such as for want of sanction from the concerned authority? ii. When the Enforcement Directorate, based on the material in its possession, is of the opinion that some other law in force is contravened, shares the information with the concerned agency for necessary action as per Section 66(2) of the PMLA and the said agency fails to initiate any prosecution against the accused, then in that event, can the Enforcement Directorate prefer a complaint under Section 200 Cr.P.C./223 of Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023 before the jurisdictional Magistrate and based on the said complaint can it initiate proceedings under the PMLA? 15. Section 3 of PMLA reads as under: “3. Offence of money-laundering.— Whosoever directly or indirectly attempts to indulge or knowingly assists or knowingly is a party or is actually involved in any process or activity connected with the proceeds of crime including its concealment, possession, acquisition or use and projecting or claiming it as untainted property shall be guilty of offence of money-laundering. Explanation.—For the removal of doubts, it is hereby clarified that,— (i) a person shall be guilty of offence of money-laundering if such person is found to have directly or indirectly attempted to indulge or knowingly assisted or knowingly is a party or is actually involved in one or more of the following processes or activities connected with proceeds of crime, namely:— a. concealment; or b. possession; or c. acquisition; or d. use; or e. projecting as untainted property; or f. claiming as untainted property, in any manner whatsoever; (ii) the process or activity connected with proceeds of crime is a continuing activity and continues till such time a person is directly or indirectly enjoying the proceeds of crime by its concealment or possession or acquisition or use or projecting it as untainted property or claiming it as untainted property in any manner whatsoever.” 16. Section 2(1)(u) of PMLA defines ‘proceeds of crime’ which reads as under: “(u) “proceeds of crime” means any property derived or obtained, directly or indirectly, by any person as a result of criminal activity relating to a scheduled offence or the value of any such property or where such property is taken or held outside the country, then the property equivalent in value held within the country or abroad; Explanation.—For the removal of doubts, it is hereby clarified that “proceeds of crime” include property not only derived or obtained from the scheduled offence but also any property which may directly or indirectly be derived or obtained as a result of any criminal activity relatable to the scheduled offence;” 17. Thus, for an investigation or an inquiry to be made as to the existence of ‘proceeds of crime’ or laundering of the money obtained through proceeds of crime can be made only if there is pre-existence of a predicate offence and not otherwise. The Apex Court in Vijay Madanlal Choudhary and Others Vs. Union of India and Others1, in paragraphs 148, 149 and 152, has held as under: “148. The next question is: Whether the offence under Section 3 is a stand-alone offence? Indeed, it is dependent on the wrongful and illegal gain of property as a result of criminal activity relating to a scheduled offence. Nevertheless, it is concerning the process or activity connected with such property, which constitutes offence of money laundering. The property must qualify the definition of “proceeds of crime” under Section 2(1)(u) of the 2002 Act. 149. As observed earlier, all or whole of the crime property linked to scheduled offence need not be regarded as proceeds of crime, but all properties qualifying the definition of “proceeds of crime” under Section 2(1)(u) will necessarily be crime properties. Indeed, in the event of acquittal of the person concerned or being absolved from allegation of criminal activity relating to scheduled offence, and if it is established in the court of law that the crime property in the case concerned has been rightfully owned and possessed by him, such a property by no stretch of imagination can be termed as crime property and ex consequenti proceeds of crime within the meaning of Section 2(1)(u) as it stands today. On the other hand, in the trial in connection with the scheduled offence, the court would be obliged to direct return of such property as belonging to him. It would be then paradoxical to still regard such property as proceeds of crime despite such adjudication by a court of competent jurisdiction. It is well within the jurisdiction of the court concerned trying the scheduled offence to pronounce on that matter. 152. Even though the 2002 Act is a complete code in itself, it is only in respect of matters connected with offence of money laundering, and for that, existence of proceeds of crime within the meaning of Section 2(1)(u) PMLA is quintessential. Absent existence of proceeds of crime, as aforesaid, the authorities under the 2002 Act cannot step in or initiate any prosecution.” 18. Further, the Apex Court in Senthil Balaji Vs. Deputy Director, Directorate of Enforcement2, in paragraph 21 has held as under: “21. Hence, the existence of a scheduled offence is sine qua non for alleging the existence of proceeds of crime. A property derived or obtained, directly or indirectly, by a person as a result of the criminal activity relating to a scheduled offence constitutes proceeds of crime. The existence of proceeds of crime at the time of the trial of the offence under Section 3 of PMLA can be proved only if the scheduled offence is established in the prosecution of the scheduled offence. Therefore, even if the trial of the case under the PMLA proceeds, it cannot be finally decided unless the trial of scheduled offences concludes. In the facts of the case, there is no possibility of the trial of the scheduled offences commencing in the near future. Therefore, we see no possibility of both trials concluding within a few years.” 19. Thus, the role of ED under PMLA commences only after a primary law enforcement agency registers a FIR or a formal criminal complaint regarding scheduled/predicate offence. The power of the ED is derivative and not original. It cannot initiate an investigation suo motu based on a standalone suspicion of money laundering. If there is no existence of the predicate offence, then in that event, proceedings under PMLA cannot exist; meaning even when the predicate offence is terminated in favour of the accused therein, the proceedings under PMLA cannot continue. 20. In the instant case, admittedly, the private complaint filed in respect of the Land Scam Case has been closed for want of sanction from the competent authority. It has reached finality. Under the circumstances, the question of any person being convicted for the said offences does not arise. Thus, under the given facts and circumstances of the case, the action of the ED continuing the investigation or inquiry into existence of proceeds of crime derived from the scheduled offences alleged in the Land Scam Case cannot be sustained. 21. In respect of the ED preferring a private complaint under Section 200 Cr.P.C/223 BNSS, when the concerned authorities did not act on the information provided by the ED under Section 66(2) of the PMLA and on the strength of the same, it has to be held that the ED is not empowered to do so for the following reasons: (i) A perusal of Section 66(2) of PMLA points out that if, during the course of investigation, the ED comes across violations of other provisions of law, it is duty bound to intimate the concerned agency and not take up the investigation by itself. In law, where an act is to be done in a particular way, it must be done in that way and in no other way. Our Constitution envisages separation of powers between different organs of government. The reason being that one institution in the country does not become supreme so that it is in a position to abuse the powers conferred on it. Law recognizes the requirements of checks and balances. The ED is not a super cop to investigate anything and everything which comes into its notice. Section 48 of the PMLA envisages the authorities under the Act. Once appointed under the PMLA, they are bound by the restraints imposed on them by the PMLA. Section 66(2) of PMLA envisages that if the ED is of the opinion that it is having certain materials which constitute a scheduled offence, then in that event, the same should be forwarded to the concerned agency. The Act does not expressly empower the ED or the authorities under the PMLA to prefer a private complaint in respect of a scheduled offence and on the strength of it, thereafter, take up investigation against the accused therein under the provisions of PMLA. In fact, maintaining law and order is the responsibility of the State and not the Centre. It is the duty of the concerned agency to examine the same and to take a decision whether to prosecute the matter further or not. The ED is not like any other person to file a complaint under Section 200 Cr.P.C./223 of BNSS. It has to exercise only those powers expressly conferred on it by the PMLA. Filing of a private complaint by the ED is not contemplated under the PMLA. Allowing the ED to prefer a private complaint, when the other government authorities deem it inappropriate to initiate any proceedings against anybody, will amount to permitting the ED to act as a super cop and it goes against the philosophy of the Act which requires a predicate offence to be in existence for the ED to initiate proceedings as contemplated under the PMLA. If the ED is permitted to file a private complaint, it will be in a position to file a private complaint against any person by itself and take up investigation against them. This is not contemplated under the provisions of the PMLA. In paragraphs 151 and 211 of Vijay Madanlal Choudhary (supra), the Apex Court has held as under: “151. It is possible that in a given case after the discovery of huge volume of undisclosed property, the authorised officer may be advised to send information to the jurisdictional police [under Section 66(2) of the 2002 Act] for registration of a scheduled offence contemporaneously, including for further investigation in a pending case, if any. On receipt of such information, the jurisdictional police would be obliged to register the case by way of FIR if it is a cognizable offence or as a non-cognizable offence (NC case), as the case may be. If the offence so reported is a scheduled offence, only in that eventuality, the property recovered by the authorised officer would partake the colour of proceeds of crime under Section 2(1)(u) of the 2002 Act, enabling him to take further action under the Act in that regard. 211. This argument clearly overlooks the overall scheme of the 2002 Act. As noticed earlier, it is a comprehensive legislation, not limited to provide for prosecution of person involved in the offence of money laundering, but mainly intended to prevent money laundering activity and confiscate the proceeds of crime involved in money laundering. It also provides for prosecuting the person involved in such activity constituting offence of money laundering. In other words, this legislation is an amalgam of different facets including setting up of agencies and mechanisms for coordinating measures for combating money laundering. Chapter III is a provision to effectuate these purposes and objectives by attachment, adjudication and confiscation. The adjudication is done by the adjudicating authority to confirm the order of provisional attachment in respect of proceeds of crime involved in money laundering. For accomplishing that objective, the authorities appointed under Chapter VIII have been authorised to make inquiry into all matters by way of survey, searches and seizures of records and property. These provisions in no way invest power in the authorities referred to in Chapter VIII of the 2002 Act to maintain law and order or for that matter, purely investigating into a criminal offence.” The decision of the Apex Court in Mukesh Singh Vs. State (Narcotic Branch of Delhi)3 is not applicable to the present case as the same pertains to the NDPS Act and not the PMLA. (ii) Examining the instant case itself, the jurisdictional police have filed a ‘B’ Report in the Land Scam Case. Thereafter, the sanctioning authority has not issued necessary sanction to prosecute the alleged accused therein. If we were to accept the contention of the ED in the instant case, it would amount to concluding that the jurisdictional police and the sanctioning authority are guilty of dereliction of their respective duties and the ED alone is correct. Similarly, in Job Scam Case, it amounts to presuming the Lokayukta Police/agency, who have not proceeded against the accused by not initiating any criminal proceedings based on the information provided by the ED, are guilty of dereliction of their respective duties and putting the ED on a higher pedestal, which is not contemplated in law. The scheme of the PMLA envisages that prosecution regarding the predicate offence has to be initiated by the respective prosecuting agency/third party, but not ED or Authorities constituted under the PMLA. 22. For the aforementioned reasons, the petitions deserve to be allowed. Hence, the following: ORDER i. All the petitions are allowed. ii. The respondents are directed to return the articles seized, if any, from the petitioner in W.P. No.18821/2024. iv. The ECIR No.BGZO/41/2023/2849 dated 30.11.2023, the summons issued pursuant thereto and the consequential proceedings including the show-cause notice dated 16.02.2024 in O.A. No.1132/2024, impugned in W.P.8914/2024 and W.P. No.22460/2025, are hereby quashed insofar as it relates to the petitioners therein are concerned. v.

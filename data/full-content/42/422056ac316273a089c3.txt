Summary: Overseas Direct Investment (ODI) enables Indian companies to establish foreign subsidiaries, enter international joint ventures and acquire overseas businesses, subject to the framework under the Foreign Exchange Management Act, 1999, the Overseas Investment Rules and Regulations, 2022, and RBI’s Master Direction on Overseas Investment. The article explains how investments in listed and unlisted foreign entities qualify as ODI, the general financial commitment limit of 400% of net worth, and the regulatory treatment of equity, loans and guarantees. It examines automatic-route investments, circumstances requiring RBI approval, restricted business activities, overseas financial-services investments, start-up funding and structures involving investment back into India. It also explains corporate approvals under Section 186 of the Companies Act, 2013, documentation through Authorised Dealer banks, Unique Identification Numbers and evidence-of-investment requirements. Continuing compliance includes reporting of financial commitments, Annual Performance Reports, Foreign Liabilities and Assets returns, disinvestment and restructuring filings, and repatriation obligations. Practical guidance addresses recordkeeping, financial planning, compliance responsibilities and exit arrangements, followed by frequently asked questions to help Indian companies manage their overseas investments lawfully and efficiently. Such investments can help companies reach new customers, access technology, strengthen supply chains and develop a presence in strategically important markets. However, investing abroad involves legal responsibilities in India as well as in the destination country. The Indian company must examine whether the proposed investment is permitted, whether its financial commitments fall within the applicable limits and whether the required approvals and reporting arrangements are in place. The Foreign Exchange Management Act, 1999, commonly known as FEMA, provides the foundation for regulating these transactions. Understanding the overseas investment before committing funds helps companies structure their expansion properly and avoid difficulties during remittance, annual reporting or eventual exit. Legal Structure Governing Overseas Direct Investment Foreign Exchange Management Act, 1999 FEMA regulates foreign exchange transactions undertaken by persons resident in India, including investments outside the country. For an Indian company, an overseas investment is therefore both a commercial decision and a regulated cross-border transaction. The company must establish that its proposed investment falls within the permissions available under the applicable. The assessment should begin before the company signs binding investment documents. If the parties agree to a payment schedule without first examining FEMA requirements, they may later find that bank scrutiny, documentation or an approval requirement prevents completion within the agreed period. Overseas Investment Rules and Regulations, 2022 The Foreign Exchange Management (Overseas Investment) Rules, 2022, and the Foreign Exchange Management (Overseas Investment) Regulations, 2022, govern important aspects of overseas investments. These include the nature of permitted investment, financial commitment limits, funding arrangements, investment evidence and reporting obligations. The rules and regulations operate together. Compliance with an investment ceiling alone does not establish that every proposed transaction is permissible. The company must also examine the foreign entity’s activity, the investment instrument, ownership rights and any additional conditions relevant to the transaction. RBI’s Operational Directions RBI’s Master Direction on Overseas Investment explains how is implemented through Authorised Dealer banks. It addresses matters such as transaction documentation, pricing, financial commitments and reporting procedures. In practice, the company should discuss the proposed transaction with its designated bank early. This allows management to identify the supporting documents required and understand how the investment will be processed. Meaning of Overseas Direct Investment Investment in an Unlisted Foreign Entity Acquisition of unlisted equity capital in a foreign entity generally falls within Overseas Direct Investment, or ODI. Subscription to the memorandum of a foreign entity is also covered. Consequently, acquiring a relatively small stake in an unlisted overseas company can still constitute ODI. The Indian investor should not assume that only a majority acquisition requires an ODI assessment. For example, a minority investment in an unlisted foreign technology company should be examined under the overseas direct investment even when the Indian company does not control its management. Investment in a Listed Foreign Entity Investment in at least 10% of the paid-up equity capital of a listed foreign entity constitutes ODI. A holding below 10% can also qualify as ODI where it gives the investor control. The assessment therefore requires a review of more than the number of shares being acquired. The company should examine voting rights, board appointment rights, management arrangements and the terms of any shareholder agreement. Continuing Classification as ODI Once an investment is classified as ODI, it continues to be treated as ODI even if the shareholding subsequently falls below 10% or the investor loses control. This continuity is important when a foreign entity issues additional shares or the Indian investor partially sells its holding. The company should review the consequences of the transaction rather than assume that a reduction in ownership automatically changes the investment’s regulatory treatment. Choosing the Appropriate Overseas Structure Wholly Owned Subsidiary A wholly owned subsidiary allows the Indian company to own the foreign business entirely. This structure can be useful where the company wants to maintain control over its brand, operations, technology and commercial strategy. Nevertheless, complete ownership also places the responsibility for funding and administration on the Indian parent. Before establishing the subsidiary, management should estimate incorporation expenses, working capital, staffing costs, licences, accounting support and other recurring expenditure. Joint Venture A joint venture allows an Indian company to invest alongside a foreign partner. The partner may contribute local market knowledge, distribution channels, technical expertise or operational facilities. The joint venture agreement should clearly explain capital contributions, decision-making powers, profit distribution and exit arrangements. It should also require timely sharing of financial information. Without these arrangements, the Indian investor may struggle to obtain the records needed for its own compliance obligations. Acquisition of an Existing Business Acquiring an established foreign business can provide faster access to customers, employees and operating assets. However, it can also expose the Indian investor to historical liabilities. Due diligence should examine financial statements, contracts, regulatory licences, intellectual property, employment arrangements and disputes. The purchase agreement should address responsibility for identified risks and explain any conditions that must be fulfilled before completion. Financial Commitment Limit for Indian Companies General Limit of 400% of Net Worth An Indian entity’s total financial commitment across all foreign entities generally must not exceed 400% of its net worth according to its last audited balance sheet, subject to the applicable provisions and exceptions. This is an aggregate assessment. The company should consider its existing overseas commitments before determining the capacity available for a new investment. Assessing each foreign subsidiary independently can produce an incorrect picture of the company’s overall exposure. Practical Illustration If a company has qualifying net worth of ₹10 crore, the general ceiling would ordinarily be ₹40 crore. However, the full amount may not remain available for a new transaction if the company already has equity investments, loans, guarantees or other commitments that must be counted. For example, management planning an overseas acquisition should review commitments already extended to its existing foreign businesses. The review should be completed before approving the acquisition budget. Maintaining a Commitment Register A company should maintain an internal register recording each foreign entity, the nature of the commitment, its value and the supporting documentation. The register should be updated whenever the company makes an additional investment or changes a financing arrangement. This gives management a reliable basis for assessing future transactions and helps reconcile legal records with accounting and banking information. Equity Investment, Loans and Guarantees Equity Investment Equity investment should be supported by documents identifying the securities acquired, the subscription or purchase price and the rights attached to the investment. The company should ensure that the board resolution, transaction agreement, remittance instructions and overseas ownership records describe the transaction consistently. Differences in the foreign entity’s name, investment amount or instrument can create questions during processing or subsequent reporting. Loans to the Foreign Entity An Indian entity extending debt must be eligible to make ODI, must have made ODI in the foreign entity and must have acquired control. The loan must be supported by an agreement and carry an arm’s-length interest rate. The agreement should explain the repayment schedule, interest calculation and consequences of default. Management should also assess whether the foreign business can service the loan. A financing structure should reflect the commercial needs and repayment capacity of the overseas entity. Corporate and Performance Guarantees Guarantees can create financial commitment even when the company does not immediately transfer money abroad. They must therefore be considered when assessing the available investment capacity. The regulations prohibit open-ended guarantees. In the case of a performance guarantee, 50% of the guarantee amount is counted towards the financial commitment limit. Before providing a guarantee, the company should understand its validity, maximum exposure and the circumstances in which it may be invoked. Automatic Route and Approval Route Investment Under the Automatic Route A qualifying overseas investment may proceed under the automatic route without case-specific prior approval, provided the relevant conditions are satisfied. The automatic route still involves compliance assessment and bank processing. The company should prepare clear documentation demonstrating the proposed activity, ownership structure, funding arrangement and available financial capacity. Transactions Requiring Prior Approval Financial commitment exceeding USD 1 billion in a financial year requires prior RBI approval even when it falls within the otherwise eligible automatic-route limit. Companies should identify approval requirements before committing to a closing date. Transaction agreements can make completion conditional on obtaining the required permissions, giving the parties a clear basis for managing the timetable. Prohibited and Restricted Business Activities Real Estate Activity The overseas investment restricts ODI in foreign entities engaged in real estate activity as defined in the rules. The definition distinguishes buying and selling real estate or trading in transferable development rights from specified development and construction activities. A property-related investment therefore needs a careful examination of the foreign entity’s actual business. The company should establish whether the entity undertakes property trading, development, construction or another activity instead of relying solely on its name or general description. Gambling Activities ODI in foreign entities engaged in gambling is prohibited under the relevant. The Indian investor should examine the proposed business and its revenue-generating activities before proceeding. For a business operating through a digital platform, the review should consider how the platform functions and what customers pay for. A broad description such as “technology services” may not accurately explain its underlying activity. Financial Products Linked to the Indian Rupee Dealing in financial products linked to the Indian rupee requires specific RBI approval under the relevant restriction. A company considering such an investment should identify the products involved and obtain a clear explanation of the business model before assessing the transaction. The nature of the underlying product is central to this review. Financial Services and Overseas Start-Up Investments Investment in Financial Services Overseas financial-services investments are subject to additional conditions. An Indian entity not engaged in financial services may invest in a foreign financial-services entity other than banking or insurance subject to conditions, including net profits during the preceding three financial years. Specified IFSC provisions differ. The company should examine both its own eligibility and the foreign entity’s business. Activities involving investment advice, lending, insurance or other financial products require particular attention to the applicable regulatory conditions. Investment in Recognised Overseas Start-Ups ODI in recognised overseas start-ups cannot be funded through borrowings. The funding review should therefore establish where the investment money originates. Treasury records, financial statements and supporting documentation should make the source of funds understandable and consistent with the proposed transaction. Overseas Entities That Invest Back into India Reviewing Subsidiary Layers Financial commitment in a foreign entity that invests into India cannot result in a structure with more than two subsidiary layers, subject to statutory exceptions. An ownership chart helps identify the relationship between the Indian investor, the overseas entity, intermediate subsidiaries and any Indian businesses within the structure. The chart should show direct and indirect holdings clearly. Reviewing Future Changes The company should consider planned acquisitions and reorganisations as well as the initial structure. A later transaction can change the compliance position of an arrangement that was acceptable when established. Before adding another entity, management should review the complete ownership chain and assess the effect on the existing investment. Approvals Under the Companies Act, 2013 Board Approval The company must separately assess the corporate approvals relevant to its investment. Section 186 addresses loans, investments, guarantees and securities, including board approval requirements. A detailed board paper should explain the commercial purpose, investment amount, proposed funding and major risks. This helps directors evaluate the transaction and provides a clear record of the company’s decision. Shareholder Approval Section 186 generally requires prior approval through a special resolution when the applicable aggregate limits are exceeded, subject to statutory exemptions. The general limits refer to the higher of 60% of paid-up share capital, free reserves and securities premium, or 100% of free reserves and securities premium. The company should examine the relevant exemptions and record the basis on which an approval is required or an exemption applies. Disclosures and Registers Corporate records should accurately reflect the investment and related financing arrangements. The company should coordinate its statutory registers, financial disclosures and internal transaction records. A consistent record reduces difficulties when auditors, directors or compliance personnel review the investment in subsequent years. Designated Bank, UIN and Investment Evidence Role of the Authorised Dealer Bank The designated Authorised Dealer bank is an important part of the overseas investment process. Early coordination helps the company understand the documentation required and resolve questions before the payment date. The company should appoint a responsible contact who can coordinate between management, the bank, professional advisers and the foreign entity. Unique Identification Number A Unique Identification Number, or UIN, must be obtained through the designated bank before the initial outward remittance or acquisition, whichever occurs earlier. The UIN helps identify the foreign entity for overseas investment records. The company should retain it with the transaction documents and use consistent identifying information in subsequent communications. Evidence of Investment Evidence of investment must generally be submitted within six months of the relevant remittance or capitalisation event. The company should arrange to receive share certificates or equivalent ownership documents promptly after completion. Obtaining these documents should be included in the closing checklist rather than left until the reporting deadline approaches. Reporting Requirements After Investment Reporting Financial Commitment Financial commitment must be reported at the remittance or commitment stage, whichever is earlier. The company should therefore assess reporting when the obligation is created. This is particularly relevant for guarantees and similar arrangements that may arise before an actual cash payment. Annual Performance Report The Annual Performance Report, or APR, is generally due by December 31, subject to prescribed exemptions. Where the foreign entity’s accounting year ends on December 31, the deadline is December 31 of the following year. The Indian investor should establish an information-sharing schedule with overseas management. Financial information and ownership changes should be collected sufficiently early to allow preparation and review. Foreign Liabilities and Assets Return The FLA return is due by July 15 where applicable and is submitted through FLAIR. Its applicability considers outstanding FDI or ODI at the relevant March-end reference dates, including the previous-year position. If audited accounts are unavailable by the deadline, provisional figures may be used, followed by revision through the prescribed approval process. The company should reconcile the return with its accounting records and coordinate its preparation with other overseas investment reports. Disinvestment and Restructuring Reports Disinvestment must be reported within 30 days of receiving the proceeds. Restructuring must be reported within 30 days of the restructuring event. These are event-based obligations. The company should inform its compliance team when a transaction is agreed or completed so that reporting is addressed alongside the commercial arrangements. Repatriation and Exit Planning Bringing Investment-Related Receipts Back to India Investment-related dues and applicable transfer or liquidation proceeds must generally be realised and repatriated within 90 days of the relevant due date or event. The company should track when amounts become payable and maintain supporting records for receipts. Delayed collection should be identified early and discussed with the relevant advisers and bank. Closing an Overseas Investment An exit should address ownership transfer, outstanding loans, guarantees, receipts and reporting. A foreign business that has stopped operating may still require formal closure and supporting documentation. Before treating the investment as closed, management should reconcile all related records and obtain evidence of sale, liquidation or other termination arrangements. Practical Compliance Management Assigning Responsibility Overseas investment compliance often involves several teams, including finance, legal, company secretarial personnel and overseas management. Assigning one person to coordinate the process helps prevent gaps. The coordinator should maintain a calendar, collect documents and retain filing acknowledgements. Responsibilities should remain clear even when employees or overseas managers change. Reviewing Records Regularly Periodic reviews should compare banking records, agreements, investment evidence, accounts and the commitment register. This review can identify differences before another investment is made. It also helps management understand whether earlier transactions have been documented and reported consistently. Conclusion Overseas Direct Investment offers Indian companies an opportunity to enter new markets, build international operations and acquire valuable business capabilities. Successful expansion requires a clear commercial strategy supported by careful legal and financial planning. Before investing, a company should review its eligibility, the foreign entity’s activity, financial commitment capacity, funding arrangements and approval requirements. After completion, it should maintain investment evidence, monitor its commitments and organise annual and event-based reporting. Coordination between management, finance, professional advisers and the designated bank makes the process easier to manage. Clear documentation and assigned responsibilities also help the company respond effectively to future acquisitions, restructuring or exit. What is Overseas Direct Investment? Ans. Overseas Direct Investment, or ODI, includes acquiring equity in an unlisted foreign entity, subscribing to its memorandum or acquiring at least 10% of a listed foreign entity’s paid-up equity capital. Investment below 10% in a listed foreign entity also qualifies as ODI if it gives the Indian investor control. Q2. Can an Indian company establish a wholly owned subsidiary abroad? Ans. Yes, an Indian company can establish a wholly owned subsidiary abroad, subject to the applicable overseas investment conditions. It must examine the proposed business activity, financial commitment limit, funding arrangements and approval requirements. Banking, documentation and reporting obligations must also be fulfilled. Q3. What is the general financial commitment limit? Ans. The general limit is 400% of the Indian entity’s net worth according to its last audited balance sheet, subject to applicable provisions and exceptions. This limit applies to commitments across all foreign entities taken together. The assessment includes relevant equity investments, loans, guarantees and other financial commitments. Q4. Does every overseas investment require prior RBI approval? Ans. No. Qualifying transactions can proceed under the automatic route when all applicable conditions are satisfied. However, certain transactions require prior approval. Financial commitment exceeding USD 1 billion in a financial year requires prior RBI approval even if it remains within the otherwise eligible automatic-route limit. Q5. Can an Indian company lend money to its foreign subsidiary? Ans. Yes, subject to the prescribed conditions. The Indian company must be eligible to make ODI, must have made ODI in the foreign entity and must have acquired control. The loan must be supported by an agreement, carry an arm’s-length interest rate and comply with the financial commitment limit. Q6. Are guarantees counted towards the overseas investment limit? Ans. Yes, guarantees can count towards the financial commitment limit even when no immediate remittance takes place. Their treatment depends on the type of guarantee and applicable conditions. For a performance guarantee, 50% of the amount is counted towards the limit. Open-ended guarantees are not permitted. Q7. Which overseas business activities are restricted? Ans. The restricts ODI in foreign entities engaged in gambling and real estate activity as specifically defined in the rules. Dealing in financial products linked to the Indian rupee requires specific RBI approval. Specified construction and development activities are distinguished from prohibited property trading. Q8. Can borrowed funds be used to invest in an overseas start-up? Ans. ODI in a start-up recognised under the laws of the host country or jurisdiction cannot be funded through borrowings. The Indian company should establish that its funding source meets the applicable requirements. Supporting financial records should clearly explain where the investment funds originate. Q9. What is a Unique Identification Number? Ans. A Unique Identification Number, or UIN, identifies the foreign entity for overseas investment records. It must be obtained through the designated Authorised Dealer bank before the initial outward remittance or acquisition, whichever occurs earlier. Allotment of a UIN does not itself constitute RBI approval of the investment. Q10. When must evidence of overseas investment be submitted? Ans. Evidence must generally be submitted to the designated bank within six months of the relevant remittance or capitalisation event. It may include share certificates or other ownership documents recognised under the host jurisdiction’s laws. Companies should arrange to obtain these documents promptly after completion.

(Delhi High Court) Summary: The Delhi High Court, in Vinod Kujmar Dandona & Ors. Vs Union of India & Anr., considered an application seeking interim protection in a writ petition challenging the Central Government’s order dated 17 August 2018 directing an investigation into the affairs of Educomp Solutions Limited (ESL) by the Serious Fraud Investigation Office (SFIO). The investigation had been ordered under Section 212(1)(c) of the Companies Act, 2013. The petitioners contended that the order was not based on genuine public interest but arose from a private corporate rivalry involving the Raffles Group. Justice Yogesh Khanna issued notice to the respondents and directed that no coercive steps be taken against the petitioners until the next date of hearing. However, the Court expressly declined to grant an interim stay of the operation of the investigation order at that stage. The petitioners sought quashing of the Central Government’s investigation order and the consequential SFIO investigation. Their principal contention was that the statutory power to direct an investigation in public interest had been improperly invoked. According to the petitioners, the action resulted from allegations made by a rival corporate group rather than independently established circumstances warranting investigation under the Companies Act. They further alleged a violation of natural justice and relied on material concerning an Economic Offences Wing (EOW) investigation into complaints made by representatives of the Raffles Group. The petitioners’ senior counsel submitted that the EOW’s final report had identified problems with the authenticity of ledgers allegedly relied upon by the Raffles Group. It was contended that the accountants and statutory auditors of Learning Leadership Foundation and Richmond Education Society had confirmed that certain ledgers accompanying a complaint dated 16 May 2019 were forged. The petitioners accordingly questioned the credibility of the material underlying allegations against them. These matters were advanced as submissions on behalf of the petitioners; the Delhi High Court did not make a final determination concerning the authenticity of those records in its order dated 26 May 2022. The petitioners also relied on earlier orders involving related proceedings. Reference was made to an order dated 3 March 2020 in Vinod Kumar Dandona Vs Reserve Bank of India & Others, W.P.(C) No. 2424/2020, concerning measures following the petitioner’s classification as a fraud. They further relied upon an order dated 10 June 2020 in Vinod Kumar Dandona Vs CBI & Ors., W.P.(CRL) No. 645/2020, which had stayed investigation against the concerned petitioner and restrained coercive action pending further orders. The latter order referred to the existence of earlier complaints, the timing of the FIR and the petitioner’s appointment as a director after certain loan facilities had already been extended. An order dated 9 July 2020 in Jagdish Prakash Vs CBI & Others was also cited, extending the protection contained in the earlier CBI-related order to connected matters. A significant part of the petitioners’ argument relied upon Parmeshwar Das Aggarwal Vs Additional Director, 2016 SCC OnLine Bom 9276. In that case, the Bombay High Court examined the statutory requirements for directing an SFIO investigation and emphasised that the Central Government must possess relevant material supporting its opinion that an investigation is necessary. Although formation of opinion involves subjective satisfaction, the existence of circumstances supporting that opinion must be demonstrable and open to judicial scrutiny. The Bombay High Court had found that the investigation order challenged in that case lacked sufficient supporting material and was improperly founded upon allegations arising from disputes between rival shareholders. The Delhi High Court reproduced substantial extracts from the Bombay High Court’s decision, including its findings that invoking Section 212(1)(c) requires more than a general assertion of public interest. The cited judgment explained that the Central Government’s opinion must have a factual foundation and cannot rest solely on an inadequate Registrar of Companies report or unresolved allegations between private shareholder groups. The petitioners also pointed out that the challenge to the Bombay High Court’s decision before the Supreme Court, in SLP(C) No. 38664/2017, had been dismissed on 19 January 2018. These authorities were placed before the Delhi High Court to support the petitioners’ request for interim relief. In the pending application, the petitioners sought two forms of interim protection. The first was a stay of the operation, effect and implementation of the Central Government’s order dated 17 August 2018 and all proceedings arising from it. The second was a direction restraining coercive action against the petitioners until disposal of the writ petition. The respondents, through counsel, accepted notice and raised the objection that the challenged investigation order was approximately four years old and that the petition suffered from delay and laches. They requested time to file a response. The Court allowed six weeks for filing the response and a further six weeks for any rejoinder. After considering the submissions, the Delhi High Court declined to grant the requested interim stay of the investigation order. Nevertheless, it directed that no coercive steps be taken against the petitioners until the next hearing and listed the matter for 28 September 2022. The order is therefore significant for the limited interim protection granted to the petitioners, rather than for any final determination of the legality of the SFIO investigation. The High Court did not quash the Central Government’s order, decide the merits of the allegations or conclusively hold that public interest was absent. Its operative direction preserved protection against coercive measures while allowing the respondents an opportunity to contest the writ petition. Cases Discussed 1. In Parmeshwar Das Aggarwal vs Additional Director 2016 SCC OnLine Bom 9276, the Bombay High Court held:- 40. Thus, the principle is that there has to be an opinion formed. That opinion may be subjective, but the existence of circumstances relevant to the inference as to the sine qua non for action must be demonstrable. It is not reasonable to hold that the clause permits the Government to say that it has formed an opinion on circumstances which it thinks exist. Since existence of circumstances is a condition fundamental to the making of the opinion, when questioned the existence of these circumstances have to be proved at least prima facie. 48. We do not think that there were materials in the present case and which can be termed as enough to warrant the exercise of power by the Central Government by resorting to section 212(1) of the Act of 2013. The Central Government, in the order under challenge, did not spell out any circumstances, except outlining its power under the above sections to order investigation into the affairs of a company in public interest. None disputes that power or its existence. In para 2 of the impugned order, however, a reference is made to the report of the Registrar of Companies, West Bengal, dated 13th January, 2016. We have already held that the findings in this report are not enough for the Central Government to exercise the drastic power. Something more was required and to be established as circumstances or material enough for exercise of the power. That is clearly lacking in this case. 49. This is the only basis, namely, the report of the Registrar of Companies, West Bengal, or its contents which has enabled the Central Government to exercise its powers under section 212(1)(c). It is, therefore, apparent that it has not necessarily acted in terms of its power conferred by section 212 to direct investigation into the affairs of the company in public interest. The foundation for reaching the opinion or satisfaction is the report of the Registrar. We have referred to the details in that report and we are of the firm opinion that based on that the Central Government could not have recorded a satisfaction or an opinion that investigation into the affairs of the company are necessary. There is no element of public interest which is projected, save and except some vague and general references to certain allegations in matters of bank finance and allotment of coal mines and alleged diversion of raw materials. There has been absolutely no details furnished nor referred in the report. Rather, the report proceeds on the basis that as far as these issues are concerned nothing can be done by the Ministry of Corporate Affairs or the Registrar of Companies. We fail to understand, therefore, how in the present facts and circumstances and based on allegations and counter allegations between two groups of shareholders can it be even held that it is necessary in public interest to direct an investigation into the affairs of the company. Once we reach the conclusion that there is lack of requisite material to arrive at the requisite opinion or record the necessary satisfaction, then, in exercise of our powers of judicial review, we can safely quash and set aside the impugned order. We find that the opinion recorded or the satisfaction reached is vitiated by total non application of mind. None of the factors which are germane and relevant for forming the opinion have been referred. The opinion or satisfaction is based only on the complaint of the Member of Parliament to the CVC and with regard to which report was called for from the Registrar. Even the contents of that report have been, as held above, misread and totally misinterpreted. Based on that no opinion could have been recorded that it is necessary to investigate the affairs of the company in public interest. 50. xxx 51.

Summary : The Madras High Court, in Aqua Terra Coke and Energy Limited Vs Income Tax Officer and connected petitions, examined whether the “clean slate” principle under the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016 automatically extinguishes income-tax and GST liabilities arising during the Corporate Insolvency Resolution Process (CIRP). Justice Mohammed Shaffiq distinguished liabilities preceding the insolvency commencement date from taxes arising while the corporate debtor continues business under insolvency management. The Court rejected an automatic extension of clean-slate protection to CIRP-period dues, while requiring examination of the actual resolution plan and its treatment of tax liabilities. The batch involved approved resolution plans, a going-concern liquidation sale, a compromise under Section 230 of the Companies Act, reassessment, return-filing delays, rectification and penalty. The Court ultimately issued case-specific remand directions rather than finally extinguishing every demand or upholding every assessment. In the principal matter, Aqua Terra Coke and Energy Limited, formerly Bhatia Coke and Energy Limited, entered CIRP on 22 May 2019. The NCLT approved the consortium’s resolution plan on 20 June 2022. The Resolution Professional had not filed income-tax returns during CIRP, leading to applications under Section 119(2)(b) for condonation of delay for AYs 2020–21 and 2021–22. A notice under Section 148A(b), dated 28 February 2024, alleged escapement of income of ₹516,34,65,083 for AY 2020–21; this was the alleged escaped income, not a quantified tax demand. The company challenged the order under Section 148A(d) and consequential reopening notice dated 30 March 2024, as well as the rejection of condonation on 10 May 2024. Cura Healthcare’s connected petition similarly concerned a missing return for AY 2020–21, a condonation application and an order under Section 148A(d) allegedly passed without considering its response. Empee Distilleries’ GST petition concerned FY 2019–20. Its CIRP commenced on 1 November 2018, and SNJ Distilleries’ ₹475 crore resolution plan received NCLT approval on 20 January 2020, followed by appellate confirmation. Empee challenged the order dated 29 August 2024 reversing ITC and imposing consequential interest and penalty under Section 73 of the TNGST Act, alleging that its detailed objections had been ignored. Its income-tax petitions involved AYs 2007–08, 2010–11 to 2015–16 and 2017–18. The judgment records that the Department lodged claims of ₹70,17,05,490 for AYs 2007–08 to 2015–16, which were not admitted, while no claim was filed for AY 2017–18. These proceedings raised distinct questions about pre-CIRP assessments and reassessments, notices sent to the former management despite communication of new contact details, penalties and appellate orders allegedly passed without considering objections. Kamachi Industries’ petition arose after CIRP commenced on 19 February 2020, liquidation was ordered on 9 December 2022, and the company was auctioned as a going concern for ₹487 crore. The NCLT approved the sale on 19 July 2024. The judgment records FY 2022–23 tax arrears of ₹50,94,30,042 in the order dated 31 July 2024 and ₹50,94,30,044 in the subsequent demand notice dated 23 October 2024. Frontier Lifeline, a cardiac specialty hospital, entered CIRP on 2 August 2018 and subsequently liquidation. A ₹75 crore proposal was approved as a Section 230 scheme read with Section 31 of the IBC, with the judgment recording its effective date as 8 April 2021. Frontier challenged a GST order for FY 2019–20 demanding ₹2,06,90,392, following a show cause notice proposing ₹2,01,66,317. These matters required separate consideration of a going-concern sale and a compromise during liquidation, rather than assuming that both had the same effect as a CIRP resolution plan. Orchid Pharma’s connected proceedings involved AYs 2012–13 and 2013–14. Its CIRP commenced on 17 August 2017; Dhanuka Laboratories’ plan was approved on 27 June 2019 and ultimately upheld by the Supreme Court on 28 February 2020. One petition challenged the penalty of ₹5,13,71,186, recorded in the factual narrative as imposed on 31 May 2023 under Section 271(1)(c), after litigation concerning PF and ESI contributions. Another challenged the rectification order dated 30 March 2024 modifying the AY 2012–13 assessment under Section 154. The Court’s operative paragraph concerning the penalty contains different date and provision references from the factual narrative. These proceedings were therefore treated as requiring fresh examination of the resolution plan and the applicable legal principles, rather than as establishing an unconditional cancellation of the underlying tax or penalty liability. The petitioners relied on Sections 31 and 238 of the IBC and the Supreme Court’s decisions in Ghanashyam Mishra and Sons Private Limited Vs Edelweiss Asset Reconstruction Company Limited and Committee of Creditors of Essar Steel India Limited Vs Satish Kumar Gupta. They argued that the approved plan binds Government authorities and that excluded statutory claims cannot later emerge as surprise liabilities defeating revival. They also invoked Section 32A and Manish Kumar Vs Union of India to explain the legislative protection afforded to a corporate debtor under new management. The Revenue argued that these principles concern earlier liabilities and do not excuse taxes generated while the business operates during CIRP. It relied on continuing compliance obligations, the statutory periods for tax adjudication, and the distinction between determination and recovery recognised in Sundaresh Bhatt, Liquidator of ABG Shipyard Vs Central Board of Indirect Taxes and Customs. The Amicus Curiae supported protection against pre-CIRP claims but emphasised that CIRP-period taxes and the precise language of each approved plan require separate attention. Tracing the clean-slate jurisprudence, the Court discussed Ghanashyam Mishra, Essar Steel, Rainbow Papers, Paschimanchal Vidyut Vitran Nigam and later decisions including Ruchi Soya, Greater Noida Industrial Development Authority, Vaibhav Goel, Electrosteel Steel and JSW Steel. It concluded that the decisions relied upon did not constitute binding precedents deciding the specific question of automatic extinguishment of taxes arising during CIRP. Applying principles concerning ratio decidendi and decisions rendered sub silentio, the Court held that general language cannot be detached from the issues and facts actually decided. Its discussion of Kalyani Transco also emphasised the distinction between problems inherited from the erstwhile management and obligations arising under insolvency management. Accordingly, the Court considered itself entitled to examine the CIRP-period issue independently, while preserving the binding force of the Supreme Court’s decisions within their proper context. The Court found internal indications in the IBC against automatic non-payment of current taxes. The Explanation to Section 14 protects licences and similar rights subject to payment of current dues, Section 14(2A) recognises payment obligations for critical supplies during moratorium, Section 17(2)(e) requires the Interim Resolution Professional to comply with applicable law, and Section 30(2)(e) requires a plan not to contravene existing law. Section 32A’s protection for prior offences was also considered in its temporal setting. The Court examined the claims-verification and information-memorandum framework under the CIRP Regulations, including the claim-submission timelines discussed in the judgment. It reasoned that future operating taxes cannot simply be treated as omitted historical claims that a Department should already have lodged. The resolution applicant must exercise due diligence using the information available under Section 29; liabilities arising from ongoing business cannot automatically be characterised as unexpected hydra-headed claims. For GST, the Court additionally considered the consequences of allowing tax collected from customers to remain with the corporate debtor or the successful resolution applicant. Drawing on Amrit Banaspati, Kalyanpur Cement and Jindal Stainless, it expressed concern that such retention would produce unjust enrichment and conflict with public policy and constitutional principles. It also examined the fiscal powers of the Union and States, the distinct allocation introduced by Article 246A, and Union of India Vs Mohit Minerals. In its reasoning, Section 238 of the IBC must be construed consistently with the constitutional position of State GST legislation. It contrasted Section 82 of the TNGST Act, which expressly preserves the IBC’s operation concerning first charge, with other GST provisions governing levy, determination and recovery. The Court acknowledged that no provision of the IBC had been challenged before it; this constitutional discussion supported its interpretation and was not an order striking down the Code. The Court rejected the proposition that Section 60(5) gives the NCLT or NCLAT a general power to review the correctness of income-tax or GST assessments. Relying on Embassy Property Developments and Gujarat Urja Vikas Nigam, it held that insolvency jurisdiction cannot displace the statutory forums entrusted with disputes in the public-law domain. Permitting parallel adjudication by insolvency and tax authorities would risk inconsistent orders and administrative confusion. It also distinguished the State-GST legislative-competence discussion from Central GST and income tax, because the latter enactments and the IBC originate from Parliament. The Court’s treatment of these issues thus preserved a distinction between determining liability under fiscal legislation and giving effect to a final insolvency plan, rather than treating insolvency approval as a general appellate decision on tax assessments. Several issues remained for examination on remand. First, whether a reassessment initiated after CIRP for a pre-CIRP period is barred must be considered with reference to the IBC definitions of claim and debt, the taxable event and the time at which liability arises. Secondly, the authorities must inspect whether the actual plan specifically treats CIRP-period taxes as insolvency resolution process costs. The Court stated that where the plan’s language covers those liabilities as CIRP costs and its NCLT approval has attained finality, the Government would be bound and a differential tax liability could stand extinguished. This qualification prevents the judgment’s general rejection of automatic protection from being read as overriding every express, final plan provision. Thirdly, the possibility of setting off dues against available ITC, despite protection against recovery, was left open. Fourthly, the consequences of Section 230 compromises and going-concern liquidation sales, including whether they amount to a business transfer under Section 85 of the TNGST Act, required consideration on their own terms. In the operative directions, the Court set aside Empee’s GST assessment in W.P.No.30364 of 2024 and directed fresh examination of its objections, the plan and the possible treatment of taxes as CIRP costs. Kamachi’s demand notice in W.P.No.33329 of 2024 was kept in abeyance pending examination, after hearing the company, of the consequences of a going-concern sale under Section 85. The eight Empee income-tax matters were remitted for consideration of clean-slate protection against reassessment relating to pre-CIRP periods. Frontier Lifeline’s assessment was set aside for examination of the Section 230 compromise, whether it could constitute a resolution plan for IBC purposes, and its treatment of tax liabilities. In Aqua Terra’s two petitions, the order under Section 148A(d) was set aside and the return-delay condonation rejection was remanded, including reconsideration of circumstances that prevented timely filing during insolvency. Cura Healthcare’s impugned order was also set aside because its response had not been considered; fresh objections were permitted within four weeks, followed by examination of the plan and CIRP-cost issues. Orchid Pharma’s penalty proceedings and rectification order were sent for fresh orders, including consideration of the plan’s terms. The Court explained that, in most matters, the resolution plan or Section 230 scheme had not been placed before either the authorities or the Court. Its specified lines of inquiry were indicative, not exhaustive: the authorities could examine other relevant matters, and the petitioners could raise all contentions. Before relying on proposed grounds, precedents or documents, the authorities must give notice and sufficient time for submissions. The writ petitions were disposed of without costs and the connected miscellaneous petitions were closed. The result therefore combines rejection of an automatic CIRP-period tax waiver with fresh adjudication of the individual liabilities and plan-specific protections. Cases Discussed JSW Steel Ltd. v.

Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); Singapore Airlines Ltd Vs Commissioner of CGST & CE (GSTAT Ernakulam) Summary: The Goods and Services Tax Appellate Tribunal, Ernakulam Bench, allowed eight appeals filed by Singapore Airlines Ltd concerning GST refund claims totalling ₹45,92,528 for October 2019 to March 2020, January 2021 and March 2021. The airline had collected and deposited GST on air tickets but subsequently refunded the entire fares, including GST, following flight cancellations during the COVID-19 pandemic. It issued corresponding credit notes and reported them in GSTR-1. However, insufficient outward supplies and the inability to report negative amounts in GSTR-3B prevented full adjustment of the credit notes. It therefore sought refunds under the category “Excess payment of Tax”. The original authority rejected the claims for want of evidence establishing excess payment and actual reimbursement to passengers. The Joint Commissioner (Appeals) also referred to the time restriction under Section 34(2) and the alleged absence of a one-to-one correlation between travel agents and passengers. Before the Tribunal, the Revenue maintained that invoices, credit notes and Billing Settlement Plan (BSP) statements did not establish that the tax component had actually reached the ultimate passengers. It sought dismissal or a remand for verification. The airline submitted that ticket numbers and passenger names connected the tax invoices, credit notes, refund vouchers and BSP records, and that refunds were processed through the original booking agent and payment channel. The Tribunal examined the BSP mechanism and the supporting documents. It found that the common ticket number and passenger name provided an identifiable connection between each invoice, refund voucher and credit note. The documents had already been placed before the lower authorities, which had failed to appreciate them. The Tribunal found that the entire amounts, including GST, had been returned to passengers and that the airline had retained no amount, thereby excluding unjust enrichment. It also considered the Supreme Court’s directions in Pravasi Legal Cell Vs Union of India concerning refunds for flights affected by the lockdown. Relying on Circular No. 137/07/2020-GST dated 13.04.2020, the Tribunal held that the airline had correctly sought a refund through Form GST RFD-01 where no output liability was available against which the credit notes could be adjusted. It rejected the appellate authority’s reasoning that the claim did not qualify as excess payment of tax. On the objection concerning reversal of input tax credit by recipients, the Tribunal observed that tickets issued to individual passengers represented B2C transactions. It also considered the amendment to the proviso to Section 34(2), effective from 1 October 2025, and held that the specific condition concerning recipient reversal introduced by that amendment did not apply to the disputed period. It accepted the airline’s evidence and declaration concerning the return of the tax incidence to passengers. Setting aside the common appellate order, the Tribunal allowed all eight appeals with consequential relief and directed the respondent to process the claims within two weeks of the order and pay the refund with applicable interest from the date of the original refund application until actual payment. Cases Discussed Pravasi Legal Cell Vs Union of India, 2020 SCC OnLine SC 799 (Supreme Court); W.P. (C) D. No. 10966 of 2020; decided on 01.10.2020. The Tribunal considered the Supreme Court’s directions concerning refunds of airfares for flights affected by COVID-19 restrictions while examining the airline’s repayment of fares, including GST, to passengers. The First Appellate Authority, i.e JC (Appeals) while rejecting their appeals vide impugned OIA dated 8-12-2022 has categorically held that the unadjusted credit notes had become time barred under Section 34(2) of the CGST Act, 2017. JC (Appeals) further held that the credit notes had been issued in favour of the travel agent and that a one-to-one correlation between the travel agent and the passenger was not possible. Aggrieved by the said order-in-appeal, the Appellant has filed the present appeals before this Hon’ble Tribunal, contending that the credit notes were issued in the name of the passengers and not in the name of the travel agent, and that the settlement amounts between the travel agent and the Appellant in respect of the refunds are reflected in the BSP settlement sheets. 3.3 He submits that the issue in subject case appears to be one of the consequent action of decision of the Hon’ble Supreme Court in a Public Interest Litigation in Pravasi Legal Cell vs UOI on the issue of the refund of amounts in tickets of flights cancelled in wake of the lockdown and subsequent restrictions imposed by the Govt. of India to contain COVID-19, wherein Hon’ble Supreme Court issued a detailed guideline in the matter and the Ministry of Civil Aviation also issued set of guidelines in the matter. 3.4 He further submits the Section 54(4) of the CGST Act, 2017, provides that ‘the application shall be accompanied by (a) such documentary evidence as may be prescribed to establish that a refund is due to the applicant; and (b) such documentary or other evidence (including the documents referred to in Section 33) as the applicant may furnish to establish that the amount of tax and interest, if any, paid on such tax or any other amount paid in relation to which such refund is claimed was collected from, or paid by, him and the incidence of such tax and interest had not been passed on to any other person: Provided that where the amount claimed as refund is less than two lakh rupees, it shall not be necessary for the applicant to furnish any documentary and other evidences but he may file a declaration, based on the documentary or other evidences available with him, certifying that the incidence of such tax and interest had not been passed on to any other person.’ 3.5 He submits that the Appellant has failed to produce any cogent, credible, and documentary evidence, as required under Section 54(4) of the CGST Act, 2017, to substantiate its contention that the tax amount in respect of the cancelled tickets was actually refunded to the ultimate passengers. In the absence of such documentary evidence, there is no sufficient material on record to establish that the incidence of tax collected from the passengers was, in fact, passed back to the respective passengers and that the Appellant had not retained or otherwise recovered the said amount. 3.6 It is pertinent to note that the statutory requirement under Section 54(4) of the CGST Act, 2017 necessarily requires the claimant to substantiate the eligibility and admissibility of the refund claim with appropriate supporting evidence. Mere production of records relating to cancellation of tickets or the issuance of credit notes, without establishing the corresponding refund of the tax component to the ultimate passenger, cannot by itself be treated as sufficient evidence for granting the refund. 3.7 In the present case, the Appellant has not furnished any documentary evidence establishing a direct nexus between the cancelled ticket, the concerned passenger, the credit note issued, the amount allegedly refunded, and the corresponding tax component. In particular, the Appellant has failed to demonstrate, through reliable records such as passenger-wise refund details, proof of payment/refund, bank records, transaction statements, or any other contemporaneous documentary evidence, that the tax amount forming part of the refund claim was actually returned to the ultimate passenger. 3.8 The BSP settlement sheet produced by the Appellant does not reflect or account for the amounts actually refunded by the travel agents to the end-customers. This omission is material, as the BSP settlement statement, by itself, does not establish that the amounts in question were ultimately refunded to the end-customers. Significantly, the same aspect has also been specifically noted in the findings of the Appellate Authority, which observed that the BSP settlement sheet relied upon by the Appellant does not disclose the amounts refunded by the travel agents to the respective end-customers. Therefore, the mere production of the BSP settlement sheet cannot, in the absence of supporting evidence of actual refunds to the end-customers, be treated as conclusive proof of such refunds. 3.9 Furthermore, he submits that the Appellant has failed to produce, for verification, copies of the actual air tickets indicating the name of passenger, the date of intended travel, the destination, the actual charges levied which were subsequently cancelled, the date of cancellation of ticket and whether any charges were levied on such cancellation. In the absence of the cancelled air tickets or any other contemporaneous documentary evidence establishing such cancellation, the mere production of invoices does not, by itself, substantiate the Appellant’s claim. The invoices relied upon by the Appellant are incomplete and, without the corresponding air tickets, cancellation records, refund details, or other supporting documents, cannot be properly verified as to their authenticity, cancellation, or actual financial implication. Consequently, the documents produced by the Appellant are insufficient to establish the alleged transactions and the claim based thereon remains unsubstantiated without verification. 3.10 In these circumstances, the finding of the learned Appellate Authority that a one-to one correlation between the travel agent and the ultimate passenger was not possible is fully justified on the basis of the material available on record. Where the Appellant itself has failed to furnish the necessary documentary evidence establishing such a direct correlation, the absence of a verifiable one-to-one nexus cannot be attributed to any deficiency on the part of the Department or the Appellate Authority. Therefore, in the absence of passenger-wise and transaction-wise documentary evidence establishing that the corresponding tax amount was actually refunded to the ultimate passenger, the Appellant’s claim remains unsubstantiated. Accordingly, the finding of the Appellate Authority regarding the impossibility of establishing a one-to-one correlation between the travel agent, the cancelled ticket, the passenger, and the refund amount, is sustainable and warrants no interference. 3.11 Ld AR, in view of his submissions above prays that the appeal of the tax payer be rejected as non-maintainable or the case be remanded to the original authority for fresh examination and verification as the JC (Appeals) has rightly rejected the appeals at the first appeals stage. 4.0 Heard both sides. Billing and Settlement Plan (BSP) : This plan is made to simplify financial relations between airlines and accredited-agents with a trusted framework and intelligent solutions. Global air travel is a complex business, with thousands of airlines and tens of thousands of travel agents transacting every second. In this high-volume, high-risk environment, a trusted financial system is essential. The IATA Billing and Settlement Plan (BSP) provides a standardized global framework that simplifies sales reporting, remittance, and settlement between airlines and IATA-accredited travel agents. For over 50 years, BSP has been the trusted backbone of airline distribution, helping airlines and accredited travel agents simplify reporting, remittance, and settlement while enhancing financial control, transparency, cash flow, and risk management through ‘BSPlink’, and supporting modern retailing and new payment methods. To have a glimpse of the BSP, there are 400+ participating airlines, 59,000+ accredited travel agents, US$242 Billion+ settled annually, and 99.99% on-time settlement rate. Therefore, either for an airline or an accredited travel agent, BSP provides a trusted framework for simplifying sales reporting, remittance, and settlement. Combined with tools that enhance financial control, transparency, cash flow, and risk management, BSP helps them operate more efficiently. We see that the BSP is a robust trustworthy billing settlement plan and all the airlines and IATA agents are using it for their settlement of amounts collected from the passengers, and also for settlement of refunds in case of the cancellations. 4.6 As regards the refund claim made by the appellant, we find that the due to the Covid-19 pandemic there were no scheduled flights of the appellant, during the relevant period Oct-19 to March-2021. The tickets which were booked in advance were also getting cancelled for the said period. Since there was cancellation of flights, the entire amount of the ticket had to be refunded to each of the passenger, as an appellant’s airline policy. In the tickets, GST amount was also charged and collected. 4.7 We are also aware that in the case of Pravasi Legal Cell vs Union Of India (WP (C) D.No.10966 of 2020) dated 1-10-2020, the Hon’ble Supreme Court had held that ‘For these reasons we are not inclined to delve any further on any of the contentions and deem it appropriate to dispose of this batch of cases with the following directions: 1. Ld AR has mentioned in his submission that since there was insufficient output tax liability against which credit note can be adjusted, they (the appellant) filed the refund application which is in accordance with instruction issued in Circular No.137/07/2020-GST dated 13.04.2020, but the JC (Appeals) has not applied the Circular in letter and spirit. The point no. 3 of the circular reads as, Subject: Clarification in respect of certain challenges faced by the registered persons in implementation of provisions of GST Laws-reg. Circular No.136/06/2020-GST, dated 03.04.2020 had been issued to clarify doubts regarding relief measures taken by the Government for facilitating taxpayers in meeting the compliance requirements under various provisions of the Central Goods and Services Tax Act, 2017 (hereinafter referred to as the “CGST Act”) on account of the measures taken to prevent the spread of Novel Corona Virus (COVID-19). It has been brought to the notice of the Board that certain challenges are being faced by taxpayers in adhering to the compliance requirements under various other provisions of the CGST Act which also need to be clarified. 2. The issues raised have been examined and in order to ensure uniformity in the implementation of the provisions of the law across the field formations, the Board, in exercise of its powers conferred under section 168(1) of the CGST Act hereby clarifies as under: 3. Goods supplied by a supplier under cover of a tax invoice are returned by the recipient. Whether he can claim refund of tax paid or is he required to adjust his tax liability in his returns ? In such a case where the goods supplied by a supplier are returned by the recipient and where tax invoice had been issued, the supplier is required to issue a “credit note” in terms of section 34 of the CGST Act. He shall declare the details of such credit notes in the return for the month during which such credit note has been issued. The tax liability shall be adjusted in the return subject to conditions of section 34 of the CGST Act. There is no need to file a separate refund claim in such a case. However, in cases where there is no output liability against which a credit note can be adjusted, registered persons may proceed to file a claim under “Excess payment of tax, if any” through FORM GST RFD-01. 4.14 The clarification issued by the CBIC is self explanatory, and has clarified that where there is no output liability against which a credit note can be adjusted, registered persons may proceed to file a claim under “excess payment of tax, if any” through Form GST RFD-01. Therefore, the procedure followed by the appellant is correct and the reasoning given by the JC (Appeals) for rejecting the appeals is not at all sustainable. 4.15 We also find that one more issue which is raised is that the refunds and credit notes are valid only if the recipient of the supply reverses the ITC claimed and further held that since the appellant issues credit notes to agents and not to the ultimate passengers/customers, the appellant must ensure that such agents/customers have reversed the ITC before reducing its GST liability which appellant has failed to do so. 4.16 In the present case, we find that all the tickets are issued in the name of individual passengers only, and all such transactions are B2C transactions, and therefore there is no question of availing ITC and subsequently reversing the same. 4.17 However, we notice that there is one B2B agent i.e. Balmer Lawrie & Co Ltd to whom the credit note was issued. With regard thereto, Appellant hereby submits that Section 34 of CGST Act, 2017 permits a supplier to issue a credit note where tax liability is reduced due to cancellation, return, or discount. Once a credit note is issued and reported in GSTR-1, the supplier’s liability is reduced accordingly. The law does not require the supplier to first ensure that the recipient has reversed ITC before reducing its liability. The obligation of reversal lies with the recipient under Section 16(2) read with Rule 37 of CGST Rules. 2017. 4.18 We also find that the appellant has filed an undertaking to the effect that they have not received any request from any of the passenger that their refund of cancelled ticket is pending or not received. 4.19 We also find that the Section 34 (2) which deals with the issuance of credit notes, reads as, (2) Any registered person who issues a credit note in relation to a supply of goods or services or both shall declare the details of such credit note in the return for the month during which such credit note has been issued but not later than 5[the thirtieth day of November] following the end of the financial year in which such supply was made, or the date of furnishing of the relevant annual return, whichever is earlier, and the tax liability shall be adjusted in such manner as may be prescribed: Provided that no reduction in output tax liability of the supplier shall be permitted, if the–– (i) input tax credit as is attributable to such a credit note, if availed, has not been reversed by the recipient, where such recipient is a registered person; or (ii) incidence of tax on such supply has been passed on to any other person, in other cases. We find that the above Proviso (i) was inserted on wef 1-10-2025, vide Section 126 of the Finance Act, 2025. Before that the proviso read as, Provided that no reduction in output tax liability of the supplier shall be permitted, if the incidence of tax and interest on such supply has been passed on to any other person. 4.20 Therefore, the question of reversal of ITC during the period Oct-2019 to March-2021 will not apply as there was no such provision. As regards, the earlier proviso, to check the incidence of tax and interest on such supply has not been passed on, the appellant has submitted a self declaration in terms of Rule 89(2)(l). Moreso, most of the customers are falling under B2C category, and having refunded the entire amount to each respective passenger, the question of passing the incidence of tax and interest does not arise. Even otherwise as an abundant caution, the GST framework ensures matching of credit notes in the recipient’s GSTR-2A/2B, thereby automatically triggering ITC reversal at the recipient’s end, if there is any such B2B buyer. But in the present case we do not find any such B2B customer nor the Revenue has brought forward any such evidence. The appellant has satisfied the conditions of Section 34 (2) while issuing the credit notes. 5.0 In view of the above, to answer the issue raised, we conclude that, The appellant-airline is eligible for the refund of GST collected from the passengers and paid by them to the GST Department, but subsequently refunded entire amount of fare including GST to the passengers, on account of cancellation of the flights due to the COVID-19 pandemic in terms of Hon’ble Supreme Court’s order. We also find that the appellant has provided sufficient evidence to ensure that the refund of the cancelled tickets have reached the respective passenger directly and there is no passing of the incidence of tax. 6.0 Accordingly, we pass the following order. ORDER The impugned OIA No. COC-GST-000-APP-197-204-2022-JC dated 8-12-2022 issued on 12-12-2022, is hereby set aside, and all the eight appeals nos. APL/3/ERN/2026, APL/4/ERN/2026, APL/5/ERN/2026, APL/6/ERN/2026, APL/7/ERN/2026, APL/8/ERN/2026, APL/9/ERN/2026 and APL/30/ERN/2026 are allowed with consequential relief. The respondent is directed to process all the refund claims within two weeks from the date of this order and make payment to the appellant along with applicable interest from the date of original refund application till the date of actual payment.

Summary: Goods moving between seller, transporter and customer at a reporting date create a cut-off question, not merely a stock-count problem. The accounting answer turns on which party controls or owns the goods under the contract at the reporting date, while valuation follows the applicable inventory standard. GST invoices and e-way bills support the movement trail but do not by themselves prove the accounting conclusion. A defensible close therefore links contractual delivery terms, dispatch evidence, transporter custody, subsequent receipt, revenue recognition, purchase recognition, inventory valuation and GST records in one shipment-level schedule. Key Takeaways Show all 19 sections Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); Why transit stock creates two-sided cut-off risk A shipment dispatched before a reporting date and received after it can be omitted by both parties or recorded by both. If the seller removes inventory and recognises revenue too early while the buyer waits for physical receipt, the goods disappear from both balance sheets. If the seller retains inventory but the buyer records the purchase on invoice date without obtaining control or ownership, the same goods are counted twice. The problem can distort inventory, trade receivables, trade payables, revenue, gross margin, input tax credit and working-capital ratios in the same period. The accounting team should not adopt a universal rule such as ‘invoice date means purchase’ or ‘delivery date means stock’. The contract, purchase order, delivery term, accepted trade practice and actual performance determine the point at which control or the relevant risks and rewards pass. Physical location is strong evidence but not always conclusive. A shipment can belong to the buyer while it is in a carrier’s custody, or remain the seller’s inventory until destination delivery, depending on the enforceable terms. CGST Act/Rules, invoice and e-way bill records Supply and movement trail, not a substitute for accounting judgment Recognition asks whether the reporting entity has an asset or an obligation. Measurement asks what cost belongs in inventory and whether net realisable value is lower. Revenue analysis asks whether the seller has transferred control of the promised goods under Ind AS 115, or satisfied the applicable recognition criteria under its accounting framework. GST asks when a taxable supply and related documentation arise. These questions can use the same evidence, but they are not interchangeable and may produce different timing for legitimate reasons. Contract terms decide the shipment cut-off—not shorthand labels alone Read the full delivery clause Terms such as ex-works, free on board, cost-insurance-freight or delivered-at-place are useful starting points, but the purchase order and accepted amendments must be read as a whole. Identify the named location, the party arranging carriage, the point where loss or damage passes, inspection or acceptance rights, the seller’s obligation to replace damaged goods, title-retention clauses, and any right to redirect the shipment. A commercial label copied into an invoice should not override the executed contract and actual conduct. Distinguish carrier custody from customer control Handover to a transporter may indicate transfer where the carrier acts for the buyer and the seller has no remaining performance obligation. It may not indicate transfer where the seller must deliver to the customer’s site, retains diversion rights, bears transit loss or must complete installation and acceptance. The close memo should state the decisive contractual fact and reference the supporting document. It should not merely attach a lorry receipt and leave the conclusion unstated. Investigate side agreements and post-dispatch changes Sales staff may agree to storage, bill-and-hold, delayed acceptance, return rights or consignment terms outside the standard order. Procurement staff may reject quantity or quality after receipt. Credit notes issued shortly after year-end can reveal that the original cut-off was wrong. Obtain written confirmation of unusual terms and scan subsequent receipts, returns and credit notes before finalising the schedule. Inventory valuation for goods that belong to the entity in transit Ind AS 2 and AS 2 measure inventories at the lower of cost and net realisable value. Cost includes purchase cost, conversion cost where relevant and other costs incurred in bringing the inventories to their present location and condition. For transit stock, this requires a disciplined split between costs already incurred at the reporting date and costs relating to a future stage. Purchase price, non-recoverable taxes, duties, freight and handling may form part of cost when they meet the standard; trade discounts and recoverable taxes are excluded from cost. A year-end accrual may be necessary for freight already received but not yet invoiced. Conversely, the entire quoted freight should not be accrued if the carrier’s service remains unperformed after cut-off. When goods are damaged, obsolete, cancelled by the customer or exposed to a price decline during transit, the lower-of-cost-and-NRV test must use conditions existing at the reporting date and evidence obtained up to authorisation of the financial statements. Insurance recovery is assessed separately and should not automatically offset a write-down without a valid basis. Indicators include a present right to payment, legal title, physical possession, significant risks and rewards, and customer acceptance, but no single indicator decides every arrangement. The buyer’s recognition analysis should normally be consistent with that transfer point. A mismatch is a signal to investigate the contract, not an automatic accounting entry. For non-Ind AS entities, apply the notified accounting framework and the substance of the sales arrangement. Document why the seller has or has not completed the conditions for revenue and why the buyer has or has not obtained the inventory. Where the seller retains control, the goods remain in seller inventory even if an invoice was generated for operational purposes. Where the buyer has obtained control before physical receipt, record inventory in transit and the related payable, subject to the entity’s policy and materiality. GST invoice and e-way bill evidence: useful, but not conclusive A GST tax invoice, e-invoice reference and e-way bill establish important facts about the declared supply and movement of goods. They can identify supplier, recipient, dispatch location, ship-to location, document date, value, HSN, transporter and vehicle details. However, their existence does not, by itself, establish that accounting control transferred before the reporting date. Equally, a delayed e-way bill update does not necessarily prove that the goods were not dispatched. Accounting teams should reconcile the electronic records to the contract and actual movement evidence. Input tax credit should be reviewed under the statutory eligibility conditions and the entity’s live portal data. The accounting cut-off schedule should flag invoices recorded in books but absent from the relevant GST statement, and invoices appearing in the GST system for goods not yet recognised. Such differences require explanation; they should not be cleared through a suspense account merely to match totals. Bill-to/ship-to transactions need specific attention because the invoice recipient and physical destination may differ. Shipment-level close schedule and audit evidence Build the cut-off population from more than one source: dispatch register, goods-received notes, sales and purchase ledgers, transporter portal, gate records, e-way bills and subsequent receipts. Select shipments around the reporting date in both directions. For each item, record invoice date, dispatch date, contractual transfer point, carrier handover, reporting-date location, actual receipt, amount, GST document references, accounting conclusion and reviewer. The schedule should reconcile to the general ledger and the physical stock records. 1. Freeze sequential sales invoices, dispatch notes and goods-received notes around cut-off. 2. Match each shipment to the executed order and delivery clause. 3. Confirm custody and location through independent transporter or warehouse evidence where material. 4. Trace receipt or delivery after the reporting date and investigate exceptions. 5. Reconcile seller revenue, buyer purchases, inventory-in-transit, payables and receivables. 6. Review subsequent returns, rejections, credit notes, damages and insurance claims. 7. Obtain preparer and reviewer sign-off on every material judgment. Does the invoice date decide who owns goods in transit? No. It is relevant evidence, but the executed delivery terms, transfer of control or risks and rewards, remaining obligations and actual conduct must be assessed. 2. Can goods be inventory even when they are not at the entity’s premises? Yes. If the entity controls or owns them under the applicable framework, they can be recorded as inventory in transit while held by a carrier or third-party warehouse. 3. Is an e-way bill proof of accounting ownership? No. It supports the movement trail and GST compliance. The accounting conclusion still requires the contract and transfer analysis. 4. Should recoverable GST be included in inventory cost? Generally no. Recoverable taxes are excluded from inventory cost; any uncertainty about eligibility should be analysed under the tax law and documented separately. 5. What if freight is unbilled at the reporting date? Accrue the portion of attributable service already received when recognition criteria are met. Do not accrue future transport merely because it is included in a quotation. 6. How should damaged goods in transit be treated? Assess inventory write-down or loss using the conditions and recoverable amount at the reporting date. Evaluate any insurance receivable separately on its own recognition basis. Key Takeaways Decide transit cut-off shipment by shipment from enforceable delivery terms and actual performance. Keep recognition, inventory measurement, seller revenue and GST documentation as linked but separate conclusions. Measure recognised inventory under Ind AS 2 or AS 2 and perform the lower-of-cost-and-NRV test. Use e-way bills and invoices as evidence, not as automatic proof of control or ownership.

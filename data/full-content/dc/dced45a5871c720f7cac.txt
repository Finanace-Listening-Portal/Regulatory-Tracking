Summary: A sale of inherited property in Tax Year 2026-27 requires a documented chain from the last previous owner who acquired the asset by a non-inheritance mode to the heir who transfers it. Under sections 72, 73 and 90 of the Income-tax Act, 2025, the computation may depend on the previous owner’s cost, eligible capital improvements, the optional 1 April 2001 fair market value for older assets, and the stamp-duty-value ceiling applicable to land or building. The strongest working paper is not a single valuation report: it is an indexed evidence file connecting title, dates, shares, historical cost, improvements, sale consideration and transfer expenses. Transactions belonging to FY 2025-26 or an earlier previous year remain within the Income-tax Act, 1961 framework and must not be relabelled as Tax Year 2026-27 merely because the return or assessment activity occurs later. A transfer occurring during 1 April 2026 to 31 March 2027 belongs to Tax Year 2026-27 and is examined under that Act. A transfer completed on or before 31 March 2026 belongs to the previous-year and assessment-year architecture of the Income-tax Act, 1961, even if the return is filed, processed or scrutinised after 1 April 2026. This cut-off should be fixed before any section number, return field or cost formula is selected. The date of inheritance is not automatically the transfer date Inheritance itself ordinarily shifts ownership without a sale consideration from the heir. The later sale, exchange or other taxable transfer is the event for capital-gains computation. Record the registered conveyance date, possession and consideration terms, any agreement and the legal transfer rule relevant to the asset. Do not use the date of death, mutation or probate as a substitute without testing the actual transaction. Section 73 carries the previous owner’s cost into the heir’s computation Section 73 of the 2025 Act deals with cost for specified modes of acquisition. Its table covers a capital asset received under a gift or will, by succession, inheritance or devolution and certain other modes. For that category, the deemed cost is the cost for which the previous owner acquired the property, increased by eligible cost of improvement incurred or borne by the previous owner or the assessee. The statutory definition of previous owner points to the last previous owner who acquired the asset through a mode other than the listed carry-over modes. The immediate predecessor may also have acquired it by inheritance, so the enquiry does not stop there. Build a dated ownership chart and continue backwards until the last owner who bought, constructed or otherwise acquired the asset through a non-carry-over mode. If that cost cannot be ascertained, section 90(11) provides a statutory rule based on fair market value when the property became that previous owner’s asset; the factual inability to ascertain cost should be documented rather than casually asserted. The 1 April 2001 option requires valuation and stamp-value evidence Section 90(9) permits an option for an older capital asset. Where the asset became the assessee’s property before 1 April 2001, the cost may be the assessee’s cost or fair market value on 1 April 2001. Where the asset came through a section 73 table mode and the previous owner held it before that date, the option compares the previous owner’s cost with fair market value on 1 April 2001. For land or building, section 90(10) caps the chosen fair market value at the stamp duty value on 1 April 2001 wherever that stamp value is available. A valuation report needs a reproducible factual foundation A registered valuer’s report is stronger when it identifies the exact property, area, tenure, rights, condition and comparable transactions, explains adjustments and attaches supporting records. The file should also show the 1 April 2001 stamp duty value wherever available, because the statutory ceiling for land or building cannot be ignored. Municipal records, old maps, sanctioned plans, photographs, tenancy details and contemporaneous sale instances can help explain the state of the property at the valuation date. Cost of improvement is a capital-expenditure test, not a renovation label Section 90 defines cost of improvement for sections 72 and 73. For a non-intangible asset held before 1 April 2001, it generally captures capital expenditure incurred on or after that date in making additions or alterations by the previous owner or the assessee. For a later asset, it covers qualifying capital additions or alterations after acquisition, including qualifying expenditure of the previous owner where the section 73 carry-over rule applies. Expenditure already deductible in computing house-property, business or other-source income is excluded from this definition. Invoice, payment and property linkage should agree For each improvement, retain the contractor or supplier invoice, payment trail, scope of work, approvals and proof that the work relates to the asset sold. Capital additions such as a documented new floor or structural extension require different analysis from routine repairs, repainting or recurring maintenance. If only part of a jointly held property was improved or sold, allocate cost on a reasoned and supportable basis and preserve the calculation. Section 72 computation should reconcile consideration, cost and transfer expense Section 72(1) computes capital gains by deducting expenditure incurred wholly and exclusively in connection with the transfer and the cost of acquisition and improvement from full value of consideration. That statutory structure calls for separate evidence schedules. Brokerage, legal documentation and other sale-connected expenditure should be supported by engagement terms, invoices, payment records and the role performed. Personal liabilities, inheritance expenses or amounts lacking a transfer nexus should not be inserted merely because they reduced the family’s net receipt. Mutation entries can support the record but may not alone prove title. Resolve contradictory deeds, family settlements or pending disputes before finalising the computation. A practical inherited-property evidence index 1. Prepare a one-page chronology from the last purchase or construction through each death, will, succession, partition, release and sale. 2. Place certified or legible copies of title instruments in chronological order and mark the ownership fraction at every stage. 3. Create separate schedules for previous-owner cost, 1 April 2001 value, eligible improvements, transfer expenses and consideration. 4. Cross-reference every number to an invoice, registered document, payment record, valuation annexure or reasoned statutory rule. 5. Reconcile sale proceeds and expenses to bank statements, the registered instrument, tax deduction records and the return utility. 6. Document unresolved gaps, the alternative computation considered and the professional conclusion adopted. Common inherited-property errors that weaken a return Using the probate value or current market value as cost without a statutory basis. Treating every payment made after inheritance as cost of improvement. Ignoring the 1 April 2001 stamp-duty-value ceiling for land or building where available. Claiming the whole property’s cost when only a fractional interest was inherited or sold. Applying the 2025 Act section numbers to a transfer completed in FY 2025-26. Keeping only a valuation certificate without comparables, title papers, measurements or payment evidence. Frequently Asked Questions on Inherited Property Cost 1. Is the value stated in a will automatically the tax cost? No. Section 73 generally carries the cost of the relevant previous owner. A value in a will may be evidentiary for another purpose but does not replace the statutory cost rule by itself. 2. Can 1 April 2001 fair market value be used when the heir inherited after 2001? It may be available where the asset came through a specified section 73 mode and the relevant previous owner held it before 1 April 2001, subject to section 90 conditions and the land/building ceiling. 3. Does a municipal mutation entry prove acquisition cost? No. It may support the ownership history, but historical cost normally needs the acquisition deed, allotment evidence, construction records or the applicable statutory substitute. 4. Are repairs made before sale deductible as improvement? Not merely because they were necessary for sale. The expenditure must meet the statutory capital-improvement test and should not be a routine repair or an amount already deductible under another head. 5. What if the previous owner’s purchase deed cannot be found? Search registration records, allotment files, bank and estate papers and certified copies. If cost truly cannot be ascertained, examine section 90(11) and document the evidence gap and valuation basis. 6. Which law applies to a property sold on 25 March 2026? That transfer falls in FY 2025-26 and remains within the Income-tax Act, 1961 assessment-year framework. The 2025 Act applies from 1 April 2026. 7. Is inherited property automatically exempt when sold? No. Receipt by inheritance and a later taxable transfer are different events. The later transfer requires computation under the applicable capital-gains provisions and any exemption must be tested separately. Key Takeaways For TY 2026-27, sections 72, 73 and 90 of the Income-tax Act, 2025 govern the core inherited-property cost analysis. Trace ownership to the last previous owner who acquired the asset through a non-carry-over mode. For eligible pre-1 April 2001 land or building, support both fair market value and the applicable stamp-duty-value ceiling. Separate capital improvements from repairs and from expenditure already deductible under another income head.

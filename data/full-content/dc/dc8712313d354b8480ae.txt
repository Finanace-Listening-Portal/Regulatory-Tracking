Summary: Ind AS 10 does not classify an event solely by the date on which a notice, order or announcement arrives. The central question is whether information obtained after the reporting period gives further evidence of a condition that already existed at the reporting date, or instead reflects a condition that arose later. That distinction controls adjustment, disclosure and documentation. This guide applies the standard to four recurring closing problems: GST demands and litigation developments, a customer’s insolvency after year-end, dividends declared after year-end, and events that undermine the going-concern basis. It also explains the authorisation window, material non-adjusting-event disclosure and the evidence an audit-ready conclusion should preserve. The end point is entity-specific. It is not automatically the audit-report date, shareholder-meeting date or regulatory filing date. Management should identify the body that has authority to approve the financial statements under the entity’s governance and applicable company law, record the approval date, and apply one consistent event cut-off to the entire closing process. Events discovered within this window can require adjustment or disclosure even when the information arrives after year-end. Conversely, an event outside the window is not brought into the completed financial statements merely because it is commercially significant, although other legal, regulatory or subsequent-period reporting duties may arise. A closing memorandum should state the reporting date, the authorising body, the authorisation date and the sources reviewed through that date. The mere fact that the tax authority issued the document later does not make the matter non-adjusting. The entity must determine whether the later document supplies evidence about an obligation or uncertainty that existed at the reporting date. For example, if an after-year-end order confirms facts already present at year-end and resolves uncertainty about an existing tax exposure, it may affect the measurement or recognition of a provision under the applicable standard. If the order results from a new post-year-end transaction, a new legislative change or conduct that first occurred after year-end, it points toward a non-adjusting event. The accounting conclusion still depends on the recognition and measurement requirements for provisions, contingencies and taxes; Ind AS 10 determines when subsequent evidence enters that assessment, not the amount by itself. Build a fact chronology before concluding The file should separate the taxable period, transaction dates, return positions, departmental inquiry dates, legal advice available at year-end, notice or order date, appeal status and financial-statement authorisation date. Management should document which later findings confirm conditions already present. A conclusion that an order is adjusting merely because it concerns an earlier tax period is too broad; the evidential connection must be explained. Equally, labelling every later order non-adjusting because it bears a later date ignores the standard’s focus on the underlying condition. Document the linkage and apply the relevant measurement standard Customer insolvency after the reporting date Bankruptcy can confirm year-end credit deterioration Ind AS 10 identifies a customer’s bankruptcy after the reporting period as an example that usually confirms the customer was credit-impaired at the reporting date. The later insolvency event may therefore require an adjustment to the carrying amount of the trade receivable. Under an expected-credit-loss model, management should integrate the subsequent evidence with information about ageing, payment defaults, disputes, financial stress and recovery prospects that existed at year-end. The word usually does not eliminate judgement. A sudden post-year-end catastrophe affecting an otherwise sound customer may represent a genuinely new condition. The file should establish whether insolvency resulted from deterioration already visible before year-end or from a discrete event that arose later. The analysis should also distinguish an accounting impairment conclusion from a tax deduction, bad-debt write-off or GST adjustment; those outcomes are governed by separate legal requirements and may occur at different dates. Evidence should connect the later failure to the year-end estimate Useful evidence includes the last receipts, credit-limit breaches, correspondence, public filings, restructuring requests, legal recovery action and the insolvency petition timeline. Management should show how the new information changes the probability-weighted recovery estimate or confirms that a receivable was already impaired. Simply writing the balance off because bankruptcy occurred after year-end may skip the measurement analysis; ignoring the bankruptcy because it occurred later may understate the year-end loss. Dividends declared after the reporting period When an entity declares dividends to holders of equity instruments after the reporting period, it does not recognise those dividends as a liability at the reporting date. At year-end there was no present obligation arising from the later declaration. The dividend is disclosed in the notes in accordance with the applicable presentation requirements when it is declared after the reporting period but before the financial statements are authorised for issue. This treatment should not be confused with a dividend validly declared before year-end but unpaid at the reporting date; the timing and legal effect of declaration differ. The board proposal, shareholder approval requirement, governing articles and applicable company law should be reviewed. The accounting team should reconcile the amount disclosed to the approved minutes and ensure it is not also recorded as a year-end payable through a routine closing journal. Going concern events can change the basis of accounting Ind AS 10 treats loss of going concern as fundamental If, after the reporting period, management determines that it intends to liquidate the entity or cease trading, or has no realistic alternative but to do so, financial statements are not prepared on a going-concern basis. Ind AS 10 explains that this is not merely an adjustment of amounts recognised under the original basis. It is a fundamental change in the basis of accounting. Management must consider all available information about the future, including events occurring in the authorisation window, together with the requirements of Ind AS 1 and applicable law. A serious event does not automatically destroy the going-concern basis. Loss of a major customer, lender action, covenant breach, fire, cyber incident, regulatory prohibition or funding withdrawal must be evaluated for its financial and operational effect, mitigation and available alternatives. When the going-concern basis remains appropriate but material uncertainty exists, transparent disclosure may be required. The assessment should not be reduced to a binary post-year-end checklist. Cash forecasts and mitigation must be contemporaneous The board paper should use supportable cash-flow forecasts, committed facilities, covenant terms, refinancing evidence, stress cases and realistic mitigation. Optimistic plans without financing or execution evidence do not neutralise an adverse event. The record should explain what information was available before authorisation, how downside scenarios were assessed, and why the chosen basis and disclosures remain appropriate. Auditor communication should begin early because a late change in basis can affect recognition, measurement, presentation and the audit timetable. Material non-adjusting events still require disclosure A non-adjusting classification does not end the analysis. If a non-adjusting event is material, the entity discloses the nature of the event and an estimate of its financial effect, or states that such an estimate cannot be made. Materiality is assessed through the information needs of users, including size and nature. Examples may include a major acquisition or disposal, destruction of a production facility after year-end, a significant capital transaction, a major restructuring announcement or an abnormal change in asset prices or exchange rates, when the underlying condition arose after the reporting date. The disclosure should be entity-specific. It should identify what occurred, when it occurred and which financial effects can be reasonably estimated without implying a precision that does not exist. If management cannot estimate the effect, the reasons and the information available should support that statement. A generic sentence saying that an event may affect operations is rarely useful when the board has already quantified insured loss, committed expenditure or affected capacity. A closing file that survives audit review A practical event register should capture the event date, date management became aware, underlying condition, reporting-date evidence, later evidence, responsible owner, adjusting or non-adjusting conclusion, affected standard and accounts, materiality, journal entry, disclosure and approval. Legal, tax, treasury, sales, credit, operations and secretarial teams should confirm events in their areas through the authorisation date. The exercise should be refreshed immediately before approval rather than completed once at the beginning of the audit. Each conclusion should distinguish fact from judgement. The chronology supports whether the condition existed; the relevant recognition standard supports the accounting; materiality supports the disclosure; and authorised minutes support the cut-off. This structure prevents a common error in which Ind AS 10 is cited as if it independently measures tax provisions, receivable impairment or restructuring costs. It instead governs how evidence and events in the post-reporting window affect the period being reported. Frequently Asked Questions on Ind AS 10 classifications 1. Is every GST order received after year-end a non-adjusting event? No. The issue is whether the order gives further evidence of a condition that existed at the reporting date. The order date alone does not determine classification. 2. Does an adjusting event always require a new liability? No. It may change an estimate, confirm impairment, require recognition under another Ind AS, or correct amounts already recorded. The applicable recognition and measurement standard must also be applied. 3. Must a customer’s post-year-end bankruptcy reduce the receivable? It usually confirms year-end credit impairment, but management should investigate causation. A wholly new post-year-end catastrophe may indicate a later condition rather than pre-existing deterioration. 4. Can a dividend declared after year-end be accrued because management expected it? No. An expectation or proposal does not create the year-end liability addressed by the standard. The later declaration is disclosed as required rather than accrued at the reporting date. 5. What if a non-adjusting event is very large? The amounts are not adjusted merely because the event is large, but material events require disclosure of their nature and estimated financial effect, or a statement that an estimate cannot be made. 6. Is the audit-report date always the authorisation date? Not automatically. The entity must identify the body authorised to approve issuance and document the actual approval process and date under its governance and legal framework. 7. Is loss of going concern just another adjusting journal? No. When the going-concern basis is no longer appropriate, the standard treats the consequence as a fundamental change in the basis of accounting, with wider recognition, measurement, presentation and disclosure effects. Key Takeaways The Ind AS 10 window runs from the reporting date to the date the financial statements are authorised for issue. Classification turns on when the underlying condition existed, not merely when a notice, order or announcement was dated. A post-year-end GST demand can adjust year-end accounting when it confirms an existing exposure, but a new later condition is normally non-adjusting. Customer insolvency after year-end usually confirms credit impairment that existed at the reporting date, subject to evidence about causation. Dividends declared after the reporting period are not year-end liabilities, although note disclosure may be required. A loss of the going-concern basis is a fundamental accounting-basis issue, not a routine adjusting entry.

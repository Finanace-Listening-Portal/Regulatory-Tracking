Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); Union of India Vs Bhushan Airways Services Private Limited (NCLT Delhi) Summary:The National Company Law Tribunal (NCLT), Principal Bench, New Delhi, in the matter of Union of India Through Serious Fraud Investigation Office Vs Bhushan Airways Services Private Limited & Ors., considered whether proceedings alleging fraudulent conduct of business under Section 339 read with Section 246 of the Companies Act, 2013, could continue against the legal heir of a deceased statutory auditor. The application was filed by Mrs. Geeta Mehra, widow and legal heir of Late Mr. R.K. Mehra, seeking deletion of his name as Respondent No. 75 from Company Petition No. 105 of 2023. The Tribunal examined the maintainability of proceedings against a statutory auditor who was not involved in the company’s management, the effect of his death on an alleged compensatory liability, and the extent to which his estate could be proceeded against through his legal representative. Late Mr. R.K. Mehra was a partner of Mehra Goel & Co., the statutory audit firm of Bhushan Power and Steel Limited (BPSL), and had signed its financial statements for financial years 2011-12 to 2016-17. He died on 23 March 2020, before the institution of the company petition. His widow contended that neither she nor her deceased husband had participated in the day-to-day management or conduct of the company’s business. She submitted that her husband had signed the financial statements in his professional capacity on behalf of the audit firm, relying upon management representations, and had received no personal benefit from the alleged transactions. She further argued that no judicial finding of fraud, misfeasance, breach of trust or professional misconduct had been recorded against him and that her impleadment merely as his legal heir was unjustified. The Applicant also relied upon the abatement of criminal proceedings against her deceased husband in SFIO Case No. CC/770/2019 before the Special Court at Dwarka, New Delhi, pursuant to an order dated 10 February 2021. She maintained that the proceedings under Sections 241 and 242 were directed towards persons involved in the company’s management and could not be maintained against an outside professional or his widow. Reliance was placed on the Supreme Court decisions in Iswar Bhai C. Patel Vs Harihar Behera, Ramesh Hirachand Kundanmal Vs Municipal Corporation of Greater Bombay and Usha Ananthasubramanian Vs Union of India. The Applicant additionally contended that Section 339 ordinarily operates in winding-up proceedings, whereas BPSL had undergone corporate insolvency resolution and its resolution plan had already been approved. She also questioned whether the Central Government had formed the requisite opinion concerning the deceased auditor under Section 241(2). Opposing the application, the Union of India through SFIO submitted that the proceedings were not intended to punish the deceased auditor but to determine and recover compensation for losses allegedly caused through fraudulent conduct of business. According to SFIO, its investigation report dated 6 May 2022 disclosed material concerning the deceased auditor’s alleged abdication of statutory responsibilities and participation in the fraudulent conduct of BPSL’s business. SFIO argued that Section 339 extends beyond directors and officers to any person who knowingly participates in fraudulent business activities, including an auditor who consciously facilitates such conduct. It further submitted that Sections 337 to 341 become applicable to proceedings under Section 241 through Section 246 and that the death of an alleged wrongdoer does not extinguish a compensatory claim enforceable against the estate inherited by the legal representatives. The Tribunal first rejected the preliminary objection concerning the authority of the Applicant’s constituted attorney. Although the original power of attorney dated 13 November 2021 related to another company petition, the Applicant had executed a fresh power of attorney dated 12 September 2023 expressly authorising the present proceedings and ratifying the earlier acts. The Tribunal held that such a defect was curable through ratification and that no prejudice had been demonstrated. It also rejected the contention that the Central Government was required to form a separate opinion under Section 241(2) regarding every individual subsequently proceeded against. The statutory requirement concerns whether the affairs of the company are being conducted in a manner prejudicial to public interest. The question whether the allegations against the deceased auditor were established was therefore left for determination in the main proceedings. On the scope of Section 339, the Tribunal held that the provision covers two distinct categories: directors, managers and officers of the company, and other persons who knowingly participate in carrying on its business fraudulently. The second category is based upon conduct and knowledge rather than designation or managerial position. Accordingly, a statutory auditor cannot claim automatic immunity merely because he was not part of the company’s management. Referring to Usha Ananthasubramanian Vs Union of India, the Tribunal explained that liability requires a nexus with the business of the company concerned. Since the deceased auditor had allegedly signed the statutory financial statements of BPSL itself, the necessary connection was alleged to exist. However, the Tribunal emphasised that mere negligence, an error of professional judgment, inadequate professional scepticism or failure to detect irregularities does not establish knowing participation in fraud. SFIO must prove the requisite knowledge and participation through cogent evidence. The Tribunal further examined the statutory relationship between Sections 241, 246 and 339 of the Companies Act, 2013. It held that Section 246 makes Section 339 applicable mutatis mutandis to proceedings instituted under Section 241. Consequently, the reference in Section 339 to proceedings during winding-up must be suitably adapted to proceedings under Section 241, and the reference to an application by a liquidator, creditor or contributory must be understood in the context of an application by the Central Government under Section 241(2). Any other interpretation would render the legislative extension under Section 246 ineffective. The Tribunal also observed that separate statutory remedies for professional lapses by auditors do not exclude proceedings under Section 339 where knowing participation in fraudulent business conduct is specifically alleged. The question whether the auditor’s conduct amounted merely to professional negligence or conscious participation in fraud could be decided only after examining the evidence. Addressing the effect of death, the Tribunal relied upon the Supreme Court decisions in Official Liquidator Vs Parthasarathi Sinha and Official Liquidator Vs P.A. Tendolkar, which recognise the survival of compensatory claims arising from misfeasance and breach of fiduciary obligations against the estate of a deceased person. The Tribunal distinguished such proceedings from criminal prosecutions, where personal penal liability ordinarily terminates upon death. It held that a legal representative may remain before the Tribunal solely for representing the deceased’s estate and cannot incur personal liability beyond the value of inherited assets. The abatement of criminal proceedings against R.K. Mehra therefore did not automatically extinguish the separate statutory claim for compensation. The Tribunal also considered the approved insolvency resolution plan of BPSL but did not accept that its approval, by itself, required deletion of the deceased auditor’s legal representative from the proceedings. Questions concerning the existence, quantum and enforceability of any liability were reserved for final adjudication. Accordingly, the NCLT declined to delete the deceased auditor’s legal heir from the array of parties, while expressly preserving her statutory and procedural safeguards. The Tribunal directed SFIO to furnish a consolidated statement specifying the alleged acts and omissions of R.K. Mehra, the relevant financial years, supporting documents and the amount proposed to be recovered from his estate. The legal heir was afforded an opportunity to respond. The Tribunal also protected her against coercive action pending final disposal, while requiring disclosure and preservation of properties inherited from the deceased. The restrictions were confined to the inherited estate and did not extend to assets independently owned by the widow. Importantly, the Tribunal did not record any final finding that R.K. Mehra had participated in fraud. It left all substantive questions concerning knowledge, participation, liability and recoverable amounts open for determination in the main company petition.

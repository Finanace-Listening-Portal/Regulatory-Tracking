Summary: The Supreme Court’s decision in Dineshchand Surana v. UCO Bank, dated 27 May 2026, examines whether moratorium provisions under Part III of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016 (IBC) protect an individual director facing proceedings under Section 138 of the Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881. The Court distinguished the criminal consequences of cheque dishonour from the recovery of compensation arising out of those proceedings. It reasoned that the moratorium under Sections 96 and 101 may protect an individual debtor against recovery of compensation, even where the liability originates from a company’s debt, but does not automatically stop criminal prosecution. Importantly, the Court referred key questions to a larger Bench. It temporarily restricts certain legal actions so that an insolvency process is not defeated by individual creditors pursuing separate remedies. However, the Code does not provide one identical moratorium for every type of debtor. Section 14, under Part II, concerns a corporate debtor undergoing corporate insolvency resolution. Part III contains separate provisions for insolvency and bankruptcy of individuals and partnership firms, including Sections 96, 101, 124 and 128. Their wording and purpose differ. The distinction matters when a company defaults on a cheque and its directors are named in a complaint under Sections 138 and 141 of the Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881. A director may also be undergoing personal insolvency proceedings. Can the director rely on the personal-insolvency moratorium to stop the entire cheque-dishonour case? Or does the protection cover only the monetary recovery element while the criminal case continues? In Dineshchand Surana v. UCO Bank, decided on 27 May 2026, the Supreme Court considered these questions in detail. The judgment is important because it examines the relationship between criminal accountability and the enforcement of a financial obligation. At the same time, the Court directed that the matter be placed before the Chief Justice of India for constitution of a larger Bench to consider specified questions. The decision should therefore be understood as an important judicial development, rather than a final resolution of every issue concerning the scope of personal-insolvency moratoriums. 1. What happened in Dineshchand Surana v. UCO Bank? UCO Bank opened a letter of credit in favour of Surana Power Ltd. for approximately ₹5.03 crore to purchase Indonesian coal. Dineshchand Surana, the company’s former Managing Director, provided a blank cheque as security for the facility. After the letter of credit devolved and the bank became liable for bills originally payable by the company, Surana issued a cheque dated 26 March 2015 towards the outstanding dues. The cheque was dishonoured on 18 June 2015 due to insufficient funds, leading the bank to initiate proceedings under Section 138 of the Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881, against the company, Surana and other accused. The company subsequently went into liquidation. Meanwhile, insolvency proceedings were initiated against Surana in his personal capacity under Section 95 of the IBC. He argued that the interim moratorium under Section 96 protected him from the pending cheque-dishonour proceedings. The Madras High Court rejected his request to quash or stay the complaint, prompting the matter to reach the Supreme Court. The central issue was whether the words “any debt” in the personal-insolvency provisions cover compensation liability that may be imposed on a director in proceedings connected with a company’s dishonoured cheque. A further issue was whether the moratorium should halt the criminal process itself or only the monetary recovery aspect. 2. Section 14 is not the same as the personal-insolvency moratorium Section 14 applies when a corporate insolvency resolution application is admitted under Part II of the IBC. It restricts specified legal and enforcement actions against the corporate debtor and its assets. These include instituting or continuing suits, executing judgments, transferring or disposing of the debtor’s assets, enforcing security interests and recovering property occupied by or held in the debtor’s possession. Sections 14(2) and 14(2A) address the continuity of essential and certain critical supplies, subject to statutory conditions. Section 14(3) sets out exceptions, including the position of a surety under a contract of guarantee to a corporate debtor. The protection is directed principally at the corporate debtor and its assets. It does not automatically grant immunity to every director, promoter, guarantor or officer connected with the company. The position of an individual must be assessed under the applicable legal provisions and the nature of the proceeding. The Supreme Court examined the corporate moratorium in P. Mohanraj v. Shah Brothers Ispat Pvt. Ltd. (2021), which considered proceedings under Sections 138 and 141 of the Negotiable Instruments Act during a corporate moratorium. Part III of the IBC provides a different framework. Section 96 provides an interim moratorium following the filing of an application under Section 94 or 95, subject to the statutory conditions. Section 101 provides a moratorium after admission of the personal-insolvency application. These provisions refer to legal actions or proceedings “in respect of any debt”. The 2026 judgment examined the breadth of this language and compared it with provisions that apply during bankruptcy. 3. The distinction between criminal proceedings and compensation Section 138 of the Negotiable Instruments Act provides penalties for cheque dishonour when the statutory requirements are satisfied. Section 141 specifies when persons responsible for a company’s business may be held liable for offences committed by the company. Proceedings under Section 138 may result in imprisonment, a fine or both, while compensation may also be ordered in accordance with the applicable law. The Supreme Court examined the criminal and compensatory aspects of cheque-dishonour proceedings separately. The criminal aspect concerns the alleged offence and the enforcement of legal accountability. The compensatory aspect concerns monetary relief awarded to the complainant and its recovery from a person undergoing insolvency. The Bench reasoned that a personal-insolvency moratorium should not allow an individual to escape criminal liability. However, it also took the view that the expression “any debt” under Sections 96 and 101 could cover liabilities imposed on a director by law, even when the company originally owed the debt. On this reasoning, the moratorium could protect the individual against recovery of compensation during the relevant insolvency process. This distinction does not establish that every step in a cheque-dishonour case must be stayed. The effect of the moratorium depends on the nature and stage of the proceedings and the applicable statutory provisions. More importantly, the Supreme Court referred questions concerning the nature of Section 138 proceedings and the extent of the moratorium to a larger Bench. The Bench’s reasoning must therefore be distinguished from a final authoritative determination of those questions. 4. Why the words “any debt” matter A key part of the judgment is its comparison of Sections 96 and 101 with Sections 124 and 128 of the IBC. Sections 96 and 101 apply during the individual insolvency-resolution process and refer to proceedings in respect of “any debt”. The Bench reasoned that the expression is not necessarily limited to debts personally incurred by the individual. It may also cover liabilities that become payable through a contractual arrangement or statutory provision. Sections 124 and 128 operate within the bankruptcy framework and focus on actions against the debtor’s property in respect of debts. The Bench considered this difference significant. During insolvency resolution, the law aims to provide breathing space for the individual to formulate a repayment plan. During bankruptcy, the focus shifts more directly towards protecting and administering the debtor’s estate for distribution among creditors. This reasoning is relevant to directors who may face compensation liability connected with a corporate cheque. The Bench considered that denying protection solely because the liability originated in a company’s debt could undermine the purpose of personal insolvency. However, the potential protection concerns monetary enforcement and does not erase the underlying allegation or grant immunity from criminal adjudication. The precise reach of this reasoning must be considered alongside the questions referred to the larger Bench and any applicable statutory amendments. 5. Relevant statutory framework The principal provisions relevant to this issue are: Section 14, IBC: Provides the corporate-debtor moratorium under Part II and restricts specified proceedings and actions affecting the corporate debtor and its assets during the corporate insolvency resolution process. Sections 94 and 95, IBC: Provide for applications to initiate personal-insolvency resolution proceedings by an individual debtor or a creditor, respectively. Section 96, IBC: Provides an interim moratorium when an application under Section 94 or 95 is filed, subject to the statutory conditions and exceptions. Section 101, IBC: Provides for a moratorium following admission of the personal-insolvency application and addresses pending and fresh legal actions or proceedings in respect of any debt. Sections 124 and 128, IBC: Address the interim moratorium and the effect of a bankruptcy order. Their scope and wording differ from those of Sections 96 and 101. Section 79(15), IBC: Defines “excluded debt” to include liability to pay a fine imposed by a court or tribunal. This provision is relevant to the distinction between criminal punishment and debt-related recovery. Sections 138 and 141, Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881: Govern cheque dishonour and the circumstances in which persons responsible for a company’s business may be prosecuted. Section 395, Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023: The judgment considered this provision in relation to compensation orders. A further statutory development requires attention. Section 51 of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (Amendment) Act, 2026 inserted Section 96(4), which excludes the operation of Section 96 where an application is filed to initiate an insolvency-resolution process in respect of a personal guarantor to a corporate debtor. The relevant amendment came into force on 26 May 2026, one day before the Dineshchand Surana judgment. The Amendment Act also inserted Section 124(4), which provides a corresponding exclusion from Section 124 where an application is filed to initiate a bankruptcy process in respect of a personal guarantor to a corporate debtor. This amendment concerns the bankruptcy framework, whereas Section 96(4) concerns the interim moratorium during the insolvency-resolution process. These amendments are important when discussing the present scope of Sections 96 and 124. Their applicability in a particular matter depends on the nature of the application and whether the individual falls within the statutory category of a personal guarantor to a corporate debtor. The fact that an individual provided a cheque as security does not, by itself, establish that status. The relevant legal documents and circumstances should therefore be examined before relying on the judgment in a current case. 6. Practical implications for professionals For directors and promoters, the main lesson is that personal insolvency should not automatically be treated as a means of terminating criminal proceedings. A director named in a cheque-dishonour complaint must examine the nature of the relief sought, the stage of the case and the relevant statutory provisions. The legal position may also depend on whether the individual is a personal guarantor and whether the amended Section 96 applies. For banks and other creditors, the judgment highlights the need to distinguish between criminal proceedings and recovery of compensation from an individual undergoing insolvency. Creditors should identify the legal basis of their claim and the person against whom relief is sought. They should also determine whether the proposed action involves criminal prosecution, compensation or enforcement against the debtor’s property. For insolvency professionals, the case underlines the importance of explaining the scope of the moratorium precisely. Overstating the protection may create false expectations for a debtor, while understating it may overlook restrictions intended to support insolvency resolution. Records should distinguish the company’s insolvency process from any personal-insolvency or bankruptcy process involving a director or guarantor. For company secretaries and legal advisers, the case is a reminder to assess each proceeding separately before advising on the effect of insolvency. The company, its director and a personal guarantor may have different legal positions. Section 14, Sections 96 and 101, and the bankruptcy provisions should not be treated as interchangeable. 7. Important caution: the matter was referred to a larger Bench The judgment dated 27 May 2026 is significant, but its procedural outcome must be described accurately. At the conclusion, the two-judge Bench directed that the matter be placed before the Chief Justice of India for constitution of an appropriate three-judge Bench. It identified questions concerning whether Section 138 proceedings are quasi-criminal in nature with a tilt towards the criminal side, and whether the Part III moratorium applies to the entire proceeding or only to its compensatory aspect. The Bench referred these questions for further consideration, so they were not finally settled by this judgment. Its observations should therefore be understood in the context of the questions referred to the larger Bench, rather than treated as a final ruling on the scope of the personal-insolvency moratorium under the IBC. The amendments to Sections 96 and 124, effective from 26 May 2026, must also be considered when analysing the current statutory position, where the individual falls within the statutory category of a personal guarantor to a corporate debtor. Conclusion The moratorium under the IBC is not a single, universal shield. Section 14 protects the corporate debtor within the limits set by Part II, while Sections 96 and 101 provide a separate framework for personal insolvency. The Supreme Court’s decision in Dineshchand Surana v. UCO Bank highlights why the wording and purpose of each provision matter. The Bench distinguished criminal liability for cheque dishonour from the recovery of compensation. It reasoned that personal-insolvency moratorium provisions should not be used to avoid criminal prosecution, while they may protect an individual debtor against recovery of compensation arising from a company’s liability. However, the Court referred key questions to a larger Bench, and its observations should not be presented as a final resolution of those issues. The 2026 amendments to Sections 96 and 124 also require consideration when assessing their current application, particularly in matters involving personal guarantors to corporate debtors. The case illustrates the need to balance accountability in commercial transactions with the IBC’s objective of giving an individual debtor a meaningful opportunity to resolve debts. For professionals, the practical task is to identify the relevant moratorium, assess the nature of the proceeding and apply the statutory framework without assuming that the company and its directors receive identical protection.

At least $52.82 billion was subsequently disbursed through International Banking Units at GIFT IFSC, while banks have also used the deposits as a source of funding and collateral for further lending.The RBI's special dollar rupee swap facility attracted $143.596 billion in foreign currency inflows by September 18, including $132.98 billion through FCNR(B) deposits, $5.32 billion through overseas foreign currency borrowings and $5.296 billion through external commercial borrowings. The FCNR(B) window was closed on August 31, a month ahead of the original deadline, after the RBI said the response had been encouraging. Advt The mechanics mean the dollars did not simply remain with Indian banks as idle foreign currency. Under the swap arrangement, banks raised the FCNR(B) deposits and swapped the foreign currency with the RBI for rupees, while the RBI took on the corresponding foreign exchange position. The arrangement therefore provided banks with rupee liquidity while adding to the central bank's foreign currency resources and forward obligations.GIFT IFSC emerges as a major channelOne of the clearest identifiable destinations is GIFT IFSC. As of August 31, 20 International Banking Units had sanctioned $54.02 billion under the RBI's FCNR(B) swap facility and disbursed about $52.82 billion. The disbursement represented about 42 per cent of the $127.2 billion FCNR(B) mobilisation reported by the RBI for the facility as of August 31.The GIFT IFSC figures provide the clearest evidence that the funds were already being converted into foreign currency financing rather than remaining entirely within the banking system. Sanctions increased sharply from $28.60 billion on August 14 to $37.26 billion on August 21 and $54.02 billion by August 31.The distinction between the $132.98 billion and $127.2 billion figures is important. The former is the later RBI-reported FCNR(B) mobilisation figure as of September 18, while the latter relates to the August 31 position used in reporting on the facility and in S&P Global Ratings' analysis. Advt Banks also leveraged the depositsAnother route has been lending against deposits. Advances against fixed deposits rose 43.2 per cent year on year to Rs2.04 lakh crore in August, from Rs1.43 lakh crore a year earlier, according to RBI data. The figure also increased sharply from Rs1.62 lakh crore in July.The surge coincided with the FCNR(B) mobilisation and reflects the ability of deposit holders to borrow against the deposits and redeploy the funds. However, RBI data do not provide a separate breakdown of loans against FCNR(B) deposits, so the entire Rs2.04 lakh crore cannot be attributed to the special FCNR(B) facility. Advt S&P Global Ratings estimates that Indian banks raised about $127 billion through FCNR(B) accounts between June 8 and August 31. It estimates that bank balance sheets could eventually expand by $190 billion-$220 billion if 50-75 per cent of these deposits are leveraged through loans against pledged FCNR(B) deposits.That estimate illustrates the leverage potential of the scheme. The original foreign currency deposit does not necessarily represent the maximum amount of credit that can ultimately be generated against it. However, the $190 billion-$220 billion figure is an S&P estimate of potential balance sheet expansion and should not be treated as money already deployed.What happened to the remaining liquidity? Advt Not all the rupee liquidity generated through the swaps immediately became loans. S&P said the initial deployment of a substantial portion of the funds into government securities and money market instruments had helped banks manage the liquidity while they gradually sought higher yielding uses.The large inflow also contributed to a sharp increase in surplus banking-system liquidity. The surplus reached about Rs11.16 lakh crore in early September before subsequently declining as the RBI absorbed liquidity through its operations. The FCNR(B) mobilisation therefore eased banks' funding constraints, but also created a sterilisation challenge for the central bank.The RBI's foreign exchange position also changed materially. The swap mechanism increased the central bank's foreign currency resources but created future obligations because the transactions have to be reversed as the underlying deposits mature. The FCNR(B) deposits have three to five year tenors, meaning the liquidity benefit today comes with a future foreign currency repayment requirement.The money trail therefore has four identifiable stages: foreign currency was raised through FCNR(B) deposits, swapped with the RBI for rupee liquidity, channelled into foreign currency lending including through GIFT IFSC, and in some cases leveraged through loans against pledged deposits. The remainder has been deployed into other bank assets or absorbed through liquidity management rather than immediately turning into fresh domestic credit.The key question for banks is now how much of the temporary funding advantage can be converted into higher yielding assets. FCNR(B) deposits have eased the pressure created by strong credit growth and slower deposit mobilisation, but the deposits will eventually mature and have to be repaid or rolled over.The scheme has therefore done more than add $133 billion to India's foreign currency pool. It has temporarily changed banks' funding mix, supplied rupee liquidity, supported foreign currency lending through GIFT IFSC and created scope for additional leverage against deposits. The eventual test will be whether banks can convert that temporary funding advantage into productive assets without creating a refinancing problem when the FCNR(B) deposits mature.

Consortium Engineers Private Limited Vs Indian Institute of Management (Calcutta High Court) Summary: The Calcutta High Court, in B.K. Consortium Engineers Private Limited Vs Indian Institute of Management, Calcutta, examined whether a contractor could invoke arbitration for outstanding price escalation claims several years after accepting the final bill under a construction contract. The petition was filed under Section 11 of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996, seeking appointment of a sole arbitrator to adjudicate disputes arising from the execution of construction works at the Indian Institute of Management, Calcutta (IIMC). The petitioner challenged the contractual provision authorising the Director of IIMC to appoint the sole arbitrator, arguing that such a unilateral appointment mechanism was inconsistent with the principles of independence and impartiality governing arbitration. The respondent opposed the application on the ground that the contractor had already accepted the final settlement and that the alleged escalation claims were hopelessly barred by limitation. Justice Shekhar B. Saraf considered both issues and held that although the unilateral appointment procedure was legally invalid, the underlying dispute was manifestly time-barred. Consequently, the Court declined to appoint an arbitrator, emphasising that the existence of an arbitration agreement does not automatically require reference of every dispute to arbitration. The dispute originated from a notice inviting tender issued by IIMC on 9 July 2008 for civil construction and basic utility works relating to a proposed residential hostel complex at Joka, Kolkata. Following the petitioner’s successful bid, a work order dated 29 August 2008 was issued for a contract valued at approximately ₹39.03 crore, with completion originally scheduled for 31 August 2009. During execution, however, substantial additions and alterations were introduced, increasing the contract value to approximately ₹80.23 crore. The petitioner maintained that the project suffered delays because the work site was not handed over in time, piling work undertaken by another contractor remained incomplete, specifications were repeatedly modified, and execution was suspended for a considerable period before resuming on 14 March 2011. IIMC granted several extensions, ultimately extending the completion date to 31 August 2014. The petitioner completed the works within the extended period and subsequently claimed that the prolonged execution period had resulted in substantial financial losses because the contract rates had been determined in 2008. According to the contractor, these circumstances entitled it to price escalation under Clause 10(CC) of the agreement. The controversy intensified when IIMC prepared the final bill on 11 March 2016. On 15 March 2016, two directors of the petitioner company signed a communication accepting the final bill as full and final settlement of all demands under the contract and undertaking not to raise further claims. A subsequent letter dated 17 March 2016 reiterated acceptance of the final bill while requesting consideration of price escalation. The respondent issued the final completion certificate on 5 May 2016. Thereafter, through a communication dated 16 May 2016, the petitioner acknowledged that the final bill had been settled and the retention money refunded but asserted that its escalation claim remained unresolved. The contractor continued pursuing the matter through correspondence dated 25 August 2017, 15 September 2017 and 22 February 2019, among other communications. IIMC rejected the escalation demand through its letter dated 12 September 2017, relying on the earlier full and final settlement. The petitioner alleged that the acceptance certificate had been issued under financial compulsion and that the subsequent correspondence effectively withdrew or superseded the earlier unconditional acceptance. The respondent disputed these allegations and maintained that no further contractual liability survived. The petitioner contended that the limitation period should be calculated from 12 September 2017, when IIMC expressly rejected the escalation claim, rather than from March 2016, when the final bill was prepared and accepted. It argued that the parties had continued discussions regarding the outstanding demand and that a committee constituted by IIMC had considered the issue, including through meetings and written submissions in January and February 2021. According to the petitioner, these developments demonstrated that the dispute remained alive and had not become an abandoned or extinguished claim. It further relied on the Supreme Court’s orders extending limitation during the COVID-19 pandemic to contend that its notice invoking arbitration on 8 March 2021 was within the permissible period. Referring to Geo Miller & Company Private Limited v. Rajasthan Vidyut Utpadan Nigam Limited, the petitioner submitted that bona fide settlement negotiations could, in appropriate circumstances, be relevant to identifying the point at which negotiations failed and the cause of action arose. It also relied on Vidya Drolia v. Durga Trading Corporation, Bharat Sanchar Nigam Ltd. v. Nortel Networks India Pvt. Ltd. and Uttarakhand Purv Sainik Kalyan Nigam Ltd. v. Northern Coal Field Ltd. to argue that disputed questions concerning limitation should ordinarily be decided by the arbitral tribunal rather than conclusively determined at the appointment stage. IIMC, on the other hand, maintained that the contractor had voluntarily accepted the final bill in March 2016 and expressly agreed not to raise further demands. The respondent argued that the subsequent allegation of coercion or financial duress was unsupported by sufficient material and could not invalidate an otherwise binding settlement. It relied on New India Assurance Company Limited v. Genus Power Infrastructure Limited to support its contention that a bare allegation of coercion was insufficient to reopen a concluded settlement. The respondent further submitted that Clause 17 of the special contractual conditions excluded escalation payments and that the substantial enhancement of the original contract value had already accommodated the additional work undertaken. On limitation, IIMC argued that repeated representations, reminders and settlement discussions could not create a fresh cause of action or extend a limitation period that had already commenced. Reliance was placed on Bharat Sanchar Nigam Ltd. v. Nortel Networks India Pvt. Ltd., Geo Miller & Company Private Limited v. Rajasthan Vidyut Utpadan Nigam Limited and Secunderabad Cantonment Board v. B. Ramachandraiah & Sons. The respondent submitted that the petitioner’s claims were clearly stale and constituted the category of disputes that courts could refuse to refer to arbitration at the threshold. The High Court first examined the validity of the contractual mechanism governing appointment of the sole arbitrator. Clause 25 empowered the Director of IIMC to appoint the arbitrator for resolving disputes between the parties. Referring to the Supreme Court decisions in Perkins Eastman Architects DPC & Another v. HSCC (India) Ltd. and TRF Ltd. v. Energo Engineering Projects Ltd., the Court held that a party interested in the outcome of a dispute could not unilaterally control the appointment of the sole arbitrator. The Court emphasised that independence, neutrality and impartiality are fundamental requirements of arbitration proceedings and that an appointment procedure permitting one interested party to select the adjudicator was contrary to these principles. It accordingly agreed with the petitioner’s challenge to the appointment mechanism. However, the Court made it clear that invalidity of the appointment procedure did not automatically establish the petitioner’s entitlement to appointment of an independent arbitrator. The Court was still required to examine whether a legally subsisting and arbitrable dispute existed. It rejected the suggestion that judicial scrutiny under Section 11 was confined to a mechanical verification of the existence of an arbitration agreement, observing that courts retain limited authority to prevent manifestly untenable disputes from entering the arbitral process. On the scope of judicial intervention, the High Court analysed the principles laid down in Vidya Drolia v. Durga Trading Corporation, Bharat Sanchar Nigam Ltd. v. Nortel Networks India Pvt. Ltd. and DLF Home Developers Limited v. Rajapura Homes Pvt. Ltd. The Court recognised that questions of limitation ordinarily involve mixed issues of fact and law and generally fall within the jurisdiction of the arbitral tribunal. Nevertheless, it observed that courts exercising jurisdiction under Section 11 may refuse reference where the claim is demonstrably time-barred, non-arbitrable or devoid of any subsisting dispute. Such intervention must remain narrow and should not result in a detailed adjudication of disputed factual questions properly reserved for arbitration. Applying these principles, the Court examined the contractual documents and correspondence to identify when the contractor’s cause of action had first arisen. It found that the petitioner had raised escalation demands even before preparation of the final bill. Consequently, when IIMC prepared the final bill on 11 March 2016 without incorporating the claimed escalation amount, that omission effectively constituted rejection of the previously asserted demand. The limitation period therefore commenced on 11 March 2016. The petitioner’s subsequent acceptance of the final bill further reinforced the conclusion that the dispute concerning escalation had already arisen by that date. The High Court rejected the petitioner’s argument that the express rejection communicated on 12 September 2017 constituted the first accrual of the cause of action. It held that once limitation began running in March 2016, subsequent correspondence, renewed demands and further refusals could not restart the statutory period. The Court also rejected the contention that the contractor’s letter dated 16 May 2016 had effectively withdrawn the earlier full and final settlement so as to revive the escalation claim. The principle emerging from Secunderabad Cantonment Board v. B. Ramachandraiah & Sons was considered relevant because later correspondence could not furnish a fresh starting point for limitation where the right to seek relief had already accrued. The Court distinguished the petitioner’s reliance on continuing negotiations from circumstances in which genuine settlement discussions might affect identification of the original cause of action. It concluded that the contractor could not rely on subsequent representations and discussions to revive a claim whose limitation period had already commenced. The COVID-19 limitation-extension orders did not cure the delay because, on the Court’s determination of the accrual date, the relevant limitation period had already expired before the pandemic-related exclusion commenced. Accordingly, the notice invoking arbitration on 8 March 2021 could not provide a basis for referring the stale escalation dispute to arbitration. In conclusion, the Calcutta High Court dismissed the petition seeking appointment of a sole arbitrator. The Court held that the contractual provision authorising unilateral appointment of the arbitrator by IIMC was unsustainable under the principles established by the Supreme Court, but the contractor’s underlying escalation claims were ex facie barred by limitation. The judgment illustrates the distinction between the validity of an arbitration agreement or appointment mechanism and the continued enforceability of the claims proposed to be referred. A defective arbitrator appointment clause may justify judicial intervention to ensure an independent appointment where an otherwise referable dispute exists, but it does not revive a claim that has already become time-barred. The decision also highlights the importance of promptly invoking arbitration when a contractual claim is rejected or when circumstances establish that a dispute has arisen. Contractors cannot ordinarily preserve limitation indefinitely through unilateral reminders, repeated demands or subsequent settlement discussions after the statutory period has begun. The Court’s ruling reinforces the limited screening function of courts at the Section 11 stage, permitting rejection of plainly stale claims while leaving genuinely disputed questions of limitation to arbitral adjudication.

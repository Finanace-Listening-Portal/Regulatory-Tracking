Ltd Vs ITO (Supreme Court of India) Summary: The Supreme Court of India declined to interfere with the Delhi High Court’s judgment in BKR Capital Pvt. Ltd. Vs ITO, dismissing the Special Leave Petition after hearing counsel and examining the material on record. The Delhi High Court had upheld reassessment proceedings initiated under Sections 148A and 148 of the Income-tax Act, 1961 for Assessment Year 2017-18, notwithstanding that the final reassessment notice was issued after 31 March 2024. The principal question was whether the period consumed in granting adjournments sought by the assessee could be excluded while computing limitation under Section 149(1). The High Court held that the statutory exclusion provision applied and that the assessee could not successfully challenge the notice merely because the Assessing Officer had granted additional time to submit objections. The Supreme Court’s order contains no independent discussion of the statutory provisions or merits; it records only that no sufficient ground existed to interfere with the High Court’s decision. The controversy arose from two writ petitions involving substantially identical facts and legal questions. The Delhi High Court considered the facts of BKR Capital Pvt. Ltd., W.P.(C) 19738/2025, as representative of both matters. The petitioner had filed its income-tax return for AY 2017-18 on 29 October 2017, declaring total income of Rs. 13,63,270. Subsequently, the Assessing Officer initiated proceedings for reassessment by issuing a show-cause notice dated 21 March 2024 under Section 148A(b). The notice required the assessee to furnish its explanation by 28 March 2024. At that stage, the ordinary six-year limitation period relevant to the controversy was due to expire on 31 March 2024. The assessee did not submit its reply within the originally stipulated period and instead sought additional time. This request became central to determining whether the eventual notice under Section 148 had been issued within the legally permissible period. On 28 March 2024, the petitioner requested an adjournment, following which the Assessing Officer deferred the proceedings to 8 April 2024. On that date, the assessee again sought approximately one week’s additional time, and the matter was consequently fixed for 13 April 2024. The petitioner ultimately filed its response on 13 April 2024. After considering the reply, the Assessing Officer passed an order under Section 148A(d) on 15 April 2024, rejecting the objections, and simultaneously issued a reassessment notice under Section 148. The petitioner challenged the notice through proceedings under Article 226 of the Constitution, arguing that the statutory authority to issue it had expired on 31 March 2024. According to the petitioner, the notice issued on 15 April 2024 was therefore without jurisdiction and void from inception. The assessee’s principal argument rested upon the first proviso to Section 149(1), as applicable before the substitution introduced by the Finance Act, 2024. The petitioner submitted that the relevant assessment year was governed by the limitation restrictions preserved under the earlier statutory framework. Since six years from the end of AY 2017-18 expired on 31 March 2024, it was contended that no reassessment notice could validly be issued thereafter. The petitioner emphasised that the issuance of a preliminary show-cause notice under Section 148A(b) was not equivalent to the issuance of a final notice under Section 148. It relied upon the Rajasthan High Court decision in Shree Cement Ltd. Vs Assistant Commissioner of Income Tax, Central Circle & Others, as well as Manju Somani Vs Income Tax Officer, (2024) 165 taxmann.com 675, to contend that reassessment proceedings could not survive beyond the statutory limitation period. The Revenue disputed the petitioner’s interpretation of the limitation provisions. It acknowledged that the final notice appeared to have been issued after the ordinary limitation date but maintained that the calculation could not disregard the statutory exclusion available under Section 149(1). The Department pointed out that the show-cause notice had been issued on 21 March 2024, when the Assessing Officer unquestionably possessed jurisdiction to initiate the statutory process. The assessee was initially required to respond by 28 March 2024 but repeatedly requested further time. The Revenue submitted that the resulting period had to be excluded while calculating limitation. It argued that the first proviso could not be interpreted independently of the other provisos governing exclusion and extension of time. Reading the provision as a whole, the Department maintained that the reassessment notice was valid and that the petitioner could not rely upon a delay resulting from its own adjournment requests to invalidate the proceedings. The Revenue further argued that the relevant provisos formed part of a composite statutory limitation framework. The first proviso restricted retrospective enlargement of limitation for earlier assessment years, whereas the subsequent provisos addressed the exclusion of time granted to the assessee and other specified circumstances. According to the Department, these provisions had to be interpreted harmoniously so that each retained its intended operation. An interpretation that treated 31 March 2024 as an absolute cut-off without accounting for the time allowed to the assessee would render the exclusion provision ineffective. The Revenue distinguished Manju Somani Vs Income Tax Officer on the ground that the statutory exclusion arising from the assessee’s own requests for additional time had not been involved in that case. It also relied upon Raminder Singh Vs Assistant Commissioner of Income Tax, (2023) 156 taxmann.com 148, while supporting the proposition that limitation must be calculated by applying the relevant statutory provisions together. The Delhi High Court examined the statutory framework under Section 149, including the amendments introduced by the Finance Act, 2021 and the provisions applicable at the relevant time. It observed that the first proviso prevented reassessment notices for specified earlier assessment years where proceedings had already become barred under the limitation provisions existing before the Finance Act, 2021. However, the Court also considered the provision permitting exclusion of the time or extended time allowed to the assessee pursuant to a show-cause notice issued under Section 148A(b). The Court identified the essential issue as whether the notice dated 15 April 2024 was barred despite the commencement of the reassessment procedure on 21 March 2024 and the subsequent adjournments requested by the assessee. The Court considered the statutory exclusion mechanism material to the computation and rejected the proposition that the ordinary limitation date could be applied without giving effect to that mechanism. The High Court found that the Section 148A(b) proceedings had commenced before 31 March 2024, when the Assessing Officer possessed jurisdiction under the applicable statutory provisions. The initial opportunity to respond was granted until 28 March 2024. The proceedings remained pending beyond that date because the petitioner requested additional time on two occasions. The assessee filed its reply only on 13 April 2024, following which the Assessing Officer passed the Section 148A(d) order and issued the Section 148 notice on 15 April 2024. These facts were not materially disputed. The Court considered that the delay in completing the preliminary procedure arose from the assessee’s own requests rather than administrative inaction alone. The Assessing Officer had accommodated those requests to provide a reasonable opportunity of hearing before deciding whether reassessment proceedings should continue. The Court recognised that principles of natural justice required the Assessing Officer to afford the assessee a fair opportunity to respond before issuing a reassessment notice. At the same time, it acknowledged that equitable considerations could not independently override statutory limitation. The validity of the notice therefore depended upon the applicable legislative exclusion, not merely upon the Assessing Officer’s bona fide conduct. The High Court held that the statutory framework permitted exclusion of the period consumed in granting the relevant opportunity and extensions. It considered the Assessing Officer’s decision to grant additional time consistent with the procedural safeguards contemplated under Section 148A. The Court also observed that denying such exclusion could encourage assessing authorities to reject reasonable requests for additional time merely because limitation was approaching, thereby undermining the purpose of providing a meaningful pre-notice hearing. In applying the relevant proviso to Section 149(1), the High Court held that the period from 28 March 2024 to 15 April 2024, during which the proceedings remained pending following the assessee’s adjournment requests, was liable to be excluded for the purpose of limitation. The Court also considered the petitioner’s conduct significant: the assessee had requested and obtained additional time, filed its reply on 13 April 2024 and had not raised its limitation objection at that stage. The objection was advanced after issuance of the final notice in the writ proceedings. Although this conduct could not independently displace statutory limitation, it supported the factual basis for applying the exclusion provision. The Court concluded that the Assessing Officer had not acted in defiance of the limitation framework and that the final notice was issued after completing the mandatory procedure within the time available after statutory exclusion. The High Court distinguished the precedents relied upon by the assessee. In Manju Somani Vs Income Tax Officer, the Court noted that the relevant notice had been issued beyond limitation without a comparable delay attributable to the assessee, and the statutory exclusion had neither been applicable nor pleaded by the Revenue. Accordingly, that decision did not govern the present controversy. The Court also distinguished Shree Cement Ltd. Vs Assistant Commissioner of Income Tax, Central Circle & Others, where the Section 148A(b) notice had been issued on 31 March 2024 and the final Section 148 notice on 1 May 2024. The High Court observed that the proceedings in that case had commenced at the very end of the limitation period and that the applicability of the relevant exclusion proviso had not been claimed or examined. The present case involved proceedings initiated earlier and adjournments expressly sought by the petitioner. The Court therefore considered the factual and statutory foundation materially different. On these findings, the Delhi High Court concluded that the Assessing Officer had acted within jurisdiction and that the notice issued on 15 April 2024 under Section 148 was not barred by limitation. It held that the period attributable to the assessee’s requests for adjournment was excludable under the applicable proviso to Section 149(1). Consequently, the petitioner’s challenge to the reassessment notice failed. The High Court dismissed both writ petitions along with the pending applications. Its decision established, on the facts before it, that the limitation period for issuing a reassessment notice must be calculated after accounting for the statutory exclusion of time granted to the assessee during the Section 148A procedure. The ruling did not treat the mere commencement of Section 148A proceedings as sufficient in every case; the exclusion of time under the applicable statutory provision was central to its conclusion. The matter thereafter reached the Supreme Court of India through a Special Leave Petition challenging the High Court’s decision. The Supreme Court recorded that it had heard learned counsel for the petitioners and examined the material on record. Finding no sufficient ground to interfere with the impugned High Court order, it dismissed the Special Leave Petition and disposed of any pending applications. The Supreme Court did not deliver an independently reasoned interpretation of Sections 148A or 149 in the supplied order. Accordingly, the detailed legal reasoning concerning exclusion of time, adjournment requests and reassessment limitation remains that of the Delhi High Court. The combined outcome is that the High Court’s dismissal of the writ petitions remained undisturbed, and the assessee’s challenge to the reassessment notice did not succeed before the Supreme Court.

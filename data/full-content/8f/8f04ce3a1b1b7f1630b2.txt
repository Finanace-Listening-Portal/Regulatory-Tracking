Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); Ashish Kakkar Vs UT of Chandigarh (Supreme Court of India) Summary: The Supreme Court of India, in Ashish Kakkar Vs UT of Chandigarh, set aside the judgment of the Punjab and Haryana High Court upholding the arrest of the appellant in a cybercrime investigation involving alleged extortion through digital loan applications. The Supreme Court held that furnishing an arrest memo containing only the accused’s identity, place of arrest and reference to a co-accused’s statement did not satisfy the mandatory requirement of communicating the grounds of arrest under Section 50 of the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973, read with Article 22(1) of the Constitution of India. Although the High Court had earlier concluded that a notice under Section 41A CrPC was unnecessary where arrest was required under Section 41(1), the Supreme Court confined its examination to the failure to furnish adequate grounds of arrest. Finding a clear constitutional and statutory violation, it invalidated both the arrest and the consequential remand order and directed the appellant’s release unless required in another case. The dispute originated from FIR No. 33/2022 dated 3 September 2022, registered at Police Station Cyber Cell, Chandigarh, under Sections 384, 420, 468, 471, 509 and 120B of the Indian Penal Code, 1860. The complainant, Arvind Kumar, alleged that he had received a message offering a digital loan through the Hugo Loan application. After allowing the application access to his mobile phone and subsequently uninstalling it, he allegedly received threatening calls and messages demanding money. The persons operating the application allegedly accessed his contacts and photographs, circulated morphed obscene images and threatened further circulation unless payments were made. The complainant stated that he transferred ₹2,045 on 24 August 2022 and two payments of ₹3,500 each on 30 August 2022, but the threats continued. During investigation, the police alleged the existence of an organised operation involving digital loan applications, multiple accused persons, financial transactions and handlers connected with China. Following the arrest of co-accused Manoj Rathore on 29 December 2024, the appellant’s name emerged through disclosure statements. He was arrested on 30 December 2024 from Hotel Jaypee Siddharth, New Delhi, and produced before the jurisdictional Magistrate, who granted three days’ police custody. Before the Punjab and Haryana High Court, the appellant challenged his arrest and remand principally on the grounds that no notice under Section 41A CrPC had been served, the grounds of arrest had not been properly communicated and he had not been afforded an adequate opportunity to obtain legal assistance during remand proceedings. His counsel relied upon Arnesh Kumar Vs State of Bihar, Prabir Purkayastha Vs State (NCT of Delhi) and Satender Kumar Antil Vs Central Bureau of Investigation. The appellant particularly emphasised the distinction between the general reasons justifying an arrest and the specific grounds of arrest that must be communicated to enable an accused person to challenge custodial detention and seek bail. The Chandigarh administration opposed the petition, contending that the appellant had allegedly avoided the investigating agency, changed locations and refused to accept notice. It maintained that the investigating officer had recorded the necessity of arrest and that the Magistrate had examined the relevant material before granting police custody. The Punjab and Haryana High Court accepted the investigating agency’s position and dismissed the petition. Examining Sections 41 and 41A CrPC, it held that notice under Section 41A was not mandatory in every case involving an offence punishable with imprisonment up to seven years. According to the High Court, Section 41A operated where arrest was not required under Section 41(1), whereas the police could proceed with arrest when the statutory conditions were fulfilled and the investigating officer had recorded the necessity of custody. The High Court referred to the case diary, the appellant’s alleged attempts to evade investigation, the arrest memo signed by him and the subsequent remand proceedings. It also noted that further remand orders dated 2 January and 5 January 2025 had been passed while the appellant was represented by counsel. On this reasoning, the High Court found no illegality in the arrest or the remand order dated 30 December 2024. It nevertheless clarified that its observations would not constitute findings on the merits of the criminal case. In appeal, the Supreme Court considered the appellant’s objections concerning non-compliance with Section 41A CrPC, absence of an opportunity of hearing at the remand stage and failure to furnish the grounds of arrest under Section 50 CrPC. However, the Court expressly restricted its consideration to the non-furnishing of grounds of arrest. On examining Annexure P-3, the Supreme Court found that the document furnished to the appellant was merely an arrest memo in the prescribed format, containing his name, the place of arrest and a statement that he had been arrested on the basis of a co-accused’s disclosure. It held that such a document could not be treated as an adequate communication of the grounds of arrest because no other worthwhile particulars had been furnished. The Supreme Court applied the principle laid down in Prabir Purkayastha Vs State (NCT of Delhi), (2024) 8 SCC 254, recognising that the constitutional requirement of informing an arrested person of the grounds of arrest is substantive and cannot be satisfied by a formal arrest memo lacking the necessary particulars. The Supreme Court consequently held that the arrest suffered from clear non-compliance with Section 50 CrPC, which gives effect to Article 22(1) of the Constitution. It set aside the High Court judgment, the appellant’s arrest and the consequential remand order. The Court directed that the appellant be set at liberty unless required in another case and allowed the appeal. The combined decisions demonstrate the distinction between the statutory power to arrest and the separate constitutional obligation to communicate the grounds of arrest. The High Court’s conclusion concerning the circumstances in which Section 41A notice may be dispensed with did not resolve the independent requirement under Section 50. The Supreme Court’s ruling establishes that even where the investigating agency claims that arrest was necessary, the arrest memo cannot substitute for meaningful communication of the grounds of arrest. The Supreme Court did not independently decide whether Section 41A notice was required in the circumstances or determine the merits of the allegations underlying the FIR. FULL TEXT OF THE SUPREME COURT JUDGMENT/ORDER Leave granted. The appellant was arrested on 30.12.2024 in connection with FIR No. 33/2022 registered under Sections 384, 420, 468, 471, 509 and 120B of the Indian Penal Code, 1860 and remanded to police custody for a period of 3 days. Vide the present appeal, the appellant has challenged both his arrest and the remand order dated 30.12.2024 on three grounds, namely, there is a clear non-compliance of the mandate under Section 41-A of the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973 (hereinafter referred to as ‘the Code’); the appellant was not heard at the time of remand and the grounds of arrest as mandated under Section 50 of the Code have not been furnished to the appellant as against the mere arrest memo. We are inclined to consider only the last issue raised by the appellant with respect to the non-furnishing of the grounds of arrest. Upon perusing annexure P-3, we can see that what has been provided to the appellant is only an arrest memo in the prescribed format, which is meant to be given to the appellant by way of an intimation. It has been filled up with the name of the appellant along with the place of arrest. Additionally, it has been written that he has been arrested based upon the statement of the co-accused. We are in agreement with the submission made by the learned senior counsel appearing for the appellant that the said arrest memo cannot be construed as grounds of arrest, as no other worthwhile particulars have been furnished to him. This, being a clear non-compliance of the mandate under Section 50 of the Code which has been introduced to give effect to Article 22(1) of the Constitution of India, 1950 we are inclined to set aside the impugned judgment, particularly, in light of the judgment rendered by this Court reported as Prabir Purkayastha v. State (NCT of Delhi) – (2024) 8 SCC 254. In such view of the matter, the impugned judgment stands set aside and the arrest of the appellant followed by the consequential remand order are also set aside. The appellant shall be set at liberty, until and unless he is required in any other case. The appeal stands allowed accordingly. Pending application(s), if any, shall also stand disposed of. Leave granted. The Court inter alia directed as under: “The appellant shall be set at liberty, until and unless he is required in any other case.” The appeal stands allowed accordingly.

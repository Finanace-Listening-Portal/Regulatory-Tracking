Summary: Incorporating a company in Dubai involves not only UAE company registration requirements but also compliance with India’s Foreign Exchange Management Act where the investor is a person resident in India. The Foreign Exchange Management (Overseas Investment) Rules, Regulations and RBI Directions, 2022 govern overseas direct investment, permissible activities, funding structures, valuation, financial commitments and reporting obligations. Resident individuals commonly make permitted investments under the Liberalised Remittance Scheme, subject to the USD 250,000 annual ceiling and applicable ODI conditions, while Indian entities operate under a separate financial commitment framework generally linked to 400% of net worth. Important considerations include determining FEMA residential status, selecting the appropriate Indian shareholder, routing remittances through authorised dealer banks, documenting the source of funds, planning shareholder loans and corporate guarantees, and assessing restrictions on step-down subsidiaries. Ongoing obligations may include initial ODI reporting, annual performance reporting, event-based disclosures and regularisation of delayed filings. The article explains these requirements through practical examples involving individual founders and Indian companies establishing Dubai subsidiaries. It emphasises that UAE incorporation, banking arrangements and Indian overseas investment records should be coordinated from the beginning to avoid complications in future financing, restructuring, repatriation and exit transactions. A founder can select a business activity, choose between mainland and free zone, submit identity documents, obtain the licence and begin opening a corporate bank account within a relatively short period. For an Indian resident, however, the UAE incorporation process is only half of the compliance story. The moment an Indian resident individual or Indian entity acquires ownership in a Dubai company, India’s foreign exchange rules may become relevant. That means a company can be validly incorporated in Dubai and still have an Indian FEMA compliance issue if the investment, remittance, ownership structure or reporting has not been handled correctly. This distinction is particularly important because many founders approach the transaction in the wrong order. They ask: “Which Dubai licence should I take?” before asking: “How am I legally permitted to own and fund this company from India?” For Indian residents, the second question should often come first. Dubai Incorporation Is a UAE Process; Ownership From India Is a FEMA Process The UAE authorities determine whether a company can be incorporated in Dubai. India determines how a person resident in India may invest in that overseas company. These are two separate regulatory systems. The current Indian overseas investment framework is principally governed by: the Foreign Exchange Management (Overseas Investment) Rules, 2022; the Foreign Exchange Management (Overseas Investment) Regulations, 2022; and directions issued by the Reserve Bank of India. The Department of Economic Affairs confirms that the 2022 framework was introduced to simplify overseas investment and distinguish more clearly between Overseas Direct Investment and Overseas Portfolio Investment. For a founder incorporating a normal private company in Dubai and acquiring its shares, the investment will commonly fall within the ODI framework. That is why FEMA review should form part of the incorporation plan itself. First Determine Whether the Investor Is a Person Resident in India Before analysing ODI, the investor’s residential status under FEMA must be established. This is not merely a question of Indian citizenship. An Indian citizen living in Dubai may be a person resident outside India for FEMA purposes, depending on the facts. Likewise, an Indian citizen who has recently relocated abroad should not automatically assume that FEMA treats them the same way as an Indian resident who continues to live and work in India. This matters because the overseas investment rules principally regulate investment made by persons resident in India. The FEMA residential-status test should therefore be examined before deciding whether the ODI framework applies. This is also different from determining residential status under Indian income-tax law. The two concepts should not be mixed. Incorporating a Dubai Company Usually Creates ODI for an Indian Resident Suppose an Indian resident subscribes to shares of a newly incorporated Dubai company. The company is privately held and unlisted. That investment will ordinarily have the characteristics of Overseas Direct Investment because the investor is acquiring equity in an overseas entity rather than making a passive portfolio investment. The 2022 Overseas Investment Rules treat investment in equity of incorporated foreign entities as a non-debt investment and create a separate framework for ODI. DEA This classification matters because ODI brings with it conditions relating to: eligibility; permissible business activities; funding; valuation; reporting; financial commitment; step-down subsidiaries; and eventual transfer or disinvestment. The share subscription should therefore not be treated like an ordinary international payment. Resident Individuals Commonly Use the LRS Route For an Indian resident individual incorporating a company in Dubai, the Liberalised Remittance Scheme is often relevant to funding the investment. RBI currently permits resident individuals to remit up to USD 250,000 per financial year under LRS for permitted current and capital-account transactions, subject to applicable FEMA restrictions. This limit is cumulative across permitted LRS remittances during the financial year. For example, if a resident individual has already used part of the LRS limit for another permitted purpose, the remaining amount available for investment in the Dubai company may be correspondingly lower. But there is an important qualification. LRS is the remittance framework. It is not a substitute for ODI compliance. RBI itself states that where a resident individual makes overseas direct investment, the transaction must comply with the Overseas Investment Rules, Regulations and Directions. A founder therefore cannot simply say: “I have USD 250,000 under LRS, so I can invest in any Dubai company.” The business and investment structure must still be permissible under FEMA. The Nature of the Dubai Business Matters Before incorporating the company, the proposed business activity should be reviewed. A normal consulting, software, trading, logistics or service business will generally present a different risk profile from a business involving restricted sectors. The Overseas Investment Rules are built around investment in a foreign entity carrying on a bona fide business activity. Some areas require additional caution, particularly where the proposed business involves regulated financial activity or sectors specifically restricted under India’s overseas investment framework. This is one reason why the UAE licence activity should not be selected only on the recommendation of an incorporation agent. The licence may be valid in Dubai but still create issues for the Indian investor. The correct sequence is to compare the UAE activity with the FEMA rules before the shareholder subscribes to the company. Do Not Pay Share Capital From an Unstructured Route A common practical mistake occurs during incorporation. The founder is asked to pay the initial share capital or establishment amount and transfers money informally from: a personal foreign account; another overseas company; a relative; a third-party account; or some other source not matching the intended shareholder. Later, the ownership documents show the Indian resident as shareholder. At that point, the paperwork and money trail no longer align cleanly. For FEMA purposes, the method of funding matters. Where the Indian resident is making ODI, the remittance should generally be routed properly through an authorised banking channel and supported by the required documents. The commercial desire to “finish the licence quickly” should not override the investment trail. A clean source-of-funds record is particularly important when the company later opens a bank account, brings in investors or is sold. The Authorised Dealer Bank Is Part of the Compliance Process For an Indian investor, the Authorised Dealer Category-I bank plays an important role in overseas investment transactions. The bank does more than send foreign currency overseas. It may examine: the nature of the investment; the foreign entity; shareholder information; source of funds; supporting documents; regulatory forms; and prescribed reporting. The Overseas Investment Regulations also contemplate reporting and compliance being handled through the designated AD bank. For serious overseas investments, it is therefore sensible to involve the bank before the transaction is completed rather than after the Dubai company has already been fully incorporated. In practice, early discussion with the AD bank often identifies documentation gaps that are easier to solve before remittance. ODI Reporting Should Not Be Treated as an Afterthought A recurring misconception is: “The company is incorporated, so the transaction is finished.” From a UAE incorporation perspective, that may be true. From an Indian FEMA perspective, it may not be. Overseas investment can give rise to reporting obligations relating to the initial investment and subsequent changes. The reporting trail becomes important because the regulations can restrict further financial commitment or transfer of investment where earlier reporting delays remain unresolved. DEA This has a practical consequence. If the founder ignores ODI reporting today, the problem may reappear later when the investor wants to: inject more funds; provide a loan; give a guarantee; transfer shares; bring in another investor; or sell the company. The better approach is to establish the reporting framework from day one. A Dubai Company Can Require More Funding Than the Initial Share Capital Another reason to plan FEMA compliance early is that most operating businesses require additional funding. A founder may incorporate a Dubai company with modest initial capital and later discover that the business requires money for: office rent; employee salaries; inventory; marketing; vehicles; warehouse deposits; licence renewals; or working capital. The method used to provide that additional money matters. The founder should not assume that every transfer to the Dubai company can simply be recorded as a “loan” or informal shareholder advance. Depending on the investor and structure, FEMA requirements relating to equity, debt or other forms of financial commitment may apply. For Indian entities, the concept of financial commitment can also include permitted guarantees and debt support. The initial incorporation should therefore be designed with the expected future funding requirement in mind. Resident Individual and Indian Company Structures Are Different Consider two Dubai companies. The first is wholly owned by an Indian resident individual. The second is wholly owned by an Indian private limited company. Both may operate the same business in Dubai. Yet the Indian regulatory framework for the investment can differ substantially. For an individual, the LRS ceiling becomes particularly relevant. For an Indian entity, the overseas investment regime allows financial commitment subject to conditions linked to the entity’s net worth and other applicable requirements. The financial capacity, reporting and financing possibilities therefore differ. This is why ownership should be selected based on long-term commercial requirements rather than convenience. A founder who personally owns the Dubai company today may later discover that the Indian operating company should have been the shareholder. Reorganising ownership afterwards can create additional valuation, FEMA and tax considerations. The 400% Rule Is Relevant for Indian Entities, Not a Universal Limit Where an Indian entity makes overseas investment, its total financial commitment is generally subject to the limits prescribed under the ODI framework. The commonly referenced limit is 400% of the net worth of the Indian entity based on its last audited balance sheet, subject to the rules and applicable exceptions. This limit should not be confused with the USD 250,000 LRS ceiling applicable to resident individuals. They address different types of investors. A company proposing a substantial Dubai expansion should therefore model: equity investment; debt funding; guarantees; and other financial support before determining whether the proposed structure fits within the automatic-route framework. Looking only at the first share-capital payment can substantially understate the group’s real financial commitment. Guarantees Can Become FEMA-Relevant Suppose an Indian company forms a Dubai trading subsidiary. After one year, a UAE bank agrees to provide the subsidiary with working-capital finance but asks the Indian parent to issue a corporate guarantee. Commercially, this may appear to be merely a banking document. Under the overseas investment framework, however, certain guarantees can form part of the Indian entity’s financial commitment. This means the guarantee can have FEMA implications. Before signing it, the Indian parent should consider: whether the guarantee is permitted; whether it falls within the applicable financial commitment limits; what reporting is required; and whether the designated AD bank needs to process or record it. This is a classic example of why ODI compliance continues after incorporation. Shareholder Loans Also Require Care Founders often prefer loans because they appear more flexible than increasing share capital. Suppose an Indian shareholder incorporates a Dubai company and later transfers an additional USD 100,000 describing it as a shareholder loan. Whether that loan is permissible depends on the investor’s status and the applicable ODI rules. For Indian entities, debt investment generally needs to fit within the permitted financial commitment framework. For individuals, the rules can be more restrictive. A transfer should therefore not be labelled as “loan” merely because that description is convenient from the UAE accounting side. The Indian legal character of the transaction must also be correct. Step-Down Subsidiaries Should Be Planned Before Incorporation Many Dubai entities eventually become regional holding companies. For example: Indian Company ↓ Dubai Company ↓ Saudi Company or Indian Founder ↓ Dubai Holding Company ↓ European Subsidiary Such structures may have genuine commercial logic. But they should be examined under FEMA before the Dubai entity begins making onward investments. The overseas investment framework specifically deals with foreign entities having subsidiaries or step-down subsidiaries. Accordingly, incorporating a Dubai company should not be treated as a method of escaping India’s overseas investment rules for subsequent international expansion. If regional expansion is already anticipated, the complete structure should be designed at the outset. Pricing and Valuation Become Important When Buying an Existing Dubai Company Not every Indian investor incorporates a brand-new company. Some acquire an existing Dubai business. Imagine an Indian company purchasing 70% of a UAE trading company from its existing owner. In such cases, valuation becomes important. The Overseas Investment framework requires pricing to be consistent with the applicable arm’s-length principles. An acquisition price should therefore be commercially supportable. Paying an arbitrary figure simply because the parties agree on it can create problems with: the AD bank; regulatory reporting; tax authorities; auditors; and future investors. The more substantial the acquisition, the more important the valuation documentation becomes. Free Zone Versus Mainland Does Not Remove FEMA Indian founders often spend significant time deciding between: Dubai mainland; DIFC; DMCC; IFZA; Meydan; Dubai South; JAFZA; or another free zone. That choice can be commercially important. It can affect activity permissions, office requirements, customs, local trading, regulation and UAE tax treatment. But from the Indian investor’s perspective, one principle remains straightforward: A UAE free-zone licence does not exempt the investment from FEMA. The question under Indian law is not whether the company is mainland or free zone. The relevant issues are who the investor is, what investment is being made, what the foreign entity does, how the investment is funded and whether the reporting rules are satisfied. A cheap or fast incorporation package therefore does not simplify the Indian regulatory side. Bank Account Opening Can Expose Earlier Compliance Weaknesses One reason to maintain proper FEMA documentation is that banking scrutiny can reveal inconsistencies. During UAE corporate bank-account opening, banks commonly examine: ownership; source of funds; business rationale; expected transactions; parent-company details; shareholder background; and initial funding. If the Indian shareholder cannot clearly explain how the overseas investment was funded or why the amount differs from the corporate records, additional questions may arise. The same issues can reappear later during: audit; due diligence; investor onboarding; mergers; sale of shares; or repatriation of proceeds. Good FEMA compliance is therefore not merely about satisfying an Indian filing requirement. It also strengthens the credibility of the entire corporate structure. Annual Compliance May Continue After Incorporation Depending upon the nature of the investment and the foreign entity, ongoing overseas investment reporting can continue after the initial subscription. This may include annual performance reporting and other event-based reporting requirements. That means the Indian investor should retain proper access to the Dubai company’s: financial statements; shareholding records; business information; and changes in capital structure. This becomes particularly important where different advisers handle India and UAE compliance. The Dubai accountant may understand Corporate Tax and VAT but may not monitor Indian ODI reporting. Likewise, the Indian accountant may not automatically receive the UAE company’s updated records. Someone within the group should therefore take ownership of the cross-border compliance calendar. Late Reporting Can Affect Future Transactions One useful feature of the current framework is that certain reporting delays can be regularised through prescribed mechanisms, including the Late Submission Fee process where applicable. But this should not encourage delayed compliance. The Overseas Investment Regulations specifically restrict further financial commitment or transfer in certain circumstances until reporting delays are regularised. DEA Imagine discovering this when a new investor is ready to acquire 30% of the Dubai company. A reporting issue that appeared insignificant three years earlier can suddenly delay a commercially important transaction. The cost of compliance is generally much lower when obligations are completed on time. Repatriation and Exit Should Be Considered From Day One Incorporation is only the beginning of the investment cycle. Eventually, the shareholder may: receive dividends; sell shares; liquidate the company; restructure ownership; or receive other overseas proceeds. FEMA also governs aspects of how those amounts are dealt with. RBI’s LRS guidance states that overseas investment made by a resident individual remains subject to the Overseas Investment framework and applicable repatriation requirements. The exit strategy therefore deserves consideration at the beginning. A structure that is easy to create but difficult to sell or unwind is not necessarily a good structure. A Practical Example: Indian Consultant Opening a Dubai Company Consider an Indian resident management consultant planning to establish a Dubai consultancy company. The expected first-year investment is AED 150,000. The founder plans to own 100% of the shares personally. At the UAE level, the process may seem simple: choose the activity; reserve the trade name; obtain the licence; secure an establishment card; obtain residence visa; open the bank account. But the Indian compliance sequence should run alongside it. Before remitting funds, the founder should establish: FEMA residential status; whether the equity subscription qualifies as ODI; available LRS capacity; the appropriate AD-bank route; required reporting; how additional funding will be provided later; and whether the company may eventually establish subsidiaries elsewhere. Now assume the same founder simply incorporates the company first, pays expenses using different personal accounts and later asks the Indian bank to “regularise everything”. The underlying commercial business may be exactly the same. The compliance quality is not. This is why sequence matters. A Second Example: Indian Company Establishing a Dubai Subsidiary Assume an Indian manufacturing company wants to establish a Dubai subsidiary for GCC distribution. Its business plan requires: AED 2 million equity; AED 3 million working-capital support; a UAE bank facility backed by an Indian parent guarantee; and a Saudi subsidiary within two years. The relevant FEMA review should therefore consider more than the initial AED 2 million. Management should look at the complete expected financial commitment. The company should also determine: which AD bank will handle the ODI; whether the proposed funding fits within the automatic route; treatment of the parent guarantee; reporting requirements; step-down subsidiary implications; and future repatriation. This is a very different compliance exercise from a founder opening a small professional-services company under LRS. Calling both transactions “Dubai company formation” hides the regulatory differences. The Most Common FEMA Mistakes in Dubai Incorporations In practice, several mistakes repeat themselves. Incorporating before deciding the Indian ownership route The UAE company is already issued in the founder’s personal name before management decides that the Indian company should have been the shareholder. Treating LRS as blanket permission The promoter assumes that staying below USD 250,000 means every overseas investment structure is automatically permissible. Using the wrong funding source Incorporation expenses or capital are paid through accounts that do not match the shareholder or regulatory trail. Ignoring additional funding The founder plans only the initial capital and does not consider how the Dubai company will receive working capital later. Forgetting guarantees The parent agrees to provide a corporate guarantee without considering whether it affects financial commitment. Ignoring step-down subsidiaries The Dubai company later expands into another country without reviewing the Indian ODI consequences. Missing reporting deadlines The investor believes that the UAE incorporation documents alone are enough. Mixing FEMA and tax residency A person assumes that because they are a non-resident for income-tax purposes, the same result automatically applies under FEMA. These issues are generally easier to prevent than repair. A Better Incorporation Sequence For an Indian resident, a more disciplined approach is: First: determine the investor. Should the shareholder be the individual, an Indian company or another eligible entity? Second: establish FEMA residential status. Do not rely only on citizenship or income-tax residence. Third: review the business activity. Confirm that the proposed overseas activity fits within the ODI framework. Fourth: map the funding requirement. Consider not only initial share capital but future equity, debt and guarantees. Fifth: select and engage the AD bank. Understand documentation and reporting requirements before funds move. Sixth: incorporate the Dubai company. Ensure corporate documents are consistent with the intended ODI structure. Seventh: remit through the correct channel. Keep a clean banking and source-of-funds trail. Eighth: complete ODI reporting. Do not wait until another transaction forces the issue. Ninth: maintain annual and event-based compliance. Keep UAE records available to the Indian compliance team. This sequence may require slightly more planning at the beginning, but it usually produces a much cleaner international structure. A Practitioner Perspective Dubai incorporation is increasingly sold as a product: licence, visa, office and bank-account assistance packaged together. For Indian residents, that description is incomplete. The UAE company is not merely a company-formation product. It is also an overseas investment asset. That becomes particularly visible once the business succeeds. A founder who invests AED 100,000 today may eventually own a UAE company worth several million dirhams. At that stage, investors, banks, auditors and advisers are no longer interested only in the trade licence. They want to know: how the shares were acquired; how the company was funded; whether regulatory filings were completed; whether the ownership trail is clean; and whether the shareholder can legally sell or restructure the investment. Good FEMA compliance creates value because it preserves optionality. Poor compliance often becomes expensive only when the company becomes valuable. Conclusion For Indian residents, forming a company in Dubai should not be treated as a UAE-only incorporation exercise. The UAE authorities determine whether the company can be established and licensed. FEMA determines how a person resident in India can legally acquire, fund, support and eventually dispose of that overseas investment. The current Overseas Investment framework provides a relatively structured route for genuine international expansion, but it still requires attention to: ODI classification; LRS limits for resident individuals; permissible business activities; financial commitment; AD-bank processes; funding routes; guarantees; debt; valuation; step-down subsidiaries; reporting; and future exit. The most important practical lesson is therefore about timing. FEMA compliance should start before the Dubai licence is issued, not after the first remittance has already been made. For an Indian founder or company planning a serious UAE business, the strongest structure is one in which the UAE incorporation documents, banking trail and Indian overseas investment records all tell the same story. That alignment makes future funding, banking, expansion, investment and exit significantly easier.

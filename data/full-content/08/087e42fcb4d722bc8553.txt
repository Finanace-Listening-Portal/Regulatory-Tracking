(Supreme Court of India) The five-judge Constitution Bench of the Supreme Court, in High Court Bar Association, Allahabad Vs State of U.P. & Ors., reconsidered the directions issued in Asian Resurfacing of Road Agency Pvt. Ltd. Vs Central Bureau of Investigation (2018) 16 SCC 299. The earlier decision had directed that interim orders staying civil and criminal proceedings would automatically expire after six months unless expressly extended by a reasoned judicial order. The Constitution Bench examined whether such automatic vacation could be imposed through the Supreme Court’s powers under Article 142 of the Constitution and whether High Courts could be directed to decide all cases involving stays within a fixed period. The Court held that both blanket directions were legally unsustainable. The Court explained that interim relief protects litigants from consequences that may render their pending legal remedies ineffective. A High Court ordinarily grants such protection after considering the prima facie case, balance of convenience and possibility of irreparable injury. Although interim orders should not be granted mechanically, their continuation cannot depend solely on whether the High Court disposes of the main proceedings within six months. Delays may arise from judicial workload, procedural circumstances or other reasons beyond a litigant’s control. Automatically withdrawing protection without examining the individual circumstances could prejudice a party who had lawfully obtained a stay and had not contributed to the delay. The Constitution Bench distinguished between an ad-interim order granted without hearing the opposing party and an interim order passed after hearing the contesting parties. It recognised that High Courts retain authority to vacate or modify stays where the beneficiary deliberately delays proceedings, suppresses material facts, misrepresents circumstances or where subsequent developments justify reconsideration. However, such decisions require judicial application of mind and observance of natural justice. The mere passage of time does not invalidate an otherwise lawful interim order. The Court emphasised that a litigant should not suffer because the court itself could not take up the matter for hearing. Examining Article 142, the Supreme Court held that its power to do complete justice cannot be exercised to nullify the substantive rights of large numbers of litigants who were not parties to the proceedings before it. The Court may issue procedural directions to improve judicial administration, but it cannot use Article 142 to override natural justice or extinguish valid judicial protection through a general rule. It also recognised that High Courts are independent constitutional courts exercising jurisdiction under Articles 226 and 227. The Supreme Court’s constitutional position does not justify imposing universal time limits that automatically terminate orders issued by every High Court. The Bench referred to DCIT Vs Pepsi Foods Ltd., in which the Supreme Court had examined the automatic vacation of stay under Section 254(2A) of the Income Tax Act. That decision held that depriving an assessee of interim protection merely because an appeal remained pending for 365 days, even when the delay was not attributable to the assessee, was arbitrary and discriminatory. The Constitution Bench applied the underlying principles of fairness and judicial accountability while reconsidering the directions in Asian Resurfacing. It also examined Article 226(3), the circumstances in which ex parte interim relief may be reconsidered, and the need for courts to decide applications for vacation of stay without unnecessary delay. The Supreme Court further held that constitutional courts should ordinarily avoid prescribing rigid deadlines for disposal of proceedings pending before other courts. Directions requiring time-bound disposal may be justified in exceptional circumstances, but prioritisation should generally remain with the court handling the matter. High Courts were advised to exercise caution while granting stays, particularly in serious criminal cases, and to give appropriate priority to applications seeking vacation of interim protection. Justice Pankaj Mithal, in a separate concurring judgment, emphasised that a reasoned stay order ordinarily continues until the main proceeding is decided or the order is modified or vacated through an appropriate judicial process. Accordingly, the Supreme Court overruled the directions in paragraphs 36 and 37 of Asian Resurfacing concerning automatic vacation of stays and mandatory time-bound disposal. It clarified that stays cannot expire automatically merely because six months have elapsed. However, the Court expressly preserved the validity of proceedings in cases where trials had already concluded following automatic vacation of stay under the earlier directions. The judgment, delivered on 29 February 2024, establishes that the continuation or vacation of judicial protection must be governed by judicial consideration, fairness and the circumstances of each case rather than a blanket expiry rule.

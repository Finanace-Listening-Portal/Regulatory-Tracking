Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); Vishal Singh Vs Jay Jay Bricks (GSTAT Lucknow) Intent to Evade Tax Not Essential Under Section 130(1)(ii): GSTAT Remands Unaccounted Stock Dispute Introduction The Goods and Services Tax Appellate Tribunal (GSTAT), Lucknow Bench, examined whether confiscation proceedings concerning allegedly unaccounted goods could be rejected merely because the department had not established intent to evade tax. The Tribunal held that the distinct clauses of Section 130(1) require separate consideration. In particular, Section 130(1)(ii) does not expressly incorporate the requirement of intent found in clauses (i) and (iv). However, the Tribunal did not finally confirm the disputed penalty and fine. It set aside the first appellate order and remanded the matter for fresh adjudication after examination of the records and relevant statutory requirements. Facts of the Case The respondent, M/s Jay Jay Bricks, was a registered partnership firm engaged in manufacturing and selling clay bricks in Hardoi, Uttar Pradesh. The Special Investigation Branch conducted an inspection at its manufacturing premises on 16 April 2025 under Section 67. During verification, officers recorded 5,66,500 bricks, valued at ₹22,25,798. The stock comprised first-class, second-class, yellow and kharanja bricks. The manager present at the premises could not produce the stock register, books of accounts or production records. He explained that the accountant was on leave and the managing partners were away attending a family function. The department treated the stock as unaccounted, issued a seizure order and subsequently initiated proceedings under Section 130, alleging failure to maintain statutory accounts under Section 35 read with Rule 56. Original Demand and First Appellate Relief The adjudicating authority passed an ex parte order dated 18 June 2025, imposing a penalty of approximately ₹1,33,548 and a fine in lieu of confiscation of ₹1,33,547.88. The aggregate liability was recorded as ₹2,67,096. The taxpayer challenged this order before the First Appellate Authority, which deleted the entire demand on 4 September 2025. The appellate authority considered the absence of proven intent to evade tax decisive against confiscation. It further held that the stock discrepancy should have been examined through Section 35(6) and the determination mechanisms under Sections 73 or 74. The Revenue challenged that decision before GSTAT. Submissions of the Revenue The Revenue argued that the absence of contemporaneous books and stock records justified proceedings under Section 130(1)(ii). It contended that this clause addresses failure to account for taxable goods and does not expressly require proof of intention to evade tax. The department also questioned the reliability of records allegedly prepared or produced subsequently. It maintained that the First Appellate Authority had granted relief without properly verifying the taxpayer’s accounts or reconciling them with the stock found during inspection. Submissions of the Taxpayer The taxpayer submitted that temporary non-production of records did not establish clandestine activity. According to the firm, the accountant’s absence explained why the books were unavailable during inspection. It argued that the bricks represented the normal accumulated output of an ongoing manufacturing cycle. A stock variation, without evidence of actual unreported supplies, could not automatically justify confiscation. The taxpayer also disputed the department’s allegation that operations were conducted from an undeclared location. It relied on judicial precedents concerning excess stock and challenged the proportionality of the combined penalty and fine. Tribunal’s Findings on Section 130 The Tribunal emphasised that Section 130(1) contains distinct statutory grounds, whose ingredients cannot be merged into a uniform test. It observed that clauses (i) and (iv) expressly refer to intent to evade payment of tax, whereas clause (ii) concerns failure to account for goods on which tax is payable. Consequently, the First Appellate Authority erred in treating proof of intent as an indispensable requirement across all categories. The Tribunal further found that the appellate order acknowledged serious record-keeping deficiencies but granted complete relief without identifying verified documentary material establishing the legitimacy of the stock. There was no satisfactory examination of contemporaneous ledgers, production logs, invoices or supporting records. The Tribunal considered reliance on uncorroborated explanations insufficient to resolve these factual issues. Judicial Precedents Distinguished The taxpayer relied on Dinesh Kumar Pradeep Kumar, Vijay Trading Company and PP Polyplast. The Tribunal distinguished these decisions on facts. It held that precedents could not be applied mechanically where officers had recorded the absence of statutory books and the appellate authority had not independently verified the accounts allegedly supporting the stock. Decision and Significance The Tribunal set aside the order dated 4 September 2025 and remanded the matter to the First Appellate Authority for fresh, comprehensive adjudication on merits. The ruling highlights the need to examine the specific clause invoked and verify the underlying records. The disputed liability remains subject to fresh adjudication; the remand does not constitute final confirmation of the penalty or confiscation fine. Submissions of the Respondent (Taxpayer); 5.1. A review of the prior record reveals that neither the original SIB panchnama dated April 16, 2025, the Show Cause Notice dated May 2, 2025, nor the final Section 130 adjudication order contains any such characterization; instead, they all reference the site simply as the taxpayer’s business premises. It is a settled principle of revenue law that the tax department cannot travel beyond the four corners of its original Show Cause Notice at the appellate stage to mount an entirely new factual case that the taxpayer was never given an opportunity to meet or rebut during the initial adjudication. 5.3. The taxpayer points out a severe procedural infirmity by demonstrating that the department itself did not treat its own inquiry as concluded when it rushed to pass the Section 130 confiscation order on June 18, 2025. According to official records and GST portal logs, the department issued a secondary reminder summons under Section 70 on July 1, 2025, and went on to record a formal statement from the owner/partner as late as July 15, 2025 nearly a month after the final demand order had supposedly wrapped up the case. The taxpayer contends that this subsequent conduct proves the department finalized its harsh confiscation proceedings prematurely, utilizing incomplete material and ‘bypassing the mandatory tax determination mechanisms before executing the seizure. 5.4. The taxpayer submits that because the entire dispute stems strictly from an alleged variation of physical stock found inside ‘heir own, factory premises without any proof or allegation of an actual, completed ‘taxable supply the department was statutorily mandated to follow Section 35(6). Section 35(6) explicitly mandates that when excess or unaccounted goods are discovered, the proper officer must’ determine the tax payable by applying the mechanisms of Section 73 or Section 74 mutatis mutandis. The taxpayer argues that by skipping this mandatory pathway and proceeding directly to the severe confiscation provisions of Section 130, the department completely acted outside its statutory authority. 5.5. The taxpayer has placed reliance upon the judgments passed by the Hon’ble Allahabad High Court in the matters of S/S Dinesh Kumar Pradeep Kumar v. Additional Commissioner, M/s Vijay Trading Company and M/s PP Polyplast Pvt. Ltd. The contention raised by the taxpayer regarding the judgments cited in support of the alleged settled legal position is not acceptable in the manner sought to be projected. The taxpayer has incorrectly construed the AS Page 7 of 13 dismissal of Special Leave Petitions by the Hon’ble Supreme Court as an affirmation of the entire reasoning and ratio of the judgments passed by the Hon’ble Allahabad High Court. 5.5.1, It is a settled principle of law, as laid down in Kunhayammed & Ors. v. State of Kerala & Anr., (2000) 6 SCC 359, that dismissal of an SLP by a non-speaking order, without grant of leave, does not result in merger of the impugned judgment with the order of the Supreme Court nor does it, by itself, constitute a declaration of law under Article 141 of the Constitution. Further, the judgments relied upon by the taxpayer cannot be applied mechanically without examining the factual matrix, nature of allegations and evidence available in the present proceedings. The applicability of Section 130 of the GST Act has to be determined with reference to the specific circumstances and statutory ingredients prescribed under the relevant clauses thereof. Therefore, the taxplyeds assertion that. the mere discovery of excess stock invariably excludes the applicability of Section 130 and that ‘the matter is conclusively governed by Sections 73/74 in every such case is an overbroad interpretation and cannot be accepted without examining the facts and evidence of the present case. 5.6 The taxpayer argues that ‘for Section 130(1)(ii) or (iv) to be legally triggered, the department bears the heavy burden. of proving-a specific ‘statutory contravention coupled with an explicit, deliberate “intent to evade payment of tax”. In the present case, a mere volumetric difference between physical stock and book stock was treated as automatic proof of evasion. The taxpayer points out that neither the initial Show Cause Notice nor the original adjudication order contains a single piece of corroborative evidence or a concrete finding showing a deceptive intent; instead, the entire state action relies on a bare presumption triggered solely by the temporary non-production of records on the day of the survey. 5.7. The taxpayer invokes established tribunal precedents confirming that a minor volume of unrecorded finished stock lying within a factory floor, absent any evidence of intended clandestine removal, cannot legally justify an order of confiscation. 5.8. The taxpayer argues in the alternative that even if the department’s case were accepted as true, the original demand order was structurally illegal as it exceeded the explicit statutory caps built into the law. The second’proviso, of Section, 130(2) explicitly mandates that the aggregate total of the fine and penalty leviable cannot exceed a maximum threshold. By imposing a cumulative demand that effectively penalized the dealer at well over 200% of the tax leviable on the underlying brick stock; the original proper officer acted arbitrarily and excessively, an independent legal infirmity that fully justifies the relief and total deletion granted by the First Appellate Authority. 6. QUESTION OF LAW; Whether the Revenue can directly initiate severe confiscation and fine proceedings under Section 130 solely on the discovery of excess or unrecorded stock during a survey at a registered business premises, or whether the statutory mandate under Section 35(6) strictly requires the department to determine tax liability through a regular assessment route under Section 73 or Section 74. Whether the mere physical presence of unrecorded inventory inside a working production unit automatically establishes a definitive “intent to evade payment of tax” under Section 130(1)(ii)/(iv), 7 Discussion and Findings; 7.1. We have meticulously considered the submissions of both the Revenue and the Respondent, perused the lower records, and examined the statutory framework of Section 130(1) along with the findings recorded in the Order-in-Appeal (OIA). 7.2. To properly analyze the statutory boundary of confiscation proceedings, it is vital to examine the literal text of Section 130(1) of the CGST/UPGST Act, 2017: “Notwithstanding anything contained in this Act, if any person — (ii) does not account for any goods on which he is liable to pay tax under this Act; or. … (iv) contravenes any of the provisions-of this-Act OF the rilles made thereunder with intent to evade payment of tax; then, all such goods or conveyances shall be liable to confiscation and the person shall be liable to penalty under section 122.” 7.3. A plain and literal interpretation the statute reveals that the Legislature has carefully drafted distinct categories of Offenses under separate clases of Section 130(1). Section 130(1)(ii) of the Act penalizes a registered taxable persort who “does not account for any goods on which he is liable to pay tax.” Crucially, unlike clauses (i) and (iv), clause (ii) does not contain the qualifying phrase “with intent to evade payment of tax.” Therefore, the mere objective failure to keep, maintain, or update records of taxable items found within a business unit satisfies the primary ingredient of this specific subsection. 7.4. We find that the First Appellate Authority, as shown in the recorded findings of the impugned OIA, completely conflated these distinct clauses. The lower appellate authority applied a sweeping generalization, ruling that an absolute “intent to evade tax” must be proved uniformly across all categories us tain a detention or confiscation order. In doing so, the authority overlooked the fact that the complete absence of any stock books, logs, or primary registers at the spot during an active search operation can independently invite the operation of Section 130(1)(ii), where the objective factor of non-accounting takes precedence over established tax intent. 7.5. Furthermore, a perusal of the impugned OIA reveals a critical internal contradiction within the First Appellate Authority’s own ruling. On one hand, the lower appellate authority explicitly notes the taxpayer’s severe statutory lapses specifically recording that the respondent failed to maintain day-to-day transaction records at the business premises, failed to furnish primary books of account at the time of verification, and entirely failed to produce the mandatory stock register when demanded: by the search officers. On the other hand, despite capturing these explicit baseline omissions, the First Appellate Authority proceeded to quash the demand without pointing single piece of document evidence on record that could legally discharge the taxpayer’s burden or demonstrate that there was no active intent to operate outside the tax net. 7.6. Crucially, there is an absolute vacuum in the lower findings regarding any actual verification of physical ledgers. The record. indicates that the respondent failed to submit verified, contemporaneous account books or authenticated production logs not just during the initial SIB search operations, but seemingly failed to substantiate them before the First Appellate Authority as well. The lower appellate order is completely silent on any systematic analysis or audit of physical files that could establish the legitimacy of the stock. In the absence of such baseline records, the First Appellate Authority’s conclusion regarding the lack of an “intent to evade tax” rests entirely on uncorroborated oral pleadings and bare assertions, which cannot override the uncontradicted physical realities documented on the field by the proper officers. 7.7. We find that the reliance placed by the respondent upon the judgments in Dinesh Kumar Pradeep Kumar, Vijay Trading Company and PP Poly plast is misplaced and distinguishable on facts, The said eases cannot be applied mechanically to the present matter, wherein the proper officers specifically recorded the complete absence of statutory books and registers at the factory premises, as required under Section 35 of the CGST Act, 201 7. Unlike those cases, the respondent failed to produce any verifiable books of accounts, invoices or supporting records during the search proceedings. Further, the First Appellate Authority failed to examine or physically verify the alleged books of accounts before granting relief merely on the basis of the respondent’s oral submissions. Thus, the factual circumstances of the present case are materially different, and the aforesaid judgments, cannot be relied upon to disregard the evidence recorded during search. Accordingly; the. Fits apellate Authority’s mechanical application of these precedents without appreciating the material\factual distinctions and statutory requirements, is not legally sustainable. 7.8. We are of the opinion that adjudication requires a balanced assessment where the y . individual elements of each charged sub-section are measured against verified evidence. Since the First Appellate Authority failed to reconcile its own findings regarding the non-production of records with the distinct statutory mechanisms of. Section 130(1)(ii) of the Act 2017, the interest of justice demands that the matter be properly re-examined. 7.9. Consequently, the impugned Order-in-Appeal dated 04.09.2025 cannot be sustained in its current form and is hereby set aside. The matter is liable to be remanded back to the First Appellate Authority for a fresh, comprehensive, and case-specific adjudication on merits. ORDER The impugned Order-in-Appeal dated 04.09.2025 is set aside. ‘The matter is remanded back to the First Appellate Authority (Additional Commissioner (Appeals)) for a fresh, adjudication on merits in accordance with the observations contained hereinabove.

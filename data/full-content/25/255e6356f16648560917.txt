Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); In re Vegan Wood Private Limited (GST AAR Gujarat) Summary: The Gujarat Authority for Advance Ruling held that the Rice Husk Board manufactured by Vegan Wood Private Limited is classifiable under Chapter 44, heading 4411, as a fibreboard. The product attracts GST at 12% (6% CGST plus 6% SGST) for the period before 22 September 2025 under entry 92(c) of Schedule II to Notification No. 01/2017-Central Tax (Rate), dated 28 June 2017. From 22 September 2025, it attracts 5% GST (2.5% CGST plus 2.5% SGST) under entry 292(c) of Schedule I to Notification No. 09/2025-Central Tax (Rate), dated 17 September 2025. The ruling expressly depends on the authenticity of the applicant’s submissions, test reports and other supporting particulars. The application, filed in March 2024, originally sought classification of boards made from rice husk powder, calcium carbonate, recycled material, processing aids and PVC resin. Gujarat AAR had treated it as non-maintainable on 21 March 2025 because of documentary and laboratory-report deficiencies. On appeal, Gujarat AAAR set aside that order and remanded the matter on 25 February 2026 for fresh consideration after an adequate hearing. The applicant subsequently furnished purchase and sales invoices, brochures, manufacturing details and fresh test reports. It argued that its product was a fibre-reinforced polymer composite manufactured through extrusion, rather than a particle board made by hot pressing, and relied on the Padmavathy Panel Boards decision and the Papaka Herbs & Spices and Tra Plast Industries advance rulings. The two laboratory reports recorded rice husk content of 32.05% and 32.25%, PVC of 27.25% and 27.30%, calcium carbonate of 18.05% and 17.90%, and remaining additives of 22.65% and 22.55%, respectively. Because the reports used the expression “Rice Husk Ash @190°C”, the Authority sought clarification. The laboratory explained in its letter dated 30 June 2026 that this referred to the rice husk-derived lignocellulosic fraction identified during thermal analysis, rather than actual ash. The temperature notation identified an analytical stage and did not describe the material’s use or processing temperature. The Authority examined headings 4410 and 4411 and their HSN explanatory notes. It accepted that fibres were extracted from rice husk and bonded with PVC resin and other substances. It distinguished this from directly combining rice husk particles with a binder. It also relied on the PVC content exceeding the 15% binder proportion generally mentioned in the notes to heading 4410. Comparing the manufacturing process and product characteristics with the fibreboard notes, it classified the product under heading 4411. Although the applicant sought tariff item 44119319, the operative ruling determines classification at heading 4411. The Authority stated that Indian Standard specifications were not mandatory for deciding the primary customs classification, which depended on composition, manufacturing process, tariff headings, the General Rules of Interpretation and relevant notes. The Authority declined to entertain a changed question on remand and treated the applicant’s reference to “Natural Fibre Composite Board” as referring to the same rice husk board covered by the original application. It observed that the wider expression could also cover boards made from bamboo, bagasse or other natural fibres. It further referred to Section 103 of the CGST Act, 2017 to explain that other applicants’ advance rulings bind only those applicants and their concerned or jurisdictional officers. Having independently examined the submitted evidence, it granted heading 4411 classification and the applicable period-wise rates, subject to the express authenticity condition. It is the applicant’s contention that their product comprising of rice husk fibres in PVC polymer matrix with calcium carbonate and other additives is a “Natural Fibre Composite Board” and is rightly classifiable under CTH 44119319. The applicant has stated that since their product “Natural Fibre Composite Board” is primarily comprising of rice husk fibres in PVC polymer matrix with calcium carbonate and other additives, the said product would be ideally covered under Chapter 44 (which covers Wood and articles of wood; wood charcoal). A rice husk board consists primarily of natural lignocellulosic biomass and silica from the husk, combined with synthetic or bio-based adhesives i.e. rice husk is a ligneous material. On going through Chapter 44, we find that there are two headings covering boards made from ligneous material viz. Tariff Heading 4410 covering ‘particle boards, oriented strand boards and the like” and heading 4411 covering ‘Fibre boards’. Tariff Heading 4410 covers boards made from wood or ligneous material and reads as ‘particle board, oriented strand board (OSB) and similar board (for example, waferboard) of wood or other ligneous materials, whether or not agglomerated with resins or other organic binding substances’. Tariff Heading 4411 reads as ‘fibre board of wood or other ligneous materials, whether or not bonded with resins or other organic substances’. We, therefore, find it prudent to go through the Tariff Headings 4410 & 4411 as appearing in the Customs Tariff Act, 1975 as also the HSN notes pertaining to these tariff headings in order to explore under which heading the applicant’s product would rightly fall. The same are reproduced hereunder: TARIFF HEADING 4410 AS APPEARING IN THE FIRST SCHEDULE TO THE CUSTOMS TARIFF ACT, 1975 (51 OF 1975): 4410 PARTICLE BOARD, ORIENTED STRAND BOARD (OSB) AND SIMILAR BOARD (FOR EXAMPLE. WAFERBOARD) OF WOOD OR OTHER LIGNEOUS MATERIALS, WHETHER OR NOT AGGLOMERATED WITH RESINS OR OTHER ORGANIC BINDING SUBSTANCES — – Of wood: 4410 11 — Particle board: 4410 11 10-~ Plain particle boards 4410 11 20— Insulation board and hardboard 4410 11 30– Veneered particle board, not having decorative veneers on any face 4410 11 90 — Others 4410 12 — Oriented strand board (OSB). 4410 12 10— Unworked or not further worked than sanded 4410 12 90 — Other 4410 19 00— Other 4410 90 – Other: 441090 10— Plain particle board 4410 90 20 — Insulation board and hard board 4410 90 30 — Veneered particle board, not having decorative veneers on any face 4410 90 40 — Of Coir 4410 90 50 — Of jute fibre 4410 90 90 — Other HSN NOTES TO HEADING 4410: 44.10 -Particle board, oriented strand board (OSB) and similar board (for example, waferboard) of wood or other ligneous materials, whether or not agglomerated with resins or other organic binding substances. -Of wood: 4410.11 — Particle board 4410.12 — Oriented strand board (OSB) 4410.19 — Other 4410.90 – Other Particle board is a flat product manufactured in various lengths, widths and thicknesses by pressing or extrusion. It is usually made from wood chips or particles obtained by the mechanical reduction of roundwood or wood residues. It may also be produced from other ligneous materials such as fragments obtained from bagasse, bamboo, cereal straw or from flax or hemp shives. Particle board is normally agglomerated by means of an added organic binder, usually a thermosetting resin, which generally does not exceed 15 % of the weight of the board. The chips, particles or other fragments constituting the particle boards of this heading are usually recognisable at the edges of the board with the naked eye. However, in some cases, microscopic examination may be required to distinguish the particles and fragments from the ligno-cellulosic fibres characterising the fibreboard of heading 44.11. This heading also covers (1) Oriented strand board, which is made from layers of thin strands of wood which are at least twice as long as they are wide. These strands are mixed with binders (usually waterproof) such as isocyanate or phenolic resins, interleaved together and laid down in layers fanning a thick mat in which the strands are generally oriented lengthwise in the surface layers and generally cross oriented or laid down randomly in the inner layers in order to give the board improved elastomechanical properties. The mat is subjected to heat and pressure producing a solid, uniform, rigid structural board. (2) Waferboard, which is made from thin wafers of wood which are less than twice as long as they are wide. These wafers are mixed with binders (usually waterproof) such as isocyanate or phenolic resins, interleaved together and laid down randomly, thus forming a thick mat. The mat is subjected to heat and pressure producing a solid, uniform, structural board having high strength and water resistance. The particle boards of this heading are usually sanded. Moreover, they may be impregnated with one or more substances not essential for the agglomeration of their constituent materials but which confer on the board an additional property, e.g. impermeability to water, resistance to rot, insect attack, fire or the spread of flame, chemical agencies or electricity, greater density. In the last instance, the impregnating substances attain an important proportion. Extruded particle board may have holes running internally from end to end. Also classified in this heading are laminated panels consisting of: (1) particle board covered on one or both faces with fibreboard; (2) several particle boards whether or not covered on one or both faces with fibreboard; (3) several particle boards and several fibreboards assembled in any order. The products of this heading remain classified herein whether or not they have been worked to form the shapes provided for in respect of the goods of heading 44.09, curved, corrugated, perforated, cut or formed to shapes other than square or rectangular and whether or not they have been worked at the surface, the edge or the end, or coated or covered (e.g., with textile fabric, plastics, paint, paper or metal) or submitted to any other operation, provided these operations do not thereby give such products the essential character of articles of other headings. The heading does not cover: (a) Plates or strips of plastics containing wood flour as a filler (Chapter 39). (b) Veneered particle board and similar board (for example, oriented strand board and waferboard), whether or not having holes running internally from end to end (heading 44.12). (c) Cellular wood panels of which both faces are particle board (heading 44.18). (d) Boards of ligneous materials agglomerated with cement, plaster or with other mineral binding substances (heading 68.08). Also excluded from this heading are goods having the character of articles or parts of articles more specifically covered elsewhere, whether obtained directly by pressing, extrusion or moulding or by other processes. TARIFF HEADING 4411 AS APPEARING IN THE FIRST SCHEDULE TO THE CUSTOMS TARIFF ACT, 1975 (51 OF 1975): 4411: FIBRE BOARD OF WOOD OR OTHER LIGNEOUS MATERIALS, WHETHER OR NOT BONDED WITH RESINS OR OTHER ORGANIC SUBSTANCES Medium density fibre board. 44111200 — Of a thickness not exceeding 5mm. 44111300 — Of a thickness exceeding 5mm but not exceeding 9 mm. 44111400 — Of a thickness exceeding 9 mm – Other 441192 — Of a density exceeding 0.8 g/cm3 —Not mechanically worked or surface covered. 44119211 — Hardboard 44119219 — Other — Other 44119221—- Hardboard 44119229 —Other 441193 — Of a density exceeding 0.5g/cm3 but not exceeding 0.8 g/cm3. —Not mechanically worked or surface worked. 44119311 — Insulation board 44119319 — Other — Other 44119321 — Insulation board 44119329 — Other 441194 — Of a density exceeding 0.5 g/cm3: — Not mechanically worked or surface covered. 44119411 — Insulation board 44119419 —Other — Other 44119421 — Insulation board 44119422 — Of Coir 44119423 — Of Jute Fabric 44119429 — Other HSN NOTES TO HEADING 4411: 44.11 -Fibreboard of wood or other ligneous materials, whether or not bonded with resins or other organic substances. – Medium density fibreboard (MDF) 4411.12 — Of a thickness not exceeding 5 mm 4411.13 – – Of a thickness exceeding 5 mm but not exceeding 9 mm 4411.14 – – Of a thickness exceeding 9 mm – Other: 4411.92 — Of a density exceeding 0.8 g/cm3 4411.93 – – Of a density exceeding 0.5 g/cm3 but not exceeding 0.8 g/cm3 4411.94 – – Of a density not exceeding 0.5 g/cm3 Fibreboard is most often manufactured from wood chips which have been mechanically defibred ( defibrated) or steam exploded or from other defibred ligno-cellulosic material (obtained e.g., from bagasse or bamboo). The fibres making up the board are recognisable under microscopic examination. They are bonded together in the board by felting and by their own adhesive properties, generally deriving from their lignin content. Additional resins or other organic bonding substances may be used to agglomerate the fibres. Impregnating or other agents may also be added during or after manufacture of the board to give an extra property, e.g., impermeability to water or resistance to rot, insect attack, fire or the spread of flame. Fibreboard may consist of a single sheet or of several sheets bonded together. The categories of fibreboard of this heading can be distinguished according to their production process and they include: (A) Fibreboard obtained by the “dry production process” This group includes, in particular, medium density fibreboard (MDF), which is manufactured in a process in which additional thermosetting resins are added to the dried wood fibres in order to assist the bonding process in the press. The density generally ranges from 0.45 g/cm3 to 1 g/cm3. In the unworked state it has two smooth surfaces. It can be used in many different applications such as furniture, interior decoration and in building. Medium density fibreboard of a density exceeding 0.8 g/cm3 is sometimes also referred to by the trade as “high density fibreboard (HDF)”. (B) Fibreboard obtained by the “wet production process” This group includes the following types of fibreboard: (1) Hardboard, which is manufactured in a wet production process in which the wood fibres in suspension in water are compressed in the form of a mat under high temperature and high pressure on a metallic mesh. In the unworked state this type of fibreboard has one smooth and one rough surface with a mesh pattern. However, it can sometimes also have two smooth surfaces obtained by special surface treatment or a special production process. It generally has a density exceeding 0.8 g/cm3. Hardboard is mainly used for furniture, in the automotive industries, for doorskins and for packaging, especially fruit and vegetable packaging. (2) Mediumboard, which is manufactured in a way similar to the one for hardboard but at a lower pressure. It generally has a density exceeding 0.35 g/cm3 but not exceeding 0.8 g/cm3. The main application is in furniture production and for interior or exterior walls. (3) Softboard. This fibreboard is not compressed as the other types of fibreboard obtained by the wet production process. It generally has a density of 0.35 g/cm3 or less. These boards are used mainly for henna or sound insulation in building. Special types of insulating board are used as sheathing or sarking materials. The products of this heading remain classified herein whether or not they have been worked to form the shapes provided for in respect of the goods of heading 44.09, curved, corrugated, perforated, cut or fanned to shapes other than square or rectangular and whether or not they have been worked at the surface, the edge or the end, or coated or covered (e.g., with textile fabric, plastics, paint, paper or metal) or submitted to any other operation, provided these operations do not thereby give such products the essential character of articles of other headings. The heading does not cover: (a) Particle board whether or not laminated with one or several, fibreboards (heading 44.10). (b) Laminated wood with a core consisting of fibreboard (heading 44.12). (c) Cellular wood panels of which both faces are fibreboard (heading 44.18). (d) Paperboard, such as multiplex paperboard, “presspan” and strawboard, which can generally be distinguished from fibreboard by their layer structure made apparent on cleaving (Chapter 48) (e) Fibreboard panels clearly identifiable as parts of furniture (generally Chapter 94). 25. As per the applicant, the Natural Fiber Composite Board is made of natural fibers of rice husk and offers a range of advantages, including exceptional durability, is termite resistant, fungal resistant, weather resistant, water resistant and fire resistant. The process of the manufacture of Natural Fibre Composite Boards is known as extrusion. Natural fibre composite (NFC) boards are sustainable, eco-friendly materials made from plant fibers (like wood, coir, bamboo, or hemp) combined with polymer resins. Also, on going through the images provided by the applicant in the brochures submitted by them, we find that they mimic the look of genuine wood. At this stage, we would find it prudent to compare the manufacturing process given by the applicant to the one mentioned in HSN notes to Heading 4410 and Heading 4411. The manufacturing process of the applicant, in brief is that the Natural fibres obtained from Rice Husk are mixed with PVC polymer resins and additives like stabilizers, impact modifiers, UV resistant additives, lubricants, processing aids are mixed in a High Speed mixer for uniform and homogeneous blending and to remove the unwanted vapours in the powder. This process continues upto about 160-180o C and then is dropped in a cooling mixture where it is cooled and taken for the process of extrusion, sizing, traction, cutting and finishing. Further, “rice husk” is a ligno-cellulosic (ligneous) material constituted of 15-30% of lignin, 35-50% cellulose, 15-25% of hemicellulose and 15-20% silica. Rice husk is classified as a natural fiber because it is a plant-based, renewable agricultural by- product composed of natural polymers (lignocellulose) and a highly protective layer of silica. While cellulose and lignin gives them the fibrous strength and rigidity, the silica content therein creates a durable, rot-resistant, and flame-retardant material. On a plain comparison of the manufacturing process given by the applicant and the details of rice husk (as obtained online) with that of the manufacturing process as well as the details of the product viz. impermeability to water, resistance to rot, insect attack fire or the spread of flame etc. mentioned in the HSN notes of heading 4411, we find the specifications of the product of the applicant matches with that of the details given in HSN notes to Tariff Heading 4411. 25.3 On going through the details given in HSN Notes to Tariff Heading 4410, we find that particle boards falling under heading 4410 are made from wood chips or particles of wood or ligneous material whereas oriented strand boards and waferboards, also falling under heading 4410 are made from strands of wood and thin wafers of wood respectively. Particle boards can also be made from ligneous materials like rice husk, however, the raw material used in the case of particle board is not rice husk fibre but fine particles of rice husk. Particle boards made from particles of rice husk are obtained by mixing the said material with a binding resin and compressing the mixture under high heat and pressure. However, the HSN note specifically states that “Particle board is normally agglomerated by means of an added organic binder, usually a thermosetting resin, which generally does not exceed 15 % of the weight of the board.” This implies that if the applicant’s product referred to by them as “Natural Fibre Composite Board” constitutes more than 15% of organic binder, it will automatically move out of the ambit of Heading 4410. However, as per the Test Reports submitted by the applicant, PVC binder constitutes about 27.25%/27.30% of the Board which is much higher than the 15% mentioned in the HSN notes of Tariff Heading. Further, in the present case, natural fibre is first extracted from rice husk and then mixed with lime powder (calcium carbonate), processing additives such as lubricants, foaming agents, foam regulators, heat stabilizers, etc. where PVC resin is used as a binding agent. If the rice husk was directly added with binders etc. without extracting the fibres, the product would be a particle board, which is not the case here. We, therefore, find that the applicant’s product would move out of the ambit of Tariff Heading 4410 for the reasons discussed above. 26. Further, the heading 4411 itself reads as “Fibre board of wood or other ligneous materials whether or not bonded with resins or other organic substances”. As discussed earlier, the product in question is manufactured by the bonding of fibres obtained from rice husk (which is a ligneous material) with PVC resins and other organic substances. Further, in this case, as per the manufacturing process given by the applicant, fibres are first extracted from the rice husk and then mixed with lime powder (calcium carbonate), processing additives such as lubricants, foaming agents, foam regulators, heat stabilizers, etc. where PVC resin is used as a binding agent. Hence, the product is a Fibreboard and is aptly classifiable under Heading 4411. In view of the discussions in the paras supra, and based exclusively on the submissions, the Test Reports as well as the other details and supporting documents submitted by the applicant, we find and hold that the product of the applicant is a fibre board and is nothing but “Natural Fibre Composite Board” OR “Rice Husk Board” classifiable under Heading 4411 of the First Schedule to the Customs Tariff Act, 1975 (51 of 1975). 27. The applicant is also found to have discussed in details about the (Indian Standard) IS specifications applicable for particle boards (which, they state, is not their product) as well as the IS specifications applicable for their product “Natural Fibre Composite Board”. Here, we would like to clarify that the IS (Indian Standard) specifications are not mandatory for finalizing the primary customs classification of goods since classification of goods is governed by the General Rules of Interpretation (GRIs), Section Notes, and the Harmonized System of Nomenclature (HSN). The legal test relies strictly on the item’s composition, material, and “essential character“. For example, Natural Fibre Composite Boards (NFC) are typically examined by referring to the Chapter, Chapter notes, the relevant Tariff Heading, relevant HSN notes, looking at their exact constituent percentages, manufacturing process etc. While IS specifications are not the basis for determining the tariff heading, they can be submitted as expert guidance to prove a product’s trade parlance or physical properties if the classification is ambiguous, which is not the case here. Therefore, in view of the discussions in paras supra, we do not find the need to look into this aspect while determining the classification of the product in question. 28.

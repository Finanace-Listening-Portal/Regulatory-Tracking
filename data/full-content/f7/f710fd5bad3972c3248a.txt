Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); HDFC Bank Limited Vs Amit Chandrashekhar Poddar (NCLT Mumbai) Summary: NCLT Mumbai, Bench Court III, condoned HDFC Bank Limited’s delay in filing a ₹11,88,71,220.69 claim in the corporate insolvency resolution process (CIRP) of Khadkeshwar Hatcheries Limited. HDFC had sanctioned ₹22,53,74,000 in cash credit and term loan facilities to R.J. Feeds Private Limited on 22 July 2010. Khadkeshwar furnished a corporate guarantee on 3 September 2010 and mortgaged its properties by deposit of title deeds on 14 October 2010. HDFC last renewed the facilities on 30 March 2023. The Tribunal commenced Khadkeshwar’s CIRP on 20 June 2023 and appointed Amit Chandrashekhar Poddar as resolution professional (RP). The RP issued the public announcement on 23 June 2023 with a claims deadline of 4 July 2023. The Committee of Creditors (CoC), constituted on 13 July 2023, approved the resolution plan submitted by Ananta Vegetable Seeds Private Limited on 15 January 2024. The tenth CoC meeting took place on 8 April 2024, and e-voting ended on 10 May 2024. HDFC notified the RP of the guarantee-backed dues on 14 May and filed its Form C claim on 24 May 2024. The RP rejected it as delayed on 27 May. HDFC applied on 15 June 2024 under Section 60(5)(c) of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016 and Rule 11 of the NCLT Rules, 2016, seeking condonation, claim verification, recognition of its charge and related relief. HDFC said it learned of the CIRP while processing renewal of the borrower’s facility and filed its claim promptly thereafter. It argued that the Regulation 12 timelines were directory. It relied on its asserted first and exclusive charge, the Form CHG-1 filing and registration certificate dated 10 February 2016, CERSAI registration and the corporate debtor’s accounts. The RP relied on the later of the 90-day period and the date of issue of the request for resolution plans, the public announcement and the bank’s capacity to monitor it. The order records two different dates for issue of that request: 15 December 2023 in paragraph 4.1(a) and 15 September 2023 in paragraph 9. The RP also cited the suspended management’s initial non-cooperation, the absence of borrower default or NPA classification and the fact that HDFC had yet to invoke the guarantee. The Tribunal found that Regulation 6A required direct communication when the last available books identified the creditor and contact information was available. The public announcement served as deemed communication only where direct communication was impracticable. Khadkeshwar’s financial statements for the year ended 31 March 2023, signed by the RP, disclosed the guarantee and ₹11,09,66,077 outstanding on the underlying loans. The registered charge was also available in MCA records. Although the suspended directors had initially been ordered to cooperate under Section 19(2), the RP acknowledged on 12 April 2024 that they had delivered the necessary documents. The Bench read Regulation 6A with the RP’s duty to collect creditor information under Section 18(1)(a) and held that the failure to communicate with HDFC was a procedural lapse. The Bench held that Regulation 14 requires the RP to estimate contingent claims from the available information and revise the estimate as warranted. Relying on China Development Bank v. Doha Bank Q.P.S.C. & Ors., it distinguished the default required to commence CIRP under Section 7 from the existence of financial debt for lodging a claim in a pending CIRP. It held that the outstanding debt and the co-extensive liability under the guarantee made HDFC a financial creditor, with the mortgage making it a secured financial creditor. The borrower’s standard account and the uninvoked guarantee did not, by themselves, justify rejecting the claim. Because the RP had failed to notify HDFC and the CoC-approved plan was still pending under Section 31 in I.A. No. 74/2024, the Tribunal condoned the delay and directed the RP to verify the claim in accordance with law. Any admission would leave prior CoC decisions intact under Regulation 12(3), as cited in the order. The CoC-approved plan provided for extinguishing HDFC’s security interest. HDFC opposed that treatment, while the RP cited Edelweiss Asset Reconstruction Company Ltd. v. Anuj Jain & Ors. in support. The Bench left the merits of the plan’s treatment of the charge open and directed the RP to act in accordance with law on inclusion and treatment of the claim and security. It disposed of the prayer for recognition of the charge on those terms, treated the interim prayers as infructuous and made no order as to costs. Cases Discussed 2024 INSC 1029, [2024] 12 SCR 2043 — China Development Bank v. Doha Bank Q.P.S.C. & Ors. (Supreme Court, Civil Appeal No. 7298 of 2022, 20 December 2024) The Tribunal relied on this decision to distinguish the right to submit a financial creditor’s claim in an existing CIRP from the default required to initiate CIRP and to address the effect of an uninvoked guarantee. Company Appeal (AT) (Insolvency) Nos. 517 and 518 of 2023 — Edelweiss Asset Reconstruction Company Ltd. v. Anuj Jain & Ors. (NCLAT New Delhi, 4 July 2023) The RP cited this decision to support extinguishment of security under the plan. The Tribunal left the treatment of HDFC’s charge to be dealt with in accordance with law and did not decide the plan provision on its merits. FULL TEXT OF THE NCLAT JUDGMENT/ORDER 1. The present application was filed by HDFC Bank Limited on 15.06.2024 under Section 60(5)(c) of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016 (Code) read with Rule 11 of the National Company Law Tribunal Rules, 2016 (NCLT Rules) seeking following reliefs: a) Pass an order condoning the delay in filing the Form-C and directing the Respondent to verify the claim of Applicant on merits and duly update the list of creditors; b) Pass an order directing the Respondent to ensure that the first and exclusive charge of the Applicant is recognized in the Resolution Plan in compliance with the provisions of the IBC; c) Pass an order allowing the Applicant to intervene in the Interlocutory Application filed for approval of the proposed Resolution Plan; d) During the pendency and final hearing of the present Application, direct the Respondent to share the relevant extract of the proposed Resolution Plan dealing with the aforesaid Mortgaged Properties; e) During the pendency and final hearing of the present Application, stay the Corporate Insolvency Resolution Process of the Corporate Debtor; f) During the pendency and final hearing of the present Application, pass an order directing that the Interlocutory Application filed for approval of the proposed Resolution Plan shall be kept in abeyance till the adjudication of the present Application; g) For interim/ ad-interim reliefs in terms of prayers (d) to (f); h) For costs of this Application; i) For such other and further orders as this Tribunal may deem fit in the facts and circumstances of the present case. 2. Facts in Brief: 2.1 HDFC Bank Limited (Applicant/HDFC) had sanctioned, vide letter dated 22.07.2010, a credit facility to M/s RJ Feeds Private Limited (Borrower) for total amount of Rs. 22,53,74,000/- (Rupees Twenty-Two Crore Fifty-Three Lakh and Seventy-Four Thousand Only) which included the cash credit and term loan facility. 2.2 The said facility was secured by the Corporate Guarantee of M/s Khadkeshwar Hatcheries Limited (Corporate Debtor) dated 03.09.2010 and a Mortgage of properties of the corporate debtor executed vide Memorandum of Creation of Mortgage by Delivery of Title Deeds dated 14.10.2010. 2.3 The credit facilities were renewed from time to time and prior to the Corporate Debtor being admitted into CIRP, the credit facility was last renewed on 30.03.2023. 2.4 Thereafter, CIRP commenced against the Corporate Debtor vide order dated 20.06.2023 and Mr. Amit Chandrashekhar Poddar (Respondent) was appointed as the Resolution Professional (RP). 2.5 The RP made public announcement on 23.06.2023 inviting claims from the creditors of the Corporate Debtor up to 04.07.2023. Subsequently, the Committee of Creditors (CoC) was constituted on 13.07.2023. 2.6 During the insolvency process of the corporate debtor M/s Ananta Vegetable Seeds Private Limited submitted its Resolution Plan on 15.01.2024 which was approved in the 10th CoC meeting. The CoC was meeting was held on 08.04.2024 and the e-voting on the plan concluded on 10.05.2024. 2.7 After the Resolution Plan was approved by the CoC, HDFC Bank sent a Letter dated 14.05.2024 informing the RP about the outstanding dues payable to HDFC Bank by the Corporate Debtor as Guarantor. HDFC Bank also submitted its claim of Rs. 11,88,71,220.69/- in Form C on 24.05.2024. 2.8 The RP rejected the claim of the applicant vide its letter dated 27.05.2024 on the ground of delay. 2.9 Aggrieved by the same, HDFC Bank has moved the present application challenging the rejection of its claim by the RP and seeking reliefs which are enumerated in paragraph (1) above. 3. Submission of the Applicant 3.1 The applicant submitted that it became aware about the insolvency commence of the Corporate Debtor only while processing the renewal of the credit facility extended to the Borrower as there was no default by the borrower till then. Immediately thereafter the applicant filed its claims with the RP. 3.2 It is further submitted that the timelines for submission of claim under Regulation 12 of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Board of India (Insolvency Resolution Process for Corporate Persons) Regulations, 2016 are directory in nature. Further, the object of the Code is to maximize the value of assets of the Corporate Debtor to enable resolution and realization of public monies and not defeating the rights of the creditors. 3.3 The Applicant submits it has first and exclusive charge over the Mortgaged Properties of the Corporate Debtor. The charge registered with the Registrar of Companies (RoC) was modified from time to time. The Corporate Debtor filed Form No. CHG-1 on 10.02.2016, in pursuance thereof, RoC issued Certificate of Registration dated 10.02.2016. Further, the charge was also registered with the Central Registry of Securitisation Asset Reconstruction and Security Interest of India (CERSAI). The security interest in favour of the applicant also reflected in the records maintained by the Corporate Debtor. 3.4 It was further contended that the financial statements for the year ended on 31.03.2023, were duly signed by the RP, disclosing the contingent liability of the corporate debtor on account of guarantee issued by the corporate debtor in favour of the applicant. However, the security interest created in favour of HDFC Bank on the properties of the Corporate Debtor has not been disclosed despite the information and documents of creation of charge being part of the public records and record maintained by the Corporate Debtor. 3.5 RP was aware of the contingent liability of the corporate debtor as a guarantor for the credit facility extended to the Borrower as recorded in the books of the accounts of the corporate debtor. Further, the mortgage over the properties of the corporate debtor was registered with the RoC and available with MCA record in public domain. RP did not send any communication to the applicant as is mandated by Regulation 6A of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Board of India (Insolvency Resolution Process for Corporate Persons) Regulations, 2016 (CIRP Regulations). 4. Reply of the RP Delay in filing claim 4.1 The RP has justified the rejection of the claim on ground of delay for following reasons: a) As per Proviso to Regulation 12(1) of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Board of India (Insolvency Resolution Process for Corporate Persons), 2016, a creditor may submit its claim with proof to the IRP or the RP, as the case may be, up to the date of issue of Request for Resolution Plans (RFRP) under Regulation 36B or ninety days of the insolvency commencement date, whichever is later. It is submitted that the ninety days of the Insolvency Commencement Date was completed on 18.09.2023 whereas the RP issued the RFRP on 15.12.2023. However, the Applicant has submitted its purported Claim only on 24.05.2024. b) The Applicant is a commercial entity and has resources, personnel and wherewithal, to note the Public Announcement made by the Respondent on 23.06.2023, however, the Applicant had evidently failed to do so. The Applicant has stated no reason for delay in submission of claim except that it was not aware of the insolvency of the corporate debtor. c) The RP submits that there was non-cooperation by the management of the Corporate Debtor, and the RP could not have the access to entirety of the Books of Accounts of the Corporate Debtor. Subsequently, the RP filed an application under Section 19(2) of the Code and this Tribunal vide its Order dated 05.09.2023 directed the suspended management to cooperate with the RP. It is submitted that that it was only thereafter that the RP received most of the books of accounts, financial statements and other records of the Corporate Debtor. However, the suspended directors did not inform about the purported claim of HDFC Bank. Corporate Guarantee not invoked 4.2 The Applicant is not a creditor of the Corporate Debtor as on the date of commencement of CIRP, there was no financial or other debt due and payable by the Corporate Debtor to the applicant. The Borrower had not defaulted and has not been declared Non- Performing Asset (NPA) and the guarantee has not in fact been invoked by the applicant. Therefore, the applicant is not a creditor of the Corporate Debtor and is not entitled to any amount under the Resolution Plan. Analysis and Findings 5. We have heard the submissions of Ld. Counsel for the parties and perused the material on record. 6. Issues that emerge for consideration are: A. Whether Regulation 6A of the CIRP Regulations casts a duty on RP to send communication to all creditors including the applicant herein, in the light of the disclosure in the books of accounts of the corporate debtor and the registered charge over the properties of the corporate debtor? B. In view of the facts and circumstances of the present case, whether the delay by the Applicant in filing the claim with RP can be condoned at this stage? 7. The RP relies on Regulation 12 of the CIRP Regulations to contend that the Applicant’s claim at such a belated stage cannot be entertained considering the fact that resolution plan has already been approved by the CoC. 8. Regulation 12 of the CIRP Regulations is reproduced below: “12. Submission of proof of claims. (1) A creditor shall submit claim with proof on or before the last date mentioned in the public announcement. Provided that a creditor, who fails to submit claim with proof within the time stipulated in the public announcement, may submit his claim with proof to the interim resolution professional or the resolution professional, as the case may be, up to the date of issue of request for resolution plans under regulation 36B or ninety days from the insolvency commencement date, whichever is later: Provided further that the creditor shall provide reasons for delay in submitting the claim beyond the period of ninety days from the insolvency commencement date.” 9. Relying on the above Regulation, Mr. Nausher Kohli, Ld. Counsel for the RP, submitted that a creditor is supposed to submit its claim with proof to the RP up to the date of issue of Request for Resolution Plans (RFRP) or 90 days of the Insolvency Commencement Date (ICD), whichever is later. In the present case, CIRP commenced on 20.06.2023 and the last date of the submission of the claim was 04.07.2023. Further, the RP issued the RFRP on 15.09.2023 and the 90th day of insolvency commencement date was 18.09.2023. The Applicant has submitted the claim on 24.05.2024. There is delay of 323 days from the last date of the submission of the claim and delay of 247 days from the cut-off date which is 90th day from the ICD as provided under Regulation 12 of the CIRP Regulations. 10. Further, there was non-cooperation from the suspended management because of which the RP did not have the access to the entire books of accounts of the Corporate Debtor. It was also submitted that the suspended directors did not mention about the credit facilities and the guarantee. 11. Mr. Rohit Gupta, Ld. Counsel for the Applicant, on the other hand referred to the Financial Statements of the Corporate Debtor prepared in October 2023 i.e. after the admission order dated 20.06.2023 and duly signed by the RP, to show that the RP has in fact included HDFC’s claim under the head of contingent liability, however did not send communication about the CIRP to the Applicant and did not comply with Regulation 6A of CIRP Regulations. 12. Regulation 6A was inserted to the CIRP Regulations vide Notification No. IBBI/2022-23/GN/REG093 dated 16.09.2022 (w.e.f. 16.09.2022). Regulation 6A is reproduced below for ease of reference: “6A. Communication to creditors The interim resolution professional shall send a communication along with a copy of public announcement made under regulation 6, to all the creditors as per the last available books of accounts of the corporate debtor through post or electronic means wherever the information for communication is available. Provided that where it is not possible to send a communication to creditors, the public announcement made under regulation 6 shall be deemed to be the communication to such creditors. 13. It would be appropriate to refer to the Discussion Paper dated 27.06.2022 wherein Regulation 6A was proposed: “14. Need for IRP /RP to communicate to call creditors to submit claims It is observed that in several cases the claimant / creditors are unable to file claims due to reasons like lack of information about the CIRP. This is a common occurrence in real estate cases where the allottees are not in touch with the CD on a regular basis. However, the information regarding claimants/ creditors / allottees is available with the CD. There is need to provide timely information to creditors regarding initiation of CIRP and the last date for filing of claims. Proposal: The IRP shall communicate the initiation of CIRP and the details of the public announcement including the last date for submission of claims to all creditors of the CD.” 14. The intention behind insertion of Regulation 6A was that in many cases the claimants are unable to file claims due to reasons like lack of information about the CIRP, even though the information regarding the claimants are available in the records of Corporate Debtor. Therefore, for the purpose of timely information to the creditors, a duty has been casted on the RP to communicate the initiation of CIRP including the last date for submission of claims to all creditors as per the books of accounts of the Corporate Debtor. 15. We have perused the Financial Statement of the Corporate Debtor for the year ended 31.03.2023 annexed to the Application. In the Notes on Accounts to the Financial Statements, duly signed by the RP, the Corporate Guarantee given by the corporate debtor to HDFC Bank is mentioned in following terms: “Note 2.2: Notes on Accounts: *** 3) Contingent Liability: The Company has given corporate guarantee for loan taken by R. J. Feeds Private Limited to HDFC Bank Limited, balance outstanding on these loans as on 31.03.2023 was Rs. 11,09,66,077/-. TDS Defaults: Undisputed demand for TDS defaults shown on TDS Traces website of Rs. 48,216/- which is not complied yet.” 16. Thus, RP having signed the financial statement of the Corporate Debtor as on 31.03.2023, disclosing the factum of contingent liability of the corporate debtor on account of guarantee issued by it in favour of the applicant for the loan availed by R J Feeds Pvt. Ltd., was fully aware of the contingent liability towards the applicant as per the records of the Corporate Debtor. 17. We note that RP had filed IA/3540/2023 under section 19(2) of the Code in which, vide order dated 05.09.2023, direction was given to the suspended directors to provide the requisite information/ documents to the RP. On 12.04.2024, the RP admitted that all the necessary documents have been handed over by the suspended directors and accordingly, the IA was disposed of. 18. RP’s submission for not complying Regulation 6A that the suspended directors did not mention about the credit facilities to the borrower and did not provide information about guarantee information, cannot be countenanced for the simple reason that the contingent liability of the Corporate Debtor was available in the records of the Corporate Debtor. 19. The next argument on behalf of RP that requires consideration is that the Applicant is not a creditor of the Corporate Debtor on the date of commencement of CIRP as there was no default by the Borrower and the guarantee had not been invoked by the applicant. According to the RP, only those creditors whose claims whose debt have become due and payable are the creditors of corporate debtor and the creditors/claimants for contingent liability are not creditors of the corporate debtor. Regulation 14 of the CIRP Regulations provides as follows: “Determination of amount of claim. 14. (1) Where the amount claimed by a creditor is not precise due to any contingency or other reason, the interim resolution professional or the resolution professional, as the case may be, shall make the best estimate of the amount of the claim based on the information available with him. (2) The interim resolution professional or the resolution professional, as the case may be, shall revise the amounts of claims admitted, including the estimates of claims made under sub-regulation (1), as soon as may be practicable, when he comes across additional information warranting such revision.” 20. The above Regulation clearly recognizes contingent claims and imposes a duty on the RP to make reasonable estimates regarding such claims. 21. Further, in a recent judgment delivered by the Hon’ble Supreme Court of India in China Development Bank vs. Doha Bank Q.P.S.C. & Ors. [Civil Appeal No. 7298/2022], the definition of debt, claims and uninvoked guarantees etc. are discussed which includes contingent claims even if there is no default under a Deed of Hypothecation (DoH). It was held there may be a debt which may be disputed or undisputed, legal or equitable, secured or unsecured and such person to whom a debt is owed, becomes a Creditor. Some of the relevant paragraphs are extracted below: “48. …In terms of sub-section (11) of Section 3, debt is a liability or obligation in respect of a claim which is due from any person and includes a financial debt or operational debt. As noted earlier, a claim is a right to payment whether or not, such right is reduced to judgment and whether it is disputed or undisputed. The right to payment can be legal, equitable, secured or unsecured. Therefore, if there is a liability or obligation in respect of a payment which is disputed, it still becomes a claim. Once there is a liability or obligation in respect of a claim, it becomes a debt. Once there is a financial debt, the person to whom a debt is owed, becomes a Financial Creditor. REQUIREMENT OF OCCURRENCE OF ‘DEFAULT’ 61. There is an argument canvassed before us that default under the DoH has not occurred. We have already quoted the definition of ‘financial debt’ under Section 5(8) of the IBC. There is no requirement incorporated therein that a debt becomes financial debt only when default occurs. Under Section 5(7) of the IBC, any person to whom financial debt is owed becomes a Financial Creditor even if there is no default in payment of debt. Therefore, this argument deserves to be rejected. 62. On this aspect, we may also note that under Section 3(12), ‘default’ has been defined. This definition of ‘default’ becomes relevant only while invoking the provisions of Section 7(1) of the IBC when the CIRP is sought to be initiated by the Financial Creditor. Section 7(1) provides that a Financial Creditor can initiate CIRP against the Corporate Debtor when there is a default on the part of the Corporate Debtor. There is no requirement under Section 5(8) of the IBC that there can be a debt only when there is a default. The moment it is established that the financial debt is owed to any person, he/she becomes a Financial Creditor. In this case, we are concerned with the claim made by the appellants. A public announcement of CIRP under Section 15(1) must contain the last date of submission of claims as may be specified. Thus, if a person has a claim within the meaning of Section 3(6), he can submit it on public announcement contemplated by Section 15 being made. A Financial Creditor has a claim as explained earlier. Therefore, for submitting the claim by a Financial Creditor, there is no requirement of actual default.” “EXTINGUISHMENT OF CONTINGENT CLAIM ON IMPOSITION OF MORATORIUM 63. Arguments have been canvassed that clause 5(iii) of the DoH is a contingent contract wherein the contingent event is the shortfall between realisation and expenses. The clause applies to the shortfall in the total liability of the borrower after necessary amount is realised from the hypothecated assets. It is contended that the contract has become impossible, since owing to the moratorium imposed, the hypothecated properties could not be sold and the shortfall could not arise. Section 14(1) imposes an embargo or prohibition on certain acts. However, it does extinguish the claim. If the argument that the claims of all the creditors of the Corporate Debtor are extinguished once the moratorium comes into force is accepted, no creditor would be able to file a claim. For example, if money advanced is secured by a promissory note or a negotiable instrument, a suit for recovery based on the said documents will not lie once a moratorium comes into force. But, the liability under the documents will continue to exist. In fact, after moratorium, no creditor can recover any dues from the Corporate Debtor. But still, there is a provision for making a claim. Hence, the argument based on moratorium deserves to be rejected. The DoH will continue to be valid. However, on the basis of the DoH, something which is prohibited by Section 14, cannot be done. Therefore, Section 14 will be of no assistance to the 1st respondent-Doha Bank. *** 65. Another argument was canvassed based on the definition of ‘claim’ under Section 3(6) of the IBC. If the right to payment exists or if a breach of contract gives rise to a right to payment, the definition of ‘claim’ is attracted. Even if that right cannot be enforced by reason of the applicability of the moratorium, the claim will still exist. Therefore, whether the cause of action for invoking the guarantee has arisen or not is not relevant for considering the definition of ‘claim’.” 22. In the present case, it is undisputed and acknowledged by the RP as disclosed in the Balance Sheet of the corporate debtor, duly signed by the RP, that the corporate debtor has executed guarantee in favour of the applicant for the credit facility to R.J. Feeds. As long as such loan remains outstanding from the borrower, there is liability on the part of the guarantor as well, as the liability of the borrower and the guarantor are co-extensive. We are also not in agreement with the submission of the RP that the applicant is not a creditor. 23. Having discussed the authoritative interpretation of debt, claims, uninvoked guarantee, by the Hon’ble Supreme Court read with Regulation 6A, we are of the considered view that the applicant is a financial creditor. Further, the execution of the Mortgage Deed dated 14.10.2010 makes the Applicant a secured Financial Creditor. The claim of the applicant could not have been rejected merely on the ground that the Principal Borrower’s Account is standard or that the guarantee is uninvoked. 24. It is the duty of the RP who has taken over the management of the Corporate Debtor, to get the relevant information about the creditors of the Corporate Debtor. Section 18(1)(a) casts an important duty upon the RP to collect all information relating to the assets, finances and operations of the Corporate Debtor and about all creditors from all possible sources which includes the audited financial statements and the certificate of registration of charges available on MCA records. The RP himself has signed the financial statement for F.Y. ended on 31.03.2023 of the Corporate Debtor and the certificate of registration of charge is available on MCA record. The RP ought to have sent communication to all creditors including the applicant about initiation of CIRP of the corporate debtor. Accordingly, question A is answered in affirmative. 25. Having failed to perform his duty by not sending the communication to the application as mandated under Regulation 6A, there is procedural lapse on the part of the RP. Further, though the CoC has approved the resolution plan, however, the plan has not yet been approved by this Tribunal under section 31 of the Code. In view of the facts of the case and the legal position as discussed above, we are inclined to condone the delay and direct the RP to verify the claims of the applicant in accordance with law. Accordingly, question B is also answered in affirmative. 26. In view of the discussions made above, prayer ‘a’ seeking condonation of delay and direction to RP to verify the claim of Applicant as Guarantor, is allowed. 27. However, we are also mindful of the fact that admission of the claim of HDFC Bank shall not affect the prior decisions taken by the Committee of Creditors as stipulated in Regulation 12(3) which reads as follows: “12. Submission of proof of claims. xxx (3) Where the creditor in sub-regulation (2) is a financial creditor under Regulation 8, it shall be included in the committee from the date of admission of such claim: Provided that such inclusion shall not affect validity of any decision taken by the committee prior to such inclusion.” 28. It is pertinent to note that the COC in its commercial wisdom has approved the resolution plan of the corporate debtor and IA/74/2024 filed for approval of the Resolution Plan is pending before the Bench. Under the CoC-approved Resolution Plan, the security interest of HDFC Bank stands extinguished. Treatment of Security Interest of HDFC in the Resolution Plan 29. Arguments were canvassed by the parties over this treatment of the security interest of HDFC Bank/ Applicant in the Resolution Plan approved by the CoC. 30. Mr. Gupta, appearing for HDFC Bank, submitted that the charge of the Bank could not be extinguished which would leave the Bank which is a secured financial creditor worse off/inferior in its claim and rights. 31. On the other hand, Mr. Kohli, for the RP, relies on the judgment passed by Hon’ble NCLAT in Edelweiss Asset Reconstruction Company Limited vs. Anuj Jain [Company Appeal (AT) (Ins) No. 517 & 518 of 2023] and submitted that extinguishment of security interest is permissible under the Plan as per the Code. However, considering the fact that the Resolution Plan is not before us, we are not inclined to consider the merits of the same. Further, since we have given a direction to RP to consider the claim of HDFC Bank, the RP shall act in accordance with law with respect to the inclusion of the claim of HDFC Bank and the subsequent treatment of the said claim and security of HDFC Bank under the Resolution Plan. 32. Thus, prayer ‘b’ seeking recognition of the security interest of HDFC Bank in the Resolution Plan as per the provisions of the IBC, is accordingly disposed of. 33. Prayers ‘c’ to ‘g’ are interim reliefs. Since we have decided the matter and passing the final order, prayers ‘c’ to ‘g’ are accordingly, have become infructuous and are accordingly disposed of. 34. Accordingly, the IA No. 3284/2024 is allowed and stands disposed of. No order as to costs.

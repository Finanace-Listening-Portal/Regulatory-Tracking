Summary: The distinction between Sections 73 and 74 of the CGST Act is fundamental to the validity of GST demand proceedings. Section 73 governs ordinary cases of non-payment, short payment, erroneous refund or wrongful input tax credit, whereas Section 74 applies where fraud, wilful misstatement or suppression of facts with intent to evade tax is established. In G.R. Infra Projects Ltd v State of Madhya Pradesh and Tata Steel Ltd v Union of India, the Supreme Court emphasised that the expiry of limitation under Section 73 does not itself justify invoking Section 74. The show cause notice must disclose specific foundational facts supporting fraud or suppression; mechanical reproduction of statutory expressions, subsequent affidavits, audit objections or protective demands cannot cure the deficiency. The article examines these rulings alongside earlier Supreme Court decisions under excise, customs and service tax laws, High Court judgments, CBIC Instruction No. 05/2023-GST and Circular No. 258/04/2026-GST. It also explains why facts already available to the department ordinarily cannot support a fresh allegation of suppression, why appellate authorities cannot supply missing jurisdictional grounds, and how Circular No. 185/17/2022-GST limits redetermination under Section 75(2). Finally, it discusses Section 74A, applicable from FY 2024-25 onwards, which introduces common limitation periods while retaining different penalties for fraudulent and non-fraudulent cases. Introduction A familiar pattern runs through a large number of show cause notices now reaching the High Court. The period for a notice under Section 73 of the CGST Act and the UPGST Act runs out. In the last available days a notice then arrives under Section 74, alleging fraud, wilful misstatement or suppression of facts, on material the department has held for years. The allegation is written in the words of the section and in nothing more. Where the notice is confirmed, the first appellate authority usually affirms it in a few lines. The question examined in this article is a narrow one. Can the expiry of the period under Section 73, by itself, open the door to Section 74? The statute, the Supreme Court and the long line of authority under the earlier indirect tax laws give the same answer. It cannot. This article sets out what the relevant paragraphs of those judgments actually say. 2. The statutory scheme Section 73 applies where tax has not been paid or short paid, has been erroneously refunded, or input tax credit has been wrongly availed or utilised, for any reason other than fraud or any wilful misstatement or suppression of facts to evade tax. Under Section 73(10) the order is to be passed within three years from the due date of the annual return for the year concerned, and Section 73(2) requires the notice to be issued at least three months before that limit. Section 74 covers the same heads of liability, but only where the default arises by reason of fraud, or any wilful misstatement or suppression of facts to evade tax. For such cases Section 74(10) allows five years for the order, the notice is to be issued at least six months before that limit, and the penalty is equal to the tax. The two sections are therefore not alternatives open to the choice of the officer. They operate on two different sets of facts. Section 73 is the rule. Section 74 is the exception, available only when the facts constituting fraud, wilful misstatement or suppression exist and are stated. Explanation 2 to Section 74, which had given suppression a wide meaning, was omitted with effect from 01.11.2024 when Section 74A was inserted, and the Supreme Court in Tata Steel (para 12) declined to let the department rest its case on it. What remains is the main provision, and its words “to evade tax” tie every one of the three grounds to intention. Nothing in either section makes the expiry of time under Section 73 a ground for proceeding under Section 74. Limitation follows from the correct classification of the case. It can never be the reason for the classification. 3. G.R. Infra Projects: the notice must speak for itself In G.R. Infra Projects Ltd v State of Madhya Pradesh, Civil Appeal No. 11277 of 2026, decided on 19.08.2026 by J.B. Pardiwala and K. Vinod Chandran, JJ., reported in 2026 LiveLaw (SC) 852, the notice dated 13.06.2025 related to FY 2018-19 and had been issued under Section 74. The paragraphs of the judgment build the answer step by step. (a) Paras 3 and 4 state the rival positions. The assessee contended that a notice under Section 74 can be issued only when fraud, wilful misstatement, or suppression is alleged, and that the allegation must be stated in the notice itself. The State accepted that the notice was beyond Section 73 and sought to justify Section 74 through a counter affidavit alleging fraud and suppression. (b) Para 5 disposes of the counter affidavit at the threshold. The Court declined to consider it, holding that a notice’s validity rests on its contents and that an affidavit filed later cannot cure its defects. This is the GST application of the rule in Mohinder Singh Gill, discussed below. (c) Paras 6 to 8 work out limitation. The annual return for FY 2018-19 was originally due on 31.12.2019 under Rule 80 and was extended to 31.12.2020, so the limit for a Section 73 notice expired on 31.12.2023. Even after excluding the period from 15.03.2020 to 28.02.2022 under the Court’s suo motu order in the pandemic, the limitation ran only up to 28.02.2025. The notice dated 13.06.2025 was therefore barred under Section 73, and the Court recorded that the only question left was whether Section 74 could be invoked. (d) Para 9 records the history relied on by the State: summons from 03.03.2025, an allegation that the assessee had not appeared on some twenty dates, a draft notice, an intimation under Section 142(1A) on 29.04.2025, service of the draft on 27.05.2025, and then the notice of 13.06.2025. This paragraph matters because it shows that even a record of non appearance and delay did not, in the Court’s view, supply the foundation for fraud. (e) Para 10 is the heart of the judgment. The Court found that the notice merely spoke of “fraud or concealment of facts” without saying how either was found. It held that the allegations must emerge from the notice itself and that a mechanical use of the words of the section is not sufficient. (f) Para 11 gives the result in these words: “We find absolutely no reason to sustain the SCN issued and find the High Court to have erroneously upheld the same. We set aside the impugned order as also the SCN and direct the respondent-State to desist from taking any further proceedings in pursuance of the SCN challenged before the High Court.” The consequence is significant. Because the Section 73 period had gone, setting aside the Section 74 notice ended the demand. No liberty was reserved. 4. Tata Steel: no protective notice, and satisfaction before notice In Tata Steel Ltd v Union of India, 2026 INSC 920, decided on 25.08.2026, the notices related to FY 2018-19 to 2020-21. Each paragraph adds a distinct proposition. (a) Paras 6 and 7: the extensions of the annual return dates under Section 44(1) placed the Section 73 limits at 31.12.2023, 31.03.2024 and 28.02.2025 for the three years. After the pandemic exclusion the limitation for all three years ran to 28.02.2025, and the notice of 13.06.2025 fell outside it. (b) Para 8 frames the question: whether a notice can take the benefit of the five year period under Section 74 without allegations of fraud, wilful misrepresentation or suppression. (c) Para 9 traces the origin. The proceedings arose from audit observations of an ITC mismatch and short payment. The department sought documents, issued a notice, and then placed the matter in the call book. It had also contested the audit point before the Public Accounts Committee. (d) Para 10 deals with a fresh notice dated 01.07.2025 that revived the earlier one and proposed a protective demand. The Court rejected the device, observing that there is “no such measure of protective assessment statutorily permitted under the GST Act”. A notice issued only to keep limitation alive is exactly such a protective measure. (e) Para 11 lays down that “the proceedings under Section 73/74 can be initiated only on the satisfaction of the Assessing Officer”. An audit observation does not replace that satisfaction. For Section 74, the officer must himself be satisfied that the mismatch or short payment was caused by fraud, wilful misrepresentation or suppression. (f) Para 12 rejects two arguments of the Revenue: reliance on Explanation 2 to Section 74, which stood omitted from 01.11.2024, and the plea that the proceedings had begun before the Section 73 period expired. The Court pointed out that the limitation under Section 73 is for issuing the order, not merely the notice. (g) Para 13 draws an inference from the department’s own conduct. By disputing the audit objection before the Public Accounts Committee, the department showed that it had not itself reached any satisfaction about mismatch, short payment or suppression. The only allegation of suppression was a bland statement that ITC had been availed “without documentary evidence and suppressed the facts”. (h) Para 14 states the governing rule. An extended period requires foundational facts, and those facts must be evident from the notice itself. In the Court’s words, “The mere employment of such words will not indicate an application of mind.” (i) Para 15 applies the rule. The notice disclosed no fact showing a deliberate device to evade tax or avail excess credit. The notice and the order in original dated 26.12.2025 were set aside, with liberty to initiate fresh proceedings under Section 74 on foundational facts stated in the notice, and to pass the order before 28.02.2027. Read together, G.R. Infra and Tata Steel mean this. Once the Section 73 period has gone, the notice survives only if Section 74 has been validly invoked. The burden lies on the department, it must be discharged from the four corners of the notice, and neither an audit objection, nor a protective purpose, nor an affidavit filed later can discharge it. 5. The High Courts The High Courts had reached the same conclusion earlier. The Gujarat High Court in Zodiac Energy Ltd held in 2025 that the Revenue cannot invoke Section 74 merely because the normal limitation under Section 73 has expired. The Allahabad High Court in Bharat Mint and Allied Chemicals v State of U.P., 2025 (6) TMI 1243, quashed a Section 74 notice where the same officer had earlier processed the issues under Section 73 without any allegation of fraud or suppression. The Allahabad High Court has also applied CBIC Instruction No. 05/2023-GST to UPGST proceedings in Khurja Scrap Trading Co, Writ Tax No. 743 of 2023, 2025:AHC:151783 (paras 11 to 13), and in Safecon Lifescience, Writ Tax No. 389 of 2023, decided on 09.09.2025. 6. The lesson of the earlier indirect tax laws Section 74 uses almost the same words as the proviso to Section 11A(1) of the Central Excise Act, 1944, the proviso to Section 73(1) of the Finance Act, 1994 and Section 28 of the Customs Act, 1962. Each of those provisions gave the department a short normal period and a longer period where fraud, collusion, wilful misstatement or suppression of facts with intent to evade duty was shown. The Supreme Court construed that language over four decades, and what it said carries over to Section 74 because the language is the same. (a) Collector of Central Excise v Chemphar Drugs and Liniments, (1989) 2 SCC 127. The respondent had not included the value of certain products in its declaration, and the department invoked the five year period on the ground of suppression. The Court held: “In order to make the demand for duty sustainable beyond a period of six months and up to a period of 5 years in view of the proviso to sub-section 11A of the Act, it has to be established that the duty of excise has not been levied or paid or short-levied or short-paid, or erroneously refunded by reasons of either fraud or collusion or wilful misstatement or suppression of facts or contravention of any provision of the Act or Rules made thereunder, with intent to evade payment of duty. Something positive other than mere inaction or failure on the part of the manufacturer or producer or conscious or deliberate withholding of information when the manufacturer knew otherwise, is required before it is saddled with any liability, before the period of six months.” The Court added that what the respondent had omitted had to be taken to be within the knowledge of the authorities, who could have taken corrective action in time. A return mismatch visible on the portal stands on the same footing. (b) Padmini Products v Collector of Central Excise, (1989) 4 SCC 275. The assessee had not taken out a licence or paid duty on agarbattis, believing them to be exempt handicrafts. Following Chemphar, the Court held that failure to pay duty or take out a licence is not necessarily due to fraud, collusion, wilful misstatement or suppression, and that “suppression of facts is not failure to disclose the legal consequences of a certain provision”. Where there was scope for a bona fide belief, the extended period was not available. Disputes on rate, classification or the taxability of royalty fall squarely within this principle. (c) Tamil Nadu Housing Board v Collector of Central Excise, 1995 Supp (1) SCC 50. The Court explained the content of the words “intent to evade”: “When the law requires an intention to evade payment of duty then it is not mere failure to pay duty. It must be something more. That is, the assessee must be aware that the duty was leviable and it must deliberately avoid paying it. The word ‘evade’ in the context means defeating the provision of law of paying duty. It is made more stringent by use of the word ‘intent’.” It then held that fraud or the other ingredients and the intent to evade must both be present before the officer can invoke the proviso, and that since the proviso extends the period of limitation from six months to five years it has to be construed strictly. (d) Cosmic Dye Chemical v Collector of Central Excise, (1995) 6 SCC 117, a bench of three judges. In para 6 the Court analysed the words of the proviso: “Now so far as fraud and collusion are concerned, it is evident that the requisite intent, i.e., intent to evade duty is built into these very words. So far as mis-statement or suppression of facts are concerned, they are clearly qualified by the word ‘wilful’ preceding the words ‘mis-statement or suppression of facts’ which means with intent to evade duty. … Mis-statement or suppression of fact must be wilful.” (e) Pushpam Pharmaceuticals Co v Collector of Central Excise, 1995 Supp (3) SCC 462. In para 4 the Court said: “… the expression ‘suppression’ has been used in the proviso to Section 11A of the Act accompanied by very strong words as ‘fraud’ or ‘collusion’ and, therefore, has to be construed strictly. It does not mean any omission. The act must be deliberate. In taxation, it can have only one meaning that the correct information was not disclosed deliberately to escape from payment of duty. Where facts are known to both the parties the omission by one to do what he might have done and not that he must have done, does not render it suppression.” (f) Anand Nishikawa Co Ltd v Commissioner of Central Excise, (2005) 7 SCC 749. In para 26 the Court, relying on Pushpam, added that mere failure to declare does not amount to wilful suppression and that there must be some positive act on the part of the assessee before suppression can be found. Continental Foundation Joint Venture v Commissioner of Central Excise, (2007) 10 SCC 337 (paras 9 to 11), applied the same principle where the assessee had taken a bona fide view on a question of interpretation. (g) Uniworth Textiles Ltd v Commissioner of Central Excise, (2013) 9 SCC 753. The Tribunal had held that non payment of duty itself showed suppression. The Court rejected this, holding in para 12 that the conclusion that mere non payment of duties is equivalent to collusion or wilful misstatement or suppression of facts is untenable, and in paras 18 and 22 that the Revenue must show a conscious and deliberate intent to evade. (h) Commissioner of Central Excise and Customs v Reliance Industries Ltd, 2023 INSC 591. The department alleged suppression because the assessee had not disclosed a particular valuation element. In para 20 the Court said: “An accusation of non-disclosure can only be made if there is in the first instance a requirement to disclose.” In paras 23 to 25 the Court found that the assessee had filed the returns the law required, that the returns contained no column for the information in question, that the Revenue had not shown any provision requiring its disclosure, and that where two plausible views were possible the invocation of the extended period was totally unjustified. A bona fide belief, the Court said, does not become mala fide because it is later held to be wrong. The reassessment provisions of the State sales tax laws were treated with the same discipline. In State of U.P. v Aryaverth Chawal Udyog, (2015) 17 SCC 324 (paras 28 to 31), the Supreme Court read Section 21 of the U.P. Trade Tax Act, 1948 with Section 147 of the Income Tax Act. It held that reassessment requires a reason to believe founded on relevant material, not a reason to suspect; that the discovery of an inadvertent mistake or non application of mind during assessment would not be a justified ground to reinitiate proceedings under Section 21(1) on the basis of a change in subjective opinion; and that the reassessment must remain confined to the grounds on which the belief was formed. CIT v Kelvinator of India Ltd, (2010) 2 SCC 723, and ITO v Lakhmani Mewal Das, (1976) 3 SCC 757, require the same rational connection between the material and the belief. If ordinary reopening under the State law required this much, an allegation of fraud under Section 74 requires more. 7. Facts already known to the department A recurring feature of the notices under discussion is that they rest on returns already filed, portal data already available, or a discrepancy already raised in an ASMT-10 or an earlier notice. The excise cases answer this directly. (a) In P and B Pharmaceuticals (P) Ltd v Collector of Central Excise, (2003) 3 SCC 599, the Court held that where a notice has been issued for an earlier period on a set of facts, another notice invoking the extended period cannot be issued on the same or similar facts on a plea of suppression, because the facts were already in the knowledge of the department. (b) In ECE Industries Ltd v Commissioner of Central Excise, (2004) 13 SCC 719 (para 4), earlier proceedings on the same subject matter were pending adjudication, and the Court held that it could not be said that there was any suppression, so the extended period was not available. (c) In Nizam Sugar Factory v Collector of Central Excise, (2006) 11 SCC 573 (para 9), the assessee was fully aware of the facts, and so was the department, when the first notice was issued. The later notice could not allege suppression of those very facts. (d) In Hyderabad Polymers (P) Ltd v Commissioner of Central Excise, 2004 (166) ELT 151 (SC), the earlier notice on a similar issue had been dropped. The Court held that once that had happened it could no longer be said that there was any suppression. The same reasoning applies where an explanation on the very discrepancy has been accepted under Section 61(2) by an ASMT-12. Nothing remains hidden once the department has examined the point and closed it. 8. The notice is the foundation In Armour Security (India) Ltd v Commissioner, CGST, Delhi East Commissionerate, 2025 INSC 982, the Supreme Court was concerned with Section 6(2)(b), but its observations on the show cause notice apply with equal force here. (a) In paras 65 to 67, the Court described the notice as the mandatory precondition for any demand and the point at which quasi-judicial adjudication begins. It held that a notice cannot be vague, that it must set out the provisions invoked, and that any material gathered in the course of inquiry and relied upon must be appended and disclosed to the noticee. (b) In paras 84 and 85, the Court held that the subject matter of the proceeding lies in the contents of the notice, that the Revenue cannot argue a case not made out in it, and that a ground not stated in the notice cannot be used against the noticee. It relied on Commissioner of Customs v Champdany Industries Ltd, (2009) 9 SCC 466 (para 50), and Gorkha Security Services v Govt. (NCT of Delhi), (2014) 9 SCC 105 (para 21), and noted that Section 75(7) gives the rule statutory form. The general principle of administrative law is the same. In Mohinder Singh Gill v Chief Election Commissioner, (1978) 1 SCC 405, the Court said in para 8: “… when a statutory functionary makes an order based on certain grounds, its validity must be judged by the reasons so mentioned and cannot be supplemented by fresh reasons in the shape of affidavit or otherwise. Otherwise, an order bad in the beginning may, by the time it comes to court on account of a challenge, get validated by additional grounds later brought out.” Para 5 of G.R. Infra is this principle applied to a Section 74 notice. 9. The department’s own instructions The CBIC has repeatedly told its officers the same thing. Instruction No. 05/2023-GST dated 13.12.2023 (para 3.3) states that Section 74 is not to be invoked for mere non payment of tax. Circular No. 258/04/2026-GST dated 26.10.2026, issued under Section 168(1), goes further. It requires the notice to state how the proceedings arose (para 4.1), to link each act to the provision invoked (para 4.3) and to contain specific allegations, quoting para 10 of G.R. Infra (para 5.1). It states that facts known to both sides are not suppression (para 5.2). It directs that Section 74 is not to be invoked on mere non payment, nor in scrutiny cases merely because a discrepancy has been noticed, but only on investigation material set out in the notice (para 5.3). It treats a second notice alleging fraud on the same facts as generally invalid (para 5.5), asks officers to avoid last minute notices (para 8), and confines the demand to Section 73 where Section 74 is not justified (para 13). The Circular is a CGST circular. For proceedings under the UPGST Act it is of persuasive force unless adopted by the Commissioner, State Tax, Uttar Pradesh. Its value lies in the fact that it records the Board’s own understanding of what the law already required. 10. Invocation to protect the officer The practical reason for most of these notices is not difficult to see. An officer who allows a demand to lapse may face audit objections and enquiries. An officer whose Section 74 notice is later quashed faces, as a rule, nothing. Section 74 thus comes to be used as a means of preserving the demand and protecting the officer, rather than as a response to any fraud that has been found. The law does not permit a statutory power to be used in this way. In State of Punjab v Gurdial Singh, (1980) 2 SCC 471, Krishna Iyer J. explained what bad faith means in the exercise of power (para 9): “Pithily put, bad faith which invalidates the exercise of power, sometimes called colourable exercise or fraud on power and oftentimes overlaps motives, passions and satisfactions, is the attainment of ends beyond the sanctioned purposes of power by simulation or pretension of gaining a legitimate goal. … The action is bad where the true object is to reach an end different from the one for which the power is entrusted, goaded by extraneous considerations, good or bad, but irrelevant to the entrustment.” The power under Section 74 is entrusted for cases of fraud, wilful misstatement and suppression. Saving limitation, or saving the officer from audit, is an end different from the one for which the power is entrusted, however understandable the motive may be. In S.R. Venkataraman v Union of India, (1979) 2 SCC 491, the Court likewise held that an order based on considerations outside the statute is vitiated by malice in law, even without any personal ill will. A notice under Section 74 issued to save time is also precisely the protective measure that Tata Steel (para 10) holds the GST Act does not permit. Section 157 protects an officer only for what is done or intended to be done in good faith. The Allahabad High Court has also shown that disregard of binding law has consequences. In Rajdhani Udyog v State of U.P., 2025 (9) TMI 251, where the order before the Court was an appellate order, the Court treated the failure to follow binding judgments as a serious breach of judicial discipline, imposed costs of Rs. 5,000 payable by the officer out of his salary, and directed the Principal Secretary, Institutional Finance, to put in place a system to keep officers informed of judicial pronouncements. In Merino Industries Ltd, Writ Tax No. 1406 of 2025, arising from a Section 74 order of about Rs. 5.82 crore, costs of Rs. 20,000 were imposed on the officer for not following Section 75(4). 11. The appellate authority cannot cure the defect Where the notice was without jurisdiction, affirmation in appeal does not change its character. In Kiran Singh v Chaman Paswan, AIR 1954 SC 340, the Court said (para 6): “It is a fundamental principle well established that a decree passed by a Court without jurisdiction is a nullity, and that its invalidity could be set up whenever and wherever it is sought to be enforced or relied upon, even at the stage of execution and even in collateral proceedings. A defect of jurisdiction, whether it is pecuniary or territorial, or whether it is in respect of the subject-matter of the action, strikes at the very authority of the Court to pass any decree, and such a defect cannot be cured even by consent of parties.” If consent cannot cure a defect of jurisdiction, an appellate order cannot. The appellate authority under Section 107 is a quasi judicial authority bound by Section 75(7), and it cannot confirm the demand on a foundation of fraud that the notice never laid. It is also bound to follow the law declared by higher forums. In Union of India v Kamlakshi Finance Corporation Ltd, 1992 Supp (1) SCC 443, the Court said (para 6): “The principles of judicial discipline require that the orders of the higher appellate authorities should be followed unreservedly by the subordinate authorities. The mere fact that the order of the appellate authority is not ‘acceptable’ to the department … cannot be a ground for not following it unless its operation has been suspended by a competent court. If this healthy rule is not followed, the result will only be undue harassment to assessees and chaos in administration of tax laws.” An appellate order that affirms a Section 74 demand without examining whether the foundational facts were stated in the notice, in the face of G.R. Infra and Tata Steel, is open to exactly this criticism. For the same reason, the existence or use of the appellate remedy does not bar the High Court. In Whirlpool Corporation v Registrar of Trade Marks, (1998) 8 SCC 1, para 15, the Court held that an alternative remedy does not operate as a bar in at least three contingencies, one of which is where the order or proceedings are wholly without jurisdiction. 12. Section 75(2) is not a safety net The department often relies on Section 75(2). It provides that where an appellate authority, tribunal or court concludes that a notice under Section 74 is not sustainable because fraud, wilful misstatement or suppression has not been established, the proper officer shall determine the tax as if the notice had been issued under Section 73(1). It is argued that the demand survives in any event. The CBIC has itself rejected that argument where the notice was late for Section 73. Circular No. 185/17/2022-GST dated 27.12.2022 clarifies that the re-determination is to be made within two years of the communication of the direction, and that it is confined to a notice that could have been issued within the time allowed under Section 73. Where the Section 74 notice was issued beyond two years and nine months from the due date of the annual return, the Circular states: “the entire proceeding shall have to be dropped, being hit by the limitation of time as specified in section 73” Where a notice covers several years, the Circular permits re-determination only for the years in respect of which the notice was within time. A Section 74 notice issued only because the Section 73 period had lapsed therefore saves nothing once the charge of fraud fails. It only postpones the end of the proceeding. 13. Section 74A: the legislature’s own acknowledgement The GST Council at its 53rd meeting on 22 June 2024 recommended a common time limit for demand notices and orders for FY 2024-25 onwards, irrespective of whether the case involves fraud, wilful misstatement or suppression. The recommendation was given effect by inserting Section 74A through the Finance (No. 2) Act, 2024. Under Section 74A the notice is to be issued within 42 months from the due date of the annual return and the order within 12 months of the notice, extendable by six months. The charge of fraud now affects only the penalty: ten per cent of the tax or Rs. 10,000, whichever is higher, under Section 74A(5)(i), against a penalty equal to the tax under Section 74A(5)(ii). The time for paying with a reduced penalty was also raised from 30 days to 60 days. Section 74A applies prospectively. It does not govern FY 2017-18 to 2023-24, which remain under Sections 73 and 74. Its significance for the earlier years lies elsewhere. By removing the link between the fraud allegation and the time limit, Parliament acknowledged the very difficulty described in this article: so long as the allegation decided limitation, it was being made to gain time. Section 74A also confirms that fraud, wilful misstatement and suppression are real charges with real consequences, to be proved on the facts, and not a device to extend limitation. The same test of foundational facts governs a penalty under Section 74A(5)(ii) today. 14. Conclusion The position may be stated in a few propositions. (a) Expiry of the period under Section 73 is not a condition for invoking Section 74 and confers no jurisdiction under it (G.R. Infra, paras 8 and 10). (b) Where the Section 73 period has expired, the notice stands or falls on whether the foundational facts of fraud, wilful misstatement or suppression with intent to evade tax are stated in the notice itself (Tata Steel, para 14), and they cannot be supplied by affidavit (G.R. Infra, para 5). (c) Inaction, non-payment, return mismatches and facts already known to the department do not amount to suppression (Chemphar, Pushpam, Uniworth, Nizam Sugar, Reliance). (d) A protective notice is unknown to the GST Act (Tata Steel, para 10), and invocation of Section 74 to preserve a demand or to protect the officer is a colourable exercise of power (Gurdial Singh). (e) The appellate authority cannot supply a foundation the notice did not lay, and its affirmation does not validate a notice issued without jurisdiction (Kiran Singh, Kamlakshi, Section 75(7)). (f) Section 75(2) does not revive a demand for which the Section 73 period had expired when the notice was issued (Circular No. 185/17/2022-GST). The distinction between Section 73 and Section 74 was drawn by Parliament with care. It is not a choice to be made at the end of the limitation period. ***** Disclaimer: The views expressed in this article are the author’s own thoughts on the subject.

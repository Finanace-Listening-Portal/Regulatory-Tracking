Mehul Chandrakant Shah (ICAI Disciplinary Committee) Balance Confirmation Is Not Proof of Recoverability: Emphasis of Matter Cannot Replace Adequate Audit Evidence Summary: The Disciplinary Committee of the Institute of Chartered Accountants of India (ICAI), in proceedings concerning CA. Mehul Chandrakant Shah of M/s. Shah Shah & Shah, Mumbai, held the respondent guilty of professional misconduct under Clause (7) of Part I of the Second Schedule to the Chartered Accountants Act, 1949. The findings were pronounced on 31 August 2026, followed by a punishment order dated 15 September 2026. The proceedings arose from the statutory audit of Shree Ashtavinayak Cine Vision Limited, which subsequently faced winding-up proceedings. Following an investigation report dated 24 June 2014 and a Central Government reference to ICAI, the respondent’s audit procedures were examined in relation to substantial loans and advances. Earlier disciplinary findings and punishment had been set aside by the Appellate Authority on 26 May 2026 for procedural reasons, without adjudicating the merits, and the matter was remanded for fresh consideration Background of the Proceedings The proceedings concerned the statutory audit of Shree Ashtavinayak Cine Vision Ltd., a company engaged in film-related activities which subsequently underwent winding-up proceedings. Following a court-directed investigation and an investigation report dated 24 June 2014, the Central Government referred the matter to ICAI for examination of alleged audit failures. The respondent’s firm had been appointed statutory auditor on 3 December 2010 and resigned on 31 March 2014. Earlier disciplinary findings and the consequential punishment order were set aside by the Appellate Authority on 26 May 2026 on procedural grounds, without deciding the merits. The matter was remanded for fresh hearing. Following reconsideration, the Committee again held the respondent guilty of professional misconduct. Substantial Funds Routed Through the Overseas Subsidiary A principal concern was an unsecured loan of ₹567.77 crore advanced to the company’s Dubai subsidiary, Shree Ashtavinayak Cinevision FZE. This represented approximately 62% of the parent company’s balance-sheet size as at 31 March 2013. In the subsidiary’s accounts, the borrowing represented approximately 99.71% of its balance-sheet size. About 93% of those borrowed funds had been deployed as capital advances to third parties for acquiring motion-picture copyrights relating to films yet to be released. The Committee noted consecutive losses and the absence of meaningful revenue-generating activity. Substantial advances, delayed projects and financial distress therefore required careful examination of whether the amounts continued to represent recoverable assets. The concern extended to long-standing advances to actors and other persons. The findings referred, among other instances, to ₹2.75 crore advanced to Shri Akshay Khanna through Shri Neeraj Vohra, which remained outstanding in 2013. Auditor Relied on Procedures and Subsidiary Audit Reports The respondent maintained that he had undertaken several audit procedures, including enquiries with the Board and Audit Committee, obtaining confirmations, examining supporting documents, reviewing internal audit reports and considering reports of subsidiary auditors. He relied on SA 240 and SA 600, emphasising that a statutory audit provides reasonable assurance and that an auditor does not possess the investigative powers of government agencies. Subsequent discovery of fraud, he argued, could not by itself establish professional negligence. The Committee, however, held that performing certain procedures did not establish their sufficiency. Their adequacy had to be assessed against the materiality of the exposure, prolonged outstanding balances, delayed projects and adverse financial circumstances. Emphasis of Matter Did Not Discharge the Audit Obligation The auditor’s report for FY 2012-13 contained an Emphasis of Matter drawing attention to the company’s financial difficulties, inability to repay loans and statutory dues, recovery proceedings and deployment of funds in delayed film projects. It also referred to management’s expectation that the difficulties were temporary and that recoverable amounts exceeded liabilities. The audit opinion remained unqualified concerning that matter. The Committee held that highlighting financial distress did not discharge the auditor’s duty to independently examine the underlying advances, their carrying values and recoverability. Management’s optimism required supporting evidence. The auditor had to consider whether an appropriate provision was necessary rather than merely drawing readers’ attention to the company’s explanation. Confirmation of a Balance Did Not Establish Its Recoverability The respondent challenged a statement in the findings that confirmations had not been obtained, pointing to other passages recording his reliance on confirmations and subsidiary auditors’ reports. In the punishment order, the Committee acknowledged that balance confirmations had been obtained from some parties. Nevertheless, it maintained that the material overseas loan required more extensive verification. The Committee distinguished acknowledgment of an outstanding balance from evidence that the debtor could repay it. Reliance on management representations and the subsidiary’s audited accounts was insufficient, in the circumstances, to establish the loan’s financial viability and recoverability. It also referred to reporting under Clause 4(iii)(b) of CARO 2003, concerning whether loan terms were prima facie prejudicial to the company’s interests. Given the magnitude of the unsecured exposure and the subsidiary’s financial condition, the Committee required independent assessment supported by sufficient appropriate evidence under SA 500. Finding and Punishment The Committee clarified that its conclusion rested on warning signs available during the relevant period, including substantial advances, delayed projects, financial distress and limited subsidiary activity. It held the respondent guilty under Clause (7) of Part I of the Second Schedule to the Chartered Accountants Act, 1949. The punishment imposed was removal from the Register of Members for six months and a fine of ₹5 lakh, payable within sixty days of receipt of the order. Author’s Comments A debtor may confirm every rupee outstanding and still be unable to repay it. Confirmation supports the existence of a balance; recoverability requires examination of financial capacity, underlying assets, project progress and realistic sources of repayment. The order also illustrates the limits of an Emphasis of Matter. Drawing attention to financial difficulties cannot resolve an underlying deficiency in evidence supporting asset values. At the same time, audit responsibility must be assessed from information available when the report was issued. Later business failure or discovery of fraud cannot automatically establish negligence. The Committee expressly based its conclusion on contemporaneous adverse indicators. For practitioners, the essential lesson is to document why material loans and advances remain recoverable, particularly where funds pass through subsidiaries into delayed projects. Management assurances and another auditor’s report require critical evaluation in light of the evidence available. Cases Discussed Tri-Sure India Ltd.

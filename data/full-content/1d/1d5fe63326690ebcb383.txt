Marak Vs ITO (ITAT Guwahati) Bank Account Outside Scheduled Area Does Not Defeat Section 10(26) Exemption Where Business Income Accrues Within It The legal principle The Guwahati Tribunal held that operating a bank account outside the specified area does not, by itself, defeat exemption under section 10(26) where the eligible assessee’s business is carried on and the income accrues within that area. For AY 2015–16, the Tribunal allowed exemption for the assessed coal-business income, rejecting the Department’s reliance on banking transactions conducted in Guwahati. For AYs 2016–17 and 2017–18, the assessee succeeded on a separate jurisdictional ground: reopening beyond three years had been approved by the PCIT instead of the authority required under the applicable section 151(ii). Business in Meghalaya, banking in Guwahati The assessee carried on the business of trading in Meghalaya coal. He claimed that he resided and conducted business within the area covered by section 10(26). His bank account, however, was maintained with Axis Bank, Beltola Branch, Guwahati, Assam. Substantial credits and debits in that account led the Department to initiate reassessment. For AY 2015–16, the Assessing Officer estimated business income at approximately ₹1.55 crore, applying an 8% rate to the bank credits. Exemption under section 10(26) was denied because the deposits and withdrawals occurred outside the specified area. The CIT(A) sustained the denial. The central dispute was therefore whether the location of banking transactions established that the business income accrued outside Meghalaya. Why the bank account was maintained outside Meghalaya The assessee explained that inadequate banking facilities and difficulty obtaining cash for business needs had prompted him to open the account in Guwahati. He furnished registration documents and returns relating to his Meghalaya business, together with details of parties whose payments were credited to the account. His case was that the credits represented coal-sale receipts and the withdrawals funded coal purchases and other business expenditure. He also explained the practical geographical convenience of banking in Guwahati rather than Shillong. These explanations supported his position, but the decisive point was the place and source of accrual of business income, rather than convenience alone. Receipt location did not determine business accrual The Tribunal found that the lower authorities themselves had recognised that the assessee carried on the coal business in Meghalaya. The business documents supported operations within the specified area. There was no dispute concerning the assessee’s eligibility apart from the Department’s objection that income was received and the bank account operated outside that area. The Tribunal held that banking outside the specified area was not a condition disqualifying the assessee from exemption. The relevant criterion was where the income accrued. Relying on ITO v. N. Takin Roy Rymbai [1976] 103 ITR 82 (SC), it concluded that the assessed business income qualified for exemption because the business was carried on within the specified area. AY 2015–16: limitation objection rejected The assessee also challenged reopening for AY 2015–16, relying on the Revenue’s concession reproduced in Union of India v. Rajeev Bansal and its application in Deepak Steel & Power Ltd. v. CBDT. The original notice had been issued on 23 April 2021. The assessee argued that notices for AY 2015–16 issued after 1 April 2021 had to be dropped. The Tribunal rejected this contention by reading the cited concession alongside the Supreme Court’s discussion concerning the ₹50 lakh threshold and surviving limitation under the earlier regime. Since the alleged escaped income exceeded ₹50 lakh, the Tribunal held that the limitation argument did not invalidate reopening in this case. Accordingly, the AY 2015–16 appeal was partly allowed: the legal challenge failed, but exemption was granted on merits. Later years: approval from the wrong authority For AYs 2016–17 and 2017–18, the notices dated 30 June 2022 recorded approval from PCIT, Shillong. The Tribunal held that, since reopening was beyond three years, approval should have been obtained from the authority contemplated by the then-applicable section 151(ii). It identified the required authority as the PCCIT and relied on Mrs. Chitra Supekar v. ITO [2023] 149 taxmann.com 26 (Bombay). The additional jurisdictional grounds were allowed, and both appeals succeeded. One sentence in the order inconsistently refers to approval from the PCCIT; the surrounding discussion clearly identifies PCIT approval as the defect. Author’s comments A bank branch is a place of collection and payment; it does not automatically become the place where business income accrues. The Department’s approach confused the movement of money with the source of income. The decision nevertheless does not grant exemption merely because an assessee resides in a specified area. The income’s connection with business operations there remained essential and was supported by the documents accepted in this case. The different outcomes across the years are equally instructive. AY 2015–16 succeeded on exemption; the later years succeeded on defective sanction. The order should not be presented as allowing every jurisdictional objection or as independently deciding the later years’ exemption claims on merits. Cases Discussed Union of India & Ors. v. Before the introduction of GST, there were two indirect taxes namely Value Added (VAT) and Central Sales Tax (CST). VAT is a state tax and one is required to get registration under state VAT for selling goods within the same state. CST is a central tax and one has to get registration under both VAT and CST if they are selling goods from one state to another. CST is an origin-based tax i.e., tax is required to be paid in the state from where goods are sold. In the present case, the assessee has taken registration under Meghalaya VAT as well as CST vide registration number 17121312085 and 17121312279 respectively. Registration Certificates are attached and marked as Annexure B. The assessee sells goods from the state of Meghalaya to the local people of Meghalaya as well as to the people located in Assam. Since the goods are sold from Meghalaya to Assam, the assessee is correctly paying CST Tax and filing CST Return. Hence, all the sales of the assessee are from Meghalaya Only. The assessee has sold goods to buyers in Assam from his place of Business in Meghalaya. And there is NIL Sales that arise in the state of Assam. 3.8. Copy of CST Return for the year under consideration is attached for your honours reference and marked as Annexure G. 3.9. Suspicion is the mother of enquiry but it cannot take the place of proof. In the present case, the addition made is based on suspicion and nothing but suspicion. During the whole period of assessment, The Income Tax Officer had not brought any evidence or material on record which suggest that the assessee is having Place of Business outside the state of Meghalaya. This finding is thus purely an assumption based on conjecture made by the officer. 3.10. Suspicion, howsoever strong, cannot take place of proof and there can be no addition on the basis of mere suspicion. Reference, in this regard, may be made to the following decisions: – Lalchand Bhagat Ambica Ram v CIT [1959] 37 ITR 288 (SC); CITv.Paras Cotton Co. [2008] 170 Taxman 431/ [2007]288 ITR 211 (Raj.); Faqir Chand Chaman Lal v. Asstt. CIT [2004] 1 SOT 914 (Asr. Trib.) [Appeal dismissed by P&H High Court in 262 ITR 295 and SLP dismissed by SC in 268 ITR 215 (St)]; Assam Tea Co. v. ITO [2005] 92 ITD 85 (Asr. -Trib.) (SB); Jhantala Investments Ltd. v. Asstt. CIT [2000] 73 ITD 123 (Mum. -Trib.) Thus, the order passed by The Income Tax Officer is without any basis and merely based on assumption. 4.

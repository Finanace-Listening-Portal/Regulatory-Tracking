Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); Manish Tuteja Vs PCIT (ITAT Delhi) Section 263 Revision Fails Where AO Adopted a Plausible View on Interest Under Section 28 of the Land Acquisition Act Summary: The Income Tax Appellate Tribunal, Delhi, in Manish Tuteja Vs Principal Commissioner of Income Tax, Rohtak, ITA No. 1569/Del/2023, for Assessment Year 2018-19, considered whether the Principal Commissioner of Income Tax could exercise revisional jurisdiction under Section 263 of the Income-tax Act, 1961, when the Assessing Officer had accepted the assessee’s claim that interest awarded under Section 28 of the Land Acquisition Act, 1894 formed part of exempt enhanced compensation. The dispute arose after the assessee received enhanced compensation aggregating to ₹3,35,75,304, including ₹1,94,53,200 awarded as interest under Section 28. The assessee claimed exemption under Section 10(37), and the assessment was completed on 12 January 2021 accepting the returned income of ₹22,05,013. The PCIT subsequently revised the assessment by an order dated 27 March 2023, considering the interest taxable under the head “Income from other sources” in light of the amendments introduced by the Finance (No. 2) Act, 2009 and the Punjab and Haryana High Court’s decision in Mahender Pal Narang v. CBDT. The appeal produced a difference of opinion between the two Members of the Division Bench. The Judicial Member upheld the revisional order, giving significance to the amended statutory provisions and the jurisdictional High Court’s interpretation. The Accountant Member disagreed, taking the view that the assessment order should not be disturbed under Section 263. The difference was referred under Section 255(4) to Vice President Shri Mahavir Singh as Third Member. The Third Member concurred with the Accountant Member, and the appeal was ultimately allowed in accordance with the majority opinion. The dispute therefore involved both the substantive character of the interest receipt and the independent jurisdictional question of whether an assessment accepting a legally plausible interpretation could be revised. On the substantive issue, the majority examined the Supreme Court’s decision in CIT v. Ghanshyam (HUF), 315 ITR 1, which distinguishes interest awarded under Section 28 of the Land Acquisition Act from interest payable under Section 34. Interest under Section 28 represents an accretion to the value of the acquired property and forms part of enhanced compensation, whereas Section 34 interest compensates for delay in paying compensation already determined. This distinction was material because the assessee’s claim under Section 10(37) depended upon the character of the receipt as compensation rather than independent interest income. The majority also considered CIT v. Govindbhai Mamaiya, Union of India v. Hari Singh, and the Gujarat High Court’s decision in Movaliya Bhikhubhai Balabhai v. ITO, 388 ITR 343. The Tribunal examined the effect of Sections 56(2)(viii), 57(iv) and the then Section 145A(b), introduced or amended with effect from 1 April 2010, together with CBDT Circular No. 5/2010 dated 3 June 2010. According to the majority reasoning, these provisions addressed the timing of taxation of interest, particularly the hardship caused by accrual-based taxation following Rama Bai v. CIT, 181 ITR 400. They did not necessarily change the underlying character of an amount already forming part of enhanced compensation. The majority considered that characterisation of the receipt must precede application of the provisions governing interest income. At the same time, the contrary Punjab and Haryana High Court approach, including Mahender Pal Narang v. CBDT, remained a significant part of the judicial controversy. Independently of the substantive taxability question, the Third Member concluded that revision under Section 263 could not be sustained where the legal issue was highly debatable and the Assessing Officer had adopted a plausible view. Applying the principles associated with Malabar Industrial Co. Ltd. v. CIT and CIT v. Max India Ltd., the majority reasoned that an assessment could not be treated as erroneous and prejudicial to the interests of the Revenue merely because the PCIT preferred a different legal interpretation. Both statutory conditions for invoking Section 263 had to be satisfied. Consequently, the Third Member agreed with the Accountant Member, and the regular Bench allowed the assessee’s appeal in accordance with the majority opinion. The ruling is significant for two distinct reasons. First, it reinforces the importance of identifying whether an amount awarded under Section 28 of the Land Acquisition Act is an integral part of enhanced compensation, rather than treating every amount labelled “interest” as income from other sources. Secondly, it emphasises the limitations on revisional jurisdiction where the assessment rests on a plausible interpretation of a contested legal issue. The majority opinion must nevertheless be read in the context of the conflicting High Court decisions, the applicable judicial hierarchy and the specific facts of the assessment. It should not be understood as overriding a binding jurisdictional High Court judgment or as resolving every dispute concerning the taxation of land acquisition interest. The controversy The Delhi Tribunal, following a Third Member reference, allowed the assessee’s appeal against revision under Section 263 concerning the treatment of interest received under Section 28 of the Land Acquisition Act, 1894. The central dispute was whether such interest retained the character of enhanced compensation or became taxable as “Income from other sources” after the amendments introduced by the Finance (No. 2) Act, 2009. The majority accepted the assessee’s position concerning the character of the receipt. It also held that, even assuming the Revenue’s interpretation was possible, a highly debatable issue could not justify revision where the Assessing Officer had adopted a plausible view. Enhanced compensation included ₹1.95 crore interest The assessee filed his return declaring income of ₹22,05,013. During the relevant year, he received enhanced compensation aggregating to ₹3,35,75,304, including ₹1,94,53,200 awarded as interest under Section 28 of the Land Acquisition Act. The receipt was claimed as exempt under Section 10(37). The case was selected for scrutiny concerning income from other sources and the refund claim. The Assessing Officer completed the assessment on 12 January 2021, accepting the returned income. Subsequently, the PCIT invoked Section 263 and passed an order dated 27 March 2023 treating the assessment as erroneous and prejudicial to the interests of the Revenue. Division Bench differed on validity of revision The Judicial Member upheld the PCIT’s order, placing significance on the jurisdictional Punjab and Haryana High Court’s decision in Mahender Pal Narang v. CBDT, concerning taxation of interest on compensation under the amended provisions. The Accountant Member disagreed and supported the assessment order. The difference was referred under Section 255(4) to Vice President Shri Mahavir Singh as Third Member. The Third Member concurred with the Accountant Member. The regular Bench consequently allowed the assessee’s appeal in accordance with the majority opinion. Section 28 interest distinguished from Section 34 interest The Third Member examined the distinction recognised by the Supreme Court in CIT v. Ghanshyam (HUF), 315 ITR 1. Interest under Section 28 relates to the excess compensation determined by the Court. It represents an accretion to the value of the acquired property and forms part of enhanced compensation. In contrast, Section 34 interest compensates for delay in payment after the compensation has been determined. The majority therefore considered the substance of the receipt decisive. An amount described as “interest” under the acquisition legislation did not necessarily become interest income for income-tax purposes merely because of that description. The 2009 amendments did not change the receipt’s character The Revenue’s position depended upon the amendments to Sections 56(2)(viii), 57(iv) and the then Section 145A(b), effective from 1 April 2010. The Third Member examined the legislative explanation and CBDT Circular No. 5/2010 dated 3 June 2010. According to the majority reasoning, the amendments addressed the hardship arising from taxation of interest on an accrual basis following Rama Bai v. CIT, 181 ITR 400. They provided for taxation in the year of receipt. They did not, in the majority’s view, convert an amount already characterised as compensation into interest. Characterisation had to precede application of the interest-taxation provisions. Where the receipt formed part of compensation governed by Section 45(5), the amendments could not alone recharacterise it as income from other sources. Supreme Court decisions and divergent High Court views examined The majority considered Ghanshyam (HUF), CIT v. Govindbhai Mamaiya and Union of India v. Hari Singh, together with the competing High Court approaches. It preferred the reasoning of the Gujarat High Court in Movaliya Bhikhubhai Balabhai v. ITO, 388 ITR 343, supporting the treatment of Section 28 interest as compensation. The contrary Punjab and Haryana High Court line formed an important part of the disagreement between the Members. The decision must therefore be understood as a majority Tribunal ruling delivered after examining conflicting judicial approaches, rather than as an issue free from controversy. Independently, a debatable issue could not sustain revision The Third Member expressly held that even if the receipt were assumed taxable under income from other sources, revision would still fail because the issue was highly debatable. Reliance was placed on Malabar Industrial Co. Ltd. v. CIT and CIT v. Max India Ltd. The Assessing Officer’s adoption of a plausible legal position could not be displaced under Section 263 merely because the PCIT preferred another interpretation. The assessee’s appeal was accordingly allowed, and the revisional order did not survive. Author’s comments The decision has two distinct dimensions: the character of Section 28 interest and the limits of revisional jurisdiction. For Section 263 disputes, the particularly useful finding is the alternative conclusion that a debatable issue, supported by a plausible assessment view, cannot automatically satisfy the statutory conditions for revision. On substantive taxability, the award and the provision under which the amount was granted remain crucial. Section 28 compensation-related interest and Section 34 delay interest cannot be treated interchangeably. Equally, the majority ruling should not be presented as overriding a High Court decision; its reasoning must be applied with due regard to the relevant judicial hierarchy and the facts of the particular case.

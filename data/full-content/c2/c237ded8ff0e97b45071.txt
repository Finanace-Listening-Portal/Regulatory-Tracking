Summary: Funding a UAE company from India requires careful consideration of whether the investment should be structured as equity, shareholder debt or a combination of both. Equity provides permanent capital, strengthens the UAE company’s balance sheet and avoids mandatory repayment obligations, making it particularly suitable for incorporation expenses, long-term working capital, business expansion and initial operating losses. Shareholder loans, on the other hand, can offer greater repayment flexibility where funding is genuinely temporary. However, the choice has important implications under the Foreign Exchange Management Act (FEMA), the Overseas Investment framework, Indian transfer-pricing provisions and UAE Corporate Tax rules. Under India’s overseas investment framework, an Indian entity may make financial commitment by way of debt to a foreign entity subject to prescribed conditions, including prior Overseas Direct Investment (ODI) and acquisition of control. Such loans must be supported by appropriate agreements and arm’s-length interest terms. Overseas financial commitment is generally subject to a ceiling of 400% of the Indian entity’s net worth, subject to applicable conditions and exceptions, and may include equity, debt and guarantees. The rules applicable to Indian resident individuals differ from those governing Indian companies, making the identity of the investor and the sequence of funding transactions particularly important. It is about funding. Should the UAE company be funded through equity? Should the Indian parent lend money to it? Should the structure use a combination of both? This is not merely an accounting choice. For an Indian investor, the answer can affect FEMA compliance, financial commitment limits, documentation, repatriation, transfer pricing and future flexibility. On the UAE side, the choice can also affect Corporate Tax treatment, interest deductibility, balance-sheet strength and banking. A funding structure that looks simple at incorporation can become inconvenient once the UAE business starts expanding. That is why equity and debt should be compared before funds move, not after the company has already accumulated shareholder balances. Why the Funding Question Matters in Dubai Many Dubai companies are incorporated with relatively modest initial capital. That may be sufficient to obtain the licence. It may not be sufficient to run the business. A trading company may need money for inventory. A consultancy may need salaries and office rent. A warehouse business may require deposits, fit-out and equipment. A regional headquarters may need significant working capital before revenues begin. The amount shown as registered capital on the UAE corporate documents therefore does not necessarily represent the actual amount the business will require. For an Indian promoter, the correct question is: How much will the UAE business realistically need over the next 12 to 24 months, and what portion should be equity versus debt? That decision should be made with both FEMA and UAE tax consequences in mind. Equity Is Usually the Starting Point Where an Indian company establishes a Dubai subsidiary, the initial investment is commonly made through equity. The Indian company subscribes to shares in the UAE entity and becomes its shareholder. From an Indian FEMA perspective, this is generally Overseas Direct Investment. The equity investment establishes the ownership relationship between the Indian company and the UAE subsidiary. It also gives the UAE company permanent capital that does not have to be repaid according to a loan schedule. For a new Dubai business, this can be particularly useful because the company may take time to generate stable cash flow. Equity can therefore provide a stronger initial balance sheet. What Equity Actually Means Commercially When the Indian parent contributes equity, that money belongs to the UAE company. It is not simply a temporary advance. The funds can be used for the UAE company’s legitimate business operations, subject to applicable law and corporate approvals. The Indian shareholder receives ownership rights in return. Unlike a loan, equity does not ordinarily carry a contractual maturity date or mandatory interest payment. That makes equity suitable for: establishment costs; long-term working capital; business development; initial hiring; office and warehouse setup; acquisition of fixed assets; and funding losses during the startup phase. This is why undercapitalising a new Dubai company and trying to fund everything through short-term shareholder advances may not always be the best approach. Why Indian Companies Sometimes Prefer Loans Debt provides a different form of flexibility. Instead of permanently increasing the UAE subsidiary’s share capital, the Indian parent may lend money to the company. The UAE entity receives working capital but also records an obligation to repay the parent. This can be attractive where the funding is intended to be temporary. For example, an Indian parent may know that its Dubai subsidiary needs AED 3 million for inventory but expects to recover the amount after customer collections begin. A properly structured loan may be commercially more appropriate than permanently increasing capital by AED 3 million. However, the word loan should not be used casually. From the Indian FEMA perspective, overseas debt funding by an Indian entity is specifically regulated. A Loan Is Not Simply an International Bank Transfer Under the Overseas Investment framework, an Indian entity may make financial commitment by way of debt to a foreign entity subject to prescribed conditions. Broadly, the Indian entity should already be eligible to make ODI, should have made ODI in the foreign entity and should have acquired control over that foreign entity before making the relevant debt commitment. The loan should also be backed by proper documentation. This matters greatly in Dubai structures. Suppose an Indian company incorporates a UAE subsidiary and immediately sends AED 2 million. If the entire amount is casually recorded as a shareholder loan without considering whether the prescribed ODI and control conditions have first been satisfied, the accounting description in Dubai may not match the FEMA position in India. The sequence of transactions matters. Equity First, Debt Later Is Often the Cleaner Sequence For an Indian corporate group establishing a wholly owned Dubai subsidiary, a common and more defensible sequence is: Indian parent subscribes to UAE equity → ownership and control are established → additional permitted funding is structured as debt if commercially justified. This sequence aligns more naturally with the overseas investment framework. It also creates a clearer distinction between: capital that is permanently committed to the UAE business; and money that is genuinely intended to be repaid. The exact structure should still reflect the commercial facts. There is little benefit in calling funding “debt” if everyone knows the UAE company will never realistically be able to repay it. Indian Entity and Resident Individual Funding Should Not Be Confused This distinction is essential. The debt-funding rules applicable to an Indian company investing in its controlled UAE subsidiary are not automatically available in the same way to an Indian resident individual. Resident individuals make overseas investments under a different part of the Overseas Investment framework and within the applicable LRS framework. Therefore, a founder who personally owns a Dubai company should not assume: “My Indian private limited company could have made a shareholder loan, so I can also lend money personally in exactly the same way.” The investor category must be identified first. This is particularly important where the Dubai company was incorporated personally but the founder later wants the Indian operating company to fund it. Ownership and funding should be aligned before money is remitted. The 400% Financial Commitment Limit Matters for Indian Entities For an Indian entity, overseas financial commitment is generally subject to the prescribed limit linked to net worth. The widely used automatic-route ceiling is generally up to 400% of the Indian entity’s net worth based on the last audited balance sheet, subject to applicable conditions, exclusions and specific regulatory requirements. Importantly, the analysis is wider than equity alone. Financial commitment may include different forms of permitted support to the overseas entity. Therefore, if an Indian company has: equity investment in the Dubai subsidiary; shareholder debt; corporate guarantees; and other permitted commitments, management should examine the aggregate position. A company should not calculate its overseas exposure only by looking at the original share subscription. Equity Can Strengthen the UAE Company’s Balance Sheet From the Dubai company’s perspective, equity can improve financial presentation. A company funded with adequate capital may appear stronger to: banks; landlords; suppliers; auditors; strategic partners; and prospective investors. This can matter in the UAE where banking and commercial counterparties often examine the economic substance of the business rather than merely the value stated on the trade licence. Consider two Dubai subsidiaries. Company A has AED 50,000 share capital and AED 5 million of related-party loans. Company B has AED 3 million equity and AED 2.05 million of related-party debt. Both may have received approximately the same amount of total funding. Their balance sheets tell a very different story. For a long-term regional operating company, stronger equity can sometimes support credibility and borrowing capacity. Debt Can Preserve Capital Flexibility The commercial advantage of debt is that it is ordinarily repayable. If a Dubai subsidiary receives a properly documented shareholder loan and later generates surplus cash, repayment of principal may provide a cleaner way to return that particular funding than reducing share capital. This can be particularly useful for temporary working-capital support. But repayment flexibility should not be confused with tax-free extraction without conditions. The transaction must remain genuine. The loan should have: proper documentation; a commercial amount; repayment terms; an arm’s-length interest rate where required; and accounting treatment consistent with those terms. If money is described as debt but behaves indefinitely like permanent equity, tax authorities or auditors may scrutinise the substance. A Proper Loan Agreement Is Not Optional For an Indian entity making permitted debt investment into its UAE subsidiary, the overseas investment regulations require the loan to be supported by a loan agreement, and the interest rate should be on an arm’s-length basis. This is more than paperwork. The agreement should ordinarily address matters such as: principal amount; currency; interest rate; repayment period; repayment schedule where appropriate; default provisions; permitted use of funds; and governing law. The Dubai accounting team should record the transaction consistently with the executed agreement. An informal Excel entry called “shareholder loan” should not replace a properly documented cross-border financing arrangement. Interest Creates a Transfer-Pricing Question Once an Indian parent lends to its UAE subsidiary, the transaction becomes a cross-border related-party financing arrangement. That immediately raises transfer-pricing considerations. The interest rate cannot simply be selected because it is convenient for one side of the group. The pricing should be commercially defensible. Factors may include: currency of the loan; term; creditworthiness of the UAE company; security; subordination; market borrowing conditions; and the risk assumed by the lender. Both India and the UAE apply arm’s-length principles to related-party transactions. Therefore, the same loan may need to withstand scrutiny in both jurisdictions. UAE Corporate Tax Changes the Debt Calculation Before the introduction of UAE Corporate Tax, some groups treated shareholder loans as though the interest deduction question was largely irrelevant in the UAE. That approach is no longer appropriate. Interest expenditure can affect the taxable income of a UAE company, but deductibility is subject to the UAE Corporate Tax rules. The UAE has a general interest deduction limitation regime. Broadly, net interest expenditure may be restricted by reference to a percentage of adjusted EBITDA once the applicable threshold is exceeded. There are also specific restrictions for certain related-party financing arrangements. Therefore, a group should not assume that every dirham of interest paid by the Dubai subsidiary to its Indian parent automatically creates a full Corporate Tax deduction. The purpose and structure of the borrowing matter. Related-Party Loans Require Genuine Commercial Purpose The UAE Corporate Tax regime contains specific restrictions aimed at related-party financing used for certain transactions. That means a shareholder loan should have a real commercial purpose. For example, an Indian parent lends AED 5 million to its Dubai subsidiary so that the subsidiary can purchase inventory for genuine UAE trading activities. That has a clear operating rationale. Now consider a different arrangement where related-party debt is introduced primarily to generate interest deductions around an otherwise exempt or related-party transaction. The tax analysis becomes more sensitive. Debt should therefore be introduced because the business genuinely needs debt financing, not merely because an interest expense appears tax-efficient. Interest Received in India Creates an Indian Tax Consequence The UAE side is only half of the transaction. If an Indian company lends money to its Dubai subsidiary and receives interest, that interest is income of the Indian lender. Accordingly, the group should model the transaction on both sides. The Dubai company may seek an allowable deduction, while the Indian parent recognises interest income subject to Indian taxation. The economic benefit of debt therefore cannot be assessed solely by looking at the UAE Corporate Tax position. A cross-border loan should be evaluated on an after-tax group basis. Equity Does Not Produce an Interest Deduction Equity funding follows a different economic model. The Dubai company does not generally claim an interest deduction simply because shareholders contributed capital. Returns to shareholders are typically obtained through dividends, appreciation in share value or eventual disposal. This may appear less attractive if management is focused exclusively on current tax deductions. However, equity offers other advantages: no scheduled repayment obligation; stronger capitalisation; reduced cash-flow pressure; fewer debt-servicing concerns; and potentially simpler long-term funding. The correct decision therefore requires more than comparing whether interest is deductible. Dividends and Loan Repayments Are Fundamentally Different This distinction should be clear in every UAE structure. If an Indian parent provides equity, amounts later distributed as shareholder returns may take the form of dividends, subject to corporate law, available profits and applicable tax treatment. If the Indian parent provides debt, repayment of principal is the settlement of a liability rather than a dividend. Interest is a separate financing cost. These payment flows have different accounting and tax consequences. A company should therefore not freely switch labels between dividend, loan repayment and management fee according to whichever treatment appears convenient at the time of payment. The legal documentation should determine what the payment actually represents. A Practical Dubai Example Consider an Indian consumer-products company forming a Dubai subsidiary to distribute products across the UAE and wider GCC. Management estimates that the UAE operation requires AED 6 million during its first 18 months. The expected use of funds is: AED 1 million for establishment and initial operating expenses; AED 3 million for inventory; AED 1 million for employees and marketing; AED 1 million for working-capital buffer. One option is to invest the entire AED 6 million as equity. Another option is to contribute AED 2.5 million equity and provide AED 3.5 million as a permitted shareholder loan. Which is better? There is no universal answer. If the AED 3.5 million is expected to remain permanently in the business, treating it as debt may create unnecessary interest and repayment mechanics. If the amount represents temporary inventory funding expected to be repaid once the UAE operation becomes cash-generative, debt may have a stronger commercial rationale. The funding profile should therefore follow the expected use and duration of the money. Another Example: Dubai Consultancy With Limited Capital Needs Now consider an Indian consulting company establishing a small Dubai subsidiary. The UAE operation requires only AED 400,000 for: licence; office; three employees; marketing; and initial operating costs. The business is not capital intensive. In this situation, creating an elaborate debt structure may provide little commercial benefit. Adequate equity funding may be simpler. This illustrates an important principle: Sophisticated financing is not automatically better financing. The funding structure should match the scale of the business. A Third Example: Warehouse and Distribution Business The answer may be different for a Dubai logistics operation. Suppose the UAE subsidiary needs AED 15 million for: warehouse deposits; equipment; vehicles; inventory; and working capital. Part of this capital may be permanent. Part may be temporary. A blended structure may therefore make sense. For example: Long-term operating base → equity Temporary working capital → debt The precise proportions would depend on the financial model, FEMA limits, commercial requirements and UAE tax analysis. The point is that equity versus debt need not be an all-or-nothing decision. Do Not Use Loans to Repair an Undercapitalised Structure One recurring mistake is incorporating the Dubai company with minimal capital because the licence allows it and then funding every subsequent expense as a shareholder loan. That may be legally possible in some structures, but it should not become the default without analysis. If the UAE company is expected to: hire significantly; hold inventory; lease a large facility; seek bank finance; or operate as a regional headquarters, management should consider whether the company’s equity base reasonably reflects the scale of its business. A company funded almost entirely through related-party debt may also attract questions about the commercial nature of the financing. The nominal minimum capital required for incorporation should not necessarily dictate the economic capital structure. Bank Financing Can Change the Equity–Debt Balance The funding decision should also consider future UAE bank finance. Banks may evaluate: paid-up capital; shareholder support; financial statements; leverage; cash flow; and guarantees. A heavily leveraged subsidiary may not necessarily be attractive to a lender simply because the debt comes from its parent. The bank may ask the Indian parent to: subordinate the shareholder loan; provide additional equity; or issue a corporate guarantee. That guarantee can itself have FEMA implications for the Indian entity. Therefore, the group’s future banking strategy should be considered when determining the initial equity-versus-loan mix. Corporate Guarantees Are Part of the Bigger Funding Picture Suppose an Indian parent funds its Dubai subsidiary with AED 3 million equity and AED 2 million shareholder debt. The UAE company then borrows AED 10 million from a local bank, supported by a guarantee from the Indian parent. The group’s overseas exposure is no longer limited to AED 5 million. The guarantee forms part of the broader financial commitment analysis under the Overseas Investment framework, subject to the applicable rules. This is why management should examine the complete financing package. A decision that appears small when viewed only as an equity subscription can become much larger once debt and guarantees are included. Currency Risk Should Not Be Ignored An Indian company lending to its UAE subsidiary also introduces foreign-exchange exposure. The UAE dirham is pegged to the US dollar, while the Indian parent may report in rupees. A long-term loan can therefore create currency movements in the Indian company’s books. Equity also carries foreign currency exposure, but the accounting and economic consequences may differ. For large UAE investments, treasury considerations should form part of the funding decision. This becomes particularly important where: the investment is significant relative to the Indian parent’s net worth; repayment will occur over several years; or the UAE company generates revenue in currencies other than AED. Tax structuring should not ignore treasury risk. Funding a Free-Zone Company Does Not Change the FEMA Principles The UAE company might be established in: DMCC; JAFZA; Dubai South; Meydan; IFZA; DIFC; or another UAE free zone. The free-zone selection may materially affect UAE licensing, commercial operations and Corporate Tax treatment. But from the Indian investor’s perspective, the funding still has to fit within FEMA. Calling the recipient a “free-zone company” does not create a separate exemption for equity or shareholder loans. The investor, ownership, control, amount, nature of financial commitment and reporting remain important. Future Investors May Prefer a Cleaner Capital Structure A Dubai company may eventually attract an external investor. At that stage, the existing balance sheet becomes important. Suppose the Indian parent has funded the UAE subsidiary almost entirely with shareholder loans. A new investor may ask: Will those loans be repaid before investment? Will they remain outstanding? Will they convert into shares? Will the new investor rank behind the parent debt? How is interest calculated? Could the loans drain future cash flow? These are commercial questions, not merely tax questions. A well-designed capital structure can therefore make future fundraising easier. Exit Planning Also Matters Imagine the Indian group eventually sells its Dubai subsidiary. If the UAE company has substantial related-party debt outstanding, the parties must determine how that debt will be treated at completion. The buyer may acquire: the shares only; the shares plus shareholder debt; or the business after debt has first been repaid. The answer affects the transaction value and cash flows. Equity and debt therefore influence not only how a business is funded today but also how it can be sold later. Common Mistake: Calling Every Transfer a Loan In owner-managed Dubai companies, additional payments are often made whenever cash is needed. The accountant may simply record them as “shareholder loan”. This can be dangerous for Indian-funded UAE companies. The correct classification should be established before remittance. The transfer might actually be: additional equity; permitted debt; payment for services; reimbursement; or another transaction. Each has different consequences. Accounting should follow the legal and regulatory character of the payment rather than create it after the event. Common Mistake: No Interest Because the Companies Are Related Another misconception is that a parent and subsidiary can lend to one another on any terms because they belong to the same group. Related-party status does not remove transfer-pricing requirements. Where an Indian entity makes a loan to its controlled UAE subsidiary under the Overseas Investment Regulations, the loan is required to be backed by an agreement and interest must be on an arm’s-length basis. The UAE’s related-party rules reinforce the same commercial principle. An interest-free or artificially priced cross-border loan should therefore not be adopted without analysing whether it is legally and commercially defensible. Common Mistake: Choosing Debt Only for a UAE Tax Deduction Tax should influence financing, but it should not be the sole reason for debt. If a business genuinely needs temporary working capital, debt can make excellent commercial sense. If the business needs permanent risk capital, equity may be more appropriate. Using debt solely to generate an interest deduction can create several problems: Indian interest income; UAE transfer-pricing scrutiny; UAE interest-deduction limitations; repayment pressure; and increased leverage. The best funding structure usually remains defensible even before the tax effect is considered. Common Mistake: Ignoring ODI Reporting for Additional Funding A UAE subsidiary may already exist and be properly reported. That does not mean future funding can be sent without further consideration. Additional equity and debt may create separate reporting requirements under the Overseas Investment framework. The designated AD bank should therefore remain involved in significant subsequent financial commitments. A clean reporting history becomes particularly important when the Indian parent later wants to: increase investment; give guarantees; restructure the group; sell the UAE company; or acquire additional overseas businesses. A Practitioner View: Start With the Business Model, Not the Tax Rate When advising on Dubai company funding, the most useful first question is rarely: “Which option saves more tax?” A better set of questions is: How much money does the UAE company actually need? For how long? When is it expected to become cash-generative? Will the funding realistically be repaid? Does the business need a stronger balance sheet for banking or suppliers? Will external investors enter later? Will the company operate only in Dubai or become a wider GCC platform? Once those questions are answered, the appropriate mix becomes clearer. For example, permanent capital required to build a long-term regional headquarters behaves economically like equity even if someone would prefer to call it a loan. Temporary funding for a defined inventory cycle behaves much more naturally like debt. The paperwork should follow the economic reality. A Better Funding Process for India–UAE Structures A disciplined process can avoid most problems. First, prepare the UAE funding forecast. Estimate the realistic cash requirement for at least the first 12 to 24 months. Second, separate permanent capital from temporary funding. Not every dirham needs the same financing instrument. Third, confirm the Indian investor. Is the shareholder an Indian company or a resident individual? Fourth, establish ODI and control. This is particularly important before an Indian entity makes debt financial commitment. Fifth, review financial commitment limits. Include equity, debt and relevant guarantees rather than analysing each item separately. Sixth, determine the equity contribution. Ensure the UAE company begins with a commercially sensible capital base. Seventh, document any shareholder loan properly. The loan terms and interest should be commercially defensible and compliant. Eighth, examine UAE Corporate Tax. Review interest deductibility, related-party rules and transfer pricing. Ninth, model the Indian tax result. Interest received by the Indian parent should be considered in the overall calculation. Tenth, complete the required FEMA reporting. Do not allow the accounting records in India and Dubai to diverge. Equity Versus Loan: The Commercial Crux The distinction can ultimately be expressed quite simply. Equity is suitable for money that is intended to remain at risk in the UAE business. Debt is suitable for money that genuinely creates an obligation to repay. That sounds obvious. In cross-border structures, however, those two concepts are frequently blurred. A founder may call permanent capital a loan because repayment appears easier. A group may contribute equity where temporary debt would provide greater flexibility. The correct decision comes from understanding what the money is actually expected to do. Conclusion Indian businesses expanding into Dubai should treat the equity-versus-loan decision as part of the original UAE structuring exercise. Both can be legitimate funding methods, but they serve different commercial purposes and create different regulatory and tax consequences. Equity provides permanent capital, strengthens the balance sheet and avoids repayment obligations. Debt can provide flexibility and a defined route for repayment, but for an Indian entity it must fit within the Overseas Investment framework, follow the required ODI and control conditions, be properly documented and use arm’s-length terms. On the UAE side, shareholder debt can also affect Corporate Tax through related-party and interest-deduction rules. The strongest structure is therefore rarely the one that maximises either debt or equity. It is the one that correctly matches the nature of the UAE company’s funding requirement. For a Dubai operation expected to become a substantial long-term business, adequate equity usually provides the foundation. For genuine temporary funding requirements, properly structured debt can then provide flexibility. The central principle is straightforward: If the money is genuinely meant to remain in the Dubai business, treat it like long-term capital. If it is genuinely meant to come back, structure and document it as real debt. When the commercial purpose, FEMA records, UAE accounts and tax treatment all align, the funding structure becomes much easier to defend and much easier to manage.

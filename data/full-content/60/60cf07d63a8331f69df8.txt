Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); Piramal Capital & Housing Finance Limited Vs Sunblink Real Estate Private Limited (NCLT Mumbai) Summary : NCLT Mumbai Bench-I approved the ₹143 crore resolution plan submitted by Ashdan Properties Private Limited for Sunblink Real Estate Private Limited on 3 November 2026. The Bench of Prabhat Kumar, Member (Technical), and Sushil Mahadeorao Kochey, Member (Judicial), allowed the resolution professional’s application under Section 30(6) of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016 read with Regulation 39(4) of the CIRP Regulations. The Tribunal held that Section 32A protection could extend only to the rights and interests Sunblink lawfully held in the disputed Worli properties. The Tribunal had admitted Piramal Capital & Housing Finance Limited’s Section 7 petition on 22 January 2025 for a repayment default of ₹845,81,31,074 as of 30 November 2023. Truvisory Insolvency Professionals Private Limited was appointed interim resolution professional. Piramal was the sole secured financial creditor and held 100% of the voting share in the Committee of Creditors (CoC), with an admitted claim of ₹4,018,60,21,110. The CoC considered the terms of the invitation for expressions of interest, the evaluation matrix and the request for resolution plans. Form G was initially issued on 26 March 2025, followed by an addendum and a reissue, and the final list of 23 prospective applicants was circulated on 28 May 2025. Jivanam Developers and Ashdan submitted plans accompanied by earnest money deposits of ₹5 crore. The Tribunal granted seven extensions, citing title and development-rights disputes, pending forfeiture proceedings, due diligence difficulties and plan negotiations. A separate NCLT order directed disclosure of asserted tenancy rights to prospective applicants and verification by the resolution professional. The CoC approved Ashdan’s revised plan dated 13 August 2026 with 100% of the votes on 4 September 2026. The plan earmarks ₹143 crore for the secured financial creditor, representing about 3.56% of its admitted claim. The ₹25 crore cash component consists of ₹5 crore within seven days of approval, subject to adjustment towards unpaid CIRP costs, followed by ₹10 crore within 30 days and ₹10 crore within 90 days. The remaining ₹118 crore is payable through issuance of non-convertible debentures on or before closing. The recorded average fair and liquidation values of the identified securities and financial assets were ₹8.635 crore and ₹5.18 crore, respectively. Ashdan furnished ₹5 crore in performance security on 10 September 2026, to be adjusted against the last instalment. The plan contemplated funding through internal accruals, capital infusion and debt or quasi-equity, with external debt to be raised as required. Payments were independent of the availability of internal accruals. The proposed monitoring committee comprised one voting representative each of the secured financial creditors, Ashdan and the resolution professional or his nominee. The Tribunal recorded that the resolution professional’s pending Section 66 application concerned alleged fraudulent transactions aggregating to ₹1,606.01 crore, identified in the transaction audit of Sunblink and five amalgamated companies. The order separately recorded allegations of irregular payments of ₹111.80 crore connected with tenancy and development rights, which were subject to investigations under the Prevention of Money Laundering Act, 2002 and related proceedings. Clause 6.15.2 provides that recoveries from avoidance proceedings would benefit Sunblink and Ashdan, which would pursue the proceedings at its own cost, with no entitlement for creditors. According to the resolution professional, Rabia Mansion, Marium Lodge and Sea View, their underlying land and the attached development rights were subject to a 9 November 2020 forfeiture order under the SAFEMA and NDPS framework. Piramal’s appeal was adjourned to 19 November 2026 for final hearing. The Special Court had also permitted attachment in the PMLA proceedings on 29 April 2026, subject to the prescribed procedure. Sunblink claimed development rights over the properties. The Tribunal held that approval could transfer only the rights Sunblink had lawfully acquired, subject to their nature, extent and enforceability. Section 32A could neither create nor validate title or development rights that Sunblink lacked. Relying on the resolution professional’s examination and the Form H compliance certificate, the Tribunal found that the plan complied with Section 30(2) and the applicable CIRP Regulations, including the priority for CIRP costs and statutory safeguards for operational and dissenting financial creditors. The order recorded that Ashdan had submitted its eligibility affidavit and undergone independent due diligence under Section 29A. The Tribunal found the plan feasible and viable in view of the funding sources, implementation timelines and supervision arrangements. Applying K. Sashidhar v. Indian Overseas Bank, it confined its review of the unanimously approved plan to statutory compliance. The Tribunal made the reliefs and waivers subject to the governing law and its specific directions. It applied the principle in Ghanashyam Mishra on extinguishment of pre-approval claims outside the approved plan, while reserving the tax department’s power to examine the proposed accounting treatment, carry-forward of losses and unabsorbed depreciation, and minimum alternate tax. GST and VAT authorities could examine eligibility to carry forward input tax credit. Requests for stamp duty, registration fee and other concessions remained subject to the applicable law, and contracts remained subject to third-party consent. The order confined plan reliefs to Sunblink, subject to the specified qualifications concerning its obligations involving subsidiaries, joint ventures and associates. The plan took effect on approval and became binding on Sunblink and the stakeholders specified in the order. The Section 14 moratorium ceased. The Tribunal directed the resolution applicant to obtain required statutory approvals and directed the resolution professional to supervise implementation, report preferably every quarter, send the records and order to IBBI, and furnish certified copies to the CoC and Ashdan. This Tribunal notes that the CIRP of Sunblink Real Estate Private Limited commenced on 22.01.2025, with the initial 180-day period expiring on 21.07.2025. However, the process could not be completed within the prescribed period due to disputes over the title and development rights of the properties, pending SAFEMA proceedings, difficulties in conducting due diligence, and repeated clarifications, negotiations and revisions of the Resolution Plans. Accordingly, this Tribunal granted seven extensions by orders dated 01.09.2025 (90 days), 03.11.2025 (60 days), 06.01.2026 (60 days), 25.02.2026 (60 days), 28.04.2026 (60 days), 17.06.2026 (60 days) and 01.09.2026 (60 days), aggregating to 450 additional days. During the 9th CoC meeting held on 08.12.2025, the CoC noted that two Resolution Plans were still under consideration and that further time was required for evaluation. Subsequently, at the 11th CoC meeting held on 12.03.2026, the CoC observed that both plans required further compliance and clarification, particularly the plan submitted by Ashdan, which contained conditional and contingent payments. The final extension was granted up to 14.10.2026. Thereafter, the revised Resolution Plan dated 13.08.2026 was approved by the CoC on 04.09.2026, and the Resolution Professional filed the Plan approval application before this Tribunal on 21.09.2026. 22. It is pertinent to note that the Transaction Audit Report identified certain fraudulent transactions in the financial dealings of the Corporate Debtor and its five amalgamated companies. Accordingly, the Resolution Professional filed IA No. 1224 of 2026 on 16.03.2026 under Section 66 of the IBC, concerning alleged fraudulent transactions amounting to Rs.13,65,19,62,000/- and Rs.2,40,81,56,000/-, aggregating to Rs.1606,01,18,000/- (Rs.1,606.01 crore). The documents also disclose allegations of irregular payments of Rs.111.80 crore in connection with the acquisition of tenancy and development rights over the properties at Worli, including payments to third parties, alleged fictitious tenants, and transfers through hawala operators, which are subject to separate investigations under PMLA and related proceedings. The application under Section 66 remains pending adjudication before this Tribunal. Further, Clause 6.15.2 of the Resolution Plan provides that any recoveries arising from avoidance proceedings shall be retained for the benefit of the Corporate Debtor and the Resolution Applicant, who shall continue the proceedings at its own cost after approval of the Plan, without any entitlement of the creditors to such recoveries. 23. The Resolution Professional has submitted that the properties forming the subject matter of the proposed redevelopment are subject to proceedings under the Smugglers and Foreign Exchange Manipulators (Forfeiture of Property) Act, 1976 (SAFEMA), and the Narcotic Drugs and Psychotropic Substances Act, 1985. By an order dated 09.11.2020, the Competent Authority and Administrator under SAFEMA/NDPS ordered the forfeiture of the properties known as Rabia Mansion, Marium Lodge and Sea View, together with the underlying lands bearing C.S. Nos. 1 and 1/1 and the development rights attached thereto, in favour of the Central Government, purportedly free from all encumbrances. The said forfeiture order was challenged by Piramal Capital & Housing Finance Limited, now Piramal Finance Limited, before the Appellate Tribunal under SAFEMA, New Delhi, in Appeal No. FPA-ND-331/MUM/2021. The appeal was listed on 13.08.2026, when the matter was adjourned to 19.11.2026 for final hearing. The Resolution Professional has further disclosed that the Directorate of Enforcement initiated proceedings under the Prevention of Money Laundering Act, 2002, and that the Special Court, by its order dated 29.04.2026, permitted attachment of the properties, subject to compliance with the prescribed procedure. Thus, the properties proposed to be developed under the Resolution Plan remain subject to statutory forfeiture and attachment proceedings. The Resolution Professional has also stated that the Corporate Debtor claims development rights over the properties rather than ownership of the underlying land. The outcome of these proceedings is therefore material to the implementation of the Resolution Plan. 24. The Project Land undertaken by the Corporate Debtor became entangled in i proceedings under the Narcotic Drugs and Psychotropic Substances Act, 1985 (“NDPS Act”), the PMLA, and the Fugitive Offenders Act, 2018. A Show Cause Notice was issued in 1999 under Section 68H of the NDPS Act, proposing forfeiture of various properties, including the subject property. Thereafter, by orders passed in 2004 and 2005, it was concluded that Mr. Iqbal Mirchi had no claim over the said property, and that the same belonged to a Trust. However, in 2019, the Narcotic Drugs and Psychotropic Substances authorities (“NDPS Authorities”) revived the investigation and issued notices to the Corporate Debtor and other entities. Subsequently, vide order dated 09″ November 2020, the NDPS Authorities directed the confiscation of various properties, including the Project Land, and their transfer to the Central Government under the Smugglers and Foreign Exchange Manipulators (Forfeiture of Property) Act, 1976 (“SAFEMA Act”). As a result, the Project’s development came to a standstill, leading to significant delays. 25. The SRA has prayed for the transfer of all assets, including the property subject matter of litigation on account of it allegedly being proceeds of crime, to the Resolution Applicant upon approval of the Resolution Plan by the Tribunal. In our considered view, the assets stand transferred to the Resolution Applicant, subject to compliance with the conditions set out in the Resolution Plan. Further, the protection granted under Section 32A of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016, cannot confer upon the Successful Resolution Applicant any ownership, title, interest, or development rights greater than those legally vested in the Corporate Debtor consequent upon the purchase of any such property. The Successful Resolution Applicant shall acquire only such rights and interests as were lawfully acquired by the Corporate Debtor, subject to their nature, extent and enforceability in accordance with the law. Approval of the Resolution Plan shall not, by itself, create or validate any title or right that did not exist in favour of the Corporate Debtor at the time of purchase thereof. 26. Upon consideration of the Resolution Plan, the submissions of the Resolution Professional, and the Compliance Certificate in Form H, this Tribunal finds that the Resolution Plan satisfies all the mandatory requirements of Section 30(2) of the Code and Regulations 37 and 38 of the CIRP Regulations. The Form H Compliance Certificate clearly demonstrates that the Plan provides for payment of CIRP costs in priority, ensures minimum statutory protection to operational creditors, makes adequate provision for dissenting financial creditors, and does not contravene any provision of law for the time being in force. The Resolution Plan submitted by the Ashdan Properties Private Limited provides for a total resolution consideration of Rs.143 crore to the secured financial creditor, comprising Rs.25 crore by way of monetary payment and Rs.118 crore through the issuance of non-convertible debentures (‘NCD’). This amount is described in the Resolution Plan as the Total Secured Financial Creditor Upfront Payment Amount and forms the principal financial consideration payable for the resolution of the Corporate Debtor. 27. This Tribunal further finds that the Plan provides a viable and feasible framework for the revival of the Corporate Debtor, with clearly identified sources of funds, defined implementation timelines, and a robust mechanism for management and supervision post-approval. The commercial terms of the Plan, having been approved unanimously by the Committee of Creditors with a 100% voting share, fall squarely within the commercial wisdom of the CoC and do not warrant judicial interference in the absence of any material irregularity or illegality. 28. Accordingly, this Tribunal is satisfied that the Resolution Plan complies with the provisions of the Code and the CIRP Regulations, is fair and equitable to all stakeholders, and achieves the objective of maximising value and reviving the Corporate Debtor as a going concern. The Plan therefore merits approval under Section 31 of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016. 29. In K Sashidhar v. Indian Overseas Bank & Others (in Civil Appeal No. 10673/2018, decided on 05.02.2019), the Hon’ble Apex Court held that if the CoC had approved the Resolution Plan by the requisite percentage of voting share, then, as per Section 30(6) of the Code, it is imperative for the Resolution Professional to submit the same to the Adjudicating Authority (NCLT). On receipt of such a proposal, the Adjudicating Authority is required to satisfy itself that the Resolution Plan, as approved by the CoC, meets the requirements specified in Section 30(2). The Hon’ble Apex Court further observed that the role of the NCLT is ‘no more and no less’. The Hon’ble Apex Court further held that the discretion of the Adjudicating Authority is circumscribed by Section 31 and is limited to scrutiny of the Resolution Plan “as approved” by the requisite percentage of voting share of financial creditors. Even in that enquiry, the grounds on which the Adjudicating Authority can reject the Resolution Plan are limited to matters specified in Section 30(2) when the Resolution Plan does not conform to the stated requirements. 31.

Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); 1Pay Mobileware Private Limited Vs Union of India & Others (Bombay High Court) Summary: The Bombay High Court allowed 1Pay Mobileware Private Limited’s challenge to provisional attachment of its current and RBI-mandated escrow bank accounts under Section 83 of the CGST Act. The company was authorised by RBI to operate as a payment aggregator and provided payment services to private merchants and several government, public-sector and statutory entities. Its accounts were frozen through attachment action initiated in August 2026. Subsequent orders permitted limited settlements to identified entities while continuing the remaining restrictions. The petitioner contended that the statutory conditions for attachment were absent, the orders were vague, and the freeze had paralysed its business and third-party settlements. DGGI alleged that the petitioner might have played a role beyond payment aggregation in an organised online money gaming syndicate. It relied on locked business premises, an allegedly empty server, non-production of records, non-compliance with summons and approximately 88 allegedly non-existent or non-functional merchants identified through GSTR-1 data. The reproduced departmental order referred to preliminary GST evasion potentially exceeding Rs. 1,000 crore. The respondents argued that attachment could extend to a person covered by Section 122(1A), that proceedings under Section 67 had commenced, and that reasons recorded in the official file and subsequently explained in the reply and objection orders supported the attachment. The petitioner disputed the jurisdictional foundation and relied principally on Radha Krishan Industries v. State of Himachal Pradesh. The Court found that the attachment orders contained vague assertions without identifying any GST liability of the petitioner. They did not disclose specific transactions, the identities of the allegedly offending merchants, the manner of GST evasion or the petitioner’s role in it. Nor did the orders or material establish that the petitioner was a taxable person or a person specified in Section 122(1A) for the purpose of the attachment. The Court noted that the GSTR-1 entries relied upon were merely commission invoices and that the petitioner, which serviced several government entities, was not a “fly by night operator”. It also held that merchant funds in the escrow account were impressed with a trust, did not belong to the petitioner and could not be attached under Section 83. In this context, it referred to Zhudao Infotech Private Limited v. Principal Additional Director General. Applying Radha Krishan Industries, the Court emphasised that provisional attachment requires tangible material demonstrating its necessity to protect government revenue, with a live link between the material and that necessity. A prima facie suspicion or alleged non-cooperation could not replace those statutory conditions. The Court also discussed Armour Security’s interpretation of “proceedings” in the context of Section 6(2)(b), noting the similar expression used in Section 83. It ultimately held that the attachment reflected non-application of mind and a complete absence of cogent and credible material. The petition was allowed in terms of prayers (a) and (g), setting aside the challenged provisional attachments and the partial defreezing orders to the extent that they continued the attachment. Before we proceed to analyse the submissions it would be apposite to refer to some of the relevant provisions of the CGST Act. Section 83 of the CGST Act provides for provisional attachment to protect revenue in certain cases. The provision is extracted for facility of convenience reading thus :- “83. Provisional attachment to protect revenue in certain cases. – (1) Where, after the initiation of any proceeding under Chapter XII, Chapter XIV or Chapter XV, the Commissioner is of the opinion that for the purpose of protecting the interest of the Government revenue it is necessary so to do, he may, by order in writing, attach provisionally, any property, including bank account, belonging to the taxable person or any person specified in sub-section (1A) of Section 122, in such manner as may be prescribed. (2) Every such provisional attachment shall cease to have effect after the expiry of a period of one year from the date of the order made under sub-section (1).” 18. Rule 159 of the CGST Rules providing for provisional attachment of property reads thus :- “Rule 159. Provisional attachment of property. – (1) Where the Commissioner decides to attach any property, including bank account in accordance with the provisions of section 83, he shall pass an order in FORM GST DRC-22 to that effect mentioning therein, the details of property which is attached. (2) The Commissioner shall send a copy of the order of attachment [in FORM GST DRC-22] to the concerned Revenue Authority or Transport Authority or any such Authority to place encumbrance on the said movable or immovable property, which shall be removed only on the written instructions from the Commissioner to that effect. (3) Where the property attached is of perishable or hazardous nature, [and if the person, whose property has been attached] pays an amount equivalent to the market price of such property or the amount that is or may become payable [by such person], whichever is lower, then such property shall be released forthwith, by an order in FORM GST DRC-23, on proof of payment. (4) Where [such person] fails to pay the amount referred to in sub-rule (3) in respect of the said property of perishable or hazardous nature, the Commissioner may dispose of such property and the amount realized thereby shall be adjusted against the tax, interest, penalty, fee or any other amount payable [such person]. (5) Any person whose property is attached may [file an objection in FORM GST DRC-22A] to the effect that the property attached was or is Not liable to attachment, and the Commissioner may, after affording an opportunity of being heard to the person filing the objection, release the said property by an order in FORM GST DRC- 23. (6) The Commissioner may, upon being satisfied that the property was, or is No longer liable for attachment, release such property by issuing an order in FORM GST DRC- 23.” 19. Section 70 of the CGST Act is a power to summon persons to give evidence and produce documents. Section 70 of the CGST Act reads thus :- “Section 70. Power to summon persons to give evidence and produce documents.- (1) The proper officer under this Act shall have power to summon any person whose attendance he considers necessary either to give evidence or to produce a document or any other thing in any inquiry in the same manner, as provided in the case of a civil court under the provisions of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 (5 of 1908). (1-A) All persons summoned under sub-section (1) shall be bound to attend, either in person or by an authorised representative, as such officer may direct, and the person so appearing shall state the truth during examination or make statements or produce such documents and other things as may be required. (2) Every such inquiry referred to in sub-section (1) shall be deemed to be a “judicial proceedings” within the meaning of Section 193 and Section 228 (45 of 1860) of the Indian Penal Code.” 20. Discussions I have carefully examined the written submissions made by the taxpayer in response to the aforesaid FORM GST DRC-22 dated 10.08.2026, the emails dated 18.08.2026 and letter dated 24.08.2026, the submissions made during the personal hearing held on 28.08.2026, as well as the material available on record in the ongoing investigation. Upon such examination, the following facts emerge : 5.1 Intelligence developed by the officers during the course of investigation has revealed that M/s. 1Pay Mobileware Private Limited is one of the key masterminds involved in orchestrating a large-scale financial transaction network. The investigation has further revealed that the syndicate allegedly facilitated the onboarding of several non-existent, fictitious or unauthorised “merchants” onto the platform of the Payment Aggregator. The investigation indicates that these merchants were fictitious entities created solely for the purpose of routing and layering illicit funds allegedly generated through online money gaming activities, in respect of which the applicable GST liability was allegedly not discharged. 5.2 During the course of investigation, it has further been revealed that M/s 1Pay was instrumental in onboarding the aforesaid fictitious entities as merchants without following the due diligence and verification prescribed under the applicable RBI guidelines relating to KYC. M/s 1Pay, being a payment aggregator licensed by the RBI, was responsible for undertaking necessary verification and preventing fraudulent/illegal activities. However, the investigation has observed that M/s 1Pay knowingly onboarded non-existing/dummy merchants for routing funds relating to online gaming activities, thereby causing serious prejudice to the financial and institutional integrity of the system and resulting in substantial loss to the Government exchequer. These findings constitute material circumstances relevant for examining whether the bank accounts through which the aforesaid transactions were routed are required to continue to remain provisionally attached for protecting the interests of Government revenue. 5.3 During the course of investigation, summons were issued to the CEO and Ex-CEO of M/s 1Pay, who were stated to be looking after the day-to-day activities of the company. In these circumstances, the non-cooperation of the taxpayer has resulted in serious impediment to and derailment of the investigation, particularly when the complete merchant-wise, reseller-wise and transaction-wise data necessary for tracing the financial transactions and determining the precise tax liability has not been furnished. 5.8 The taxpayer has relied on the judgement of Hon’ble Supreme Court in the case of M/s Radha Krishan Industries vs the State of Himachal Pradesh. In this regard, I find that the facts of the case of M/s Radha Krishan Industries are different and not relevant to the instant case. In the said case, the attachment ordered vide 28.10.2020 has continued to be in vogue even after notice dated 18.02.2021 was issued to the taxрayer under Section 74 of the CGST Act, 2017. Further, in the said case a provisional attachment order dated 19.01.2019 was earlier issued and withdrawn later on 30.01.2019 and again the attachment order dated 28.10.2020 was issued on the same grounds, without any change of circumstances, which continued to exist even after notice was issued under Section 74 of the CGST Act, 2017. The facts of the said case are distinguishable from the facts of the instant case as provisional attachment was done following the guidelines issued by CBIC and well within the legal frame work of the GST law. In the facts of the instant case, the investigation is still in progress and the judgement relied upon by the taxpayer is not applicable to the facts of the instant issue. 5.9 I therefore find that there exists sufficient material, at this stage of the investigation, to form a prima facie view that the bank accounts of the taxpayer have a direct nexus with the financial transactions under investigation and that continued provisional attachment of the said accounts is necessary to protect the interests of direct Government revenue. 5.10 However, HDFC Bank, vide its email dated 02.09.2026 has informed that Account No. 50200062341241 is a regulated Escrow account maintained as per the RBI’s Payment Aggregator guidelines. The funds lying in the account represent merchant settlement amounts payable to various beneficiaries, including several large PSU/Government-linked merchants. Due to the current freeze, the customer is unable to process time-bound merchant settlements, resulting in operational disruption and potential non-compliance with RBI-prescribed settlement timelines. Further, vide email dated 03.09.2026, HDFC bank has submitted that, due to freezing of the Escrow Account No.50200062341241, several Government Departments and PSUs were unable to receive settlements, resulting in operational disruption and potential customer issues. HDFC Bank requested that settlements in respect of the aforesaid Government/PSU entities be permitted. HDFC Bank has furnished the details of the following Government/PSU entities in this regard : i. Indian Railway Catering and Tourism Corporation; ii. The Institute of Chartered Accountants of India; iii. HDFC Bank Ltd.; iv. Tamil Nadu Power Fin and Infrastructure Development Corporation Ltd.; v. LIC Housing Finance Ltd.; vi. Bharat Petroleum Corporation Ltd.; vii. Rehabilitation Council of India; and viii. Jorhat Municipal Board. 6. In the present case, the investigation has revealed a large-scale financial transaction network involving several alleged fictitious/dummy merchants, routing and layering of funds allegedly generated through online money gaming activities, non-submission of material records and preliminary GST evasion estimated to be more than Rs.1000 crore. Further, the bank accounts under consideration are themselves being used for routing the financial transactions under investigation. The continued non-submission of merchant-wise, reseller-wise and transaction-wise records has prevented the investigation from fully establishing the complete trail and quantum of transactions. Therefore, on the basis of the material presently available on record, I am satisfied that the statutory requirement of forming an opinion that provisional attachment is necessary for protecting the interest of Government revenue is met in the present case. However, in view of the submissions made by HDFC Bank regarding the genuineness of several entities associated with the Escrow Account, as discussed in Para 5.10 above, I find merit in the contention that the transactions pertaining to such entities are genuine and are not liable to remain under attachment. Accordingly, the transactions pertaining to the said entities are ordered to be unfreezed.” Therefore, the attachment of the accounts may continue insofar as it is necessary for protecting the interests of revenue, while genuine settlements to the specifically identified Government/PSU entities may be permitted from the Escrow Account, subject to the conditions specified in the operative portion of this order. A reading of paragraph 2 of the aforesaid order reveals that in response to DRC-22, the taxpayer, vide emails dated 18th August 2026 and letter dated 24th August 2026, requested de-freezing of the aforesaid bank accounts. Accordingly in compliance with the principles of natural justice and the provisions of Rule 159(5) of the CGST Rules, an opportunity of personal hearing was granted on 15th August 2026. Further the order reveals that the personal hearing was conducted on 28th August 2026. The order also records that HDFC Bank vide the email dated 2nd September 2026 informed that the account maintained with them is a regulated escrow account maintained as per RBI’s Payment Aggregator guidelines. The funds lying in the account represent merchant settlement amounts payable to various beneficiaries, including several large PSU/Government-linked merchants. It is then pointed out by HDFC Bank that difficulties were faced as a result of the freeze. 25. Mainly it is the submission of the respondents that since the petitioner is not cooperating with the investigation, and as there is a suspicion about the involvement of the petitioner beyond their role as a payment aggregator in playing a significant role in the alleged evasion of GST by an organised online money gaming syndicate, that the provisional attachment is issued to protect the interests of the revenue. 26. At this juncture it would be pertinent to bear in mind the dictum of the Hon’ble Supreme Court in Radha Krishan Industries (supra). Facts stated briefly indicates that the assessment of GM Powertech was finalised and fake Input Tax Credit (ITC) was passed on to Radha Krishan Industries. Since the goods were sold by Radha Krishan Industries using fake ITC to one Fujikawa Power, the payments due from Fujikawa Power were attached under Section 83 of the CGST Act. In paragraphs 30, 31 and 33, Their Lordships equated the power under Section 83 of the CGST Act with Order XXXVIII Rule 5 of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 and referred to the principles laid down in Raman Tech. & Process Engg. Co. and Anr. vs. Solanki Traders7. The Court also referred to the test of a “fly by night operator”. 27. Their Lordships then in the context of requirement of cogent material observed that in the absence of cogent or credible material for the purpose of provisional attachment, the exercise of power would amount to malice in law. When the opinion is challenged, the officer must be able to demonstrate the material on the basis of which the belief was formed. There must be a live link between the “necessity of attachment” and the “tangible material” on record. It was further held that the opinion must reflect the “necessity for attachment” and not merely a prima facie case on merits. The “necessity for attachment” is to be distinguished from the exercise of power merely because it is “expedient or profitable or practicable to attach”. The test laid down is whether “only” such attachment is necessary to protect the interests of the revenue. Their Lordships then held that proportionality is an ingredient of the exercise of the power of provisional attachment. 28. Learned Senior Advocate for the petitioner placed reliance on the decision of this Court in Nivara Infradevelopers LLP vs. The Union of India and others8 in support of the submission that the material and reasons justifying provisional attachment must be reflected in the attachment order itself. 29. In Armour Security vs. Commissioner9, the Hon’ble Supreme Court has interpreted the phrase “initiated any proceedings” under Section 6(2)(b) of the CGST Act, to mean only those proceedings which are quasi-judicial in nature and where a notice is issued and an order is passed. It is specifically held that a Section 70 summons inquiry is not covered by the word “proceedings”. Section 83 of the CGST Act uses the words “initiation of any proceedings”, which are similar to the words “initiated any proceedings” under Section 6(2)(b) of the CGST Act. 30. We find that the impugned orders contain only vague statements which do not show any GST liability of the petitioner. From the impugned orders and the subsequent partial defreezing orders and the affidavit-in-reply filed by the respondent No.2, we are of the opinion that the entire case relates to money laundering by unnamed third parties and there is not a whisper of any GST liability of the petitioner. The opinion recorded in the impugned order is only the following few vague sentences “the Directorate General of GST Intelligence (DGGI), Hyderabad Zonal Unit, is investigating a case involving an organised online gaming syndicate engaged in large-scale financial transactions through multiple fintech companies and shell entities/merchant establishments, allegedly used for routing and layering funds generated from online gaming activities without payment of the applicable GST. During the course of investigation, M/s. 1Pay Mobileware Private Limited has emerged as one of the entities allegedly involved in routing such funds.” 31. It is therefore apparent that the respondent No.2 has exercised the powers under Section 83 of the CGST Act as there is some suspicion of money laundering. There is hardly any material in the impugned order which reflects the basis on which the opinion is arrived at. Further, the provisions of Section 83 of the CGST Act clearly provide that the attachment can be of any property belonging to the taxable person or any person specified in sub-section (1A) of Section 122. The order does not record nor there is any material to indicate that the petitioner is a taxable person or is a person specified in sub-section (1A) of Section 122, in which case the exercise of the power under Section 83 of the CGST Act is contrary to law. 32. The impugned order of attachment does not mention any details of the specific transactions, the entities involved, the manner of evasion of GST and most importantly the petitioner’s role in such evasion. The allegation appears to be that the evasion of the GST has been done by routing it through a digital platform. Considering that the petitioner is the payment aggregator for several government entities, we are in agreement with the submission of learned Senior Advocate for the petitioner that the petitioner is not some “fly by night operator”. 33. The affidavit-in-reply indicates that some 88 merchants are using the petitioner’s platform and are non-existent and involved in online money gaming. The petitioner’s customers are onboarded only after their KYC is done. The names and identities or the basis details of the transactions of these 88 merchants are not disclosed in any of the orders or even in the affidavit-in-reply. 34. The affidavit-in-reply rely on the statement of outward supplies filed by the petitioner itself when its GSTR-1 on the GST portal reflects the invoice wise details of transactions undertaken by the petitioner. Admittedly these are merely commission invoices. Neither the impugned orders of provisional attachment, nor the partial defreezing orders nor the affidavit-in-reply mentioned the exact illegality suspected to be committed by these 88 unknown merchants pointing out to the involvement of the petitioner’s role in evasion of GST. It is further material to note that so far as the monies in the escrow account are concerned, the same are impressed with a trust and not “property of the petitioner” and hence cannot be attached under Section 83 of the CGST Act. The said monies in the escrow account do not belong to the petitioner. 35. The Delhi High Court in Zhudao Infotech Private Limited vs. Principal Additional Director General and Another10 has held that attachment of bank accounts by DGGI of a payment aggregator, wherein the allegation was that the payment aggregator was “supporting” illegal gambling products and that the merchants had made a web of illegal gaming entities, when the payment aggregator itself is not being held liable to pay on any transaction, its accounts cannot be attached to recover liabilities which may become due in the hands of third parties. 36. We find that the affidavit-in-reply proceeds on the basis of alleged non-cooperation of the petitioner in the investigation. The petitioner has indicated its willing to cooperate in so far as it pertains to 88 entities referred to in the affidavit-in-reply. 37. Further, the orders of provisional attachment cannot be justified through affidavits-in-reply. Neither the impugned orders nor the affidavit-in-reply contain any “opinion”. The same is indicative of only official inquiry. There is no material on record to show that there is any GST liability on the petitioner. 38. It is pertinent to note that learned counsel for the respondents submitted that the Additional Director General has recorded reasons to believe in the file before ordering attachment of bank accounts of the petitioner. The categorical stand has been taken that the reasons are now stated in the reply filed in the writ petition and also in the orders dated 4th September 2026 and 18th September 2026. It is significant to note the relevant observations in the summary of findings in Radha Krishan Industries (supra). Paragraphs 76.4, 76.5, 76.6 and 76.7 needs to be extracted which read thus :- “76.4. The power to order a provisional attachment of the property of the taxable person including a bank account is draconian in nature and the conditions which are prescribed by the statute for a valid exercise of the power must be strictly fulfilled. 76.5. The exercise of the power for ordering a provisional attachment must be preceded by the formation of an opinion by the Commissioner that it is necessary so to do for the purpose of protecting the interest of the government revenue. Before ordering a provisional attachment the Commissioner must form an opinion on the basis of tangible material that the assessee is likely to defeat the demand, if any, and that therefore, it is necessary so to do for the purpose of protecting the interest of the government revenue. 76.6. The expression “necessary so to do for protecting the government revenue” implicates that the interests of the government revenue cannot be protected without ordering a provisional attachment. 76.7. The formation of an opinion by the Commissioner under Section 83(1) must be based on tangible material bearing on the necessity of ordering a provisional attachment for the purpose of protecting the interest the government revenue.” 39. Bearing in mind the aforesaid principles and in the facts of the present case, we are of the opinion that there was a clear non-application of mind by the Joint Commissioner to the provisions of Section 83 of the CGST Act, rendering the provisional attachment illegal. The opinion is based completely on suspicion and there is a complete absence of cogent and credible material for exercising the power under Section 83 of the CGST Act of provisional attachment in the present case. Based on the material on record we have no hesitation in forming an opinion that the test laid down in Radha Krishan Industries (supra) are not at all satisfied in the present case. The writ petition is therefore allowed in terms of prayer clauses (a) and (g). 40. No order as to costs.

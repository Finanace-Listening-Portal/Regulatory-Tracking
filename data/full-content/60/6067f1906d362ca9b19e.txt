Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); Helios Exports Limited Vs PCIT (Supreme Court of India) Summary: The Supreme Court condoned the delay and dismissed the Special Leave Petition, stating, after hearing the learned Additional Solicitor General, that it was not inclined to interfere with the High Court’s impugned order. The supplied Supreme Court order contains no further substantive reasoning. The detailed reasons concerning invalid prior approvals under Section 153D of the Income-tax Act, 1961 appear in the accompanying Bombay High Court judgment. Read HC Judgment in this case: PCIT Vs Citron Infraprojects Limited (Bombay High Court) The High Court considered Revenue appeals against the ITAT’s common order dated 30 April 2024. The Tribunal had found that the Additional Commissioner’s prior approvals under Section 153D suffered from total non-application of mind, and consequently quashed the Section 153A assessment orders on that jurisdictional ground alone. The High Court first condoned filing delays ranging from one to fifty-one days. When asked about appeals involving tax effects below ₹2 crore, Revenue submitted that the accommodation-entry exception under the CBDT circulars applied. The Court proceeded on merits without deciding whether those appeals actually fell within that exception. Revenue contended that the Tribunal had identified discrepancies in only four or five cases but allowed thirty-four assessee appeals and dismissed thirty-one Revenue appeals. It argued that approval within twenty-four hours did not establish non-application of mind because the officers had discussed the matters during the assessment proceedings. Elaborate reasons were unnecessary, the accompanying draft orders had been considered, and the Tribunal had invalidated the assessments on technical grounds without examining their merits. The High Court rejected these submissions: the examples were illustrative and covered several appeals, while the assessee’s charts recording the infirmities had not been controverted by the Departmental representative. The Court noted that proposals were received at 5.02 p.m. and over thirty approvals were granted within minutes, despite the draft assessment orders addressing different facts and issues. More significantly, draft orders accompanying the proposals already contained the dates and numbers of approvals that had yet to be issued. This unexplained inconsistency suggested that approval was treated as a foregone conclusion or a trivial formality. Routine discussions between the officers could not explain these defects or replace a proper application of mind. The identically worded approvals and the contemporaneous circumstances disclosed no meaningful scrutiny. The specific defects included investigation information concerning Platinum Textiles Limited for AYs 2016–17 to 2018–19 and Helios Mercantile Limited for AYs 2017–18 and 2018–19, received at 5.02 p.m. on 26 February 2021, followed by assessment and approval on the same day. The Tribunal also identified invocation of Section 115BBE for AY 2012–13, before its stated effective date, and application of the 60% rate for AYs 2013–14 to 2016–17. It recorded assessments involving an entity not in existence and approvals concerning Helios Exports Limited for AYs 2012–13 and 2013–14, and Shri Vallabh Pittie Industries Ltd for AY 2014–15, although the respective companies had not been incorporated during those years. The supplied judgment separately refers to approvals on 26 February 2024; its differing date references are retained. The High Court explained that prior approval protects both Revenue against omissions in taxing the correct income, person and year, and taxpayers against arbitrary or baseless liability. Although approval need not resemble a reasoned judgment, the order or contemporaneous record must show at least minimal application of mind. The Tribunal’s finding that departmental approval procedures were breached reinforced its conclusion; those procedures were treated as binding instructions traceable to Section 119. The Court relied on the principles discussed in Serajuddin and Co., Shreelekha Damani, Ashish Agarwal and the cited Section 151 sanction cases. It also referred to Shiv Kumar Nayyar, where approval of forty-three cases in one day without independent scrutiny had been held invalid. Without valid prior approval under Section 153D, the Section 153A assessments could not stand; the Tribunal was therefore justified in deciding that jurisdictional issue without examining the additions on merits. Under Section 260A, the High Court could not reassess factual findings absent perversity. The underlying facts were undisputed, and the Tribunal’s inferences were reasonable and legally sound. Finding no substantial question of law, the High Court dismissed all Revenue appeals, allowed the delay-condonation applications and made no order as to costs.

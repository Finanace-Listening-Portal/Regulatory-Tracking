Vs DCIT (ITAT Mumbai) Summary: The Income Tax Appellate Tribunal (ITAT), Mumbai Bench “H”, in Tata Chemicals Ltd. Vs DCIT, considered a wide-ranging income-tax appeal for Assessment Year 2015-16 involving transfer pricing, disallowance of expenditure relating to exempt income, deduction for captive power generation, interest on borrowings used for acquiring subsidiary companies, post-retirement medical benefits, scientific research expenditure and foreign tax credit. The assessee, engaged in manufacturing inorganic chemicals, fertilisers and other products, had declared total income of Rs. 663.53 crore under the normal provisions of the Income-tax Act, 1961. Following scrutiny assessment, transfer pricing proceedings and directions of the Dispute Resolution Panel (DRP), the Assessing Officer determined total income at approximately Rs. 869.36 crore through the final assessment order dated 30 October 2019. The Tribunal examined the disputed additions and deductions separately, considering the assessee’s submissions, the Revenue’s objections, documentary evidence and judicial precedents. By its order dated 4 February 2026, the Tribunal partly allowed the appeal for statistical purposes, granting relief or directing further examination on certain issues while sustaining other disallowances. The first major controversy concerned a transfer pricing adjustment of Rs. 3,62,01,946 arising from the Revenue’s recharacterisation of preference share investments in Bio Energy Venture-1 (Mauritius) Private Limited, an associated enterprise, as loans carrying notional interest. The assessee maintained that the investments represented genuine preference share capital, supported by share certificates, statutory records, financial statements and regulatory compliances. It argued that preference shares carrying a non-cumulative dividend of 5% could not be treated as loans merely because the associated enterprise was loss-making or had not declared dividends. The Transfer Pricing Officer, however, questioned the commercial rationale of investing substantial funds without a realistic expectation of return and benchmarked the deemed interest at LIBOR plus 2%. The Tribunal observed that the assessee had failed to establish a satisfactory distinction between preference shares arising from earlier loan conversions and shares subscribed through fresh capital contributions. Although recognising the legal principles laid down in PCIT Vs Aegis Ltd., it distinguished that judgment on the facts and upheld the recharacterisation and consequent transfer pricing adjustment. Accordingly, the first ground of appeal was dismissed. The second issue related to disallowance under Section 14A read with Rule 8D. Tata Chemicals had earned exempt income of approximately Rs. 100.32 crore and voluntarily disallowed Rs. 9,46,335 towards expenditure attributable to such income. The Assessing Officer rejected the assessee’s allocation methodology and calculated a substantially higher disallowance of Rs. 4,96,40,588, resulting in an additional disallowance of Rs. 4,86,94,253. The assessee contended that it possessed substantial interest-free funds, with average own funds of approximately Rs. 8,833.40 crore against average relevant investments of approximately Rs. 867.15 crore. It also argued that Rule 8D could not be applied mechanically without properly examining its accounts and recording dissatisfaction with the voluntary disallowance. The Tribunal examined the statutory requirements, the source of investments and the principles governing allocation of expenditure to exempt income. It partly allowed the ground, addressing the distinction between interest expenditure and administrative expenses and the need for computation consistent with the applicable legal principles. Another substantial dispute concerned deduction of Rs. 40,04,53,625 under Section 80-IA in respect of captive power generation facilities. The Assessing Officer had questioned the deduction on several grounds, including the valuation of electricity supplied for captive consumption, the treatment of steam generated by the undertaking and the computation of eligible profits. The assessee argued that electricity generated by an eligible industrial undertaking and consumed by its own manufacturing units could qualify for deduction under Section 80-IA, and that the market value of transferred power should be determined in accordance with the applicable statutory provisions and judicial decisions. The proceedings involved examination of electricity tariff benchmarks, captive consumption, the treatment of steam and the principles governing the determination of profits attributable to an eligible power undertaking. The Tribunal considered decisions including CIT Vs Reliance Industries Ltd., PCIT Vs Gujarat Fluorochemicals Ltd. and other authorities dealing with captive power generation and market-value determination. Its analysis addressed the proper method of computing eligible profits rather than treating captive consumption, by itself, as sufficient to reject the deduction. The assessee also challenged disallowance of interest expenditure amounting to Rs. 146.73 crore under Section 36(1)(iii), relating to borrowings utilised for acquiring controlling interests in subsidiary companies. Tata Chemicals contended that the investments were commercially connected with its business and that interest on borrowed capital should remain deductible notwithstanding the use of funds for acquiring shares. It relied on judicial authorities, including DCIT Vs Core Health Care Ltd., to support its position that interest on capital borrowed for business purposes was deductible subject to the statutory requirements. The Revenue maintained that the borrowings were used to acquire capital assets in the form of controlling interests and that the expenditure was capital in character. After examining the purpose of the borrowings, the nature of the investments and the applicable provisions, the Tribunal sustained the disallowance. It also rejected the alternative claim for capitalisation of the interest expenditure in the manner sought by the assessee. The separate ground concerning classification of Rs. 9.55 crore of interest income from intercorporate deposits and staff loans was not pressed and was accordingly dismissed. The remaining expenditure-related issues involved post-retirement medical benefits of Rs. 6.37 crore and weighted deduction for in-house scientific research and development expenditure of Rs. 4,24,13,526 under Section 35(2AB). In relation to post-retirement medical benefits, the assessee argued that the liability arose from contractual obligations towards employees and was supported by actuarial valuation, rather than being a contribution attracting deduction only on actual payment under Section 43B. The Tribunal considered the treatment of the provision and the evidentiary requirements and directed further examination of the claim. On scientific research expenditure, the assessee maintained that its approved research facilities were eligible for deduction and challenged the rejection of expenditure identified in the auditor’s certificates as falling outside Department of Scientific and Industrial Research guidelines. The Tribunal observed that approval of a research facility did not automatically establish the eligibility of every expenditure item claimed. Since the individual expenses required proper examination against the statutory requirements and supporting records, the matter was directed to be reconsidered. These findings underline the importance of documentary substantiation, item-wise verification and compliance with the specific conditions governing employee-benefit provisions and research deductions. The final issue concerned foreign tax credit of Rs. 15,71,220 relating to tax withheld in Kenya on corporate guarantee commission income earned from an overseas associated enterprise. Although the assessee had not claimed the credit in its original return, it raised the claim during assessment proceedings. The Assessing Officer rejected it by relying on Goetze (India) Ltd. Vs CIT, while the DRP directed verification and allowance of credit in accordance with the applicable Double Taxation Avoidance Agreement if the corresponding income had been offered to tax in India. The Tribunal clarified that the restriction recognised in Goetze (India) Ltd. did not curtail appellate powers or relieve the Assessing Officer of the obligation to implement binding DRP directions. However, since the dispute concerned non-implementation of the DRP’s directions rather than a substantive rejection of foreign tax credit, the Tribunal directed the Assessing Officer to examine and dispose of any rectification application filed by the assessee in accordance with law. Overall, the appeal was partly allowed for statistical purposes, with the decision addressing the boundaries of transfer pricing recharacterisation, business expenditure deductions, captive power incentives, research-related claims and procedural compliance in granting foreign tax credit. List of Cases Discussed: 1. PCIT Vs DCM Shriram Ltd. (Delhi High Court); [2025] 170 taxmann.com. Discussed in connection with deduction under Section 80-IA and determination of the appropriate value of power transferred for captive consumption. 2. CIT Vs Jindal Steel & Power Ltd. (Supreme Court); (2024) 460 ITR 162. Considered in relation to captive power generation, electricity valuation and the statutory principles governing deduction under Section 80-IA. 3. SVK Power and Infrastructure Ltd. Vs DCIT (ITAT Visakhapatnam); [2023] 150 taxmann.com 534. Relied upon by the assessee in support of its argument that share application money intended for preference share subscription should not be recharacterised as a loan for imputing notional interest. The Tribunal distinguished the authorities relied upon by the assessee on the facts. 4. CIT Vs Reliance Industries Ltd. (Supreme Court); (2020) 421 ITR 686. Discussed in relation to the valuation of electricity generated by captive power plants and transferred for internal consumption for the purposes of Section 80-IA. 5. PCIT Vs Aegis Ltd. (Bombay High Court); (2019) 102 taxmann.com 495. Distinguished. The Tribunal acknowledged the principle governing the treatment of genuine preference share investments but held that the assessee had failed to establish the true nature and origin of the disputed investment through satisfactory evidence. 6. PCIT Vs Tops Group Electronic Systems Ltd. (Bombay High Court); Income-tax Appeal No. 1721 of 2016. Distinguished because the precedent concerned investment in equity shares, whereas the present dispute involved preference share investments potentially arising from conversion of earlier loans. 7. ACIT Vs Vireet Investment Pvt. Ltd. (ITAT Delhi Special Bench); (2017) 58 ITR (T) 313. Followed for computing administrative expenditure disallowance under Rule 8D(2)(iii). The Tribunal directed consideration of investments that actually yielded exempt income during the relevant previous year. 8. DCIT Vs Core Health Care Ltd. (Supreme Court); (2008) 298 ITR 194. Relied upon by the assessee in support of the deductibility of interest on borrowed capital under Section 36(1)(iii). The Tribunal examined the applicability of this principle to borrowings utilised for acquiring controlling interests in subsidiary companies. 9. Goetze (India) Ltd. Vs CIT (Supreme Court); (2006) 284 ITR 323. Distinguished in the context of foreign tax credit. The Tribunal clarified that the restriction concerning fresh claims before the Assessing Officer does not curtail appellate powers or the obligation to implement DRP directions. 10. Godrej & Boyce Manufacturing Co. Ltd. (Bombay High Court). Discussed in connection with Section 14A and Rule 8D, including the requirement for the Assessing Officer to examine the correctness of the assessee’s expenditure allocation before applying the prescribed disallowance mechanism. 11. PCIT Vs Gujarat Fluorochemicals Ltd. (Gujarat High Court). Discussed in connection with captive power generation and determination of eligible profits under Section 80-IA. 12. CIT Vs Srishti Securities Pvt. Ltd. (Bombay High Court); (2010) 321 ITR 498. Relied upon in arguments concerning the business purpose of investments and deductibility of interest expenditure under Section 36(1)(iii). 13. CIT Vs R. Amritaben Shah (Bombay High Court); (1999) 238 ITR 777. Considered in the context of interest expenditure and the distinction between funds borrowed for business purposes and funds deployed for acquiring investments. 14. Kedarnath Jute Manufacturing Co. Ltd. Vs CIT (Supreme Court); (1971) 82 ITR 363. Discussed in relation to the principles governing statutory deductions and the treatment of claims under income-tax law. 15. Ahmedabad Electricity Co. Ltd. Vs CIT (Supreme Court); (1993) 199 ITR 351. Referred to in the discussion concerning appellate powers and the consideration of legal claims in income-tax proceedings. 16. Addl. CIT Vs Gurjargravures (P.) Ltd. (Supreme Court); (1978) 111 ITR 1. Discussed in relation to the scope of appellate proceedings and consideration of claims not originally accepted during assessment.

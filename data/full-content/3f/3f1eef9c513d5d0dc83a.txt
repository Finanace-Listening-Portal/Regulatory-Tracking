Summary:Access to justice is a constitutional guarantee, and free legal aid plays a pivotal role in ensuring that economic and social disadvantages do not prevent individuals from seeking judicial remedies. Article 39A of the Constitution directs the State to promote equal justice and provide free legal aid, while the Supreme Court has recognised access to justice as a facet of Articles 14 and 21. The Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987 establishes an institutional framework for providing legal services through national, state, district and taluk-level authorities and committees, including committees for the Supreme Court and High Courts. Sections 12 and 13 govern eligibility and entitlement to free legal services, covering specified vulnerable groups and persons satisfying prescribed income criteria. Lok Adalats offer an alternative mechanism for resolving eligible disputes through compromise and settlement, potentially reducing litigation costs and facilitating speedy justice. Landmark decisions, including Anita Kushwaha v. Pushap Sudan, M.H. Hoskot v. State of Maharashtra and Khatri (II) v. State of Bihar, underscore the constitutional importance of access to justice and legal assistance for persons unable to secure representation in criminal proceedings. The article also advocates extending legal aid to remote communities through village-level initiatives, institutional dialogue and communication responsive to local needs. It emphasises that legal aid is not charity but a constitutional responsibility of the State, whose effectiveness must be measured by its ability to reach the poorest and most marginalised sections of society. It is important to keep in mind that legal aid and access to justice must go hand in hand; without legal aid for victims or aggrieved persons, we cannot aim for or achieve universal access to justice. The term “Access to Justice” has come to the limelight as aggrieved and affected parties are becoming more aware of the availability of legal aid services for their cases, despite the fact that they cannot afford legal representation. A related principle is expressed in the Latin legal maxim Ubi jus ibi remedium, which means where there is a right, there is a remedy. The principle was articulated by Lord Holt in Ashby v. White (1703) 92 ER 126. In India, Bhim Singh v. State of Jammu & Kashmir (1985) 4 SCC 677 illustrates the availability of a monetary remedy for violation of fundamental rights. Access to Justice Under the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals Access to justice is one of the priorities of the United Nations 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development. Target 16.3 seeks to promote the rule of law at national and international levels and ensure equal access to justice for all. Legal aid plays a pivotal role as a catalyst in promoting access to justice. From Directive Principles of State Policy to Fundamental Rights Constitutional Mandate Under Article 39A The provisions on legal aid and access to justice can be traced to our Constitution and international instruments to which India is a party. Article 39A provides for equal justice and free legal aid. Its crux is that the State must ensure that opportunities for securing justice are not denied to any citizen by reason of economic or other disabilities. Access to justice opens the door to citizens without discrimination, and free legal aid is an important means of achieving this objective. Legal aid reduces financial barriers and facilitates access to judicial remedies. Eligibility for Free Legal Services Under Sections 12 and 13 Section 12 of the Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987 identifies categories of persons eligible for free legal services, including women and children, members of Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes, persons with disabilities, victims of trafficking or begar, industrial workmen, persons in custody, persons affected by specified disasters, violence or atrocities, and persons meeting prescribed income criteria. Under Section 13, entitlement is subject to the concerned authority being satisfied that the eligible person has a prima facie case to prosecute or defend. The framework helps ensure that legal assistance reaches weaker and marginalised sections of society. Institutional Framework of Legal Services Authorities in India The Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987 provides for authorities and committees at different levels: National Legal Services Authority (NALSA) – Section 3. Supreme Court Legal Services Committee – Section 3A. State Legal Services Authority – Section 6. High Court Legal Services Committee – Section 8A. District Legal Services Authority – Section 9. Taluk Legal Services Committee – Section 11A (also described in local administrative contexts as Mandal-level legal services committees, where applicable). These institutions were established to provide legal services to eligible persons, including those who cannot afford access to justice, and to carry out the other functions assigned under the Act. Role of Lok Adalats in Providing Speedy Justice Lok Adalats as an Alternative Dispute Resolution Mechanism The Lok Adalat is popularly described as the People’s Court and is conducted at regular intervals to facilitate settlement of disputes. In Madhya Pradesh State Legal Services Authority v. Prateek Jain, (2014) 10 SCC 690, the Supreme Court discussed Lok Adalats as an innovative Indian contribution to jurisprudence rooted in Gandhian principles. The Lok Adalat movement in Gujarat dates back to the early 1980s, before the enactment of the Legal Services Authorities Act. Under Sections 19 and 20, ordinary Lok Adalats facilitate compromise or settlement of eligible pending and pre-litigation disputes. These can include property disputes, matrimonial matters capable of settlement, land acquisition cases, recovery of money, bank loan recoveries, labour disputes, compoundable criminal cases involving assault or defamation where legally permissible, and public utility disputes. Ordinary Lok Adalats have no jurisdiction over non-compoundable offences. Permanent Lok Adalats for public utility services operate under a distinct statutory framework in Chapter VI-A. Expanding Legal Aid Through Village-Level Lok Adalats Many people are opting to settle their disputes through Lok Adalat because of its benefits. In our view, access to Lok Adalat can be made even easier by conducting village-level Lok Adalats to settle petty compoundable criminal and civil cases, wherein relevant authorities, with the help of the Sarpanch or a village elder in facilitating outreach, can expedite the process. Any such settlement must remain voluntary and comply with the applicable statutory framework. This can reduce cases in court and help people exercise their right to speedy justice. Access to Justice as a Fundamental Right Under Articles 14 and 21 Supreme Court Recognition of Access to Justice In Anita Kushwaha v. Pushap Sudan, (2016) 8 SCC 509, the Constitution Bench of the Supreme Court of India held that access to justice is a facet of the right to life under Article 21 and also recognised its connection with the guarantee of equality under Article 14. It is also a responsibility of the State under the Directive Principles of State Policy. Paragraph 9 of the judgment traces the historical origins of the concept, including the Magna Carta, which declares: “To no man will we sell, to no one will we deny or delay right to justice.” The Court identified four essentials of access to justice: an effective adjudicatory mechanism, reasonable geographical accessibility, speedy adjudication and affordability. Judicial Evolution of the Right to Free Legal Aid The judgments in M.H. Hoskot v. State of Maharashtra (1978) 3 SCC 544, Hussainara Khatoon v. Home Secretary, State of Bihar (1980) 1 SCC 98 : (1979) 3 SCR 532, Khatri (II) v. State of Bihar (1981) 1 SCC 627 and Suk Das v. Union Territory of Arunachal Pradesh (1986) 2 SCC 401 establish the importance of free legal assistance as an element of fair procedure under Article 21, particularly for accused persons who cannot afford legal representation and whose liberty is at stake. The obligation is not confined to a particular court. Statutory entitlement to legal services in other matters is governed by the applicable legislation. Concluding Remarks: Taking Constitutional Justice to the Last Mile The journey from Article 39A’s promise on paper to Anita Kushwaha’s recognition of access to justice as a facet of Article 21 shows that legal aid in India has travelled far from a constitutional directive to an enforceable guarantee in appropriate circumstances. As the familiar saying “Justice delayed is justice denied” suggests, judges time and again aim to provide speedy justice to the people of India. The right to a speedy criminal trial is recognised under Article 21. On 24 January 2026, Chief Justice of India Surya Kant, while delivering the Fali Nariman Memorial Lecture organised by the Bombay Bar Association, warned that “justice delayed is not justice denied, it is justice destroyed”. Yet a right that exists only in judgments and statute books serves no one; it must reach the undertrial who does not know they can ask for a lawyer, the daily-wage worker who cannot afford one, and the villager who has never seen the inside of a court. As the CJI’s remarks in Indore remind us, that last step is not merely a matter of drafting better law but of institutions willing to listen, in the language of the people they serve, before attempting to resolve their problems. Legal aid, in that sense, is not charity dispensed by the State; it is the State keeping its constitutional word. The measure of our justice system will not lie in how many rights it declares, but in how many of the poorest citizens it actually reaches.

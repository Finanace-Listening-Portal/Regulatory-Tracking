Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); Prasanth Narayanan Vs Canara Bank & Another (Bombay High Court) Introduction The Bombay High Court quashed Canara Bank’s proceedings classifying the account and transactions of Carnival Technopark Private Limited as fraud. The proceedings were founded on alleged non-disclosure of an existing loan when the company obtained another credit facility. The Court held that non-disclosure alone does not establish fraud without intent to deceive. Considering the company’s audited financial disclosures, the bank’s admitted knowledge and its subsequent conduct, the Court found that fraudulent intent had not been established in the circumstances. Background of the Case Prasanth Narayanan, a director of the company’s suspended board, challenged the show-cause notice dated 23 June 2025 and the resulting fraud declaration dated 1 April 2026. In March 2017, Syndicate Bank sanctioned a ₹130 crore Lease Rental Discounting facility to the company. The facility was intended principally to repay an existing ₹115 crore IndusInd Bank loan, with the balance available for other business purposes. The facility was secured through rental receivables, property-related security, pledged shares and guarantees. Syndicate Bank subsequently merged with Canara Bank in April 2020. Before obtaining the Syndicate Bank facility, the company had entered into a ₹75 crore loan arrangement with Reliance Capital Limited (RCL), along with co-borrowers. Canara Bank alleged that this liability had not been disclosed when its predecessor sanctioned the loan. Petitioner’s Arguments The petitioner disputed the allegation of deliberate concealment. He submitted that the RCL borrowing appeared in the company’s audited financial statements, which were regularly furnished to the bank. Particular reliance was placed on Canara Bank’s email dated 16 April 2021, referring to defaults concerning the Reliance loan and seeking details and regularisation. This contradicted the show-cause notice’s assertion that the borrowing came to light only after insolvency proceedings. The petitioner also argued that the facilities had distinct security and repayment arrangements. There was no allegation that the Syndicate Bank loan proceeds had been diverted or misused. Despite knowing about the RCL borrowing, the bank had continued the contractual relationship without invoking the relevant default provisions on that ground. Bank’s Defence Canara Bank maintained that borrowers must make full and accurate disclosure of existing liabilities when seeking financial assistance. It argued that the RCL loan was material to assessing the company’s financial position and repayment capacity. Subsequent disclosure could not cure the original omission, which might have influenced the sanction decision or security requirements. The bank further contended that fraud classification was an administrative and regulatory action, distinct from criminal proceedings. Intent to Deceive Was Essential The Court examined paragraph 6.1(iv) of the RBI Master Directions dated 15 July 2024, concerning cheating through concealment of facts with an intention to deceive. It held that the relevant category required more than concealment: intention to deceive was an essential component. Relying on Harjas Rai Makhija v. Pushparani Jain, the Court emphasised that fraud cannot be inferred merely from non-disclosure without supporting proof of deceptive intent. It also referred to the definitions of fraudulent conduct under Section 25 of the Indian Penal Code, applicable to the relevant period, and Section 17 of the Indian Contract Act, 1872, both of which emphasise intent. Financial Disclosures and Prior Knowledge Were Material The Court found that the bank’s assertion about discovering the RCL loan only after insolvency proceedings was inconsistent with its April 2021 email. The sanction terms required audited financial statements and related documents within seven months of the financial year’s closure. The bank did not allege that these reports had never been supplied or that the RCL loan remained concealed in the audited accounts from 2017 onwards. On these facts, the Court rejected the argument that disclosure at sanction alone mattered and that subsequent disclosure was inconsequential. The conclusion depended on the particular contractual obligations and surrounding circumstances. Contractual Safeguards and Bank’s Conduct The loan agreement permitted the bank to inspect records, verify financial information and appoint independent professionals. It also provided remedies for material false or misleading information, including recall and recovery action. The Court noted that the bank had sufficient means to examine the borrower’s financial position through ordinary due diligence. Despite knowledge of the RCL loan, the bank had not terminated the agreement or taken action on that ground. These circumstances weighed against the allegation of wilful concealment with intent to deceive. Final Decision The High Court allowed the petition and quashed the entire proceedings initiated through the show-cause notice, including the fraud declaration. The judgment underscores that fraud classification under the invoked RBI category requires a supported finding of deceptive intent. An alleged disclosure lapse must be assessed alongside audited records, the lender’s knowledge, contractual safeguards and the parties’ conduct. REASONS AND CONCLUSION 32. We have considered these submissions. The RBI had issued master directions on fraud risk management in commercial banks including the Regional Rural banks and All India Financial Institutions dated 15.07.2024. Paragraph-6.1 of Chapter VI mentions ‘Reporting of Incidents of Fraud to Reserve Bank of India (RBI)’. It is provided that to ensure uniformity and consistency while reporting incidents of fraud to RBI through Fraud Monitoring Returns (FMRs) using online portal, banks shall choose the most appropriate category from any one of the categories provided under that paragraph. Category (iv) refers to ‘cheating by concealment of facts with the intention to deceive any person and cheating by impersonation’. The present case revolves around this clause and, therefore, it has to be seen whether in this case the non concealment of RCL loan by the company to the Syndicate Bank amounted to cheating by concealment of fact with the intention to deceive the Syndicate Bank. Thus, requirement of this particular category is not mere concealment of facts, but it is coupled with the intention to deceive any person. Thus, in this case, it has to be decided whether concealment of RCL loan to the Syndicate Bank was made by the company with the intention to deceive Syndicate Bank. 33. The show-cause notice refers as to why it was issued for declaring the Petitioner’s account as ‘fraud’. The exact statement in the show-cause notice is as follows: “The borrower and its directors Sh. Prashanth Narayanan and Sh. Sony Ravindranath have actively concealed the fact that they have availed an unsecured loan from M/s. Reliance Capital Limited at the time of sanction of loan from our bank. The matter came to light only after the admission of the company petition bearing No.383/2023 before Hon’ble NCLT, Mumbai.” The contention, that matter came to light only after the admission of the company petition in the year 2023 before NCLT, Mumbai is not correct as can be seen from the record. The e-mail dated 16.04.2021 issued by the Respondent No.1-Bank to the company mentioned in paragraph-4 as follows: “4. there are defaults with reliance for unsecured loan, company to provide full details of the loan and also regularise the same.” This shows that the Respondent No.1-Bank was aware of the loan from RCL, at least, in the year 2021 itself. 34. The impugned order dated 01.04.2026 specifically records the company’s contention that, at the time of availing the term loan, the company had provided the financial details to the bank as per the requirement of the bank and the same was provided on yearly basis. On 16.04.2021, in an e-mail addressed to the company, the bank conveyed the observations of the Central Auditor in which one of the observations was regarding the unsecured loans availed by the company and the company was instructed to regularize the same. Having considered the company’s contentions, it was held that, at the time of availing the facilities from the bank, the company and its Directors did not disclose the existence of previously sanctioned loan from RCL as disclosure of such information had a direct bearing on company’s financial position and repayment capacity. Omitting such information amounted to suppression of material fact and vitiated the genuineness of loan application. Such non-disclosure amounted to false and misleading reporting. The company’s reference to the e-mail dated 16.04.2021 did not absolve it from primary failure of disclosure at the time of loan sanction. 35. The sanction letter dated 17.03.2017 issued by the Syndicate Bank to the company has a special paragraph titled as “OTHER TERMS & CONDITIONS”. Clause 13 of that paragraph reads as follows: “13. Audited Financial Statement along with Audit report of the company as well as Due Diligence Certificate to be provided within seven months from the closure of the financial year. Penal interest shall be charged @1%p.a. Thus, it was the duty cast on the company to furnish audited financial statement within seven months from the closure of the financial year. It is not the case of the Respondent No.1- Bank that such audited reports were never supplied by the company to the Respondent No.1-Bank. It is not the case that the loan given by RCL was not mentioned in the audited report of the company throughout the years from 2017 onwards. Shri. Shinde, learned counsel for the Respondent No.1 submitted that the subsequent disclosure would not make any difference as the disclosure before sanction of loan was important, as it could have impacted the decision to grant loan and the bank could have imposed certain other security measures. 36. In this case, we are testing whether said non disclosure was with intent to cheat the bank. If the company had never disclosed this fact and if the Bank was never aware of the fact of sanction of RCL loan, it would have been a different matter. But as stipulated in the sanction letter of the bank itself it was compulsory for the company to have furnished the audited report. There are no allegations that there was concealment in the audited report regarding the RCL loan. The bank never raised the issue of non disclosure of RCL loan at the time of sanctioning there own loan for a long period from the year 2017. In that context, we find force in the reliance of Mr. Joshi on the observations of the Hon’ble Supreme Court in the case of Harjas Rai Makhija. Paragraph-20 of the said judgment is important; which reads thus: “20. We agree that when there is an allegation of fraud by non-disclosure of necessary and relevant facts or concealment of material facts, it must be inquired into. It is only after evidence is led coupled with intent to deceive that a conclusion of fraud could be arrived at. A mere concealment or non-disclosure without intent to deceive or a bald allegation of fraud without proof and intent to deceive would not render a decree obtained by a party as fraudulent. To conclude in a blanket manner that in every case where relevant facts are not disclosed, the decree obtained would be fraudulent, is stretching the principle to a vanishing point.” (emphasis was on “intent to deceive”) 37. As we have discussed, the company was bound to disclose the RCL loan immediately, at least, within seven months from the transaction with the Syndicate Bank, and hence, from the record it does not appear that there was any intent to conceal this fact. We are unable to agree with the submission of Shri. Shinde that concealment at the time of sanctioning the loan only mattered and subsequent disclosure made soon after was inconsequential, in this particular case. In the case of Bhagwani Bai, Madhya Pradesh High Court, in paragraph-11 has observed that, mere non- disclosure of fact, material or not, does not ordinarily amount to misrepresentation unless it was fraudulent. 38. Shri. Joshi relied on the observations of the Hon’ble Supreme Court in the case of Union of India and others Versus N. Murugesan and others4. Paragraph-25 of the said judgment is important; which reads thus: “25. Acquiescence would mean a tacit or passive acceptance. It is implied and reluctant consent to an act. In other words, such an action would qualify a passive assent. Thus, when acquiescence takes place, it presupposes knowledge against a particular act. From the knowledge comes passive acceptance, therefore instead of taking any action against any alleged refusal 4 (2022) 2 Supreme Court Cases 25 to perform the original contract, despite adequate knowledge of its terms, and instead being allowed to continue by consciously ignoring it and thereafter proceeding further, acquiescence does take place. As a consequence, it reintroduces a new implied agreement between the parties. Once such a situation arises, it is not open to the party that acquiesced itself to insist upon the compliance of the original terms. Hence, what is essential, is the conduct of the parties. We only dealt with the distinction involving a mere acquiescence. When acquiescence is followed by delay, it may become laches. Here again, we are inclined to hold that the concept of acquiescence is to be seen on a case-to-case basis.” 39. The clauses in the original agreement between the Syndicate Bank and the company are important. Clause-10 of the said agreement dated 28.03.2017 has a heading “OTHER TERMS AND CONDITIONS”. Sub-clause 10 of Clause-10 reads thus: “10. The Borrower shall submit Due Diligence Report from Company Secretary or Chartered Accountant or Cost Accountant of the borrower Company, on an annual basis.” Sub-clause 13 of Clause-10 reads thus: “13. The Lender reserves the right to periodically inspect their records and books of accounts to ensure the correctness of information furnished by them. Further, the Lender shall have a right to appoint any other independent consultants / agencies / auditors etc. as desired by them at any time during the tenor of the loan, the cost of which shall be borne by the Borrower.” Sub clause 15 of Clause-10 reads thus: “15. Audited Financial Statement along with Audit report of the Borrower as well as Due Diligence Certificate to be provided within seven months from the closure of the financial year. In case of failure to do, penal interest shall be charged @1%p.a.” All these clauses give sufficient control to the lender bank to verify the documents and financial status of the borrower company. It is neither the case in the show-cause notice nor in the final order that such audit reports were never supplied by the company to the bank periodically or that the company prevented the bank from inspecting their reports. Therefore, it is not possible to accept that there was willful concealment with the intent to deceive the bank. Before sanctioning the loan, the bank on its own independently had to show due diligence. ‘Defaults’ are listed in Clause-23 of the said agreement. Sub-clause 10 of Clause-23 is about ‘supply of misleading information’; which reads thus: “10. If any information given by the Borrower in its application for financial assistance or any representation/declaration/warranty under this Term Loan Agreement to the Lender or otherwise is false or misleading in any material respect.” 40. Enter into and upon and take possession of the Leased Units and any future assets comprised in the Indenture of Mortgage and after the taking of such action, the Mortgagor (under the Indenture of Mortgage) shall take no action inconsistent with or prejudicial to the right of the Lender quietly to possess, use and enjoy the same and to receive the income, profits and benefits thereof without interruption or hindrance by the Mortgagor (under the Indenture of Mortgage) or by any person or persons whomsoever, and upon the taking of such action, the Lender shall be freed and discharged from or otherwise by the Mortgagor (under the Indenture or Mortgage) well and sufficiently saved and kept harmless and indemnified of, from and against all former and other estates, titles, claims, demands and encumbrances whatsoever.” 41. The important question is whether such non-disclosure would amount to ‘fraud’ and whether it was made with the intent to deceive the bank, as we have set out in our discussion. 42. From the above discussion, it is clear that the company had to disclose this RCL loan within a short period and not later than seven months from the closure of financial year 2017. Thus, at the cost of repetition, it has to be noted that, it is not the case that they had concealed the said loan even in their financial status or audited report for the financial year on 31.03.2017 onwards. The Lender bank had sufficient powers and safeguards under the said agreement to terminate the agreement immediately and to recover the amount forwarded to the company and to take over the property. In the year 2021, the Respondent No.1-Bank had sent an e-mail indicating that they were aware of the RCL loan. Thus, in spite of the knowledge, the bank chose not to terminate the agreement or take any action. 43. Section 25 of the IPC which was then applicable, defines ‘Fraudulently’ as follows: “25. “Fraudulently” – A person is said to do a thing fraudulently if he does that thing with intent to defraud but not otherwise.” In this definition also, ‘intent’ is an important ingredient. It has to be independently established. 44. Section 17 of the Contract Act, 1872 Section 17 of the Contract Act, 1872 defines ‘Fraud’ as follows: “17. “Fraud” defined – “Fraud” means and includes any of the following acts committed by a party to a contract, or with his connivance, or by his agent, with intent to deceive another party thereto or his agent, or to induce him to enter into the contract : (1) the suggestion, as a fact, of that which is not true, by one who does not believe it to be true; (2) the active concealment of a fact by one having knowledge or belief of the fact; (3) a promise made without any intention of performing it; (4) any other act fitted to deceive; (5) any such act or omission as the law specially declares to be fraudulent. Explanation.-Mere silence as to facts likely to affect the willingness of a person to enter into a contract is not fraud, unless the circumstances of the case are such that, regard being had to them, it is the duty of the person keeping silence to speak, or unless his silence is, in itself, equivalent to speech.” Under this provision also, ‘intent’ is an important consideration. Intent can be gathered from the attending circumstances. 45. The loan taken from RCL was not a secret document. The bank had means of discovering the said contract with ordinary due diligence. In the present case, the bank had all the means to examine the financial status of the borrower company before sanctioning the loan to the company. The bank was required to be given Financial Report of the company every year. The default and its consequences clauses referred to herein above gave sufficient power to the bank to take steps to even takeover the property of the company. The company stood to lose heavily by deliberate concealment of any relevant fact. Therefore, we do not find that there was intent on the part of the company to commit fraud on the bank. 46. As a result of the above discussion, we find merit in the submissions and the contentions raised on behalf of the Petitioner. Hence, we are inclined to allow the petition. 47.

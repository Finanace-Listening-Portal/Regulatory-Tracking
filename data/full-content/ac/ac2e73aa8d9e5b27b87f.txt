Ramalingam & Others Vs Government of Tamil Nadu Represented by Its Secretary & Others (Supreme Court of India) State Cannot Receive Temple Inam Land by Gift: Supreme Court Invalidates Transfer, Enhances Rent and Imposes Costs Issue involved Whether a temporary trustee could gift temple inam land to the State Government for constructing a Taluk Office, and whether registration or the public purpose of the transaction could validate a transfer prohibited by the governing statute. The Court also considered the terms on which the Government could continue occupying the property. Background of the case The dispute concerned 1 acre and 10 cents in Kodumudi Village, Erode District, belonging to Nagapalayam Mariamman-Ponnachiamman Temple. On 30 August 2017, a temporary trustee executed a gift deed transferring the land to the Government of Tamil Nadu for construction of a Taluk Office. The Tahsildar accepted the gift, having issued a certificate identifying the executant as the temple’s trustee for registration purposes. The hereditary trustee challenged the transaction. However, the Madras High Court dismissed the writ petition, reasoning that section 34 of the Tamil Nadu Hindu Religious and Charitable Endowments Act, 1959 did not expressly mention a gift. It also treated the applicability of the Act as a disputed factual question. Devotees of the temple obtained permission to challenge that decision before the Supreme Court. During the appeals, the State acknowledged that the property was inam land granted for the deity’s support and maintenance. It agreed to abandon the gift and occupy the land on lease. A committee fixed rent at ₹13,235 per month. Applicable provision: Section 41, not Section 34 The Supreme Court held that the admitted character of the property determined the applicable statutory provision. Section 34(5) expressly excludes the inams governed by section 41. Consequently, section 41 governed the disputed transfer. Section 41(1) declares specified alienations of religious inam property, including gifts, null and void. Although its proviso permits Government sanction for certain transactions considered necessary or beneficial to the institution, it expressly excludes gifts from that possibility. The Court therefore held that a gift of such inam property is void ab initio and cannot be validated by subsequent sanction. The gift deed dated 30 August 2017 was accordingly invalid. Registration and trustee’s authority The Court held that the deed was also incapable of valid registration under section 22-A of the Registration Act, as applicable in Tamil Nadu. Registration under one statute cannot validate a transaction declared void by the statute governing the property. The Court rejected the suggestion that a temporary trustee enjoyed unrestricted authority to dispose of temple assets. Sections 27 and 28 impose duties of lawful, prudent and beneficial administration upon every trustee. A gratuitous transfer conferred no corresponding benefit on the institution and conflicted with the fiduciary character of the office. State’s duty as custodian The Court emphasised that the State is a custodian and regulator of temple property, not its owner. Administrative convenience or a laudable public purpose could not authorise appropriation of property dedicated to a deity. It criticised the State for defending the gift for years despite its statutory obligation to protect the temple’s assets. The concession came only after the Supreme Court required clarification of the legal position. Continuation of lease and enhanced rent Since a functioning Taluk Office had already been constructed using public funds, the Court declined to annul the lease. Applying the valuation approach discussed in the judgment, it enhanced the rent to ₹31,000 per month with effect from 8 March 2019, when the land was handed over for construction. The Court directed that rent be re-fixed once every three years under section 34-A, without falling below ₹31,000 per month. The temple trust would determine the lease period, renewal and remaining terms in accordance with law, treating the temple’s benefit as paramount. Renewal could not proceed mechanically without fresh determination of fair rent. Decision The Supreme Court allowed the appeals and set aside the High Court’s judgment. It declared the temple’s ownership and the gift deed and its registration legally ineffective. Government occupation was preserved through the lease arrangement. The State was directed to pay rent arrears within three months, after adjusting payments already made, and ₹1 lakh as costs to the temple within eight weeks. Author’s comments The central principle is that public purpose does not override statutory protection of religious endowments. The State cannot use its supervisory position to become the gratuitous beneficiary of property it must protect. The distinction between sections 34 and 41 is decisive. This was temple inam property, and section 41 expressly prevented validation of a gift. The ruling should therefore be cited with that statutory foundation. The Court also separated invalid ownership from permissible occupation. The Taluk Office was allowed to remain under a remunerative lease; the unlawful gift was not regularised. Finally, the rent and costs directions give practical effect to custodial accountability. Protection of temple ownership required restoration of an income stream and recognition of the consequences of the State’s prolonged defence of an unsustainable transfer. List of Cases Discussed 1. Before turning to the rival contentions, it is useful to recall why property belonging to religious institutions is treated differently from ordinary property. Our country’s religious institutions are not merely places of worship, they are, in law and, in fact, repositories of considerable wealth, land, buildings, jewellery and specie, accumulated over centuries through the offerings of ordinary devotees and the endowments of kings and commoners alike, given in the faith that what was dedicated to the deity would remain dedicated to the deity in perpetuity. That wealth, once given, ceases to belong to the donor, to the priest, to the trustee, or to the State; it vests in the deity, and every hand through which it subsequently passes holds it only in trust for the deity and, through the deity, for the community of worshippers it was meant to serve. Every custodian holds it only in trust for the deity and the worshippers it serves. 24. A seven-Judge Bench of this Court in Commissioner, Hindu Religious Endowments, Madras v. Sri Lakshmindra Thirtha Swamiar of Sri Shirur Mutt4, held that while Articles 25 and 26 of the Constitution protect religious belief and practice, they do not immunise the secular and economic administration of religious property from regulation by law. The same view was reaffirmed in A.S. Narayana Deekshitulu v. State of Andhra Pradesh5, where the administration of temple funds and offices was held to be a secular function distinct from the freedom of worship itself. 25. It is against this backdrop that Sections 23, 27, 28, 34, 34-A and 41 of the 1959 Act, extracted above, must be read. The object of the said provisions is to shield temple property from 4 (1954) 1 SCC 412 5(1996) 9 SCC 548 dissipation, whether at the hands of an improvident trustee or, as the facts of this case illustrate, an overreaching arm of the State itself; a custodian does not cease to be bound by the terms of its custodianship merely because it is also the sovereign. FINDINGS A. THE CHARACTER OF THE SUBJECT LAND AND THE VALIDITY OF THE GIFT 28. The character of the subject land in the instant case must first be determined, for the applicability of Section 34 or Section 41 of the 1959 Act turns upon it. The affidavit dated 23.03.2022, extracted in paragraph 9 above, states in express terms that the subject land is an inam land granted to the deity for its support and maintenance under Inam Title Deed No. 388. Section 34(5) of the 1959 Act expressly excludes from the operation of Section 34 “the inams referred to in section 41”. It follows that Section 41 of the 1959 Act, and not Section 34, governs the transaction impugned in these appeals. 29. This is not a distinction without a difference; it carries decisive consequence for a transaction of gift. Section 34(1) of the 1959 Act empowers the Commissioner to sanction “any exchange, sale or mortgage and any lease for a term exceeding five years” as being necessary or beneficial to the institution. Section 41(1) of the 1959 Act, while similarly declaring “any exchange, gift, sale or mortgage and any lease for a term exceeding five years” of an inam property to be null and void, permits the Government to sanction only “any transaction of the nature aforesaid (not being a gift)”. 30. The Legislature has thus consciously withheld from a gift of an inam property, the very possibility of subsequent validation, by the Commissioner or the Government alike. A gift of an inam property dedicated to the support of a deity is, in consequence, void ab initio and no sanction, however solemnly accorded, could have breathed life into it. 31. Tested on this anvil, the Gift Deed dated 30.08.2017, executed by temporary trustee in favour of the Government of Tamil Nadu, is null and void under Section 41(1) of the 1959 Act. It was equally incapable of valid registration, having regard to Section 22-A of the Registration Act, 1908, as applicable to the State of Tamil Nadu, since no sanction “issued by the competent authority as provided under the relevant Act” preceded its registration, and, being a gift of an inam property, no such sanction could at all have been granted. The fact that the document nonetheless came to be registered does not cure this infirmity; registration under one statute cannot validate what another statute, governing the very property in question, declares void. 32. We may add, for completeness, that even upon the alternative hypothesis, contrary to the Government’s own affidavit, that the subject land fell within the general class of properties governed by Section 34 of the 1959 Act rather than Section 41, the outcome would be no different. It is not in dispute that no sanction of the Commissioner, preceded by the publication and invitation of objections contemplated by the first proviso to Section 34(1) of the 1959 Act, and no prior approval of the Government under the second proviso, was obtained before 30.08.2017. The gift on this hypothesis too, would be null and void for want of sanction. 33. We are unable to accept the submission that a temporary trustee’s authority over trust property is unconstrained. Sections 27 and 28 of the 1959 Act oblige every trustee, permanent or temporary, to administer the institution’s properties “as carefully as a man of ordinary prudence would deal with such affairs, funds and properties if they were his own”, and to obey all lawful orders issued under the Act. A gift, being a transfer without consideration, cannot by its very nature be characterised as an act of ordinary prudence in the management of another’s property. It confers no corresponding benefit on the donor institution and does not fit in with the fiduciary character of the office of trustee. A gift of temple property to the Government, being wholly gratuitous, militates against the very object of the supervisory role assigned to the Commissioner under Sections 23 and 34 of the 1959 Act. 34. The submission that the Revenue Department was, in these circumstances, entitled to receive and hold the subject land by way of gift proceeds upon a misconception. The State, acting through the Commissioner and other functionaries under the 1959 Act, is a custodian and regulator of temple properties, not their owner. An acceptance of a gift of temple land by a Revenue functionary, however laudable the public purpose it is intended to serve, offends the object of the 1959 Act. A temple property is held for the deity and not for gratuitous disposal by whoever happens to hold the office of trustee, however well-intentioned. B. CONDUCT OF THE STATE 35. We are constrained to note that the State did not change its stand on its own initiative. The deed was executed and registered on 30.08.2017. In W.P. No. 26913 of 2017, the Assistant Commissioner, Hindu Religious and Charitable Endowments Department, had asserted on affidavit that the property belongs to the deity and that the executant had no authority to convey it. Even so, the Revenue Department mutated the lands in the name of the Taluk Office, reclassified them as “Sarkar Poramboke” and began construction. Before the High Court, the State defended the transaction and succeeded. Even after this Court issued notice on 19.08.2019, the State persisted in that defence. In the counter affidavit dated 23.10.2019, it maintained that the lands had vested absolutely in the Government and would “not belong to any others or any religious institutions”. It reiterated that stand in I.A. No. 14519 of 2021, seeking vacation of the order of status quo. 36. The State abandoned the gift only after this Court, by its order dated 21.02.2022, required the Secretary in charge of Endowments to clarify on affidavit whether a trustee can gift away temple property. It was then that the State accepted that the lands belong to the Temple, that a gift of such lands is prohibited by Section 41 of the 1959 Act, and that the Revenue Department could hold them only as a lessee. A department of the State, which is the statutory custodian of temple properties, ought to have recognised this position at the threshold. It should not have taken a challenge before this Court, and the probing of the issue by this Court, to bring it about. The State’s belated concession, cannot obscure the fact that for over four years the property of the Temple was treated as that of the Government on the strength of a deed which, by the State’s own admission, had no sanction in law. C. DETERMINATION OF FAIR RENT 37. It is noteworthy that the Revenue Department, by using public funds, has already constructed a Taluk Office upon the land which serves the administrative needs of the Taluk. We are, therefore, not inclined to set aside the lease granted in favour of the State Government. However, the issue with regard to the quantum of fair lease rent needs to be dealt with. The Fair Rent Committee, by its decision dated 09.02.2022, fixed the rent at Rs. 13,235/- (Rupees Thirteen Thousand Two Hundred Thirty Five only) per month. The State Government has issued G.O. (MS) No. 200 dated 02.11.2018 under which, lands of religious institutions which are required to be made over to the Government are to be valued at 225% of the guideline value or the market value whichever is higher. The Collector in his communication dated 22.05.2019, while referring to the aforesaid G.O. had proposed that the land belonging to the Trust should be valued in terms of the aforesaid G.O.(MS). We are conscious of the fact that the State Government has constructed the building. However, since the trust has been wrongfully deprived of use of its property, with a view to ascertain the fair rent, we apply the formula evolved by the Fair Rent Committee. 38. The material on record shows that according to Registration Department, in respect of an adjoining developed land, the market value was indicated at Rs. 167/- (Rupees One Hundred Sixty Seven only) per square feet. To ascertain the fair rent, we take the market value of the subject land at Rs. 167/- (Rupees One Hundred Sixty Seven only) per square feet. Applying 225% thereof as per the aforesaid G.O., the value works out to Rs. 375.75 (Rupees Three Hundred Seventy Five and Seventy Five Paise only) per square feet, and for the subject land admeasuring 47,916 square feet, to Rs. 1,80,04,437/- (Rupees One Crore Eighty Lakh Four Thousand Four Hundred Thirty Seven only). As per the formula adopted by the Fair Rent Committee, the value of the building has to be added to this market value. From a perusal of the Fair Rent Fixation Statement, it is evident that the depreciated value of the building is Rs.1,24,33,603/- (Rupees One Crore Twenty Four Lakh Thirty Three Thousand Six Hundred Three only). Thus, the total value comes to Rs.3,04,38,040/- (Rupees Three Crore Four Lakh Thirty Eight Thousand Forty only). As per the formula adopted by the Fair Rent Committee, the rent has to be fixed at the rate of 0.1% of the total value. Thus, the fair rent comes to Rs. 30,438/- (Rupees Thirty Thousand Four Hundred Thirty Eight only). We, therefore, enhance the rent and round it off to Rs. 31,000/- (Rupees Thirty One Thousand only) per month which shall be payable with effect from 08.03.2019, that is, the date on which the subject land was handed over for construction. 39. We make it clear that the continuation of this lease is not to be read as conferring upon the Revenue Department any degree of unilateral control over its terms. The trust, acting through its trustee or, in the absence of a duly constituted Board of Trustees, the Fit Person appointed under the 1959 Act, in consultation with the Committee empanelled for the purpose, shall retain the power to determine, and, as occasion requires, to revise, the terms and conditions of the lease in favour of the Government, including the quantum of rent, on each occasion of renewal, strictly having regard to the prevailing market rental value and the best interests of the institution. An extension or renewal of the lease upon expiry of its current term shall be granted only upon fresh determination of fair rent by the Committee, and not as a matter of course. DIRECTIONS 40. We consider it necessary, in view of what has transpired in this case, where a gift of the temple’s inam property was executed and registered without any authority of law, and was allowed to stand until the State conceded before this Court in 2022, to issue following directions henceforth: (i) the authorities of the State Government and the Hindu Religious and Charitable Endowments Department shall strictly comply with the procedure prescribed under Sections 34, 34-A and 41 of the 1959 Act, and the Rules framed thereunder, before effecting or sanctioning any exchange, sale, mortgage or lease of property belonging to, or given or endowed for the purpose of, any religious institution. (ii) Every decision touching the properties of a religious/charitable institution must be guided solely by the best interest and benefit of the trust, and never by the administrative convenience of the Government. (iii) The State, as custodian, cannot unilaterally usurp, nor permit any of its officers to usurp, the property of the religious/charitable institutions it is charged to protect; a transaction that offends this principle, whatever its label or ostensible public purpose, is liable to be struck down. CONCLUSION 41. In the result, the judgment and order dated 19.03.2019 passed by the High Court is set aside. It is declared and directed that: (i) The subject land bearing Survey Nos. 36/4, 36/5 and 41/2 of Kodumudi Village, Erode District, is an inam property belonging to and vested in the Temple; (ii) The Gift Deed dated 30.08.2017 executed in favour of the Government of Tamil Nadu, is declared null and void under Section 41(1) of the Tamil Nadu Hindu Religious and Charitable Endowments Act, 1959, and its registration, being contrary to Section 22-A of the Registration Act, 1908, as applicable to the State of Tamil Nadu, is of no legal effect; (iii) The lease in favour of the Government shall not be annulled. If the lease has expired, it shall be renewed. In either event, the lease rent shall stand enhanced to Rs. 31,000/- (Rupees Thirty One Thousand only) per month with effect from 08.03.2019, the date on which the subject land was handed over for construction. The rent shall be re-fixed once in three years under Section 34-A of the 1959 Act, but not below that amount. The period of the lease, its renewal and the remaining terms shall be determined by the trust of the Temple, in accordance with the 1959 Act, with the benefit of the Temple as the paramount consideration. (iv) The arrears of rent from 08.03.2019 till date, after adjusting any amount already paid, shall be paid by the State to the Temple within three months from today. (v) The State was under a statutory obligation to act as custodian of the subject land, however, the State not only accepted the gift of temple property but also continued to defend it before the High Court and before this Court, until the Chief Secretary of Endowments was called upon to place his stand on affidavit. Therefore, we deem it appropriate to direct the State to pay costs quantified at Rs.1,00,000/- (Rupees One Lakh only) within a period of eight weeks to the Temple. (vi) The respondent-authorities shall, henceforth, strictly comply with the procedure prescribed under Sections 34, 34-A and 41 of the 1959 Act, as directed in paragraph 40 above. 42. We place on record our appreciation for the able assistance rendered by Mr. Shailesh Madiyal, learned amicus curiae, and by learned counsel for the parties. 43. The appeals are allowed in the above terms. Pending application(s), if any, shall stand disposed of.

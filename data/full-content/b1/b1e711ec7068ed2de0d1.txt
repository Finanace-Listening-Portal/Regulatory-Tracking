Background of the property dispute and original claim of ownership: The Delhi High Court, in Vijay Gupta Vs Gagninder Kr. Gandhi & Ors., delivered a significant judgment on 4 July 2022 concerning the scope of Order VI Rule 17 of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 (CPC), which governs amendment of pleadings in civil proceedings. Justice C. Hari Shankar considered a petition under Article 227 of the Constitution of India challenging an order dated 22 October 2021 passed by the Principal District and Sessions Judge in CS DJ 10306/2016, whereby the plaintiff’s application seeking amendment of his plaint had been dismissed. The underlying dispute concerned property bearing No. A-148, Defence Colony, New Delhi. The petitioner, Vijay Gupta, had purchased the first and second floors and terrace of the property from Sumitra Devi through registered sale deeds dated 11 July 2001 and 7 August 2003. According to the petitioner, Sumitra Devi had also executed an undertaking dated 11 July 2001, agreeing that neither she nor her legal heirs would transfer the ground floor without first offering him an opportunity to purchase it. The undertaking further provided that, if the ground floor was sold to a third party, the petitioner would be entitled to enjoy the rear parking space on the ground floor as its owner. Following Sumitra Devi’s death on 21 April 2012, her legal heirs inherited the ground floor. After other heirs relinquished their interests in favour of Respondent No. 3, he executed a sale deed dated 26 August 2014 transferring the ground floor to Respondent Nos. 1 and 2, including rights relating to the rear parking space. Alleging that this transaction violated the earlier undertaking, the petitioner instituted a civil suit seeking, among other reliefs, a declaration that he was the absolute owner of the rear parking space to the exclusion of the purchasers. The controversy before the High Court arose when the petitioner subsequently sought to modify that declaration from ownership of the parking area to a perpetual right to use and enjoy it. 2. Proposed amendment, objections and rejection by the Trial Court: Respondent Nos. 1 and 2 contested the original suit and disputed the authenticity of the undertaking dated 11 July 2001, describing it as fabricated and forged. They also argued that the undertaking, even if genuine, did not create any ownership rights in the rear parking area. Before tendering his affidavit-in-evidence, the petitioner moved an application under Order VI Rule 17 CPC seeking amendment of prayer clause (a) of the plaint. He explained that the original prayer claiming absolute ownership had resulted from confusion between proprietary rights and the right to enjoy a parking facility. According to the petitioner, the undertaking did not transfer ownership of any portion of the ground floor but conferred a continuing right to use the rear parking space. The proposed amended prayer therefore sought a declaration recognising his absolute right of use and enjoyment of the parking space in perpetuity, excluding Respondent Nos. 1 and 2. He also sought permission under Order VII Rule 14 CPC to place a site plan identifying the parking area on record. The Trial Court rejected the application on 22 October 2021. Referring to decisions including Abdul Rehman Vs Mohd. Ruldu, Pankaja Vs Yellappa, A.K. Gupta & Sons Ltd. Vs Damodar Valley Corporation, N.C. Bansal Vs Uttar Pradesh Financial Corporation and Vaishnavi Sai Shri Mahalaxmi Jagdamba Shikshan Sanstha Vs Purva Vidarbha Mahila Parishad, it concluded that the amendment would fundamentally change the nature of the suit. The Trial Court reasoned that the petitioner was withdrawing his original claim of ownership and substituting a new case based on a perpetual right of use. It also referred to the delay in seeking amendment, the framing of issues, the filing of an affidavit-in-evidence and the interim relief already obtained on the original pleadings. The Trial Court considered these circumstances sufficient to refuse the amendment, prompting the petitioner to approach the Delhi High Court. 3. Rival submissions concerning alteration of relief and commencement of trial: Before the High Court, counsel for the petitioner argued that the proposed amendment did not introduce a new cause of action or change the factual foundation of the suit. Both the original and amended prayers rested exclusively upon the undertaking executed by Sumitra Devi on 11 July 2001. The petitioner maintained that the earlier claim of ownership had arisen from an inadvertent drafting error and that the amendment merely sought to correctly describe the right flowing from the undertaking. The document, its execution, the subsequent transfer of the ground floor and the alleged interference with the petitioner’s parking rights remained unchanged. Consequently, the petitioner submitted that the amendment neither introduced a new factual controversy nor required the defendants to meet an entirely different case. It was further argued that the application had been filed before commencement of trial because the plaintiff’s affidavit-in-evidence had not yet been tendered. Reliance was placed upon Mohinder Kumar Mehra Vs Roop Rani Mehra, A.K. Gupta & Sons Ltd. Vs Damodar Valley Corporation, Sarjit Singh Awla Vs Kuldeep Singh Awla and GCG Transglobal Housing Project Pvt. Ltd. Vs Surakshit Exports Pvt. Ltd. to support a liberal approach towards amendments necessary for determining the real controversy. Respondent Nos. 1 and 2 opposed the petition, asserting that the original suit claimed ownership while the proposed amendment asserted an entirely different right of perpetual use. They relied upon M. Revanna Vs Anjanamma and the Bombay High Court decision in Vaishnavi Sai Shri Mahalaxmi Jagdamba Shikshan Sanstha. The respondents also referred to an earlier suit, CS(OS) 940/2010, instituted by the petitioner against Sumitra Devi, which had been disposed of following a settlement, and alleged suppression of material facts. They maintained that the Trial Court’s discretionary order did not warrant interference under Article 227. The High Court therefore examined the statutory requirements governing amendments, the stage at which trial commences, the meaning of due diligence and the circumstances in which a modification of relief can legitimately be treated as a change in the character of a suit. 4. Interpretation of Order VI Rule 17 CPC and the distinction between mandatory and discretionary amendments: The Delhi High Court undertook an extensive examination of the language and structure of Order VI Rule 17 CPC. The provision empowers courts to permit parties to alter or amend their pleadings at any stage of proceedings on such terms as may be just and requires amendments necessary for determining the real questions in controversy between the parties. The Court analysed the different expressions used in the provision, particularly the permissive word “may” and the mandatory word “shall”. It explained that the opening part confers jurisdiction upon courts to permit amendments, while the subsequent requirement concerning amendments necessary for resolving the actual dispute operates in mandatory terms. The Court identified three questions relevant to the statutory test: what constitutes the controversy between the parties, what are the real questions arising from that controversy, and whether the proposed amendment is necessary for determining those questions. Where an amendment satisfies this test, the Court explained, it ordinarily must be permitted, subject to the statutory proviso. However, the Court cautioned against the logical error of assuming that every amendment not strictly necessary for determining the controversy must automatically be rejected. Order VI Rule 17 does not exhaustively identify every permissible amendment or every ground for refusal. Those principles have been developed through judicial decisions. Referring to Andhra Bank Vs ABN Amro Bank N.V., the High Court observed that delay alone is not necessarily sufficient to reject an amendment. The Court further distinguished between amendments sought before trial and those sought after commencement of trial, noting that the latter are subject to the additional statutory requirement of due diligence. The judgment discussed Chander Kanta Bansal Vs Rajinder Singh Anand and Consolidated Engineering Enterprises Vs Principal Secretary, Irrigation Department while explaining that due diligence requires reasonable prudence and reasonable effort in the circumstances, rather than the performance of every conceivable act. The Court’s analysis emphasised that procedural rules should facilitate adjudication of substantive disputes and should not be applied so restrictively that legitimate claims are defeated by correctable defects in pleadings. 5. Commencement of trial, due diligence and judicial principles governing amendments: The High Court examined when trial can be regarded as having commenced for the purposes of the proviso to Order VI Rule 17. It considered the Supreme Court’s decisions in Mohinder Kumar Mehra Vs Roop Rani Mehra, Vidyabai Vs Padmalatha, Rajkumar Gurawara Vs S.K. Sarwagi & Co. (P) Ltd. and Gurbakhsh Singh Vs Buta Singh. The Court explained that the framing of issues, fixing of a matter for evidence and filing or tendering of examination-in-chief must be considered in light of the procedural stage actually reached. It recognised that amendments sought before commencement of trial receive more liberal consideration because the opposite party generally retains an opportunity to respond to the amended pleading. By contrast, amendments after trial has commenced attract the statutory restriction requiring the applicant to demonstrate that, despite due diligence, the matter could not have been raised earlier. The Court examined a substantial body of precedent governing amendments, including Rajesh Kumar Aggarwal Vs K.K. Modi, Pirgonda Hongonda Patil Vs Kalgonda Shidgonda Patil, B.K. Narayana Pillai Vs Parameswaran Pillai, Ramchandra Sakharam Mahajan Vs Damodar Trimbak Tanksale and Ragu Tilak D. John Vs S. Rayappan. The judgment recognised that amendments are ordinarily permitted when they assist in resolving the real dispute, avoid multiplicity of proceedings and do not unfairly prejudice the opposing party. At the same time, an amendment may legitimately be refused where it fundamentally changes the nature of the action, introduces an entirely new and inconsistent factual foundation, defeats an accrued legal defence or causes prejudice that cannot adequately be compensated. The Court emphasised that the question must be decided by examining the substance of the proposed change rather than merely comparing the language of the original and amended prayers. In the present case, the affidavit-in-evidence had not been tendered, and the Court treated the proposed amendment as one sought before commencement of trial. The more restrictive proviso therefore did not prevent consideration of the application on its merits. 6. Change from ownership to perpetual parking-use rights did not alter the cause of action: Applying the governing principles to the facts, the Delhi High Court held that the Trial Court had incorrectly characterised the proposed amendment as introducing an entirely new case. The central factual foundation of the suit remained the undertaking allegedly executed by Sumitra Devi and the subsequent sale of the ground floor to Respondent Nos. 1 and 2. Before amendment, the petitioner asserted that the undertaking entitled him to ownership of the rear parking space. After amendment, he sought recognition of a perpetual right to use and enjoy that same parking space. The Court observed that the amendment did not introduce a different transaction, a new document, a separate cause of action or an additional factual basis for relief. Rather, it reduced the scope of the declaration sought by the petitioner. The Trial Court would still have to determine whether the undertaking was genuine, whether it was legally enforceable and what rights, if any, it conferred upon the petitioner. The respondents remained free to challenge the authenticity and legal effect of the undertaking. The Court therefore concluded that the change in the form of relief did not alter the nature or character of the suit. It explained that the plaintiff’s underlying case was always that the undertaking restricted the subsequent transfer of the ground floor and conferred rights concerning the rear parking space. Whether those rights amounted to ownership, perpetual use or no enforceable interest at all was a matter for determination during adjudication of the suit. The Court also considered the petitioner’s submission that the right claimed was an easementary right. Referring to Section 4 of the Indian Easements Act, 1882, it clarified that easementary rights are not ownership rights. An easement is a right enjoyed over land belonging to another person for the beneficial enjoyment of land owned or occupied by the claimant. The Court rejected the suggestion that easementary rights could be regarded as a species of ownership. Nevertheless, that distinction did not undermine the proposed amendment, which was independently permissible under Order VI Rule 17. The High Court accordingly held that the Trial Court’s approach was unduly restrictive and inconsistent with the principles governing amendment of pleadings. 7. Final ruling allowing amendment and its significance for civil litigation: The Delhi High Court ultimately allowed the petition and set aside the Trial Court’s order dated 22 October 2021 rejecting the application under Order VI Rule 17 CPC. The Court held that the proposed modification of the prayer clause did not change the nature and character of CS DJ 10306/2016 because both the original claim of ownership and the amended claim of perpetual use arose from the same undertaking and the same underlying transactions. The petitioner was not attempting to introduce an unrelated cause of action or rely upon a new set of facts. Instead, he sought to substitute a narrower form of relief that, according to him, more accurately reflected the rights flowing from the undertaking. The Court explained that procedural legislation should not be interpreted in a manner that defeats substantive rights merely because the original pleading incorrectly described the relief available. It referred to Mahila Ramkali Devi Vs Nandram in recognising that procedural rules should serve the administration of justice rather than operate as obstacles to the determination of legitimate claims. The High Court also made clear that permitting the amendment did not amount to accepting the petitioner’s claim concerning the parking space. The respondents retained the right to contest the undertaking, its authenticity, its enforceability and the legal consequences flowing from it. Those issues would remain for determination by the Trial Court on their merits. In paragraph 56, the High Court expressly quashed and set aside the impugned order and allowed the petitioner’s application for amendment of the plaint. No costs were imposed, and pending applications were disposed of. The decision establishes that a modification of the relief sought, without changing the essential facts or cause of action, does not necessarily amount to altering the nature of a civil suit. It also reinforces the distinction between ownership and easementary rights, the importance of identifying the real controversy between litigants and the liberal approach ordinarily applicable to amendments sought before commencement of trial. The judgment is particularly relevant where a plaintiff seeks to correct an erroneous description of a legal right without introducing new facts, transactions or substantive allegations.

Summary: The Delhi Bench of the GST Appellate Tribunal, in Ram Manohar Meena v. SRH Technologies, held that retrospective cancellation of a supplier’s GST registration does not, by itself, justify denial of input tax credit to a purchaser. The dispute concerned ITC of ₹37,184 for FY 2018–19 on purchases from Maa Gayatri Infotech, whose registration was subsequently cancelled retrospectively from 1 July 2017. The Tribunal emphasised that the Department must examine the genuineness of transactions and the applicable conditions under Section 16 of the CGST Act rather than treating retrospective cancellation as conclusive evidence of ineligibility. The show cause notice did not allege fictitious invoices, non-receipt of goods, supplier non-payment of tax or collusion. The taxpayer produced invoices, GSTR-2A entries, ledger records and bank statements, while the Department had not established a contrary factual case. The Tribunal also relied on Section 75(7), which restricts confirmation of demands on grounds outside the notice, and recognised that the burden of proving ITC eligibility under Section 155 remains with the claimant. Distinguishing Ecom Gill Coffee Trading and Aastha Enterprises, the Tribunal dismissed the departmental appeal and upheld relief to the taxpayer, including refund of deposits in accordance with law. The ruling highlights the importance of transaction-specific scrutiny, properly framed notices and comprehensive purchase documentation. Supplier’s GST Registration Cancelled Retrospectively: Why a Genuine Buyer Should Not Lose ITC Automatically – Delhi GSTAT’s ruling in SRH Technologies—and the records every business should preserve A businessman purchases goods from a supplier whose GST registration is shown as active. He receives the goods, pays through his bank, records the purchase and claims input tax credit. The supplier reports the invoices. Years later, the Department cancels that supplier’s registration with effect from an earlier date. Can that later cancellation, by itself, take away the buyer’s credit? In Ram Manohar Meena v. SRH Technologies, the Delhi Bench of the GST Appellate Tribunal answered that question in the negative. The Tribunal held that retrospective cancellation alone does not make ITC inadmissible on invoices issued while the supplier was registered. The Department must examine whether the transaction was genuine and whether the statutory conditions were satisfied; it cannot substitute a cancellation entry for that examination. The important words are “by itself.” The ruling protects a genuine transaction from automatic disallowance. It does not protect fictitious purchases, non-receipt of goods, collusion or a claim otherwise contrary to Section 16. The Tribunal expressly preserved the Department’s power to investigate those matters and issue a notice containing the actual allegations. Table of Contents▸ 1. The Dispute: ₹37,184 of ITC and Several Years of Proceedings Particulars Details Case Ram Manohar Meena v. SRH Technologies Forum GSTAT, State Bench Delhi, Court No. II Appeal number APL/59/DEL/2026 Date of pronouncement 9 October 2026 Members Arun Kumar Singal, Judicial Member, and Sanjeev Kumar Jain, Technical Member Financial year 2018–19 Disputed ITC ₹37,184 Penalty imposed in adjudication ₹10,000 Result Departmental appeal dismissed; taxpayer’s relief confirmed; deposits directed to be refunded in accordance with law Although the cause title names an individual officer as appellant, the reasoned order identifies the appeal as a departmental appeal filed by the Assistant Commissioner, Dwarka Division, under Section 112(3). SRH Technologies, a proprietary concern trading in computer systems and peripherals, purchased laptops from Maa Gayatri Infotech under five invoices issued between 22 September and 4 October 2018. The invoice values recorded in the order total ₹2,43,750. The supplier’s registration was subsequently cancelled retrospectively from 1 July 2017. The Department treated that cancellation as sufficient reason to deny the buyer’s credit. That was the central defect in the proceeding. 2. What the Notice Alleged—and What It Did Not The show cause notice dated 9 November 2023 was issued under Section 73 following scrutiny of returns. It proposed recovery because the supplier’s registration had been cancelled retrospectively from the date of registration. The Tribunal recorded that the notice did not allege: that the laptops had not been received; that the invoices were fabricated; that the supplier had failed to pay the tax; or that the buyer had participated in a fraudulent arrangement. This distinction was decisive. A notice saying “your supplier’s registration was cancelled” is not the same as a notice saying “you did not receive these goods” or “the tax on these supplies was not paid.” Each allegation raises a different factual question and requires a different answer. The adjudicating authority nevertheless confirmed the demand, relying on Section 16(2)(a) and paragraph 7 of Annexure B to CBIC Instruction No. 02/2022-GST dated 22 March 2022. The first appellate authority set aside the demand. The Department then carried the matter to the Tribunal. 3. Registration on the Invoice Date: The Tribunal’s Reading of Section 16(2)(a) Section 16(2)(a), as considered in the order, required possession of a tax invoice issued by a supplier registered under the Act. The Tribunal read that requirement with reference to the date on which the invoice was issued. On the five invoice dates, the supplier held a registration granted by the Department, and the portal showed a registered taxable person. The buyer was dealing with that disclosed position. The Tribunal did not question the power under Section 29(2) to cancel registration retrospectively. It rejected the further assumption that exercising that power necessarily wipes out every intervening invoice and every recipient’s credit. Its reasoning was that Section 29 does not expressly impose such an automatic consequence on third-party purchasers. This is an important distinction between two separate proceedings: Proceedings concerning the supplier’s entitlement to retain its registration. Proceedings concerning the recipient’s entitlement to credit on identified purchases. The first may provide a reason to examine the second. The Tribunal held that it does not, without more, decide the second. 4. Do Not Confuse the Cancellation Order Date with Its Effective Date The supplier’s cancellation was effective from 1 July 2017. The purchases took place in September and October 2018. Therefore, the purchases were not before the effective date of cancellation. They were, however, made before the later cancellation was ordered. The Tribunal expressly corrected the first appellate authority’s inaccurate statement on this point. For a taxpayer preparing a reply, the chronology should distinguish: Date Why it matters Date registration was granted Establishes the original registration history Invoice and supply dates Establishes when the transaction occurred Date cancellation was ordered Establishes when the subsequent departmental action occurred Retrospective effective date Identifies how far back the cancellation purports to operate A reply should not merely state, “The purchases were made before cancellation,” without explaining which date is intended. In this case, that loose expression would conceal the very issue the Tribunal decided. 5. A Scrutiny Instruction Cannot Replace a Statutory Enquiry Paragraph 7 of Annexure B to Instruction No. 02/2022-GST states that the recipient is not entitled to ITC on invoices issued after the effective date of retrospective cancellation. It directs scrutiny officers to verify such claims. The wording is therefore a real compliance concern; it cannot simply be ignored. The Tribunal nevertheless held that the instruction cannot add a disqualification absent from Section 16. It treated the cancellation entry as a scrutiny trigger, not as conclusive proof that the underlying credit was inadmissible. Its observation deserves attention: “A flag raised in scrutiny is the beginning of an enquiry into whether the supply was real and the tax was paid; it is not the conclusion of one.” That sentence explains the practical significance of the ruling. A departmental system may identify a risky supplier. The next step is verification of the recipient’s transactions—not automatic conversion of the risk indicator into a demand. The Tribunal did not purport to strike down the instruction generally. It rejected its use as an independent basis for disallowing this recipient’s credit. 6. Why the Taxpayer’s Documents Mattered SRH Technologies did not succeed merely by claiming to be an innocent buyer. It placed transaction records before the authorities. The Tribunal referred to: five purchase invoices identifying the laptops by model and serial number; invoice entries in GSTR-2A; the supplier’s ledger account; bank statements showing transfers to the supplier; and portal records concerning the supplier’s return filings. The Department had not questioned those documents. The supplier’s September and October 2018 GSTR-3B returns were also recorded as having been filed. At the Tribunal hearing, the Department’s representative admitted that the supplier had deposited the tax, although he expressed uncertainty about its correspondence with the respondent’s credit. The Tribunal found that uncertainty insufficient to sustain the demand on the record before it. For businesses, the lesson is practical: the legal argument became effective because the purchases were supported by identifiable records. The Evidence Should Explain the Transaction, Not Merely Fill a File A useful purchase record should allow an officer or appellate authority to follow this sequence: Purchase order → supplier invoice → dispatch or delivery → receipt in stock → payment → subsequent sale, consumption or business use. That is a recommended evidence arrangement, particularly where the Department questions actual receipt. It is not a claim that every document in that sequence is mandatory for every type of supply. 7. GSTR-2A and Bank Payment Are Helpful—but Neither Proves Everything The Tribunal expressly accepted that payment by the buyer to the supplier is not the same as payment by the supplier to the Government. It observed that a conclusion resting on the bank statement alone would not have been sufficient. The records serve different purposes: Record What it helps establish What it does not establish by itself Purchase invoice Particulars of the supply and tax charged Actual delivery GSTR-2A entry Supplier’s reporting of the invoice Conclusive proof of receipt or complete tax discharge Bank payment Payment to the supplier Supplier’s payment to Government Stock or receipt record Entry of goods into the business Supplier’s tax compliance Return-filing status Filing history Invoice-specific satisfaction of every ITC condition This distinction follows the Tribunal’s separate examination of invoice validity, receipt of goods and tax payment. Its decision was based on the combined record, the allegations actually made and the departmental admission—not on a rule that GSTR-2A appearance alone guarantees credit. 8. The Department Could Not Repair the Notice with a New Doubt in Appeal The original demand rested on retrospective cancellation. In appeal, the Department questioned whether tax payment had been adequately verified. The Tribunal invoked Section 75(7), which prevents confirmation on grounds other than those specified in the notice. It rejected an attempt to sustain a cancellation-based demand on the different suggestion that the supplier might not have paid tax, particularly when that suggestion was expressed as uncertainty rather than supported by evidence. This gives taxpayers an important drafting point. A reply should identify precisely: the ground stated in the notice; the documents relied upon; the statutory condition said to have failed; and any new ground introduced during adjudication or appeal. However, this procedural protection should not be mistaken for an exemption from maintaining proof. The taxpayer should preserve the relevant records even where the notice is narrowly framed. 9. The Burden of Proving ITC Was Not Removed The Department relied on State of Karnataka v. Ecom Gill Coffee Trading Private Limited and Aastha Enterprises v. State of Bihar. The Tribunal did not discard the principle that the claimant must establish eligibility. It expressly recognised the burden under Section 155. It distinguished Ecom Gill because genuineness and movement of goods had been put in issue there, whereas the notice against SRH Technologies rested only on cancellation. It distinguished Aastha Enterprises as involving supplier non-payment, unlike the finding in the present record. Accordingly, this decision should not be described as holding that invoices and bank payments are always enough. Nor should it be described as relieving buyers from proving receipt when receipt is specifically disputed. The better reading is narrower: the Department must state its case, and the taxpayer must meet that case with evidence. 10. The First Appellate Authority’s Section 76 Reasoning Needs Caution The first appellate authority had referred to Section 76 and the supplier’s liability where tax is collected but not paid. The Department disputed the suggestion that this prevented proceedings against a recipient. The Tribunal’s final relief should not be converted into a universal rule that the Department must always exhaust recovery against the supplier before examining the buyer’s ITC. Paragraph 15 expressly recognises that a recipient may face a properly framed notice where material shows non-receipt, non-payment of tax or participation in fraud. A taxpayer’s stronger argument is therefore transaction-specific: “The stated ground of retrospective cancellation does not establish failure of the applicable ITC conditions, and the enclosed records substantiate these purchases.” That is more defensible than saying, “Any default is the supplier’s problem and can never affect us.” 11. Apply the Law of the Relevant Financial Year The disputed credit belonged to FY 2018–19. The Tribunal noted that clauses (aa) and (ba) of Section 16(2), considered by the first appellate authority, were inserted later and did not govern that year. This matters in old-year proceedings. Current portal controls and later statutory conditions should not be treated as though they governed every earlier purchase. The reverse is equally important: a decision concerning 2018–19 does not dispense with conditions applicable to later periods. The correct defence begins with the law governing the year under dispute, not with whichever version of Section 16 is easiest to find. 12. What Taxpayers and Business Owners Should Do Now The following are practical risk-control measures drawn from the documentary issues in this case—not a list of additional statutory conditions. At Vendor Onboarding Verify the GSTIN, legal name and business particulars. Save a dated portal-status record. Check that the invoice and payment instructions correspond to the supplier. Investigate unexplained third-party bank accounts or inconsistent business details. At Receipt of Goods Preserve delivery acknowledgments, goods-receipt notes and transport documents where relevant. Record model, serial or batch numbers where commercially appropriate. Link the purchase to stock entries and subsequent sale or consumption. During Return Reconciliation Match invoice details with the relevant portal statement. Follow up on missing or incorrect reporting. Retain correspondence and reconciliations rather than relying on oral assurances. If Retrospective Cancellation Is Discovered Establish the invoice dates, cancellation-order date and effective date separately. Seek the cancellation order and the material relied upon. Preserve historical records; a current portal status does not by itself explain the position when the purchase occurred. Prepare an invoice-wise reconciliation of receipt, payment and ITC. These measures respond directly to the evidentiary strengths and gaps examined in the Tribunal’s order. 13. A Focused Paragraph for a Taxpayer’s Reply The following can be adapted only after verifying the client’s facts: “The proposed disallowance proceeds solely on the retrospective cancellation of the supplier’s registration. At the time of the purchases, the supplier’s registration was shown as active. The enclosed invoice-wise statement and supporting records establish the purchases, receipt of goods, accounting and payment. The notice does not identify any specific non-receipt, fictitious invoice or material establishing non-payment of tax in respect of these supplies. In Ram Manohar Meena v. SRH Technologies, APL/59/DEL/2026, pronounced on 9 October 2026, the Delhi GSTAT held that retrospective cancellation does not, by itself, render such credit inadmissible. It is requested that the transaction records be examined and that any contrary material relied upon be supplied before an adverse conclusion is reached.” The proposition attributed to the Tribunal is contained in paragraph 15; the remaining factual statements must be supported by the particular taxpayer’s records. 14. A Small Demand Can Still Cause Substantial Hardship The disputed ITC was ₹37,184 and the penalty ₹10,000. The Tribunal noted its discretion under Section 112(2) concerning low-value appeals, but nevertheless heard the Department’s appeal on merits because it was pressed as a recurring question of principle. It ultimately criticised the avoidable litigation and the harassment caused to the taxpayer. It did not award costs, but expected more careful scrutiny before such appeals were filed. The ruling should therefore not be reported as saying that all appeals below ₹50,000 are prohibited. The Tribunal exercised its jurisdiction and decided this appeal; its criticism concerned the merits and the decision to prolong the dispute. 15. Relief After Success Must Be Followed Through The Tribunal dismissed the departmental appeal, confirmed the first appellate order and held that the respondent was not liable for the disputed ITC, interest or penalty. It also directed refund, in accordance with law, of amounts deposited against the demand, including the first-appeal pre-deposit. For the business owner, the practical work does not end with obtaining the order. A useful follow-up file should contain: the appellate and Tribunal orders; challans and ledger-debit particulars; identification of the nature of each deposit; refund correspondence and acknowledgments; and reconciliation of the outstanding demand shown in departmental records. The operative order grants refund relief, but it does not quantify the deposit or adjudicate a separate rate of refund interest. Those matters should not be presented as findings of this case. Conclusion The Delhi GSTAT drew a necessary line between suspicion and proof. Retrospective cancellation may justify asking questions about a supplier’s transactions. It does not automatically answer those questions against every buyer. For a genuine business, the strongest protection remains a clear transaction record. Registration verification, invoice reporting, delivery evidence, stock accounting and bank payment should support one consistent account of what was purchased and how it entered the business. SRH Technologies is valuable because it requires the Department to examine that account instead of treating a later cancellation as a complete case against the purchaser. It is not a certificate of immunity for every transaction with a cancelled supplier; it is a reasoned rejection of automatic ITC denial on cancellation alone.

(NCLT Delhi) Summary: The NCLT, New Delhi Bench (Court-II), in J.C. Flowers Asset Reconstruction Pvt. Ltd. Vs Stargaze Entertainment Pvt. Ltd., rejected the revised resolution plan submitted through IA-37/ND/2026 and ordered liquidation of the corporate debtor. CIRP had commenced on 13 May 2024 on a financial creditor’s application alleging default of ₹70,33,51,777.91. Following assignment of the debt, CFM Asset Reconstruction Pvt. Ltd. became the sole financial creditor and sole member of the Committee of Creditors (CoC). The revised plan of Cine Corp Filmdom Pvt. Ltd., approved with 100% voting share, offered ₹50.25 lakh: ₹49.50 lakh to the financial creditor, ₹50,000 to statutory authorities and ₹25,000 to other operational creditors, with CIRP costs addressed in the plan’s distribution provisions. The Tribunal had earlier rejected the original ₹50 lakh plan and remitted the matter to the CoC and RP after noting that recoveries from pending asset-possession applications, estimated at approximately ₹3 crore, would benefit the successful resolution applicant. The revised plan increased the total offer by ₹25,000 and changed the treatment of avoidance recoveries and surplus sale proceeds in favour of the CoC. The CoC maintained that the cinema business was closed, assets at different locations were disputed or outside the corporate debtor’s possession, liquidation recovery was uncertain and operational creditors would otherwise receive nothing. The Tribunal nevertheless found that its substantive concerns remained unresolved. On examining the Section 29 and Regulation 36″formation Memorandum (IM) and valuation material, the Tribunal found discrepancies between the asset inventory and the plan-approval application. It noted that several assets, including a ₹50 crore deposit with the holding company, were excluded from the valuation, and that the position regarding recovery or control of that deposit remained unclear. It also criticised the delayed transaction-audit exercise under Regulation 35A. The RP had sought directions in four applications to obtain assets and security deposits from third parties, yet proceeded with plan approval without securing control of those assets and subsequently withdrew the applications. The Tribunal considered this inconsistent with the duties under Sections 17, 18 and 25(2)(a), and questioned how the invitation process and Information Memorandum had been completed without control of the entire asset base. The Tribunal further found that the proposed revival measures did not explain how the causes of default would be addressed. It questioned reliance on further borrowing where higher borrowing and interest costs were themselves cited as causes of failure, and held that the plan lacked a revival vision and did not comply with Regulation 38(3)(a). Describing the CIRP as opaque and fundamentally vitiated, it rejected the revised plan despite unanimous CoC approval. Its discussion of commercial wisdom emphasised the broader objectives of revival, employment, creditor survival and statutory dues, rather than treating recovery of some amount by the financial creditor as sufficient justification. Invoking Section 33(1)(b), the Tribunal appointed Gorang Moudgil as liquidator and restored IA-5441/ND/2024, IA-5442/ND/2024, IA-1779/ND/2025 and IA-2236/ND/2025. For this case, it directed that the sole CoC member’s initial distribution be confined to the amount accepted under the resolution plan, followed by distribution to statutory authorities and other creditors; any remaining distributable balance would then be dealt with under Section 53. It required the RP to transfer CIRP records and account for asset-sale money, directed completion of liquidation within 180 days, continued the CoC during liquidation, and stated that no further opportunity to restore CIRP would be available under Section 33(1B). These distribution directions are reported as the Tribunal’s case-specific order. FULL TEXT OF THE NCLT JUDGMENT/ORDER CIRP in the captioned matter was initiated by J.C. Indubitably, without taking control and possession of assets of the CD (supra), the Applicant preferred IA-23/ND/2025 for approval of the resolution plan, thus the IA preferred for approval of resolution plan deserve to be rejected on this ground alone. To take the position worst, the Applicant could withdraw all the four applications on 27.07.2026. Thereafter, he filed the present application. As can be seen from the provisions of Section 17(2) of IBC, 2016, the RP is responsible for complying with the requirements under any law for the time being in force on behalf of the Corporate Debtor. The Section 17(2)(e) reads thus:- “17: Management of affairs of corporate debtor by interim resolution professional. … (2) The interim resolution professional vested with the management of the corporate debtor shall- (e) [be responsible for complying with the requirements under any law for the time being in force on behalf of the corporate debtor.]” 21. Further in terms of the provisions of Section 18(1)(f) of the Code, the IRP is required to take control and custody of any assets over which the CD has ownership rights as recorded in the balance sheet of the CD or with Information Utility or the depository of the securities or any other history that records the ownerships of assets. The Section 18(f) reads thus:- “18. Duties of interim resolution professional. – … (f) take control and custody of any asset over which the corporate debtor has ownership rights as recorded in the balance sheet of the corporate debtor, or with information utility or the depository of securities or any other registry that records the ownership of assets including – i. assets over which the corporate debtor has ownership rights which may be located in a foreign country; ii. assets that may or may not be in possession of the corporate debtor; iii. tangible assets, whether movable or immovable; iv. intangible assets including intellectual property; v. securities including shares held in any subsidiary of the corporate debtor, financial instruments, insurance policies; vi. assets subject to the determination of ownership by a court or authority.” 22. In the present case, at the first instance, the RP filed application for approval of the plan, when the applications for issuance of direction to handover control of certain assets of CD were still pending and when this Tribunal refused to entertain the application for approval of plan, the four applications preferred for issuance of directions to third parties to handover the assets of CD to RP were withdrawn. Apparently, the present application preferred by RP is in disregard of the provisions of Section 17 and 18 of the Code. We also fail to appreciate that how without taking control of the assets of the CD, the RP could take steps under Regulation 36A of IBBI (CIRP) Regulations, 2016. It is also not understood that how the RP could prepare the Information Memorandum (IM) in terms of the provisions of Section 29 of IBC, 2016, when he was unable to take control of entire assets of the CD, as per Section 18 and 25(2)(a) of the Code. The entire process has been conducted in extremely opaque and absurd manner. There is no clarity even about taking control of the deposits of Rs. 50 Cr. by CD with its holding company. The foundation of process of CIRP in itself is vitiated, thus the plan cannot be approved and the same is rejected. Though, the object of CIRP is to rescue the Corporate Debtor and to put it back to its feet and liquidation should not be priority, in the present case, even after the rejection of the application for approval of resolution plan and remission of the same back to CoC, we noted vital irregularities in the process and even fresh application preferred for approval of the revised resolution plan is not found acceptable. In such situation, at this stage, the only way forward left is to order liquidation of the Corporate Debtor. However, since on two different occasions, the sole member of CoC could accept the amount offered to be distributed to it and the resolution plan could be approved with 100% vote share, in our view, it would be in the interest of justice and fair play, if distribution of the amount to be recovered in liquidation process in favour of the sole member of the CoC is confined, at the first instance to the amount distributed in its favour as per resolution plan and the balance amount is distributed to statutory authorities and other creditors. If after discharge of the claim of statutory authorities and other creditors as per Section 30(2)(b) of IBC, the distributable amount remain balance, the same would be distributive as per Section 53 of IBC, 2016. Here, it would not be out of context to discuss the term “Commercial Wisdom”. Wisdom is the practical ability to use knowledge, life experience and deep understanding to make choices and act with sound judgment. “Commercial Wisdom” is a specialized, legally recognized business judgment used by financial stakeholders to evaluate the economic viability and feasibility towards the way forward in a given situation. In view of the object of IBC, the “Commercial Wisdom” can be classified in micro and macro spheres. The vision of IBC is to encourage entrepreneurship and innovation. Some business ventures will always fail but they will be handled rapidly and swiftly. Entrepreneurs are lenders need to move on rather being bogged down with the decision taken in the past. Unfortunately, such aspect of IBC has not been understood even after a decade and consciously or unconsciously, in most of the cases, the initiator of CIRP approach the Tribunal with a thought and idea to recover the debt, while a separate mechanism is provided for the purpose. In few other cases, the applications under Section 7, 9 and 10 are preferred in a designed way either to extend the benefit of moratorium to CD or to help the CD to avoid paying the statutory dues/discharging the debt of banks and financial institutions. The micro understanding of “Commercial Wisdom” can be a thought of a creditor that when the Corporate Debtor is not in a position to discharge the debt, it is better to resort to a mechanism, in terms of which at least some recovery can be there from its resources. The “Macro Commercial Wisdom” can be a thought which only banks and financial institution can have. Such wisdom can also be classified in two parts viz. i. A thought that if the Corporate Debtor is rescued, it will be in need of further finances and would again approach the bank/financial institutions for financial facility, which would result in expansion of business of the bank/financial institutions and will make good the haircut sustained by it in CIRP; ii. Concern for the economy at larger level i.e., the revival of CD would generate resources and employment which would create revenue for deposits in the banks/financial institutions, creating scope for them to extend further financial facilities to those who are in need of the same resulting in expansion of the economy. With such concern, the financial creditors who are entitled to distribution as per Section 53 of IBC, 2016 take haircut, to ensure that some amount of money goes also to Operational Creditors, so that their business also survive and they also continue contributing to the economy, by expanding scope for employment and generating revenue. With such macro thought, the CoC/financial creditors also ensrue that some of the statutory dues are also discharged so that a contribution also goes to public fund/services. 23. However, it would not be gainsaid that the “Commercial Wisdom” is largely not understood in correct macro perspective and is perceived only as a synonym of the thought that “Something is better than nothing”. With such thought, the members of CoC are mostly keen to usurp large part of the plan value, leaving peanuts for the creditors in other category. Such approach cannot be perceived as “Commercial Wisdom” in terms of vision of IBC. With narrow understanding of “Commercial Wisdom”, even the provisions contained in Section 30(2)(b) of IBC, 2016 are diluted. When the provisions provide that the payment of debt of the Operational Creditor should not be less than the amount to be paid to such creditors in the event of liquidation of the Corporate Debtor under Section 53. Similar provision is there in clause ‘ba’ of sub-section 2 of Section 30 of the Code regarding dissenting creditors. Though, the clause b above specifically provides that higher amongst amount payable to the Operational Creditor in the event of liquidation or as per distribution in accordance with Section 53(1) of the Code should be paid, still there is very strong perception that those included in clause ‘b’ of sub- section of Section 1 of Section 53 are entitled to retain the amount received as plan value or liquidation value to the extent and satisfaction of their entire claim and there may or may not be any distribution in favor of other creditors. Could it be true then there could not be no need for keeping any amount to be paid to other creditors as an alm only. Nevertheless, in the present case, the sole member of CoC has accepted a particular amount of distribution in the CIRP, thus as has been viewed hereinabove, at the first instance, it would be entitled to same amount and only after distribution of fair share of receipts (liquidation value) in favour of other creditors, in liquidation process, the balance amount would be distributed as per Section 53 of IBC in second round.

Decoding Entry 11 of Notification No. 12/2017-Central Tax (Rate): The Builder Floor GST Trap Summary: This article examines whether pure labour contracts for constructing stilt-plus-four and similar multi-floor residential properties qualify for GST exemption under Entry 11 of Notification No. 12/2017-Central Tax (Rate). The central controversy is whether each self-contained floor designed for one family can constitute a “single residential unit” or whether the complete building, because it contains multiple dwelling units and common infrastructure, becomes a “residential complex” outside the exemption. The discussion explains the statutory concepts of pure labour contracts, original works, works contracts and composite supplies and considers architectural factors such as separate kitchens, entrances and utility meters alongside shared foundations, lifts, staircases, parking and water systems. It also contrasts advance-ruling approaches with legacy service-tax precedents relied upon in support of independent residential units. Pasted text The article further distinguishes genuine family-funded redevelopment from commercial arrangements involving builders, JDAs and third-party purchasers. Particular emphasis is placed on contemporaneous documentation—including labour-only contracts, owner-paid material invoices, banking records, worker muster rolls, sanctioned plans, architectural certificates and separate utility connections. The article ultimately highlights that the GST treatment of such projects is highly fact-dependent and that claiming the exemption requires both an appropriate contractual structure and evidence supporting the actual nature of the construction. Sprawling ancestral bungalows, once the private domains of joint families, are being razed to make way for multi-tier vertical residences, predominantly organised as stilt-plus-four independent floors. Driven by spiralling land valuations, municipal floor-area ratio (FAR) relaxations, and demographic realities of generational family division, these vertical properties have redefined urban redevelopment. Yet, beneath the polished marble, high-speed elevators, and manicured terrace gardens lies an indirect tax landmine ticking quietly in plain sight. Property owners and civil contractors routinely structure these multi-crore redevelopments under what they assume to be tax-exempt “pure labour contracts.” They proceed under the comfortable operational belief that executing a project on a labour-only basis, where the landowner procures every bag of cement, truck of aggregate, and metric ton of reinforcing steel, insulates the transaction entirely from the Goods and Services Tax (GST) net. This assumption frequently collapses when the Directorate General of GST Intelligence (DGGI) or state audit wings arrive with a notice. Assessing officers across multiple jurisdictions are mounting multi-million-rupee tax assessments against these builds, demanding an 18% tax on the entire contract value, compounded by mandatory statutory interest under Section 50 and punitive fraud penalties under Section 74 (or Section 74A) of the Central Goods and Services Tax (CGST) Act, 2017. At the epicentre of this enforcement wave is the friction between the exemption carved out under Entry 11 of Notification No. 12/2017-Central Tax (Rate) and the physical realities of multi-tier builder floors. The controversy hinges on an ostensibly simple statutory phrase: what constitutes a “single residential unit otherwise than as a part of a residential complex”? When an ancestral bungalow is demolished and rebuilt into four self-contained, independent vertical dwellings connected by common foundations, staircases, and stilt parking, does the construction labour qualify as exempt work on autonomous residential units, or does it represent the taxable erection of an integrated residential complex? Resolving this question demands a rigorous dissection of statutory construction, physical architectural segregation, conflicting advance rulings, and the forensic doctrines of legacy service tax jurisprudence. Anatomy of Entry 11 and Core Statutory Concepts The architecture of the residential construction exemption under the GST regime is deliberately restrictive. Entry 11 of Notification No. 12/2017-Central Tax (Rate), dated June 28, 2017, grants an unconditional Nil rate of tax to a tightly circumscribed category of construction activity: “Services by way of pure labour contracts of construction, erection, commissioning, or installation of original works pertaining to a single residential unit otherwise than as a part of a residential complex.” To decipher the scope of this exemption, the statutory definitions embedded in Paragraph 2 of Notification No. 12/2017-Central Tax (Rate) must be parsed alongside the fundamental concepts of indirect tax law. Single Residential Unit Clause 2(zx) defines a “single residential unit” as: “a self-contained residential unit which is designed for use, wholly or principally, for residential purposes for one family.” The provision establishes two essential legal criteria: self-containment, meaning the physical and functional autonomy of the living quarters, and single-family architectural design. Residential Complex Clause 2(zz) defines a “residential complex” as: “any complex comprising a building or buildings, having more than one single residential unit.” The threshold established by the executive notification is mathematical and structural: if an immovable mass is classified as a single building having more than one single residential unit, the building itself constitutes a residential complex by statutory definition. Original Works Clause 2(zs) defines “original works” to include: “(i) all new constructions; (ii) all types of additions and alterations to abandoned or damaged structures on land that are required to make them workable; (iii) erection, commissioning or installation of plant, machinery or equipment or structures, whether pre-fabricated or otherwise.” The exemption does not extend to routine additions, repairs, alterations, or maintenance of existing sound buildings; it requires ground-up construction or foundational structural intervention. Pure Labour Contract, Works Contract and Composite Supply The application of Entry 11 requires a clear understanding of the tax code’s baseline mechanisms: Pure Labour Contract: A supply of service where the executing contractor provides exclusively human effort, technical execution, and project supervision, without transferring title or property in any materials, consumables, or civil ingredients into the immovable structure. If even nominal materials are supplied, the contract moves outside this classification. Works Contract: Defined under Section 2(119) of the CGST Act as an integrated contract for building, construction, fabrication, completion, or alteration of immovable property wherein the transfer of property in goods (whether as goods or in another form) is involved. By statutory fiction under Schedule II, Paragraph 6(a), a works contract is treated uniformly as a composite supply of services. Composite Supply: Defined under Section 2(30) of the CGST Act as a supply comprising two or more taxable supplies of goods or services, or both, naturally bundled and supplied in conjunction with each other in the ordinary course of business, with one serving as the principal supply. Much like an unsegregated traditional meal where one cannot surgically detach the dessert to claim a separate tax rate, when a contractor supplies labour alongside formwork oils, binding wire, nails, or machine rentals, the department invokes Section 2(30) read with Section 8 to treat the entire engagement as an indivisible composite works contract taxable at 18% under Heading 9954. Pari Materia: The established canon of statutory interpretation holding that statutes or statutory notifications addressing the same subject matter, purpose, or class of persons must be construed together as an integrated whole. This doctrine serves as a central litigation bridge between the legacy Service Tax Mega Exemption (Notification No. 25/2012-ST) and GST Notification No. 12/2017-CT(Rate). Reverse Charge Mechanism (RCM): A statutory shift wherein the liability to discharge tax is transferred from the supplier of the service to the recipient under Section 9(3) or Section 9(4) of the CGST Act. While manpower supply to corporate entities previously triggered joint or full reverse charge under legacy service tax and specific GST notifications, pure labour contracts for construction work are governed by the forward charge regime, placing the initial payment responsibility directly on the contractor. The tension between these classifications becomes apparent when an assessing authority encounters a contract for the redevelopment of a residential parcel. The contractor relies on Entry 11 by arguing that the physical output consists of discrete, self-contained residential units. The revenue authority counters that the architectural whole is a single structural mass consisting of more than one dwelling, which converts the entire property into an ineligible residential complex under Clause 2(zz). The Physical Anatomy on Trial: Autonomous Dwelling vs. Collective Complex In modern urban architecture, the stilt-plus-four (S+4) builder floor represents a hybrid structural typology. It is neither a freestanding single-family bungalow nor a high-rise residential township spread across acres of manicured land. It occupies a single cadastral plot, commonly measuring between 250 and 1,000 square yards, rising four stories above an open ground-level stilt area designed for vehicle parking. During audits conducted under Section 65 of the CGST Act, revenue officers systematically evaluate the project’s physical characteristics. They assemble structural evidence to establish that the building cannot qualify as an isolated single residential unit. The taxpayer’s legal defence must, in turn, demonstrate that each floor is physically, functionally, and legally self-contained. Features Supporting Autonomous Residential Units The physical features supporting an exemption under Clause 2(zx) centre on operational self-containment. Each floor plan incorporates a dedicated, fully plumbed modular kitchen, gas pipeline provision, and independent drainage line. Under cross-examination in tax disputes, the absence of a shared mess or common kitchen is the primary evidentiary benchmark confirming that the floor is designed for an autonomous family unit. State electricity distribution utilities install separate, tariff-sanctioned electrical meters for each floor, ensuring each dwelling operates on an isolated electrical circuit with its own distribution board and distinct consumer account numbers. Access to each floor is strictly gated from the common lift lobby or landing by a secure entrance door, providing privacy and spatial separation from other floors. Furthermore, urban local bodies allocate distinct property identification numbers and compute distinct municipal property tax liabilities for each independent floor, while sub-registrar records treat each floor as an independent parcel of real estate with distinct market values and stamp duty assessments. Shared Infrastructure Supporting the Residential Complex View Conversely, audit teams disallow the exemption by systematically documenting the shared, indivisible elements of the building to prove a collective residential complex under Clause 2(zz). The building rests on an integrated reinforced cement concrete (RCC) foundation, such as a raft or combined footing, with continuous load-bearing columns running unbroken from subterranean footings through the stilt to the terrace parapet. A contractor cannot physically cast the third-floor slab without relying directly on the structural continuity of the first and second floors. The ground floor stilt is typically unpartitioned, designed to house multiple vehicles under a unified structural ceiling with shared ramps and automated gates. A single vertical circulation core serves the entire building, featuring a shared concrete lift well, elevator mechanical assembly, motor room, and fire escape stairwell. Water distribution relies on shared underground and overhead storage tanks, and the roof terrace remains an unpartitioned common asset housing central solar water heaters, HVAC heat pumps, and elevator machine rooms. This architectural tension forms the basis of departmental audits. An assessing officer viewing the property from the street observes a single four-storey concrete edifice erected on a single cadastral plot under a single municipal sanction plan. From this perspective, the structure appears to be an unsegregated building containing more than one residential unit, the exact definition of a residential complex under Clause 2(zz). The taxpayer’s defence, by contrast, relies on a micro-level operational analysis: the contractor was engaged to build specific, self-contained residential dwellings, each intended for a distinct family unit. Resolving this conflict depends heavily on which line of judicial precedent an adjudicator chooses to follow. The Judicial Rift: Advance Ruling Orthodoxy vs. Appellate Pragmatism The legal landscape surrounding Entry 11 is divided between the revenue-centric rulings issued by state Authorities for Advance Rulings (AAR) and the pragmatic, substance-over-form jurisprudence developed by the Customs, Excise and Service Tax Appellate Tribunal (CESTAT) and the High Courts. Advance Ruling Approach State Advance Ruling authorities routinely adopt a literal, plot-centric approach. Under this interpretation, the cadastral parcel is the decisive legal benchmark. If more than one self-contained dwelling unit is constructed within a single continuous structural envelope on that parcel, the property is categorised as a residential complex under Clause 2(zz), eliminating the exemption under Entry 11. A prominent example of this stance appears in the ruling rendered by the Tamil Nadu Authority for Advance Rulings in In re Johnson Lifts Private Limited (2020). The applicant sought a determination on the applicable GST rate for supplying, erecting, and commissioning elevators in a building consisting of a ground-plus-three-floor configuration on a single plot. The property owner argued that the independent floors were designed for personal family residential use, accommodating the owner, his father, and his brother in distinct, autonomous units. The Authority examined the definition of a “residential complex” in Notification No. 11/2017-CT(Rate) and Notification No. 12/2017-CT(Rate). It observed that where a single structural mass comprises multiple units, each self-contained and allocated to different family branches, the total structure constitutes a complex containing “more than one single residential unit”. The Authority concluded that the presence of common lift wells, combined stairs, and an integrated structural design makes the overall project a residential complex, disqualifying individual floors from being treated as standalone single residential units. A similar approach was adopted by the Kerala AAR in In re Smartech Elevators (2022). There, the authority held that once a building plan sanctioned by a local municipality displays more than one kitchen and multiple independent living zones, the edifice ceases to be a single residential unit and falls into the definition of a residential complex under Clause 2(zz). Under Section 103 of the CGST Act, advance rulings bind only the specific applicant and their jurisdictional tax officers. In day-to-day audit practice, however, departmental audit teams frequently cite these rulings to issue show-cause notices against pure labour contractors executing multi-tier private residential projects. Legacy Service Tax Jurisprudence and Pari Materia Argument To construct an effective defence against this narrow approach, advocates look to the doctrine of continuity, supported by extensive case law from the legacy Service Tax regime. The statutory phrasing in Entry 11 of Notification No. 12/2017-CT(Rate) is identical (pari materia) to Entry 14(b) of the legacy Mega Exemption Notification No. 25/2012-ST, which exempted services by way of construction, erection, commissioning, or installation of original works of a single residential unit otherwise than as a part of a residential complex. The definition of a “single residential unit” under legacy Notification No. 12/2012-ST and Notification No. 25/2012-ST, an independent residential unit with specific facilities for living, cooking, and sanitary requirements, mirrors the wording in Clause 2(zx) of the GST notification. Under established administrative and statutory interpretation principles, when Parliament or the executive imports language from an earlier statute into subsequent tax legislation, it is presumed to adopt the judicial construction placed upon those terms by superior courts. Macro Marvel Projects Ltd. The cornerstone of this defence is the landmark CESTAT decision in Macro Marvel Projects Ltd. v. Commissioner of Service Tax, Chennai [2008 (12) S.T.R. 603 (Tri.-Chennai)], which was affirmed by the Supreme Court of India [2012 (25) S.T.R. J154 (SC)]. In Macro Marvel, the assessee constructed multiple individual residential houses on behalf of plot owners. The department sought to tax the activity under “Construction of Complex” service under Section 65(30a) and Section 65(105)(zzzh) of the Finance Act, 1994, arguing that the collective execution of numerous units on a larger layout constituted a complex. The Tribunal rejected the revenue’s position, establishing two key principles: the physical nature of the individual dwelling unit governs its tax character, not its proximity to other units, and the law recognises an individual independent house as a single residential unit. Constructing individual units cannot be taxed under a category reserved for multi-unit integrated complexes unless all elements of a complex are legally satisfied within that individual dwelling. A.S. Sikarwar, J.R. Construction and Esha Homes This principle was reinforced by the Tribunal in A.S. Sikarwar v. Commissioner of Central Excise, Indore [2012 (28) S.T.R. 479 (Tri.-Del.)] and J.R. Construction v. Commissioner of Central Excise. These decisions held that constructing multiple independent units, each with dedicated living, cooking, and sanitary facilities, does not create a taxable residential complex merely because the units are constructed concurrently or share common civil boundaries. In Esha Homes v. Commissioner of Central Excise [Appeal Nos. ST/30265 & 30266/2017, CESTAT Hyderabad], the Tribunal reviewed both pre-2012 and post-2012 disputes. The bench observed that where individual residential units are constructed pursuant to agreements with plot owners and building plans are approved for distinct units, the activity remains excluded and exempt from service tax under Entry 14(b) of Notification No. 25/2012-ST. The bench confirmed that the core legal issue remains settled under Macro Marvel Projects. This conflict between administrative advance rulings and appellate judicial authority illustrates the structural divergence in the system. Advance ruling authorities, bound by their mandate, frequently prioritise the literal text of Clause 2(zz) to treat any structural volume containing more than one kitchen as a taxable complex. Appellate tribunals and High Courts, by contrast, focus on economic substance and statutory continuity: they evaluate whether the contractor was engaged to build a series of distinct single residential units, each intended for separate single-family habitation. The Evidentiary Fault Line: Ancestral Partition vs. Commercial Development Beyond the architectural specifications of the building, tax audits frequently centre on a separate inquiry: what is the underlying economic relationship governing the construction? Adjudicating authorities scrutinise the project to determine whether it is a genuine family-funded home reconstruction or a disguised commercial real estate enterprise. Genuine Joint-Family Redevelopment In a genuine joint-family redevelopment, a parent and their adult children demolish their dilapidated family home to build an S+4 structure. They intend to provide each branch of the family with modern, private living quarters while maintaining shared ownership of the ancestral plot. The owners self-fund the project using pooled personal savings or joint home improvement loans. They engage a civil contractor exclusively to supply labour and site supervision, while family members directly purchase every truckload of RMC concrete, structural steel, brick, and plumbing fixtures. The floors are not built for sale, transfer, or commercial lease; they serve exclusively as the private residences of the co-owners. From an indirect tax perspective, this arrangement aligns closely with Entry 11. Each floor is designed for and occupied by a specific branch of the family, matching the definition of a “single residential unit… for one family” under Clause 2(zx). The absence of commercial intent, sales agreements, and marketing materials provides strong contemporaneous evidence that the site is an ancestral home, not a commercial real estate development. Commercial Redevelopment The tax exposure shifts dramatically in commercial redevelopments. In this scenario, the landowner enters into a Collaboration Agreement or Joint Development Agreement (JDA) with a professional builder. The builder agrees to construct the S+4 building at their own cost, often paying the landowner an additional monetary sum. In exchange, the builder receives unencumbered ownership rights to one or more upper floors, complete with proportionate undivided shares in the underlying land. Long before construction concludes, the builder markets their allocated floors to third-party purchasers via brokers or online platforms. They enter into separate “Agreements to Sell” and execute distinct conveyance deeds upon completion. To optimise indirect taxes, the parties often attempt to fragment the transaction: they draft separate “pure labour contracts” between the prospective floor buyers and a construction entity linked to the builder, while processing material procurements through separate trading passes. During audits conducted by DGGI or state anti-evasion wings, investigators routinely look past these contractual structures to uncover the underlying commercial arrangement. They cross-reference municipal demolition approvals and water connections against pre-construction memoranda of understanding (MOUs) and registered General Powers of Attorney (GPAs) granted to developers. Officers summon digital and banking records to trace circular fund flows, where property buyers make synchronised payments to “material suppliers” and “labour contractors” sharing common directors, registered addresses, or GST logins. Furthermore, tax authorities inspect RERA project registration portals. Where builders register small plots to enhance marketability, tax authorities use the RERA registration as proof that the project is a commercial housing project rather than a private single-family residence. Burden of Establishing the Exemption Taxpayers defending an exemption face a significant evidentiary standard. While Section 155 of the CGST Act explicitly places the burden of proof on the taxpayer when establishing eligibility for Input Tax Credit, broader tax jurisprudence applies an equally stringent burden to tax exemptions. In Commissioner of Central Excise, New Delhi v. Hari Chand Shri Gopal [2010 (260) E.L.T. 3 (S.C.)], a Constitution Bench of the Supreme Court held that exemption notifications must be strictly construed. The assessee must demonstrate through clear contemporaneous evidence that their transaction falls within the four corners of the exemption. Only after the taxpayer clears this threshold can the beneficial terms of the exemption be applied broadly. Under Entry 11, the initial burden rests on the contractor and property owner to demonstrate that the contract was executed strictly on a pure labour basis, involving zero supply of materials; that the architectural design was created for independent single-family dwelling units; and that the construction was not executed as an integrated commercial complex. Once the taxpayer introduces documentary evidence substantiating these facts, the burden of proof shifts to the tax department to demonstrate the contrary Such as proving the transfer of materials by the contractor or documenting third-party sales prior to the issuance of a completion certificate. If the contractor and property owners fail to build a contemporaneous evidentiary record during construction, adjudicating officers readily rely on Section 155 and the Hari Chand Shri Gopal precedent to confirm tax demands under the default 18% rate. The Field Manual: Compliance and Documentation Blueprint Relying solely on abstract legal arguments rarely succeeds during a departmental GST audit. Auditing officers base their assessments on physical invoices, store logs, ledger reconciliations, and contract clauses. To withstand scrutiny under Sections 65, 73, or 74 of the CGST Act, both contractors and property owners must implement a rigorous documentation strategy before work begins on site. Draft the Pure Labour Contract Precisely The primary construction contract must be drafted with precise, unambiguous operational terms. Standard construction contracts downloaded from the internet frequently combine labour, consumables, and minor materials, which proves fatal under audit. The agreement must state that the contractor is engaged exclusively to supply civil construction labour, engineering supervision, and manpower services, with no supply, sale, or financing of goods, civil materials, consumables, or structural components. The contract should include an explicit zero-material exclusion clause specifying that under no circumstances shall the contractor purchase, supply, or incorporate any materials, goods, or consumables into the permanent works, requiring that all cement, steel, bricks, aggregates, ready-mix concrete, shuttering plywood, binding wire, and curing chemicals be procured directly by the property owners under invoices issued to their individual names and discharged through their personal bank accounts. Equipment, Staging and Machinery Furthermore, the contract must clarify the treatment of equipment and staging. A common audit challenge arises when contractors charge an all-inclusive rate per square foot that covers both human labour and mechanised equipment such as tower cranes, concrete mixer trucks, vibrators, and steel shuttering plates. Departmental auditors seize on the provision of external plant and machinery to argue that the contract represents a broader “composite equipment service” rather than a pure labour contract. The agreement must clarify that external staging, scaffolding, and machinery are either provided directly by the property owner, or that the contractor is compensated strictly for equipment operation (manpower), with fuel, machinery rentals, and staging secured by the property owner through independent third-party vendor arrangements. Maintain a Client-Side Material Procurement Trail A frequent cause of lost tax exemptions occurs when a contractor, seeking to expedite site work, purchases minor items, such as binding wire, waterproof coatings, PVC conduits, or construction adhesives, and invoices them as client reimbursements. Once these material purchases appear on the contractor’s books, audit officers treat the entire engagement as an indivisible composite supply of a works contract under Section 2(119), taxing the entire contract value at 18%. To maintain an unassailable client-side material procurement audit trail, every material purchase order, tax invoice, and delivery challan must be billed directly to the individual property owners, matching the physical address of the construction site. Payments must clear directly from the property owners’ personal bank accounts to the material vendors via traceable banking channels such as RTGS or NEFT, without routing vendor funds through contractor accounts. The job site must maintain a client-supplied material log where the property owner’s representative verifies delivery challans before issuing materials to the contractor’s work crews. Document the Labour Supply When defending an exemption under Entry 11, the contractor must prove that its billings reflect genuine labour output. The contractor should maintain site-specific daily attendance muster rolls that identify workers by name, trade, and Aadhaar identification. Payment records must reflect actual wage distributions, supported by statutory compliance registers under the Building and Other Construction Workers (BOCW) Act, 1996, alongside Employee Provident Fund (EPF) and Employee State Insurance (ESIC) deposit challans. Contractor invoices must reference Service Accounting Code (SAC) 9954 (specifically 995411 for single or multi-dwelling residential buildings) and contain an explicit statutory declaration confirming that the services are supplied by way of a pure labour contract of original works of a single residential unit, exempt from GST vide Serial No. 11 of Notification No. 12/2017-Central Tax (Rate). Architectural and Municipal Documentation The property’s architectural and municipal documents provide foundational legal evidence defining the building’s physical character. During the initial architectural design phase, collective terms like “Residential Complex,” “Multi-Unit Housing Project,” or “Commercial Apartment Block” must be avoided. The application submitted to the urban local authority should identify the project as the sanction of independent residential floors or single residential units for personal multi-generational family habitation. Prior to commencing construction, parties should secure a formal certificate from a Council of Architecture-registered architect confirming that each floor is structurally designed as an autonomous, self-contained residential unit featuring independent cooking, living, and sanitary facilities configured for distinct family units. Finally, formal applications must be filed with local utility providers for separate electrical connections, individual consumer numbers, separate water sub-meters, and distinct piped natural gas metering lines, anchoring the claim that the individual units are functionally and structurally independent. Strategic Litigation Roadmaps: Handling Audits and Crushing SCNs When tax practitioners receive a departmental communication regarding an S+4 builder floor development—whether an informal audit observation, a Pre-Show Cause Notice Consultation in Form GST DRC-01A, or a formal Show Cause Notice in Form GST DRC-01 under Section 73, 74, or 74A—the litigation response must be executed with procedural and substantive precision. Jurisdiction, Limitation and Suppression Counsel must address threshold jurisdictional and limitation issues at the outset. A substantial number of notices issued on pure labour contracts invoke extended limitation periods for alleged fraud, wilful misstatement, or suppression of facts. If the contractor regularly filed returns in Form GSTR-1 and GSTR-3B, reporting turnover under exempt service columns, the department cannot sustain a charge of wilful suppression. The legal debate surrounding the definitions of a “single residential unit” and a “residential complex” represents a bona fide dispute of statutory interpretation, which negates the extended limitation period. Limited Binding Effect of Advance Rulings Furthermore, where the show-cause notice relies on conservative AAR orders such as Johnson Lifts or Smartech Elevators, the response must assert Section 103 of the CGST Act. An Advance Ruling binds the applicant and their specific jurisdictional officer; it carries no general binding precedential value over third-party audits and cannot displace appellate tribunal or High Court jurisprudence. Harmonious Construction of Clauses 2(zx) and 2(zz) Substantive arguments on the merits must be built on three integrated statutory arguments. The response should advance a harmonious construction of Clause 2(zx) and Clause 2(zz). If every building containing more than one floor were automatically deemed a “residential complex” under Clause 2(zz), the exemption for a “single residential unit” under Entry 11 would be rendered virtually inoperative for urban areas, where single-storey residences are increasingly uncommon. The proper interpretation is that the exemption protects the construction of an autonomous single residential unit, even if other independent units exist within the broader structural envelope, provided the project is not a shared-infrastructure commercial township. Legacy Precedents and Statutory Continuity The response should incorporate the binding principles of Macro Marvel Projects, A.S. Sikarwar, and Esha Homes under the doctrines of pari materia and statutory continuity. The argument must establish that the words used in Entry 11 mirror Entry 14(b) of Notification No. 25/2012-ST, and that judicial interpretation confirming the exemption of autonomous individual dwellings remains effective under GST. Build the Evidentiary Dossier Finally, counsel must assemble a comprehensive evidentiary dossier to reverse the statutory burden of proof. By placing on record the client-supplied material procurement ledger, third-party material invoices, proof of client bank transfers, segregated worker muster rolls, and the Council of Architecture zoning certificate, the taxpayer satisfies their primary onus under Section 155 and Hari Chand Shri Gopal, forcing the department to prove commercial alienation or taxability. Conclusion The builder floor indirect tax controversy illustrates the friction between modern high-density urban redevelopment and static statutory drafting. Entry 11 was conceived to protect individual citizens constructing modest family homes from the compounding costs of indirect taxation. However, the shift toward multi-tier vertical residences has outpaced this statutory framework, creating ongoing friction between local audit officers and taxpayers.

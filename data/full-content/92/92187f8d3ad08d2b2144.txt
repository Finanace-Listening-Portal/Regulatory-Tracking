(Calcutta High Court) The Calcutta High Court, in Tata Chemicals Limited Vs M/s. Kshitish Bardhan Chunilal Nath & Ors., considered two connected appeals concerning allegations of fraudulent excess rebates granted to fertilizer distributors through the alleged collusion of a company employee. The dispute arose from commercial transactions between Tata Chemicals Limited and its distributors, M/s. Kshitish Bardhan Chunilal Nath (KBCN) and Ashok Kumar Saha, proprietor of Ashok Sales Agency (ASA). Tata Chemicals alleged that its Deputy Manager (Accounts), Sudip Kumar Singh, had manipulated the company’s accounting system by issuing unauthorised credit notes substantially exceeding the discounts and rebates permitted under its official price circulars. The alleged irregularities occurred during financial years 2014-15 to 2017-18 and resulted in substantial reductions in the amounts recoverable from the distributors. Tata Chemicals instituted two money recovery suits and sought interim protection against the transfer or alienation of specified properties belonging to the defendants. Although an interim injunction was initially granted on 17 July 2019, the Single Judge vacated it by an order dated 16 March 2021, holding that the claims were essentially for unascertained and unliquidated damages. Tata Chemicals challenged that decision before the Division Bench, arguing that its claims were quantified, supported by documentary evidence and required protection pending adjudication. The company explained that its fertilizer distribution business operated through an established system of price circulars specifying the discounts and rebates available to authorised distributors. These benefits were recorded through credit notes generated in the SAP enterprise resource planning system. According to Tata Chemicals, Sudip Kumar Singh was responsible for calculating and recording such benefits but allegedly used his authorised access to extend excessive credits to selected distributors in exchange for unlawful financial benefits. The company initially discovered irregularities in October 2016, when certain excessive credit notes were identified and subsequently reversed. Further discrepancies were detected during account verification in July 2017, leading to a broader investigation into transactions undertaken over the preceding years. Tata Chemicals suspended the employee in September 2017 and subsequently appointed KPMG to investigate the suspected misconduct. The company maintained that the distributors were aware of the excessive credits and had knowingly benefited from transactions outside the authorised commercial arrangements. It relied on electronic communications, accounting records, price circulars, reconciliation statements and alleged admissions to establish that the transactions were not ordinary accounting mistakes but part of a coordinated arrangement causing substantial financial loss. In relation to KBCN, Tata Chemicals stated that fertilizers worth approximately ₹218.18 crore had been supplied during the relevant period. Under the applicable price circulars, the distributor was allegedly entitled to rebates of approximately ₹8.55 crore, whereas credit notes aggregating ₹40.32 crore had been issued, resulting in excess credits of ₹31,77,22,561. In the case of ASA, Tata Chemicals alleged that goods worth approximately ₹39.37 crore had been supplied and that the distributor received credits of approximately ₹4.55 crore against an entitlement of ₹1.52 crore, resulting in excess benefits of ₹3,02,46,742. Tata Chemicals also relied on meetings and correspondence in which representatives of the distributors allegedly acknowledged receiving excessive credits and expressed willingness to repay certain amounts. KBCN had made payments aggregating ₹1.50 crore, while ASA had made a payment of ₹9,05,161 following discussions concerning the disputed transactions. However, the respondents subsequently challenged the company’s calculations and disputed the alleged admissions. Tata Chemicals argued that these partial repayments, together with the account reconciliations and other documents, supported the existence of identifiable monetary liabilities rather than merely speculative claims for damages. The allegations were further supported, according to Tata Chemicals, by forensic investigations undertaken by KPMG and BDO India LLP. KPMG’s investigation examined the employee’s financial dealings and identified substantial cash deposits, investments, insurance payments, jewellery purchases and other transactions that Tata Chemicals alleged were disproportionate to his declared income. The company also referred to evidence suggesting that the employee had received kickbacks from distributors in exchange for issuing excessive credit notes. BDO India LLP was subsequently appointed in October 2018 to undertake a forensic examination and quantify the alleged excess rebates. Its report dated 4 February 2019 identified discrepancies in the credit notes issued to both distributors and examined electronic communications, including spreadsheets allegedly exchanged between the employee and distributor representatives. The report calculated excess credits of ₹31,77,22,561 for KBCN and ₹3,02,46,742 for ASA. After accounting for repayments and adjustments, Tata Chemicals asserted that ₹28,25,48,558 remained recoverable from KBCN and ₹2,58,68,781 from ASA as of 5 March 2019. The company contended that the forensic findings, alleged acknowledgements and financial records established a strong prima facie case of fraudulent diversion of commercial benefits. The respondents strongly contested these allegations and maintained that the disputes arose from accounting differences and unresolved commercial reconciliations rather than fraudulent transactions. ASA argued that the discounts and incentives granted by Tata Chemicals depended on several commercial factors, including freight adjustments, cash discounts, price revisions and additional rebates, and that the price circulars relied upon by the company had not been properly communicated. It also questioned the reliability of the forensic reports, contending that the investigations were conducted unilaterally without allowing the distributors to examine the underlying records or explain the transactions. KBCN similarly asserted that the company’s accounting practices were inconsistent and that certain subsequent credit notes and reconciliations indicated amounts recoverable by the distributor from Tata Chemicals. KBCN claimed that approximately ₹3.30 crore was payable to it after reconciliation, while ASA also asserted an entitlement to recover amounts from the company. The respondents further contended that alleged admissions had been withdrawn or explained and that the employee’s statements and electronic records could not be treated as conclusive evidence without proper proof and cross-examination. They submitted that allegations of fraud, collusion and financial misconduct raised substantial disputed questions of fact requiring a full trial. The principal legal issue before the Division Bench was whether an interim injunction restraining the alienation of properties could be granted in connection with a money recovery suit involving allegations of fraud and disputed commercial accounts. The respondents relied on the principles governing attachment before judgment under Order XXXVIII Rule 5 and injunctions under Order XXXIX Rule 1(b) of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908. They argued that such extraordinary relief could not be granted merely because a plaintiff had instituted a money suit or apprehended difficulty in recovering an eventual decree. They also submitted that Tata Chemicals had failed to establish any definite intention on the part of the defendants to dispose of their assets for the purpose of defeating creditors. In support of their submissions, they referred to judicial precedents including Raman Tech and Process Engineering Co. v. Solanki Traders, Prem Raj Mundra v. Md. Manek Gazi, Raa Projects Ltd. v. Seaways Shipping Ltd. and Jai Balaji Industries Ltd. v. Hyquip Systems (P) Ltd. Tata Chemicals, on the other hand, argued that the documentary evidence demonstrated identifiable financial losses arising from unauthorised credit transactions and that interim protection was necessary to prevent frustration of any decree ultimately passed in its favour. The company relied, among other authorities, on Rahul S. Shah v. Jinendra Kumar Gandhi and Harleen Jairath v. Prabha Surana concerning effective judicial protection and enforcement of monetary claims. After considering the pleadings, forensic reports, correspondence, financial records and competing submissions, the Calcutta High Court concluded that Tata Chemicals had established a sufficiently strong prima facie case to justify interim protection. The Division Bench considered the Single Judge’s approach of treating the claims as unascertained damages inadequate in the circumstances, particularly when the company had placed detailed calculations, accounting records and supporting material identifying the alleged excess credits. The Court considered the documentary evidence, alleged acknowledgements, partial repayments and financial circumstances surrounding the disputed transactions relevant to the exercise of its equitable jurisdiction. It recognised that the respondents’ objections and the allegations of fraud would ultimately require adjudication on evidence, but held that the existence of contested factual questions did not, in the circumstances of the case, eliminate the need to preserve the subject matter of effective recovery. The Division Bench allowed both appeals and connected applications, restored the interim injunction dated 17 July 2019 and confirmed the interim protection granted on 30 April 2021. The Court clarified that its observations were confined to deciding the interlocutory applications and would not prejudice the final determination of the recovery suits. The judgment thus illustrates that a quantified monetary claim supported by substantial prima facie documentary evidence may justify protective interim relief in an appropriate commercial dispute, while leaving the ultimate determination of fraud, liability and recoverable amounts to trial.

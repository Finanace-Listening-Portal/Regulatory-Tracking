Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); Heimerle Refinery Private Limited Vs Union of India & Others (Bombay High Court) Summary: The Bombay High Court allowed Heimerle Refinery Private Limited’s writ petition concerning the clearance of studded platinum jewellery imported before the publication of Notification No.02/2026-27 dated 1 April 2026. The notification changed the import policy for goods falling under CTH 7113 from “Free” to “Restricted”. The consignments had arrived in India on 1 April 2026 at 00:42 a.m. and 2 April 2026 at 01:39 a.m., before the notification was published in the electronic Official Gazette on 2 April 2026. The dispute ultimately concerned three consignments covered by Bills of Entry Nos.8590663 and 8590666 dated 10 April 2026 and 8735308 dated 17 April 2026. Neither the rate of customs duty nor the value of the jewellery was disputed, and the petitioner did not challenge the Government’s power to amend the Foreign Trade Policy or the validity of the notification itself. Earlier, the High Court had permitted release of two consignments against ITC bonds and directed provisional release of two others against a combination of bank guarantee and ITC bond. The petitioner approached the Supreme Court concerning the remaining consignments. Pursuant to an interim direction, import authorisations were issued on 9 July 2026. The Supreme Court subsequently disposed of the special leave petition, leaving the High Court to decide the writ petition on its own merits, uninfluenced by subsequent observations or developments. The petitioner maintained that the applicable import policy depended on the date of import as defined under the Foreign Trade Policy and Handbook of Procedures, rather than the subsequent filing of bills of entry or clearance for home consumption. It relied particularly on the Gujarat High Court’s decision in Enero Jewels Pvt. Ltd. and the Delhi High Court’s decision in Bright Metal Refiners, which had permitted clearance in comparable circumstances. The Revenue argued that the notification applied to consignments awaiting clearance, expressly excluded transitional protection and prevailed over the general policy provisions. It also relied on the DGFT’s Office Memorandum dated 13 May 2026 and Supreme Court decisions concerning the completion of imports and determination of customs duty. The Court held that paragraph 1.05(b) of the Foreign Trade Policy, read with paragraphs 2.17(a) and 11.11 of the Handbook of Procedures, governed the relevant import date. Under these provisions, the date is reckoned with reference to shipment or dispatch from the supplying country. For air imports, the relevant airway bill must represent the date on which the goods left the last airport in the exporting country. Once the policy itself defined the import date, the Revenue could not substitute the date applicable under the Customs Act. Filing a bill of entry or obtaining clearance after the restriction was therefore irrelevant to the applicability of the policy change. The decisions in Ganesh Das Bhojraj and Kiran Spinning Mills were distinguished because they concerned customs duty and Section 15 of the Customs Act. The Court further held that the notification could acquire legal force only upon Gazette publication and could not retrospectively affect goods already dispatched and arrived in India. It followed the reasoning in the comparable Gujarat and Delhi High Court decisions, noting that the Revenue had accepted and acted upon them. It also rejected the Office Memorandum as an executive clarification lacking authority to override the Foreign Trade Policy, rewrite the prescribed import date or retrospectively extend the notification. Relying on Ratan Melting and Wire Industries and Kanak Exports, the Court held that the subsequently issued import authorisations did not redress the petitioner’s grievance, as their conditions exceeded the DGFT’s authority. Accordingly, the Court directed expeditious release of the three remaining consignments and declared that the notification did not apply to goods that had arrived in India before its publication. It also granted the reliefs concerning clearance for home consumption and submission of Bill of Entry No.8590666 without insisting on an import authorisation or licence. The connected interim application was disposed of. Cases Discussed M/s. Bright Metal Refiners Vs Dheeraj Rastogi & Others — Review Petition No.269/2026, order dated 2 July 2026 (Delhi High Court). The FTP on which the petitioner relies is formulated by the Central Government by exercising its power under Sections 3 and 5 of the FTDR, whereas the Notification dated 1st April 2026 has also been issued under Sections 3 and 5 of the FTDR Act, 1992, which amended the import policy of goods falling under CTH 7113 of ITC (HS) 2022, Schedule-I (Import Policy) to change from “Free” to “Restricted” with immediate effect. 13. We have heard learned counsel. We have perused the pleadings, the materials on record and the decisions relied upon in support. 16. The petitioner imported Studded Platinum Jewellery under 5 Airway Bills dated 30th March 2026 and 31st March 2026. There is no dispute that the goods arrived in India prior to the publication of the notification dated 2nd April 2026 under the FTP in respect of goods from “free” to “restricted”. There is also no dispute that the Bills of Entry for release of the goods were filed after the notification dated 2nd April 2026 was published in the Official Gazette. There is no dispute about the rate of duty or the value of the imported jewellery in the present case. The petitioner is neither disputing the power to amend the FTP nor the validity of the notification. The only contention is that the restriction does not apply to goods already arrived in India before change of FTP. Thus, the issue is the date of import for applicability of restriction imposed under the FTP on the goods, which undisputedly arrived in India well before the publication of the notification amending the CTH 7113. 17. The respondents contend that since the goods were pending clearance for home consumption, the restriction on their imports are covered by the notification and are seeking to apply the date of import under the Customs Act, 1962. Paragraph 1.05 (b) of the FTP defines the date of import for the purpose of policy. It reads thus :- “1.05(b) ……… The importability/ exportability of a particular item is governed by the policy as on the date of import/ export. The date of import/export is defined in para 2.17 of HBP 2023. Bill of Lading and Shipping Bill are the key documents for deciding the date of import and export respectively. In case of change of policy from ‘free’ to ‘restricted/prohibited/state trading’ or ‘otherwise regulated’, the import/export already made before the date of such regulation/restriction will not be affected. … ….. ” 18. In the present case, admittedly the Bill of Lading and Shipping Bill are the key documents which indicate the date of import and export respectively which is well before the change of policy from ‘free’ to ‘restricted/prohibited/state trading’ or ‘otherwise regulated’. Thus, the import/export was already made before the date of such regulation/restriction. In our view and as rightly submitted by Mr. Shah, learned Senior Advocate, once the date of import is defined under the FTP, it is not open for the respondents to take recourse to the date of import under the Customs Act. The goods were shipped in terms of the policy. 19. The issue involved in the present case is squarely covered by the decisions of the High Court of Gujarat in Enero Jewels Pvt. Ltd. & Anr. (supra) and of the High Court of Delhi in M/s. Bright Metal Refiners (supra). The goods were permitted to be cleared even though the bills of entry were filed post the publication of the notification and pending clearance with the Customs. The respondents have accepted the judgments of the High Court of Gujarat and the High Court of Delhi. Their Lordships have decisively concluded the applicability of the notification to the goods which arrived prior to the publication of the notification. 20. In Enero Jewels Pvt. Ltd. & Anr. (supra), the High Court of Gujarat directed clearance of the consignment without insisting on import authorisation. No bill of entry was filed by the petitioner before the date of the notification. IGM No.3020909 was filed by the Airline. It is only post the judgment, that the petitioner filed the bill of entry only on 22nd April 2026 and was permitted to clear the goods for home consumption without demur. 21. In M/s. Bright Metal Refiners (supra), all the goods arrived in India before the publication of Notification and some of the bills of entry were filed post the publication of notification on 3rd April 2026 and 7th April 2026. The High Court of Delhi held that the restriction did not apply to goods imported prior to the Notification coming into force and were permitted clearance in accordance with the conditions and legal position that prevailed before the issuance of the said notification. Even in M/s. Bright Metal Refiners (supra), the goods were allowed to be cleared for which the bills of entry were filed post the publication of the Notification and one of the respondents before the High Court of Delhi was the Commissioner of Customs, Air Special Cargo, Mumbai. 22. Thus, the judgments rendered in Enero Jewels Pvt. Ltd. & Anr. (supra) and M/s. Bright Metal Refiners (supra) have not only attained finality but have been acted upon. It is a settled principle of taxation jurisprudence that the Revenue is bound to maintain consistency in applying the law to similarly situated assesses. Mr. Shah, learned Senior Advocate for the petitioner, placed reliance on the decision of the Hon’ble Supreme Court in Pr. CIT, New Delhi vs. Maruti Suzuki (India) Limited16, and that of the High Court of Allahabad in Samsung India Electronics Pvt. Ltd. vs. State of U.P.17 in support. 23. It is pertinent to refer to paragraph 2.17(a) read with paragraph 11.11 of the Handbook of Procedure, 2023 (“HBP”) for the purpose of FTP, which indicates that the date of reckoning of import is to be decided with reference to the date of shipment/dispatch of goods from the supplying country and not the date of arrival of goods at an Indian port or on the date of clearance for home consumption. Paragraph 2.17(a) of the HBP is extracted below for the facility of convenience :- “2.17(a) Date of reckoning of import is decided with reference to date of shipment/dispatch of goods from supplying country as given in Paragraph 11.11 of Handbook of Procedures and not the date of arrival of goods at an Indian port.” 24. The petitioner is not claiming any benefit of transitory provisions contained in paragraph 1.05(b) of the FTP. The transitory provisions apply to goods not arrived in India before change of FTP. Paragraph 1.05(b) of the FTP is extracted below for the facility of convenience :- “1.05(b) Item wise Import/Export Policy is delineated in the ITC (HS) Schedule I and Schedule II respectively. The importability/exportability of a particular item is governed by the policy as on the date of import/export. The date of import/export is defined in para 2.17 of HBP 2023. Bill of Lading and Shipping Bill are the key documents for deciding the date of import and export respectively. In case of change of policy from ‘free’ to ‘restricted/prohibited/state trading’ or ‘otherwise regulated’, the import/export already made before the date of such regulation/restriction will not be affected. However, the import through High Sea sales will not be covered under this facility. Further, the import/export on or after the date of such regulation/restriction will be allowed for importer/exporter who has a commitment through Irrevocable Commercial Letter of Credit (ICLC) before the date of imposition of such restriction/ regulation and shall be limited to the balance quantity, value and period available in the ICLC. For operational listing of such ICLC, the applicant shall have to register the ICLC with jurisdictional RA against computerized receipt within 15 days of imposition of any such restriction/regulation. Whenever, Government brings out a policy change of a particular item, the change will be applicable prospectively (from the date of Notification) unless otherwise provided for.” 25. Further, paragraph 11.11 of the HBP specifically provides that, in the case of import by air, the date of shipment shall be the date of the relevant Airway Bill, provided it represents the date on which the goods left the last airport in the country of export. For facility of convenience paragraph 11.11 of the HBP is extracted which reads thus :- “11.11 Date of shipment/Dispatch in respect of Imports Date of shipment/dispatch for imports will be reckoned as under :- S.No. Mode of Transportation Date of Shipment/Dispatch (i) By Sea The date affixed on the Bill of Lading (ii) By Air Date of relevant Airway Bill provided this represents date on which goods left last airport in the country from which the import is effected. (iii) From land-Locked Countries Date of dispatch of goods by rail, road or other recognised mode of transport to consignee in India through consigrunent basis. (iv) By Post Parcel Date stamp of office of dispatch on the packet or dispatch note (v) By Registered Courier Service Date affixed on Courier Receipt/Waybill (vi) Multimodal Transport Date of handing over goods to first carrier in a combined transport Bill of Lading 26. The Hon’ble Supreme Court in Viraj Impex Pvt. Ltd vs. Union of India18 relying on its earlier decision in Union of India vs. G. S. Chatha Rice Mills19 held that a notification cannot acquire the force of law prior to its publication in the Official Gazette and that in the era of e-Gazette, the precise hour and minute of digital upload is decisive; and that such a notification cannot operate retrospectively unless authorised by statute. 27. Thus in view of what has been held by Their Lordships, we have no hesitation in holding that the Notification acquired the force of law only on 2nd April 2026 at 20:52:58 hours, and it can be validly applied only to the goods dispatched from the country of export after its publication and not to goods already dispatched and arrived in India prior to its publication. 28. We may make a useful reference to the decision of the High Court of Delhi in M. D. Overseas Limited (supra) which holds that the date of import, for the purpose of application of the provisions of the FTP and HBP, would be the date of shipment/dispatch of goods from the supplying country of export. This date would ordinarily be the date of airway bill issued by the last port from which the goods departed en route to India. M. D. Overseas Limited (supra) has been approved by the Hon’ble Supreme Court in G. S. Chatha Rice Mills (supra). 29. Let us now deal with the submission of Mr. Mishra, learned counsel for the respondent, that the date of filing of bills of entry is relevant for determining the applicability of the notification issued under the FTP. In our view, the date of filing bills of entry or its clearance/out-of-charge post the restriction is wholly irrelevant and alien to the applicability of a change in the FTP. In terms of Section 15 of the Customs Act, the date of filing of bills of entry is relevant for the rate of duty of customs and, in the present case, the rate of duty is the same prior to and post the Notification. The contention of Mr. Mishra, learned counsel for the respondent is that the decision in Enero Jewels Pvt. Ltd. & Anr. (supra) and M/s. Bright Metal Refiners (supra) are per incuriam having not considered and followed the ratio of the decisions of the Hon’ble Supreme Court in Union of India and others vs. Ganesh Das Bhojraj (supra) and Kiran Spinning Mills (supra). We are in agreement with Mr. Shah, learned Senior Advocate that the aforesaid decisions in Union of India and others vs. Ganesh Das Bhojraj (supra) and Kiran Spinning Mills (supra) are distinguishable. The issue before the Hon’ble Supreme Court was not the one in dispute in the present case, viz. which is the date to be reckoned for import under the FTP. 30. In Union of India and others vs. Ganesh Das Bhojraj (supra), the petitioner had imported the goods and filed bill of entry on 5th February 1987 and claimed clearance duty free. Vide notification issued one day prior to filing of the Bill of Entry, the Government had imposed 25 per cent duty. The petitioner therein contended that the said notification though published was not made available to the public at large and therefore customs duty cannot be levied. This Court allowed the writ petition and directed clearance of goods without payment of duty. The Hon’ble Supreme Court, on a reference, held that the notification came into force on the date of its publication in the official Gazette and negatived the contention that the since the notification was not made available to the public at large it did not come into force. Thus, Union of India and others vs. Ganesh Das Bhojraj (supra) dealt with a case regarding change of rate of duty and not applicability of the rate of duty under the Customs Act. Under Section 15(1)(a) of the Customs Act, the relevant date for the rate of customs duty is the date of filing of the bill of entry. Therefore, the rate of duty will be as applicable on the date of filing of bill of entry and not the arrival of the goods in India. Thus, the issue before the Hon’ble Supreme Court was in the context of Section 15 of the Customs Act. 31. Let us now look at the facts in Kiran Spinning Mills (supra). The importer deposited the goods in the bonded warehouse. Pending clearance of the goods for home consumption, additional duty of excise was imposed vide ordinance dated 3rd October 1978. The Importer contended that the said ordinance was not promulgated at the time of import into India. The Hon’ble Supreme Court negatived the contention holding that Section 3(6) of the Ordinance makes the provisions of the Customs Act applicable and this would bring into place Section 15 of the Customs act, which inter alia provides the rate of duty which will be payable on the date on which the goods are removed from the bonded warehouse. 32. It would therefore be clear that for the purpose of Customs Act, the import would be completed when the goods cross the customs barriers and not the day on which the goods had landed in India. On the other hand, for the purpose of reckoning the date of import for applicability of the provisions of FTP is specified in paragraph 1.05(b) of the FTP read with paragraph 2.17 read with paragraph 11.11 of HBP. Paragraph 1.5 (b) of FTP clearly provides that the date of import/export is defined in paragraph 2.17 of HBP. Bill of Lading or shipping bill are key documents for deciding the date of import and export respectively. Having defined the date of import under the FTP, the date of import under the Customs Act cannot be applied, otherwise the date of import defined in the Policy shall be redundant and meaningless. We are of the clear opinion that paragraphs 2 and 3 of the notification have no relevance to the goods imported by the petitioner as they have admittedly arrived in India before the Notification came into force. That the Notification is not made retrospective is admitted by the respondents. 33. Mr. Mishra, learned counsel for the respondent relied on an Office Memorandum (“OM”) dated 13th May 2026 issued by the DGFT after the filing of the present writ petition, in which the Joint Director General of Foreign Trade has, in paragraph 3 thereof, purportedly clarified that “any import consignment not cleared before 1st April 2026 would be governed by the revised ‘Restricted’ policy, irrespective of the date of arrival of goods or fling of Bills of Entry ….”. We are in agreement with the submission of Mr. Shah, learned Senior Advocate that the said OM is not only contrary to the FTP but in the teeth of what has been held in Enero Jewels Pvt. Ltd. & Anr. (supra) and M/s. Bright Metal Refiners (supra). The said OM has no statutory force and cannot override the FTP. The OM is not even subordinate legislation and no authority of law is pointed out in exercise of which the said OM is issued. The Hon’ble Supreme Court in CCE, Bolpur vs. Ratan Melting and Wire Industries20 has held that any clarification of the Board contrary to law declared by the Hon’ble Supreme Court and Hon’ble High Courts is not binding. It was further held that a clarification contrary to statutory provisions has no existence in law. It is well settled that FTP can be amended by the Central Government in exercise of powers under Section 5 of the FTDR Act. The purported clarification by the OM, would literally amount to amending the FTP, which cannot be issued in such a manner contrary to law. The purported clarification amounts to giving retrospective effect to the Notification, which is beyond the jurisdiction of the issuing authority (Officers of the DGFT in the present case) as held by the Hon’ble Supreme Court in Director General of Foreign Trade vs. Kanak Exports21. In these circumstances, the OM being a mere executive clarification, cannot rewrite/override the statutory definition of “date of import” defined in paragraph 2.17 and paragraph 11.11 of the HBP, and cannot make the impugned Notification retrospective. 34. We therefore have no hesitation in holding that the import authorisation cannot be treated as the redressal of the petitioner’s grievance, in view of the decisions in Enero Jewels Pvt. Ltd. & Anr. (supra) and M/s. Bright Metal Refiners (supra). The said authorisation-imposed conditions, which were beyond the jurisdiction and authority of the DGFT. 35. The Writ Petition therefore deserves to be allowed. The respondents are directed to release the goods/consignments covered under Bills of Entry Nos.8590663 dated 10th April 2026, 8590666 dated 10th April 2026 and 8735308 dated 17th April 2026 expeditiously. 36.

(GSTAT Bengaluru) Summary: GSTAT Bengaluru allowed Fortinet Technologies India Private Limited’s appeal concerning July 2017 to March 2018 and set aside the adjudication order dated 25.04.2023 and appellate order dated 27.06.2025. The disputed demand comprised CGST of Rs. 1,09,815 and SGST of Rs. 1,09,815, together with interest and a 10% penalty. The adjudication table recorded an aggregate demand of Rs. 4,75,500. The dispute arose from a GST audit that initially raised four observations. After the taxpayer’s explanations, several objections were dropped, while two office-meal expense entries remained disputed under a general heading referring to hiring charges, loan documentation charges and reverse charge under Section 9(3). Fortinet separately agreed to reverse the disputed Krishi Kalyan Cess credit and paid the amount relating to blocked credit; it contested the meal-expense demand. The department issued only a rectified Form GST DRC-01 dated 21.03.2023, referring to the final audit report, without a substantive show-cause notice explaining the taxable supplies, valuation, rate or precise legal basis. The adjudication order preceded Fortinet’s detailed reply communicated on 28.04.2023, and no personal hearing was granted. Fortinet argued that registered catering vendors had charged GST under forward charge, that certain entries represented reimbursements, provisions or reversals, and that it had not collected meal charges from employees. It also relied on Circular No. 172/04/2022-GST dated 06.07.2022 concerning contractual employment perquisites. The Revenue maintained that the audit supplied the necessary grounds and relied on the catering supplier’s registration date and invoices relating to another financial year. The Tribunal held that a notice under Section 73(1) is the foundation of adjudication. Rule 142 requires the DRC-01 summary to accompany that notice; the summary cannot replace it. An audit report in Form GST ADT-02 likewise does not dispense with the statutory notice. Relying on the cited Supreme Court, High Court and tribunal authorities, GSTAT held that the complete absence of a notice was not a curable defect under Section 160. Fortinet had consistently raised the objection, and the dispute concerned absence of the notice itself rather than defective service. Independently, Section 75(4) required a personal hearing because an adverse decision was contemplated. A reply submitted beyond 30 days did not extinguish that right. The Tribunal did not accept the separate argument that absence of DIN/RFN invalidated the communications for the relevant period. It explained that the CBIC circulars bound CBIC officers, while Karnataka’s specific RFN circular was issued on 23.05.2023, after initiation of the proceedings in question. Although it considered the Supreme Court’s decision in Pradeep Goyal and the Andhra Pradesh decisions on DIN, including the Supreme Court’s stay in the Novelty Reddy matter, it declined to invalidate these particular communications on that ground. GSTAT further held that the first appellate authority could not sustain the demand on a new legal basis involving unregistered catering supplies under Section 9(4), or employee recoveries under forward charge, without first putting Fortinet on notice. A factual explanation advanced in defence did not become a charge against the taxpayer. On merits, outdoor catering was not among the services notified under Section 9(3). The Tribunal distinguished that provision from the then applicable Section 9(4), whose operation during the disputed year was confined to 01.07.2017–12.10.2017, subject to the Rs. 5,000 daily exemption for intra-State purchases from unregistered suppliers. The authorities had neither identified qualifying supplies within that period nor applied the exemption, despite demanding tax for the whole year. They also failed to establish any taxable employee recovery, its value or the person liable. Decisions concerning mere citation of an incorrect provision were distinguishable because this case lacked a proper notice and clear factual charges. The demand therefore failed on merits as well as procedure; consequential interest and penalty also fell. The Tribunal directed refund of any pre-deposit with interest in accordance with law and made no order as to costs. Cases Discussed Rohit Vij Proprietor of RRR Enterprises v. (ii) Whether an adverse adjudication order passed without extending an opportunity of hearing to the Appellant violates Section 75(4) of the Act and the principles of natural justice? (iii) Whether the Form GST DRC-01 and the adjudication order are invalid for want of a Document Identification Number (DIN) or Reference Number (RFN)? (iv) Whether the first appellate authority could sustain the demand on grounds not set out in the adjudication order? (v) Whether, on merits, the Appellant was required to pay GST under RCM under Section 9(3) of the Act on the expenses towards ‘meals’, ‘office meals’ ? No SCN issued; Principle of natural justice 4.3 On the first issue the Appellant vehemently argued that the adjudication proceedings are invalid for the reason that only a DRC-01 summary was issued, rather than a detailed notice under Section 73(1) of the Act, which did not identify the taxable value, factual basis, or reasoning for tax, interest, and penalty. The Appellant relies on the decision of Hon’ble Guwahati High Court in the case of Construction Catalysers Pvt. Ltd. 24 Centax 122 (Gau.) /2025 and the decision of Hon’ble Jharkhand High court in the case of NKAS Services Pvt Ltd 2022 (58) G.S.T.L. 257 (Jhar.). 4.4 We observe that neither the Adjudicating authority nor the Appellate Authority examined this contention or gave their findings in the respective orders. A Show cause notice is the foundation of the adjudication proceedings under Tax laws. 4.5 Section 73 deals with issue regarding ‘Determination of tax not paid or short paid or erroneously refunded or input tax credit wrongly availed or utilised for any reason other than fraud or any willful-misstatement or suppression of facts’. Section 73 (1) and (2) is relevant here which reads for the relevant period thus: (1) Where it appears to the proper officer that any tax has not been paid or short paid or erroneously refunded, or where input tax credit has been wrongly availed or utilised for any reason, other than the reason of fraud or any wilful-misstatement or suppression of facts to evade tax, he shall serve notice on the person chargeable with tax which has not been so paid or which has been so short paid or to whom the refund has erroneously been made, or who has wrongly availed or utilised input tax credit, requiring him to show cause as to why he should not pay the amount specified in the notice along with interest payable thereon under section 50 and a penalty leviable under the provisions of this Act or the rules made thereunder. (2) The proper officer shall issue the notice under sub-section (1) at least three months prior to the time limit specified in sub-section (10) for issuance of order. [Emphasis supplied] 4.6 Sub section (1) of Section 73 makes it mandatory on the Proper officer if it appears to him that tax is unpaid, short-paid, wrongly refunded, or that input tax credit (ITC) was wrongly claimed, by a person, without any fraud, wilful misstatement or suppression of facts to evade tax, to issue a show-cause notice on such person, to explain why he should not pay the specified tax amount along with applicable interest under Section 50 and applicable penalties under the Act or its rules. It is trite law that any order passed by the quasi-judicial authorities in contravention of the principles of natural justice, cannot be sustained. 4.10 A Noticee must know the case he has to meet. This communication would happen only by issue of a proper notice show causing the party as to why there was a short payment etc. The Honourable High Court of Kerala in K.T. Saidalavi v. It was held by Hon’ble Supreme Court in CCE v. Brindavan Beverages (P.) Ltd. (2007) 5 SCC 388 that the show cause notice was the foundation on which the department had to build up its case. If the allegations in the show cause notice were not specific and were on the contrary vague, lacked details and/or unintelligible that is sufficient to hold that the noticee was not given proper opportunity to meet the allegations indicated in the show cause notice. 4.12 The above decisions were rendered under the erstwhile Central Excise Law. Under the GST Law also the Honourable Apex Court and High Courts have interfered with adjudication proceedings that violated from the principle of natural justice. 4.13 It was held by the Hon’ble Supreme Court in Armour Security (India) Ltd. v. The relevant para of the Judgement reads: 65. A show cause notice is a document served on a noticee, requiring them to explain why a particular action should not be initiated against them. Under the GST regime, issuance of a show cause notice is a mandatory precondition for raising a demand. It forms the bedrock for proceedings related to the recovery of tax, interest, and penalty. The notice ensures adherence to the principles of natural justice by granting the assessee an opportunity to present their case before any adverse action is taken. In essence, it serves as both a procedural safeguard and a legal necessity, marking the commencement of quasi-judicial adjudication under the Act. 66. A show cause notice sets the law in motion concerning the liability under the statute, containing charges that a specific person is called upon to answer. In other words, it sets out the alleged violations of legal provisions and requires the assessee to explain why the duty should not be recovered from them. Thus, a show cause notice cannot be vague, nor can any allegations be made without evidence being commensurate with the gravity of the charges levelled against the noticee. 67. The materials obtained through summons and relied upon for issuing the show cause notice must be appended and disclosed to the assessee. In essence, a show cause notice enumerates the charges levelled against the noticee. 4.14 The Hon’ble Guwahati High Court in the case of M/s Construction Catalysers Pvt. Ltd. (B) The Show Cause Notice to be issued in terms with Section 73 (1) of the Central Act or State Act cannot be confused with the Statement of the determination of tax to be issued in terms with Section 73 (3) of the Central Act or the State Act. No opportunity of personal hearing extended; Principle of natural justice 4.24 The summary of the demand in Form DRC 01 dated 21.03.2023 was served on the Appellant on 23.03.2023. It reads: (4) An opportunity of hearing shall be granted where a request is received in writing from the person chargeable with tax or penalty, or where any adverse decision is contemplated against such person. In Eminent Logistics vs. Assistant Commissioner of State Tax (Calcutta HC, WPA 3877 of 2025), 2025 (6) TMI 1843- Calcutta High Court set aside a final assessment order issued under Section 73 because the Show Cause Notice (SCN) was “conspicuously silent” on the date and time of a personal hearing. It was held by the Allahabad High Court in Bharat Mint and Allied Chemicals v. Commissioner Commercial Tax, 2022 (3) TMI 492 – Allahabad High Court that if an adverse order is being planned, a personal hearing must be given regardless of whether the taxpayer requested it. It further ruled that the only scenario where a personal hearing is not required is when the tax authority fully accepts the taxpayer’s written explanation and drops the demand entirely. 4.27 Similar provisions were available under the erstwhile Indirect tax laws also. An opportunity to be heard is intended to be afforded to the person who is likely to be prejudiced when the order is made, before making the order thereof. “The provision of section 11A(1) and (2) of the Central Excises and Salt Act, 1944 make it clear that the statutory scheme is that in the situations covered by the sub-section(1), a notice of show cause has to be issued and sub-section(2) requires that the cause shown by way of representation has to be considered by the prescribed authority and then only the amount has to be determined. The scheme is in consonance with the rules of natural justice. Further, alternatively, it held that the amount was considered as amount collected from Employees towards supply of meals but confirmed the tax under Section 9(3) of the Act. No supporting ledger account or proof of payment was referred to before confirming the demand. 4.62 The audit report in Form GST ADT-02 dated 28.12.2022 described the amount as ‘Tax Payer deducted Amount from Employee and for the same TP is to discharge Output Tax’. Yet no action was taken to demand tax on this account under forward charge. The impugned amount, however, was available in Audit Report under expenses head and not under receipt head. In GST Tax is charged on a supply. Tax under the Act is levied on a supply. Without establishing a supply, the value of the supply and the person liable, tax cannot be demanded. 4.63 In view of the above discussion on merits we are of the view that the Respondent failed to establish their case that the Appellant was required to pay GST under RCM under Section 9(3) of the Act or even under Section 9(4) of the Act on the expenses under the heads ‘70710-097- 398-Office meals’ and ‘70710-097- 637-Office meals’. The demand fails on merits as well. Issue (v) is answered in favour of the Appellant. 4.64 As the demand of tax fails, the interest under Section 50 and the penalty confirmed in the impugned order also fall. 5.1 In the result, we order as follows: (i) The Adjudication Order dated 25.04.2023 and the Impugned order dated 27.06.2025 is set aside. (ii) The demand of CGST of Rs 1,09,815 and SGST of Rs 1,09,815 is set aside along with interest confirmed under Section 50 of the Act and penalty imposed at 10% on the tax confirmed under CGST and SGST. (iii) The pre-deposit, if any, shall be refunded with interest in accordance with law. (iv) There shall be no order as to cost. 5.2 The appeal is allowed. Order pronounced on 27th October 2026.

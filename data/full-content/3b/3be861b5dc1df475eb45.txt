Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); Bharugauasharam Dharbudi Tirthdham Vikash Trust Vs ITO (ITAT Rajkot) Free Meals at a Pilgrimage Site Can Qualify as Charity for Section 80G Approval: ITAT Rajkot Rajkot ITAT examines the nature of Annashetra expenditure Can a charitable trust be denied Section 80G approval because it serves free meals to pilgrims? In Bharugauasharam Dharbudi Tirthdham Vikash Trust v. ITO (Exemption), ITA No. 840/RJT/2025, decided on 20 November 2026, the Rajkot ITAT examined whether running an Annashetra at a pilgrimage site made the trust’s expenditure religious. The Bench considered who could access the meals, the trust’s wider charitable objects and the 5% limit under Section 80G(5B). It directed the Commissioner of Income Tax (Exemption) to consider and allow the claim in accordance with law. Why the Commissioner rejected Form 10AB The trust, registered under the Gujarat Public Trust Act in 1996, applied for approval under Section 80G(5) in Form 10AB on 12 February 2025. Its deed provided for an Atithi Gruh, Bhojanalay and Annashetra to offer accommodation and food at Dhrabudi in Mandvi, Kutch. Its other objects included medical relief, education, scholarships, assistance to the poor, cattle protection, disaster relief and rural development. The CIT(E), Ahmedabad, treated the substantial expenditure on the Annashetra as religious because the facility primarily served pilgrims visiting the Tirthdham. On that classification, the Commissioner concluded that religious expenditure exceeded the 5% ceiling. The Commissioner also invoked Section 80G(5)(ii), which deals with provisions in the governing instrument permitting application of income or assets for purposes other than charity, and Explanation 3, which excludes purposes that are wholly or substantially wholly religious. Public access mattered more than the location The trust submitted that the Bhojanalay and Annashetra served free food to the public, including poor and needy persons, without restrictions based on caste, creed or religion. Although the trust deed referred to pilgrims, the facility was open to the wider public. The trust argued that serving people who had travelled for religious reasons did not, by itself, change the character of the meal service. The Tribunal examined the deed as a whole and found the trust’s objects significantly charitable. Its activities included medical and financial assistance, educational support and provision of food, clothing and shelter. In that setting, the Annashetra’s public character was material to deciding whether its expenditure belonged in the religious category. A beneficiary’s reason for visiting the place does not, by itself, determine the purpose for which the trust spends its money. The 5% test applies to religious expenditure for each year Section 80G(5B) overrides Section 80G(5)(ii) and Explanation 3 within its stated scope. It allows an institution to remain eligible where expenditure of a religious nature does not exceed 5% of its total income for the same previous year, subject to the other applicable conditions. The exercise therefore begins by identifying expenditure that is religious in character and then measuring it against the income of that year. The trust’s figures showed nil religious expenditure in FY 2021–22 and FY 2023–24. For FY 2022–23, it reported religious expenditure of ₹10,04,020 against total income of ₹2,81,35,657, or about 3.56%. The Commissioner’s conclusion that the ceiling had been breached depended on including Annashetra expenditure in the religious category. The dispute over classification therefore directly affected the outcome of the statutory calculation. Earlier decisions considered by the Tribunal The Bench referred to Shri Balaji Samaj Vikas Samiti, 91 taxmann.com 26 (Allahabad), concerning the preparation and supply of midday meals under a government scheme. That decision supported the proposition that supplying meals can serve a charitable purpose under Section 2(15). The more direct precedent was Gurukrupa Foundation v. CIT(E) [2025] 172 taxmann.com 737 (Ahmedabad ITAT). The coordinate Bench held that a substantially religious object among several charitable objects did not determine the character of the entire trust and directed verification of actual expenditure under Section 80G(5B). The trust also distinguished Upper Ganges Sugar Mills Ltd. v. CIT [1997] 93 Taxman 645 (SC), cited by the Commissioner. That judgment concerned the law before the insertion of Section 80G(5B) with effect from 1 April 2000. The statutory tolerance for limited religious expenditure was therefore absent from the framework considered in that case. Author’s comments The practical point is that classification comes before computation. Where a trust runs a free kitchen at a religious destination, evidence of public access and the manner in which meals are served can establish the character of the activity. A year-wise reconciliation of kitchen expenses and spending on worship or ceremonies can then show which amounts should enter the 5% calculation. The distinction matters especially where the deed mentions pilgrims but the actual service extends to everyone. The operative direction also matters. The Tribunal allowed the appeal for statistical purposes and directed the CIT(E) to consider and allow the claim in accordance with law, following the coordinate Bench decision. The ruling thus gives the trust the benefit of reconsideration under the correct statutory test, with the Commissioner responsible for giving effect to the direction. Vismruti Social And Charitable Trust vs CIT(E) ITA No 1551/Ahd/2024(Ahd-Trib.) We have heard the rival contentions and perused the material on record. It would be useful to reproduce the relevant extracts of the Section 80G(5) which states that this section applies to donation to any institution or fund only if it is established in India for a “charitable purposes”. Explanation 3 to Section 80G states that the term “charitable purpose” does not include any purpose the whole or substantially the whole of which is of a religious nature. Further, Section 80G(5B) states that notwithstanding Explanation 3 any institution or fund which incurs expenditure which is of a religious nature for an amount not exceeding 5% of it’s total income for that previous year shall be deemed to be an institution or fund to which the provisions of Section 80G shall apply. Now from a combined reading of these provisions, it is apparent that in case any trust applies expends less than 5% of it’s income towards religious purposes, then it cannot be denied benefit of deduction under Section 80G of the Act on this basis alone. Further, even the definition of the term “charitable purposes” has been explained to mean the purpose “the whole and substantially the whole” of which is of a religious nature. Therefore, even as per Explanation 3, referred to above, in which to qualify as “charitable purpose”, the only qualification is that the activities should not be wholly or substantially religious. Therefore, in view of the statutory provisions quoted above, we are of the considered view that the application for grant of deduction under Section 80G cannot be denied to the assessee only on the ground that one of the objects content the term “religious”. Shri Sant ZolebabaSansthan Chikhali v. This fact is further fortified by sub-section (5B) of section 80G which provides that institution or fund which incurs expenditure during any previous year, which is of a religious nature for an amount not exceeding 5% of its total income in that previous year, shall be deemed to be an institution or fund to which the provisions of this section apply.

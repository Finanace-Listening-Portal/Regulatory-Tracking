Summary: Section 74A of the CGST Act, 2017, introduced by the Finance (No. 2) Act, 2024 and effective from 1 November 2024, establishes a unified framework for GST demands relating to FY 2024-25 onwards, replacing the earlier distinction between Sections 73 and 74 for determining tax liabilities. Under the new provision, show cause notices must generally be issued within 42 months from the due date of the relevant annual return or the date of an erroneous refund, while adjudication orders must ordinarily be passed within 12 months of the notice, extendable by six months subject to prescribed conditions. Although fraud and non-fraud cases are covered under the same provision, penalties remain different: non-fraud cases attract 10% of the tax or ₹10,000, whichever is higher, whereas fraud, wilful misstatement or suppression may attract a penalty equal to the tax, with substantial reductions available for timely payment. Taxpayers can avoid penalties in non-fraud cases by paying tax and interest within 60 days of the notice, while fraud cases offer reduced penalties of 15%, 25% or 50%, depending on the payment stage. Businesses receiving GST notices should carefully verify the applicable financial year, statutory provision, limitation period, allegations of suppression, reconciliation of GSTR-1, GSTR-3B and GSTR-2B, and opportunities for early payment to minimise penalties and litigation. Brief: From FY 2024-25, GST demands are no longer raised under Section 73 or Section 74. A single provision, Section 74A, now applies, with one 42-month time limit and a revised penalty scheme. This article explains which section governs which year, how the new time limits and penalties work, and what a taxpayer should check first when a notice for FY 2024-25 arrives. In practice, a common error, both in replies and at times in the notices themselves, is to treat these years exactly like the earlier ones. They are not the same. For the first seven years of GST, demands were raised under two separate provisions. Section 73 covered cases where tax was short paid or input tax credit wrongly availed for reasons other than fraud. Section 74 covered cases involving fraud, wilful misstatement or suppression of facts. Each had its own time limit and its own penalty. The choice of section was often the first battleground in a dispute, because it decided how far back the department could go and how heavy the penalty could be. The Finance (No. 2) Act, 2024 changed this. It inserted Section 74A in the CGST Act, which now governs demands for FY 2024-25 onwards. Sections 73 and 74 continue, but only for periods up to FY 2023-24. This change matters for two reasons. First, a notice for FY 2024-25 issued under the old sections cites a provision that does not govern that period. Second, the time limits and penalty rules are different, so the strategy for replying, and for deciding whether to pay part of the demand early, is also different. This article sets out the position in simple terms and ends with a short checklist that can be used when a notice arrives. Which GST Demand Section Applies to Which Financial Year? Section 74A was inserted by the Finance (No. 2) Act, 2024 and brought into force from 1 November 2024 through Notification No. 17/2024-Central Tax. It applies to tax not paid or short paid, erroneous refunds, and input tax credit wrongly availed or utilised for FY 2024-25 onwards. Up to FY 2023-24: Section 73 (cases without fraud) and Section 74 (fraud, wilful misstatement or suppression of facts) continue to apply. FY 2024-25 onwards: Section 74A applies to all cases. The fraud or non-fraud question still matters, but now only for penalty, not for the choice of section or the time limit. The first thing to check in any notice is therefore simple: which tax period does it cover, and under which section has it been issued? A notice for FY 2024-25 issued under Section 73 or Section 74 cites a provision that does not apply to that period. This is a point worth raising in the reply, along with the merits. Equally, a notice for FY 2023-24 or earlier cannot be issued under Section 74A. No notice is to be issued under Section 74A where the tax involved for a financial year is less than Rs 1,000. Time Limits for GST Notices and Orders Under Section 74A Notice: within 42 months from the due date of the annual return for the financial year to which the demand relates, or within 42 months from the date of the erroneous refund. Order: within 12 months from the date of the notice. Where the proper officer cannot pass the order in time, the Commissioner, or an officer authorised by him who is senior to the proper officer and not below the rank of Joint Commissioner, may extend this by up to 6 months, after recording reasons in writing. Under the old scheme, there was no fixed outer limit for the notice itself. Instead, the order had to be passed within 3 years (Section 73) or 5 years (Section 74) from the due date of the annual return, and the notice had to be issued at least 3 months or 6 months before that. In effect, the last date for a notice was 33 months for non-fraud cases and 54 months for fraud cases. The single 42-month limit therefore cuts both ways. A non-fraud case can now be opened 9 months later than before, while the window for a fraud case is 12 months shorter. As an illustration, the annual return for FY 2024-25 was due on 31 December 2025. Unless that due date was extended, a notice for FY 2024-25 can be issued up to 30 June 2029. For a detailed comparison of the old and new limitation periods, see Impact of Budget 2024 with respect to GST. Penalty Under Section 74A of the CGST Act Where There Is No Fraud, Wilful Misstatement or Suppression Penalty is 10% of the tax or Rs 10,000, whichever is higher. No penalty is payable if the tax and interest are paid before the notice is issued. No penalty is payable if the tax and interest are paid within 60 days of the notice. The proceedings are then deemed to be concluded. Where There Is Fraud, Wilful Misstatement or Suppression Penalty is equal to the tax. Reduced to 15% of the tax if tax, interest and penalty are paid before the notice. Reduced to 25% of the tax if paid within 60 days of the notice. Reduced to 50% of the tax if paid within 60 days of the order. One more point is often missed. Section 75(2) has been amended so that where an appellate authority or court holds that the charge of fraud or suppression is not sustained, the tax and penalty are redetermined as if the notice had been issued for a non-fraud case. Even so, it is far better to contest an unsupported allegation of suppression at the reply stage itself, rather than wait for appeal. What to Check When a GST Notice Arrives 1. Download the full notice and its annexures from the GST portal, not just the summary in DRC-01. The summary does not carry the department’s full reasoning, and replies drafted only on the summary often miss the actual point of dispute. 2. Check the period and the section. For FY 2024-25 onwards, the notice should be under Section 74A. For earlier years, it should be under Section 73 or Section 74. 3. Check the time limit against the due date of the annual return for that year, keeping in mind any extension of that due date. 4. Check whether fraud or suppression is alleged, and on what basis. The penalty consequences differ sharply, so a bare allegation without supporting facts should be answered directly. 5. Reconcile the figures in the notice with GSTR-1, GSTR-3B, GSTR-2B and the books before drafting the reply. Many demands arise from timing differences, credit notes or amendments that a reconciliation explains. 6. Decide on payment early. Where part of the demand is clearly correct, paying that portion with interest within 60 days of the notice removes the penalty on it in a non-fraud case and reduces it in a fraud case. The 60-day window runs from the notice, so this decision cannot wait until the hearing. For procedural guidance, refer to GST DRC-01 Notice Under Section 73, 74, 74A: A Guide to Rules & Reply. Conclusion: Preparing for GST Demands Under Section 74A Section 74A brings a welcome uniformity to GST demands. There is now one section, one time limit and one procedure for all cases from FY 2024-25 onwards, with the fraud or non-fraud distinction moved to the stage of penalty. For taxpayers, this removes some of the old uncertainty about which section the department would invoke. At the same time, the new provision does not make replies any less technical. The longer window for non-fraud cases means that businesses should preserve records and reconciliations for FY 2024-25 well into 2029. The 60-day payment window makes an early, honest assessment of the notice more valuable than ever, because the penalty saved by timely payment can be significant. In practice, the order of work matters. Getting the period, the section and the limitation right comes first. Then the figures need to be reconciled and the basis of any fraud allegation tested. Only after this does a reply on merits carry its full weight. A careful reply at the first stage often avoids a long appeal later.

The level is being watched closely by global investors because a further rise in US yields could increase the relative attractiveness of dollar assets and raise the global cost of capital. Advt The pressure is already visible in Indian debt markets. The yield on the benchmark 6.94% 2036 government bond moved towards 7% as US Treasury yields rose and crude oil prices climbed. India's 10-year bond yield subsequently touched the 7% mark intraday before closing below it.Indian equities have also come under pressure as higher global yields, elevated crude prices and foreign selling weigh on sentiment. The impact, however, is unlikely to come from US yields alone, with oil prices, expectations for US monetary policy, domestic liquidity and valuations also influencing the market.Why a 5% US yield matters for IndiaUS Treasuries serve as a benchmark for global borrowing costs and are widely regarded as a relatively low-risk dollar asset. When Treasury yields rise, investors reassess the returns they can obtain from emerging-market assets relative to US securities.A further rise towards 5% could therefore increase the risk of foreign portfolio outflows from markets such as India, particularly if the move is accompanied by a stronger dollar or expectations of tighter US monetary policy. It does not, however, automatically mean that foreign investors will sell Indian assets. India's growth outlook, valuations, currency expectations and domestic liquidity would also determine the direction of flows. Advt The transmission to India can also happen through the rupee. Higher US yields can support the dollar and make emerging-market currencies more vulnerable. A weaker rupee, combined with higher crude prices, could increase India's imported inflation pressures.Indian bonds face pressureThe rise in US Treasury yields is already feeding into India's government bond market, although domestic factors are also playing a role.The benchmark 10-year Indian government bond yield touched 7% during trading on September 2 and settled at 6.98%, up from 6.96% previously. Dealers attributed the move partly to higher US yields and crude prices. Advt Higher domestic yields have implications for existing bondholders because bond prices and yields move inversely. When market yields rise, the prices of outstanding fixed-rate bonds generally fall, creating mark-to-market pressure, particularly for longer-duration securities.At the same time, higher yields on newly issued government securities can make fixed-income investments more attractive. The relevant issue for investors is therefore not simply whether bonds become attractive, but whether the rise in yields is large enough to compensate for the additional interest-rate and market risk.Equity valuations could face a reset Advt The biggest impact on Indian equities could come through valuations rather than an immediate deterioration in corporate earnings.Higher US Treasury yields raise the global risk-free rate. This increases the discount rate used to value future corporate cash flows and can reduce the price investors are willing to pay for those earnings.Expensively valued parts of the Indian market could therefore face greater pressure if the US 10-year yield moves towards 5%. Richly valued consumer stocks, new-age technology companies and the more expensive segments of the small- and mid-cap universe could be particularly vulnerable to multiple compression.That does not necessarily imply a broad market crash. If India's domestic growth and corporate earnings remain strong, the adjustment could instead take the form of a rotation away from expensive stocks towards companies with more reasonable valuations and stronger cash flows.Crude oil could amplify the impactThe rise in US yields is taking place alongside a sharp increase in crude oil prices, creating an additional risk for India.Brent crude rose to around $95 a barrel as escalating tensions in the Middle East raised concerns over energy supplies. India, as a major crude importer, is particularly sensitive to sustained increases in oil prices.Higher crude prices can increase India's import bill, put pressure on the rupee and raise inflation expectations. If the increase proves persistent, it could also reduce the scope for monetary easing or increase market expectations of future rate increases.This creates a potentially adverse combination for Indian markets: higher global yields can put pressure on capital flows, while higher oil prices can simultaneously affect inflation, the currency and interest-rate expectations.What it means for foreign flowsThe impact on foreign investors will depend heavily on the relative return available from Indian assets.A US Treasury yield approaching 5% could make dollar-denominated fixed-income assets more competitive, particularly if investors also expect the dollar to strengthen. This could increase selling pressure on emerging-market equities and bonds.Foreign investors have already returned to selling Indian equities, with recent outflows occurring against a backdrop of higher crude prices and US bond yields.But India is not entirely dependent on foreign flows. Strong domestic institutional participation and household investment through systematic investment plans can provide some cushion against global selling.The RBI and rupee are important buffersThe eventual impact on Indian markets will also depend on the RBI's response.The rupee has faced pressure from higher oil prices and global rate expectations, but RBI intervention has helped contain volatility. The currency closed at around Rs 94.49 per dollar on September 7, while the central bank has been actively managing liquidity and foreign-exchange conditions.Indian government bonds also retain support from strong domestic fundamentals and banking-system liquidity, meaning a rise in US yields does not automatically translate into a proportionate rise in Indian yields.What investors should watchThe key question is not simply whether the US 10-year Treasury yield touches 5%, but whether it stays there and why it gets there.A temporary move caused by market positioning would have a different impact from a sustained rise driven by higher US inflation, larger fiscal deficits or expectations of tighter monetary policy. The latter would pose a greater risk to emerging-market assets.For Indian investors, the main indicators to watch will therefore be the US Federal Reserve's policy signals, US inflation and employment data, crude oil prices, the rupee, FII flows and India's government borrowing programme.A sustained rise in US yields towards 5%, particularly if accompanied by higher oil prices and a stronger dollar, could create a difficult environment for Indian bonds and expensive equity segments. But the threshold itself should not be treated as a trigger for a market crash.The bigger risk for India would be a prolonged tightening of global financial conditions, rather than the US 10-year yield simply crossing the 5% mark.

(NCLAT Delhi) Summary: The National Company Law Appellate Tribunal (NCLAT), Principal Bench, New Delhi, dismissed the appeal filed by Rahee Jhajharia E to E JV against MB Power (Madhya Pradesh) Ltd., upholding the NCLT Indore Bench’s order dated 25 September 2024 rejecting an application under Section 9 of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016. The appellant sought recovery of approximately Rs.16.08 crore arising from railway siding construction work associated with MB Power’s thermal power project in Anuppur, Madhya Pradesh. The central question was whether the project owner could be treated as the corporate debtor when the purchase orders, work orders and invoices related to another company within the same corporate group. The appellant contended that MB Power had initiated the tender, issued preliminary contractual communications, participated in meetings, arranged financial instruments, supervised project execution and signed the completion certificate. It also argued that MB Power had funded the project and ultimately owned the completed infrastructure. Relying on these circumstances and the Supreme Court’s judgment in Arcelormittal India Private Limited v. Satish Kumar Gupta & Ors., (2019) 2 SCC 1, the appellant submitted that the corporate veil of Hindustan Thermal–EPC should be lifted to establish MB Power’s liability. MB Power disputed the existence of any debtor-creditor relationship. It maintained that the purchase order and work order were issued by Hindustan Thermal–EPC, that the appellant raised its invoices against that company and that the principal payments were also made by Hindustan Thermal–EPC. MB Power further submitted that the appellant had initially served a demand notice on Hindustan Thermal–EPC concerning 92 invoices, withdrawn that notice after receiving a disputed reply, and subsequently proceeded against MB Power using the same invoices. According to the respondent, project ownership and participation in correspondence did not transfer another company’s contractual liabilities to it. The NCLAT examined the contractual records and agreed with the NCLT that no privity of contract or debtor-creditor relationship had been established between the appellant and MB Power. It held that the respondent’s involvement in project supervision, reconciliation discussions and execution of a completion certificate did not establish liability for invoices issued to a separate legal entity. The Tribunal also examined correspondence from Hindustan Thermal–EPC raising objections concerning delays, quality issues, price variation and substantial counterclaims. These communications demonstrated a pre-existing dispute concerning the underlying work. The Appellate Tribunal further held that even if contractual privity between the appellant and MB Power were hypothetically assumed, the Section 9 application would still fail because of the pre-existing dispute and counterclaims. It therefore declined to accept the appellant’s corporate-veil argument as a basis for admitting insolvency proceedings. The appeal was dismissed, the rejection of the Section 9 petition was affirmed, and the appellant was granted liberty to pursue remedies available under applicable law. No order as to costs was passed. Cases Discussed Arcelormittal India Private Limited v. Satish Kumar Gupta & Ors., (2019) 2 SCC 1 (Supreme Court) — Relied upon by the appellant to support lifting the corporate veil. The NCLAT was not persuaded that the argument overcame the absence of contractual privity and the established pre-existing dispute. We find that the Adjudicating has rightly concluded that since the invoices were raised by the Appellant against Hindustan Thermal – EPC, the majority payment was received by the Appellant from Hindustan Thermal – EPC and even the work order was re-ceived by the Appellant from Hindustan Thermal – EPC and the reasons for Appellant to file the Application against the Respondent were not understood. AA has also noted that mere-ly because the Respondent tried to reconcile or settle between the Hindustan Thermal – EPC or merely because the work completion was signed by the Respondent, being the owner of the project does not entitle the respondent to be liable for the outstanding amount. There-fore, it did not find any privity of contract between the Appellant and the Respondent. We do not find any infirmity in the findings and the conclusions of the Adjudicating Authority. 39. The Appellant claims that Respondent is the ultimate beneficiary and therefore corporate veil of Hindustan Thermal – EPC needs to be lifted. In its support the Appellant has cited the judgment of Arcelor mittal India Private Limited (supra). We are not persuaded to agree with the arguments of the Appellant when we find pre-existing dispute as noted earlier. Presuming a hypothetical scenario that there was a privity of contract between the Appellant and the Respondent, we proceed to deter-mine the prayer of the Appellant for Section 9 Petition. We find that the same set of invoices were used against Hindustan Thermal – EPC in the initial Demand Notice and later on they were used for issuing a Demand Notice and Petition against the Respondent-MB Power. The Appellant claims that the Respondent, being a part of the same corporate group, re-mains responsible for the debt incurred under the contract, regardless of the involvement of a subsidiary. Hindustan Thermal – EPC had raised counter claims and the dispute. We note that if a dispute and counter claim was raised by Hindustan Thermal – EPC, it will continue to remain a dispute for the same reasons as noted above for the Respondent-MB Power al-so as the ground situation remains the same. Therefore, even after presuming that there was privity of contract between the two parties, The Appellant will not get any support in the case. And the Petition, even if it was maintainable due to privity of contract, will have to be rejected basis pre-existing dispute. 40. In the facts and circumstances of the case, we find that the Application filed un-der Section 9 of the Code is not maintainable for there being no Debtor-Creditor relation-ship between the Appellant and the CD, the facts and circumstances of the present case clearly establish that no invoices were raised by the Ap-plicant to the CD, also no payments have been made by the CD to Applicant and as such there exists no privity of contract between the parties. Orders: 41. As noted above, in the facts and circumstances of the case the Appeal is therefore, dismissed. The Appellant is at liberty to pursue remedies as available to him under applicable laws.

Summary: Indian individuals investing in Dubai companies must comply with the Foreign Exchange Management Act, 1999, and India’s overseas investment framework, even when the UAE incorporation process is straightforward. The key requirements include determining FEMA residential status, distinguishing Overseas Direct Investment (ODI) from Overseas Portfolio Investment (OPI), checking the USD 250,000 Liberalised Remittance Scheme (LRS) limit and assessing whether the proposed business activity and ownership structure are permitted. Obtaining a Dubai residence visa, incorporating a free-zone company or opening a UAE bank account does not automatically establish FEMA compliance. The article examines 27 common mistakes, including incorporating a Dubai company before consulting an Authorised Dealer bank, using funds belonging to another person, incorrectly combining family members’ LRS limits, providing shareholder loans without checking applicable restrictions, overlooking ODI reporting and Annual Performance Reports, and failing to maintain supporting documentation. It also addresses valuation requirements when acquiring existing UAE businesses, restrictions concerning step-down subsidiaries, changes in ownership and control, and the importance of maintaining consistent Indian banking and UAE accounting records. Overseas Investment by Indian Individuals: Common FEMA Mistakes For Indian individuals looking at Dubai as a business or investment destination, the practical process often appears straightforward. A person may decide to establish a consultancy in Dubai, buy shares in an existing UAE business, invest in a startup, acquire a property-linked business interest or participate in a regional venture. From the UAE side, the transaction may be relatively simple. A company can be incorporated, shares can be issued, a trade licence can be obtained and a corporate bank account can be opened. But for a person resident in India, there is another layer that cannot be ignored: FEMA. India’s overseas investment framework regulates how resident individuals can acquire, fund, hold, transfer and report investments outside India. This becomes particularly relevant in Dubai because many founders first focus on the UAE incorporation process and only later ask how the investment should have been made from India. That order can create avoidable problems. The issue is not whether Indian individuals are allowed to invest overseas. They are. The real issue is whether the investment has been structured and reported in the manner required under the Foreign Exchange Management framework. The most common mistakes usually arise not from complex transactions, but from ordinary business decisions made without considering the Indian side. An Indian citizen may obtain: a Dubai investor visa; Emirates ID; UAE residence; and ownership of a UAE company. That does not automatically establish that the person has become a person resident outside India for FEMA purposes. FEMA residence depends on the statutory test and the person’s circumstances, including the purpose and nature of stay. This matters because the Overseas Investment Rules apply differently depending on whether the investor is a person resident in India. Consider an Indian founder who obtains a Dubai residence visa but continues living and carrying on most personal and professional activity from India. The visa alone should not be treated as the answer to the FEMA residence question. Residential status should be established before the overseas investment route is selected. Mistake 2: Believing the USD 250,000 LRS Limit Is a Blanket Permission The Liberalised Remittance Scheme is frequently misunderstood. A resident individual can generally remit up to USD 250,000 in a financial year under LRS for permitted current and capital account transactions. Overseas investment can fall within this framework. But the existence of available LRS capacity does not mean every foreign investment is automatically permitted. For example, a founder may say: “My Dubai company only requires USD 100,000, so FEMA is not an issue.” That conclusion is incomplete. The remittance may be within the LRS monetary ceiling, but the investment itself still needs to comply with the Overseas Investment Rules. The correct analysis is: Is the amount within the LRS limit? and separately: Is the proposed overseas investment permitted under the ODI framework? Both questions need a satisfactory answer. Mistake 3: Incorporating the Dubai Company Before Checking FEMA This is perhaps the most common practical error. A founder visits Dubai, meets a corporate service provider and incorporates a company immediately. The licence may be issued within days. The founder then returns to India and asks the bank how to remit the share capital. Only at that stage does the bank ask questions such as: who owns the company; when the shares were issued; what consideration was agreed; whether the investment qualifies as ODI; how the amount will be remitted; and what reporting is required. The problem is not necessarily that the structure is prohibited. The problem is that the legal documents may already have been created before the Indian regulatory route was considered. A better sequence is: FEMA review → shareholder decision → banking route → Dubai incorporation → remittance → reporting rather than incorporating first and trying to reconstruct the compliance trail later. Mistake 4: Treating a Dubai Company as a Simple Overseas Purchase When an Indian resident acquires shares in a private UAE company, the investment will commonly fall within the definition of Overseas Direct Investment. ODI is not the same as buying an ordinary overseas financial product. For example, subscribing to the shares of a newly incorporated Dubai consultancy company is fundamentally different from making a small portfolio investment in listed foreign securities. Once an investment is classified as ODI, specific rules relating to the foreign entity, reporting and ongoing compliance become relevant. This distinction matters particularly for entrepreneurs. Owning 100% of a Dubai operating business is not a passive overseas investment merely because the company is small. Mistake 5: Using the Wrong Person to Fund the UAE Company A common problem arises where the shareholder and remitter are not aligned. Suppose an Indian resident incorporates a Dubai company personally. The founder then asks: a spouse; another family member; an Indian company; or an overseas associate to pay the initial company expenses or capital. From a commercial perspective, the founder may see all these funds as belonging to the same family or business group. FEMA does not necessarily view them that way. The legal shareholder, source of funds and actual remitter should be consistent with the permitted investment structure. If one person legally owns the Dubai shares but another person funded the acquisition without a properly structured transaction, the compliance trail can become difficult to explain. This becomes particularly relevant during bank due diligence or a later sale of the business. Mistake 6: Assuming Any Dubai Business Activity Is Automatically Permissible Dubai offers an enormous range of business activities. A founder can establish companies for: technology; consulting; trading; logistics; media; healthcare; professional services; e-commerce; real estate-related businesses; and financial services. But the fact that a Dubai authority issues a licence does not automatically settle the Indian overseas-investment question. The Overseas Investment Rules contain restrictions relating to certain activities. Regulated financial services may also require additional examination. A founder should therefore not select a UAE activity purely because a free zone or incorporation consultant says it is available. The Indian investor should first understand whether the activity creates any FEMA restriction. Mistake 7: Ignoring the Difference Between ODI and OPI Resident individuals can make different forms of overseas investment. One important distinction is between Overseas Direct Investment and Overseas Portfolio Investment. For a normal Dubai private company, a founder acquiring unlisted equity will commonly be making ODI. A portfolio investment generally has a different character. This distinction affects reporting and the permitted structure. For example, an individual buying listed foreign securities as a passive investor should not automatically apply the same compliance process as someone incorporating and controlling a Dubai operating company. The labels matter because the FEMA obligations differ. Mistake 8: Giving the UAE Company a Shareholder Loan Without Checking the Rules This often happens after incorporation. The founder invests the initial capital correctly. Six months later, the Dubai company needs additional working capital. The founder simply transfers more money and records it in the UAE books as a shareholder loan. That accounting treatment in Dubai does not automatically mean the transfer is permitted under FEMA. Resident individuals have specific restrictions and conditions under the Overseas Investment framework. Additional debt funding should therefore be examined before the money is remitted. The fact that the individual already owns the Dubai company does not create unrestricted freedom to send any amount in any form. This is one of the reasons future funding needs should be considered when the structure is originally designed. Mistake 9: Forgetting That LRS Covers More Than the Dubai Investment The USD 250,000 LRS limit is an overall annual limit for covered transactions. It is not a separate USD 250,000 allowance specifically for overseas companies. Suppose an Indian resident during the same financial year has already remitted money for: overseas education; investment in foreign securities; maintenance of family abroad; and another permitted LRS transaction. The amount available for the Dubai company investment may therefore be less than USD 250,000. A founder should check total LRS utilisation before committing to the UAE investment amount. This becomes particularly important where share capital, incorporation funding and subsequent capital needs are expected in the same financial year. Mistake 10: Assuming Husband and Wife Can Freely Combine LRS Limits Into One Investment Families sometimes approach overseas investment as though all remittance limits can simply be pooled. For example, a Dubai company may be owned entirely by the husband, while both husband and wife remit money towards the investment. Family members may individually have LRS capacity, but combining remittances does not mean ownership and funding structures can be ignored. If more than one person is investing, the corporate documentation should accurately reflect the underlying ownership and investment arrangement. A cleaner structure might involve both individuals actually subscribing to shares in the proportions intended. The key principle is that the banking trail should match the legal arrangement. Mistake 11: Ignoring the Designated AD Bank The Authorised Dealer bank plays an important role in overseas investment. Some founders treat the bank simply as the institution that transfers money to Dubai. In reality, the bank is also part of the regulatory reporting and compliance process. For ODI, documentation may need to be routed through the designated AD bank. Using different banks or remittance channels without maintaining a coherent investment record can complicate matters later. For a serious UAE investment, it is usually preferable to establish a clear relationship with the designated bank and maintain continuity. The bank should understand the structure, investment amount and foreign entity. Mistake 12: Treating ODI Reporting as Optional Paperwork Another recurring issue is failure to complete the Indian reporting after the investment has been made. The founder may think: “The money has already reached Dubai and the company is operational, so the transaction is complete.” From a FEMA perspective, it may not be. The overseas investment regulations prescribe reporting obligations. Resident individuals making ODI may need to complete relevant filings through the designated AD bank. The reporting history later becomes particularly important when the founder wants to: make another investment; sell the Dubai company; restructure the ownership; transfer shares; or bring proceeds back to India. A small compliance omission at incorporation can become a major inconvenience when the company grows. Mistake 13: Failing to Maintain Evidence of the Investment A clean Dubai investment should have supporting documentation. Depending upon the circumstances, the investor should retain records such as: UAE incorporation certificate; current trade licence; constitutional documents; share certificate or share register; proof of remittance; bank advice; ODI reporting records; valuation documents where applicable; and evidence of any subsequent changes in shareholding. Five years later, relying on old emails and WhatsApp conversations is not an effective compliance strategy. The investor should be able to establish: what was acquired, when it was acquired, how much was paid and how the money was remitted. This becomes especially important where the Dubai company is later sold at a substantial valuation. Mistake 14: Ignoring Annual Performance Reporting For some overseas investments, compliance does not end after the original ODI filing. Annual Performance Report requirements may apply. The RBI’s overseas-investment framework specifically provides for APR reporting in applicable cases, including situations involving resident individuals. This means the investor may need ongoing access to information about the UAE company’s financial performance. A founder should therefore ensure the Dubai company’s accounting records remain current. This is good practice anyway because a UAE company may also have accounting and Corporate Tax obligations locally. The same financial statements can therefore become relevant for both UAE and Indian compliance. Mistake 15: Letting UAE Accounting and Indian FEMA Records Develop Separately This is more common than it sounds. The Dubai accountant records one version of the transaction. The Indian bank records another description. The shareholder remembers a third version. For example, the UAE financial statements may show an amount as shareholder loan while the Indian records indicate that the remittance was made as equity investment. Such inconsistencies can create questions later. The Dubai accountant and Indian compliance adviser should understand the same transaction structure. Cross-border compliance works best when the documents tell one consistent story. Mistake 16: Allowing the Dubai Company to Create Another Foreign Subsidiary Without Reviewing FEMA Many founders initially establish a company in Dubai and later expand. A common structure becomes: Indian resident founder ↓ Dubai company ↓ Saudi or European subsidiary This may be commercially sensible because Dubai can act as a regional headquarters. However, the creation of step-down subsidiaries can have implications under the Overseas Investment framework. Resident individuals are subject to specific conditions where the foreign entity in which they have invested acquires or establishes subsidiaries. The founder should therefore review the FEMA position before the Dubai company creates additional entities. The fact that the second investment is being made by the UAE company rather than directly from India does not automatically make Indian overseas-investment rules irrelevant. Mistake 17: Acquiring Control Later Without Rechecking FEMA A resident individual may initially acquire a minority interest in a Dubai company. Later, the founder may negotiate additional shares and obtain control. That change can alter the nature of the investment and the applicable analysis. Similarly, the UAE company itself may change its structure after the initial investment. FEMA compliance should therefore be reviewed whenever there is a meaningful change in: ownership percentage; control; capital; subsidiaries; or the nature of the foreign business. An investment structure that was compliant on day one should not simply be assumed to remain unchanged forever. Mistake 18: Buying an Existing Dubai Company Without Proper Valuation Indian founders sometimes acquire existing UAE entities instead of incorporating new companies. Suppose an Indian resident wants to acquire a Dubai trading company from its current owner. The parties agree on AED 2 million. The amount should have a commercial basis. The overseas investment framework contains pricing principles requiring relevant transactions to take place at an arm’s-length value. This means the purchase price should not be invented simply to facilitate the remittance. A proper valuation is particularly important where: the business has significant goodwill; related parties are involved; the acquisition price is materially different from book value; or the company may later be sold. Good valuation documentation protects both the investor and the integrity of the transaction. Mistake 19: Confusing a UAE Company Licence With the Value of the Company This is particularly relevant in Dubai. A seller may say: “The company has been operating for five years, so it is worth AED 1 million.” An old licence does not automatically create value. An investor should examine: revenue; profitability; customers; contracts; bank history; debts; tax position; legal claims; and regulatory history. A UAE acquisition involves buying the company’s past as well as its future opportunity. The valuation should therefore reflect the actual business, not merely the age of the trade licence. Mistake 20: Believing a Free-Zone Company Creates Different FEMA Freedom Indian entrepreneurs often spend significant time choosing among Dubai and UAE free zones. They may consider: DMCC; Meydan; IFZA; JAFZA; Dubai South; or another jurisdiction. The free-zone selection can be very important from a UAE commercial and tax perspective. But it does not eliminate Indian FEMA rules. From the Indian investor’s perspective, the key issues remain: who is investing; what entity is being acquired; how much is being invested; what activity the company undertakes; and how the investment is funded and reported. A low-cost free-zone package does not create a low-compliance FEMA structure. Mistake 21: Assuming a Dubai Corporate Bank Account Solves the Source-of-Funds Issue Opening a UAE corporate bank account is an important milestone. But it does not correct an improperly structured Indian investment. Banks increasingly examine: ultimate beneficial ownership; source of wealth; source of funds; expected business activity; shareholder profile; and transaction history. If the Indian shareholder cannot reconcile the initial investment with the legal ownership documents, questions can arise during banking reviews. A transparent FEMA trail therefore supports UAE banking as well as Indian compliance. Mistake 22: Using Personal and Company Money Interchangeably A Dubai owner-managed company can make the distinction between personal and corporate funds feel blurred. A founder may: pay company expenses personally; withdraw money from the company; send additional money when needed; or use the corporate account for personal transactions. This is risky from both a governance and compliance perspective. The UAE company is a separate legal entity. Transfers between the shareholder and company should have a clear basis. They might represent: capital; salary; reimbursement; dividend; or another legitimate transaction. The accounting treatment in Dubai and the FEMA treatment in India should be consistent. Mistake 23: Forgetting Indian Income-Tax Reporting After FEMA Compliance A founder may complete the ODI process correctly and assume that the Indian compliance is finished. FEMA and income-tax reporting are different. A Resident and Ordinarily Resident Indian taxpayer may also need to consider foreign-asset and foreign-income reporting in the Indian income-tax return. That can include disclosure of the Dubai company shareholding and other UAE financial interests. A FEMA-compliant investment can therefore still create a separate income-tax disclosure obligation. The two systems should be managed together. Mistake 24: Assuming the UAE Company’s Profit Is Automatically the Shareholder’s Personal Income The opposite mistake is also common. A resident individual owns 100% of a Dubai company. The company earns AED 1 million. That does not automatically mean the shareholder personally earned AED 1 million. The UAE company has its own legal identity. Amounts paid personally to the shareholder—such as salary, dividends or other distributions—need separate analysis. At the same time, the location of management may create other tax considerations, including possible questions around effective management. The correct approach is to respect the legal structure while analysing the relevant Indian tax consequences separately. Mistake 25: Managing the Entire Dubai Company From India Without Considering Tax Residency This is technically an income-tax issue rather than purely a FEMA issue, but it frequently arises alongside overseas investment. A founder may incorporate a UAE company, open a Dubai office and obtain a residence visa while continuing to make all significant management decisions from India. That may create Indian tax-residency questions for the foreign company depending on the facts. The broader lesson is important: A legally valid FEMA investment does not automatically produce the intended tax result. FEMA, Indian income tax and UAE Corporate Tax need to be considered together. Mistake 26: Ignoring the Exit Until a Buyer Appears An investor should think about eventual exit from the beginning. Suppose an Indian resident invests AED 100,000 in a Dubai technology company. Five years later, an international buyer offers AED 8 million. At that point, the founder will need a clean record of: original investment; share ownership; FEMA reporting; valuation; changes in capital; and sale consideration. Transfer and repatriation requirements may also need to be satisfied. A valuable company magnifies compliance weaknesses. Good documentation is therefore not bureaucracy for its own sake. It protects the investor’s ability to monetise the investment later. Mistake 27: Ignoring Delayed Reporting Because the Transaction Is Already Old Sometimes a founder discovers that a FEMA filing was missed two years earlier. The worst response is to ignore it because the investment is already old. The current overseas investment framework provides mechanisms for dealing with certain reporting delays, including Late Submission Fee procedures where applicable. Importantly, unresolved reporting delays can affect subsequent financial commitment or transfer of the overseas investment. Therefore, where a historical Dubai investment has reporting gaps, the investor should identify and regularise them rather than carrying the problem forward indefinitely. A Dubai-Focused Example Consider an Indian resident entrepreneur who wants to establish an e-commerce company in Dubai. The planned investment is AED 300,000. The founder expects to: own 100% of the UAE company; obtain a Dubai residence visa; hire three employees; maintain a UAE corporate bank account; and later establish a Saudi subsidiary. The business plan itself may be commercially sound. The FEMA review should nevertheless happen before the company is formed. The founder should determine: FEMA residential status; whether the shareholding constitutes ODI; available LRS limit; appropriate banking route; initial equity structure; future funding requirements; reporting obligations; and implications of a future Saudi subsidiary. Now imagine that the founder instead forms the company immediately, pays expenses through a friend’s UAE account, sends capital from different personal accounts and waits a year before asking about Indian reporting. The underlying business may still be genuine. But the compliance trail has become unnecessarily complicated. The difference is not the business. It is the sequencing. Another Example: Buying Into an Existing Dubai Business Assume an Indian resident is offered 40% of an existing Dubai marketing company. The business is profitable, and the founder wants to invest AED 750,000. Before transferring money, the investor should consider: whether the investment qualifies as ODI; whether the amount fits within the applicable LRS capacity; how the target should be valued; whether the share transfer is properly documented; what rights the 40% shareholding provides; whether control will arise; what UAE liabilities already exist; and how future investment will be funded. The UAE company should also be reviewed from a commercial perspective. A FEMA-compliant investment into a poorly managed Dubai company is still a poor investment. Regulatory compliance and commercial due diligence solve different problems. Both are necessary. A Practitioner Observation: Most FEMA Problems Start With Convenience The most common overseas-investment mistakes rarely begin with an intention to breach the law. They usually begin with convenience. The founder is told: “Pay this amount now and we can issue the Dubai licence today.” The shareholder needs money urgently: “Just transfer it and we will classify it later.” The bank needs a guarantee: “Sign it first; we will check India compliance afterwards.” The UAE company wants to expand: “Let’s form the Saudi subsidiary now and ask the accountant later.” Each decision may appear commercially harmless in isolation. Together, they can produce a structure that is difficult to reconcile. The better approach is not necessarily slower. It is simply more deliberate. For a Dubai investment involving an Indian resident, every movement of ownership or money should have a clear legal character before it occurs. A Better Process for Indian Individuals Investing in Dubai A disciplined process can prevent most of the mistakes discussed above. Step 1: Establish FEMA residential status. Do not assume citizenship or a UAE visa provides the answer. Step 2: Decide what the individual is actually investing in. Is it a new Dubai company, an existing business, listed securities or another foreign asset? Step 3: Determine whether the investment is ODI or OPI. The classification affects the compliance process. Step 4: Review available LRS capacity. Consider all remittances already made during the financial year. Step 5: Review the UAE business activity. Identify any restricted or regulated sectors before incorporation. Step 6: Decide the ownership structure before issuing the UAE shares. The legal shareholder and source of funds should align. Step 7: Coordinate with the AD bank. Understand the required documentation and reporting. Step 8: Remit funds through the appropriate channel. Maintain a clear banking trail. Step 9: Complete ODI reporting. Do not treat reporting as an optional post-incorporation formality. Step 10: Maintain annual records. Keep the UAE company’s financial statements and ownership records accessible. Step 11: Review every major subsequent transaction. Additional funding, guarantees, changes in control, step-down subsidiaries and exits can all have FEMA implications. Conclusion Dubai offers Indian individuals a practical route to establish businesses, invest internationally and participate in one of the world’s most active commercial centres. The FEMA framework does not prevent genuine overseas investment. It provides the regulatory structure within which a person resident in India can make that investment. Most problems arise when the Indian investor focuses exclusively on the Dubai side of the transaction. The trade licence may be valid. The company may be profitable. The corporate bank account may be operating normally. But the Indian investment trail still needs to answer basic questions: Who invested? How much was invested? Under which FEMA route? How was the money remitted? Was the investment reported? What happened after the initial investment? For Indian individuals building businesses in Dubai, these questions should be answered at the beginning rather than reconstructed years later. The most useful principle is simple: A Dubai company may take only a few days to incorporate, but the overseas investment behind it should be designed for the entire life of the business. When the FEMA structure, UAE corporate records and banking trail remain consistent, future funding, expansion and exit become significantly easier.

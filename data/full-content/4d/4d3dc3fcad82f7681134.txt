(Bombay High Court) RBI Alone Can Sanction Prosecution of ICICI Bank’s Former MD and CEO Under Section 19 of the Prevention of Corruption Act Background of the Case The Bombay High Court quashed the sanction granted by ICICI Bank’s Board of Directors to prosecute its former Managing Director and Chief Executive Officer, Chanda Kochhar, under the Prevention of Corruption Act, 1988. Justice Milind N. Jadhav held that the Reserve Bank of India was the competent authority to grant sanction in her case. The Court also found that the Board had acted on the CBI’s letter and report without considering the underlying investigation material. The prosecution arose from allegations that Kochhar had indirectly received illegal gratification in connection with loans sanctioned to the Videocon Group. The CBI registered an FIR on 22 January 2019 and filed its chargesheet on 28 March 2023. It sought sanction from ICICI Bank on 27 March 2023, and the Board granted it on 15 May 2023. Kochhar challenged the sanction and the consequential action taken by the Special Court. Who Was Competent to Grant Sanction? Kochhar argued that the authority competent to remove her from office was the RBI under the Banking Regulation Act, 1949, and that the power to grant prosecution sanction under Section 19 followed that removal power. The CBI relied on the Companies Act, 2013 and Article 151(a) of ICICI Bank’s Articles of Association, which provided for the Board to appoint and remove managerial personnel. It argued that the RBI’s removal power operated in specified circumstances alongside the Board’s powers. The High Court read Sections 5A, 35B(1)(b), 36AA and 36AC of the Banking Regulation Act together. Section 35B(1)(b) makes the termination of appointment of specified bank executives effective only with the RBI’s previous approval. Section 36AA empowers the RBI to order removal in the public interest, to protect depositors or to secure proper management, subject to recorded reasons and a reasonable opportunity to make a representation. Section 36AC gives appointments and removals under the specified provisions overriding effect, while Section 5A gives the Act precedence over conflicting corporate instruments and resolutions. On that statutory scheme, the Court held that the RBI alone was competent to grant sanction in Kochhar’s case. An averment in her petition describing the Board as the competent authority could confer no such power. The Court applied the principle that there is no estoppel against a statute. Sanction Requires Independent Scrutiny of the Evidence The Court separately examined what the Board had considered. The sanction order and the extract of the Board minutes showed that the CBI’s request letter and report had been circulated. The underlying material collected during the investigation had not been placed before the Board. The Court found no discussion of witness statements or supporting documents and no reasons showing how the Board had independently assessed the allegations. Relying on CBI v. Ashok Kumar Aggarwal, (2014) 14 SCC 295, and State of Karnataka v. Ameerjan, (2007) 11 SCC 273, the Court explained that the investigating agency must put the entire relevant record before the sanctioning authority. That includes material that may favour the accused and weigh against granting sanction. The authority must scrutinise the record independently before deciding whether prosecution should be sanctioned. On the facts, the Court found that the Board’s decision was mechanical and that the failure to consider the relevant record had caused prejudice. Jurisdictional Defect Can Be Examined at the Threshold The CBI urged the High Court to leave the challenge to the sanction for determination at trial. The Court treated a sanction issued by an incompetent authority as equivalent to an absence of sanction. Relying on Nanjappa v. State of Karnataka, (2015) 14 SCC 186, it held that a valid sanction is a condition precedent to the Special Court’s jurisdiction to take cognizance of offences covered by Section 19. A challenge going to the authority’s statutory competence could therefore be decided at the threshold. The High Court allowed the writ petition, quashed the sanction dated 15 May 2023 and set aside the consequential action taken by the Special Court pursuant to it in CBI Special Case No. 874 of 2023. It also declined the CBI’s request for an eight-week stay of the judgment. Author’s Comments The judgment turns on two distinct questions. The first is who has the statutory power to sanction prosecution. For Kochhar’s office, that question had to be answered by examining the Banking Regulation Act alongside Section 19. The second is how the sanctioning authority exercised its power. A request letter and an investigation report could not establish independent scrutiny when the underlying record had never been placed before the Board. For a Special Court considering cognizance, the identity of the authority that signed the sanction is therefore a substantive jurisdictional question. Where the governing statute plainly vests that power elsewhere, the defect can be addressed before the accused is required to face trial.

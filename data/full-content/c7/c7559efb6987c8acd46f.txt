(Supreme Court of India) Summary: The Supreme Court of India granted bail to Dr. Rajinder Rajan in a case registered under Sections 8, 22 and 29 of the Narcotic Drugs and Psychotropic Substances Act, 1985, involving the alleged unlawful possession of 2,000 Tramadol tablets recovered from Corporate Medicos, an indoor pharmacy situated inside Corporate Hospital, Amritsar. The Supreme Court directed that the appellants be released on bail forthwith upon furnishing bail bonds to the satisfaction of the trial Court and complying with any additional conditions imposed by it. The appeals were allowed in terms of the signed order. The supplied Supreme Court operative order does not disclose detailed reasons for granting bail, while the accompanying High Court material explains the prosecution allegations, the defence submissions and the grounds on which regular bail had earlier been refused. The prosecution case originated from an investigation conducted by the Narcotics Control Bureau (NCB), Amritsar Zonal Unit, following the recovery of 31,900 Tramadol tablets from Ballista Pharmaceuticals on 1 May 2025. During subsequent searches, NCB officers allegedly recovered 2,000 tablets from Corporate Medicos on 2 May 2025 and arrested Dr. Rajinder Rajan. Further recoveries and arrests were reported at other medical establishments in Amritsar. The petitioner approached the High Court under Section 483 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023, seeking regular bail. Before the High Court, the petitioner submitted that he was an experienced orthopaedic surgeon practising for approximately 25 years and that the tablets were intended for the treatment of hospital patients rather than illegal distribution. His counsel asserted that only 200 tablets had been ordered through a letter dated 19 April 2025, but the supplier inadvertently delivered 2,000 tablets under an invoice dated 21 April 2025. According to the defence, the excess tablets remained unopened and unused, and a letter dated 27 April 2025 sought the return of 1,800 tablets. It was further argued that continued detention was unnecessary because the trial would take considerable time. The NCB opposed bail, contending that the seized tablets constituted commercial quantity and had been stocked without the requisite licence. The High Court examined the conditions attached to the pharmacy’s drug licence and observed that Tramadol (oral) was specifically excluded from the substances the pharmacy was permitted to stock. It also noted that the invoice recorded 2,000 tablets, that the alleged return letter had not been produced before the investigating agency at the time of seizure, and that no satisfactory explanation had been offered for the purchase. The High Court additionally recorded that the retail drug licence of Corporate Medicos had been cancelled following reported violations of its conditions. On this basis, the High Court held, for the purpose of deciding bail, that the alleged possession attracted the restrictions under Section 37 of the NDPS Act because the seized quantity was treated as commercial quantity. Without expressing any opinion on the merits of the criminal case, it dismissed the regular bail petition. Subsequently, the Supreme Court granted leave, directed the appellants’ immediate release on bail subject to appropriate conditions, allowed the appeals in terms of its signed order and disposed of pending applications. The Supreme Court’s operative direction must be distinguished from the High Court’s earlier reasoning; the supplied material does not establish that the Supreme Court finally determined the legality of possession, the applicable licensing requirements or the petitioner’s criminal liability.

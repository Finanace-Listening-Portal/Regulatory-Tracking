Summary: A business’s value depends not merely on its revenue, history or the owner’s years of effort, but on its ability to generate reliable future cash flows without depending entirely on its founder. Businesses with transparent financial records, documented processes, recurring revenue, capable management teams, clear ownership of assets and intellectual property, and effective legal and tax compliance are generally better positioned to attract buyers, investors and lenders. This applies even to owners who never intend to sell, because the same scrutiny arises when raising capital, borrowing, buying out a partner, transferring ownership to the next generation or managing an unexpected transition. Seven essential questions help assess whether a business is ready for scrutiny: Are its financial numbers reliable? Will revenue continue? Can operations run without the owner? Is the business profitable and cash-efficient? Does the company legally own the assets it uses? Are there undisclosed liabilities? Is future growth supported by evidence? Due diligence examines financial, tax, legal, commercial, people-related, technology and data risks, with identified problems potentially resulting in price reductions, indemnities, escrow arrangements or failed transactions. Owners can improve transferability through monthly financial closes, reconciled tax returns, formal employment terms, documented contracts, reduced customer concentration, delegated relationships, a second line of leadership and an organised data room. Preparing a business for sale is therefore not necessarily an exit strategy; it is a way to preserve choice, strengthen operations, improve financing prospects and protect the value built over many years. The first attracts several offers. Buyers like the clean books, written processes, long-term contracts and capable management team. The deal closes within months. The second can barely generate interest. The accounts need “explaining.” Key customer relationships exist only in the owner’s head. Half the staff are on informal terms. Some sales were never fully recorded. Buyers either walk away or offer a fraction of what the owner hoped for. Same revenue, very different firms. The gap was built over twenty years — one habit at a time. Why it matters even if you never sell Plenty of owners say: “I’m not selling. This is for my children.” That’s a perfectly good choice. But think of all the moments when a business is examined as if it were up for sale: Raising equity from investors. Applying for a sizeable loan. Buying out a departing partner. Handing over to the next generation and valuing it fairly between heirs. A sudden transition forced by illness or emergency. An unexpected offer you would genuinely like to accept. Each time, someone asks the buyer’s question: Can this business operate, earn and grow without its current owner holding it together? The ideal day to prepare a business for sale is the day you start it. The next best day is today. What history teaches: the house built for strangers Builders in many cultures share a version of the same saying: build every house as though a stranger will live in it. A house designed only around its owner’s habits — doors only he can open, wiring only he understands — is worth little to anyone else. Companies are no different. In Built to Sell (2010), John Warrillow tells the story of an owner who discovers his firm is worth little to buyers because everything revolves around him. The idea the book made famous: a business has value when it can run without you. What buyers really pay for Buyers don’t pay for effort, history or sentiment. They pay for future cash flows — and for confidence that those cash flows are real and dependable. The Seven Buyer Questions Every serious buyer, investor or lender asks seven questions. Score your business honestly against each one. 1. Are the numbers reliable? Clean, timely, reconciled accounts that agree with tax returns — audited or reviewed by a credible firm. Profits that depend on “adjustments” explained by the owner are marked down heavily. 2. Will the revenue last? Contracts, subscriptions, repeat buyers and a wide customer base. Heavy reliance on one customer, product, channel or supplier reduces value. 3. Can it run without the owner? A capable second tier of management, documented processes and customer relationships that belong to the company — not only to the founder. 4. Is it profitable and cash-efficient? Steady margins and well-managed receivables, inventory and payables. 5. Does the company legally own what it uses? Assets, trademarks, domains, software, licences and property held in the company’s name, and contracts that survive a change of ownership. 6. Are there liabilities hiding anywhere? Tax, labour, environmental, data-protection and company-law compliance that’s current and defensible. Undisclosed liabilities are among the largest deal-breakers. 7. Where will growth come from? A clear growth story for the next phase, backed by evidence. Quick recap The short version: value comes from future cash flows that are believable without you — reliable numbers, lasting revenue, independence from the owner, efficiency, clear ownership, no hidden liabilities and credible growth. What due diligence uncovers Due diligence is the buyer’s investigation, and it typically covers: Financial: quality of earnings, revenue recognition, working-capital trends, debt and contingent liabilities. Tax: open assessments, notices, GST and withholding compliance, transfer pricing on group transactions. Legal: contracts, litigation, licences, title to property and IP, change-of-control clauses. Commercial: customer concentration, churn, pricing power, market position. People: employment contracts, statutory contributions, dependence on key people, disputes. Technology and data: systems, cybersecurity, data protection. Every issue uncovered turns into one of three outcomes: a lower price, a specific indemnity or escrow, or a reason to walk away. The hard reality Owners frequently believe their company is worth much more than buyers will pay. Part of that’s emotional — years of sacrifice feel as though they should count for something. But part is structural. Plenty of owners spent years optimising for the lowest tax bill and maximum control rather than for transparency and transferability. Unrecorded income, informal staff, related-party deals and owner-held relationships felt practical at the time. In a sale, each one becomes a discount. You can’t hide value for twenty years and then expect a buyer to pay for it on the day you sell. Signs of progress A sellable business reveals measurable independence: Owner dependence — the business runs for a month without the owner. Customer concentration — no customer above roughly 15–20% of revenue. Recurring revenue — a rising share from contracts, subscriptions or repeat customers. Clean accounts — audited statements with no unexplained adjustments. Documented processes — key processes written down and actually followed. Second-line leaders — at a minimum two people capable of running major functions. The decade ahead India will see a wave of business transfers as founders from the 1990s and 2000s retire. Private equity funds, strategic acquirers and family offices are increasingly active in mid-sized firms, and they will use data rooms, AI-assisted due diligence and quality-of-earnings reviews. Companies with clean data, strong compliance and low dependence on their owners will command better valuations — or just be the only ones that can be sold. Next steps for each role Owner: answer the Seven Buyer Questions honestly and score each from 1 to 5. CFO: prepare a three-year normalised profit statement that strips out personal and one-off items. Leadership team: document the five most significant processes. Advisers: arrange an informal valuation to see where value is leaking. A smaller-scale example Take a hypothetical owner of a profitable ₹20 crore engineering firm who receives an approach from an acquirer. Due diligence finds that 60% of revenue depends on relationships only he manages, the accounts include family expenses and several key contracts are unsigned. The buyer offers a low price, mostly payable over five years and dependent on performance. The owner declines, spends three years building a sales team, cleaning up the accounts and getting contracts signed — and later sells for nearly twice the original offer, most of it paid upfront. Even had he not sold, the business would have been far easier to run. The buyer’s first hour Seasoned buyers form a view fast. In the first hour of looking at a business, most focus on five things: three years of audited accounts, the ten largest buyers and their share of revenue, the organisation chart, open legal and tax matters, and how much the owner personally controls. If any of these is murky, the price drops or the conversation ends. You can prepare for that hour now. Keep those five items in one folder, update it each quarter and ask an outside adviser to review it as a buyer would. What makes a company easy to buy also makes it easier to run, finance and hand on. A final thought on sellability Plenty of owners insist, “I’ll never sell.” Life frequently decides otherwise: health, family, partners or market shifts can force a sale on a timetable you didn’t pick. Building a sellable business is not planning your exit. It’s making sure you have a choice when the moment arrives. Frequently asked questions “How are small businesses typically valued?” Often as a multiple of normalised profit, adjusted for risk, growth and dependence on the owner. “How long does it take to make a company sellable?” Typically two to three years of deliberate work on the accounts, processes, team and customer mix. “Should we talk to potential buyers before we’re ready?” Early conversations can be instructive, but don’t share sensitive information without proper agreements in place. Where things stand (October 2026): Large Indian transactions may need approval from the Competition Commission of India, including under the deal-value threshold introduced in 2024; cross-border deals involve FEMA pricing and reporting rules; and tax on the sale depends on the structure (share sale versus business transfer). Specialist advice should begin months before any sale process. The toughest pushback — and a reply The pushback: “Formalising everything costs money and tax. My informal business puts more in my pocket today than a ‘clean’ one would.” The reply: Perhaps — today. But informality carries a hidden cost that reveals up later: less borrowing capacity, higher interest, lost contracts with sizeable customers, legal exposure and a business worth nearly nothing to anyone but you. Formalising is an investment that pays back: a company that can be financed, insured, sold, inherited or just left alone for a month. The income you save informally today is frequently borrowed from your company’s value tomorrow. Ten things to begin this year 1. Close the books monthly and have the accounts reviewed or audited by a credible firm. 2. Reconcile tax returns with the financial statements each year. 3. Reduce customer concentration so no single client can sink the business. 4. Transfer key customer relationships from you to your team. 5. Document critical processes: sales, purchasing, production, collections and compliance. 6. Put every significant asset, IP right and licence in the company’s name. 7. Formalise employment terms and statutory contributions for all staff. 8. Place related-party transactions on documented, arm’s-length terms. 9. Build a second tier of leadership and give it genuine authority. 10. Keep a straightforward data room — key documents, organised and up to date. Common mistakes to avoid Keeping two sets of numbers, formal and informal. Leaving tax notices or litigation unresolved for many years. Signing customer contracts that end on a change of ownership without understanding the consequences. Relying on verbal arrangements with suppliers or buyers. Telling yourself “we’ll tidy up before we sell.” Rushed clean-ups are obvious to buyers. Your 30-minute exercise: the imaginary buyer Imagine a serious, well-advised buyer will examine your business in twelve months’ time. Score it on the Seven Buyer Questions from 1 to 5. Then ask: “What would I be embarrassed for them to find?” Write down the top three answers and give each an owner and a deadline. Fix them first — not for the buyer’s sake, but because they’re already costing you money, time and peace of mind. Key takeaways Your company will be scrutinised like a sale whenever you raise money, borrow, buy out a partner, plan succession or face an emergency — even if you never sell. Buyers pay for believable future cash flows: reliable numbers, lasting revenue, independence from the owner, efficiency, clear ownership, no hidden liabilities and credible growth. Issues found in due diligence become price cuts, indemnities or collapsed deals. Informality saves money today by borrowing against your company’s value tomorrow. Begin with monthly closes, reconciled returns, documented processes, assets in the company’s name and a genuine second line of leadership. A company that’s ready to sell is a company that’s a pleasure to own.

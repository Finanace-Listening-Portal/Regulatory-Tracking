Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); Kunwar Mrityunjay Vs Deepak Lamination (GSTAT Lucknow) Summary: GSTAT Lucknow allowed the departmental appeal by way of remand in a dispute concerning ₹67,500 input tax credit claimed by M/s Deepak Lamination on invoices issued by M/s M.H. Enterprises, which was reportedly found non-existent during an SIB investigation dated 9 November 2021. The First Appellate Authority had quashed the demand by its order dated 28 February 2024. The department argued that invoices, banking payments and the supplier’s active registration did not establish actual movement and receipt of goods, and that the recipient had not discharged the burden under Section 155 read with Section 16(2)(b) of the UPGST Act, 2017. Although the disputed ITC was below the ₹20 lakh departmental appeal threshold, the department invoked the recurring-interpretation exception under paragraph 4(iv)(e) and paragraph 4(vi) of Circular No. 207/1/2024-GST dated 26 June 2024. After considering the Commissioner’s opinion, the Bench decided to hear the appeal on merits in the interest of revenue. The respondent maintained that the supplier’s registration remained active, that GSTR-1 and GSTR-3B returns had been filed and tax paid, and that invoices and banking transactions supported genuine purchases. It also argued that the recipient-facing portal did not reveal whether the supplier’s filed returns contained taxable supplies or were NIL returns. The order describes the dispute as relating to FY 2020-21, while recording the respondent’s transaction-period submission as 1 August to 30 September 2021. The respondent relied on R.T. Infotech and Clear Secured Service; the Tribunal left their application to be examined against their holdings, the statutory conditions and the evidence in this case. The Tribunal held that actual receipt under Section 16(2)(b) and payment of tax to the Government under Section 16(2)(c) are distinct requirements, and the latter cannot substitute for the former. Drawing on the decisions reproduced in the order, it emphasised the cumulative statutory conditions and the claimant’s burden to substantiate genuine supplies through invoices, vehicle and freight details, delivery acknowledgements, e-way bills and transportation documents, and other corroborative records. Active registration, invoices and banking payments were relevant but not independently conclusive where supplies were disputed. Equally, the supplier’s absence at its declared premises during a subsequent investigation did not conclusively prove that earlier supplies never occurred. The SIB report required evaluation against the relevant dates, investigation, registration and return records, and the cumulative evidence. The decisive defect affected both lower orders. The adjudicating authority had invoked Section 74 but had not adequately considered the documents reportedly furnished or explained why they were insufficient or unreliable. The appellate authority had concentrated on the supplier’s active registration without independently verifying actual goods movement. Although paragraph 7.4 records non-production or non-uploading of supporting papers despite Tribunal instructions, paragraph 8 also records the respondent’s assertion that invoices and e-way bills had been uploaded and furnished below, and finds inadequate consideration of that material. Both orders were therefore set aside. The matter was remanded for fresh adjudication, verification of the documentary evidence and applicable ITC conditions, and specific, clear and reasoned findings after giving the respondent a reasonable and effective opportunity to produce supporting documents and be heard. The Tribunal neither finally granted the credit nor restored the demand on merits. Cases Discussed Shaurya Alloys Pvt. Ltd. v. UNION OF INDIA, SCA NO. 7369 of 2025 with SCA NO. 11022 of 2025, Gujarat High Court dt. 01.05.2026., 2026 SCC OnLine Guj. 4013 has held in the following paras hat – 78. Now, the proposition advanced by the petitioners that the provisions of Section 16 of the CGST Act and the clauses mentioned therein are to be read independently is misconceived. It was contended before us that the Revenue must stop at the stage of clause (b) of sub-section (2) of Section 16 of the CGST Act and, once that exercise is complete, cannot proceed to clause (c). We do not subscribe to the submissions advanced by the petitioners. 79. ITC falls under Chapter V and Section 16 of the CGST Act deals with the eligibility and conditions for availing such credit. The provision itself contains the necessary checks and balances for claiming input tax credit. The first and foremost condition is that a dealer (registered person) must be in possession of a tax invoice or debit note issued by a supplier registered under the Act. The second condition, as stipulated in clause (b), is that the registered person must have received the goods or services or both. This clause further incorporates twin conditions under sub-clauses (i) and (ii), which mandate that the goods are delivered by the supplier to the recipient or to any other person on the direction of the registered person, and that services are provided by the supplier to any person on account of such registered person. Clause (c) further stipulates compliance with the requirement of actual payment of the tax charged to the Government. 80. Thus, the Revenue cannot be directed to stop at clause (b), since eligibility for input tax credit is established only after the receipt of goods or services or both, and upon the tax charged in respect of such supply being duly paid to the Government. A registered person (dealer) cannot be held entitled to claim input tax credit unless all the conditions up to clause (c) are satisfied. As per the provisions of Section 155 of the CGST Act, the burden lies upon the dealer to establish entitlement up to the stage of clause (c), and such benefit cannot be availed by merely satisfying the requirement under clause (b). Hence, the clauses under sub-section (2), from (a) to (d), are to be read conjointly and not independently of each other. 84. This proposition in context of ITC was reiterated in the decision rendered in the case of Kailash Chandra vs. Mukundi Lal, [2002] 2 SCC 678. In paragraph No.11, following has been laid down : “11. A provision in the statute is not to be read in isolation. It has to be read with other related provisions in the Act itself, more particularly, when the subject-matter dealt with in different sections or parts of the same statute is the same or similar in nature.” 86 (b) The Court must interpret a taxing statute in the light of what is clearly expressed: it cannot imply anything which is not expressed; it cannot import provisions into the statute so as to supply any assumed deficiency. c) A provision in the statute is not to be read in isolation; rather, it has to be read along with other related provisions itself, more particularly when the subject matter inter-relates with different Sections or parts of the same statute. d) A taxing statute is to be interpreted literally, and further, it is in the domain of the legislature as to how much tax credit is to be given under what circumstances. e) ITC is a form of concession extended to a dealer under the statutory scheme. The concession can be received by the beneficiary only as per the scheme of the statute. f) Whenever a concession is given by statute or notification, etc., the conditions thereof are to be strictly complied with in order to avail such concession Sumetco Alloys Pvt. Ltd. Vs. Union of India, D.B. CWP No. 9323/2026 dt. 13.08.2026, Hon’ble Jaipur High Court has held in the following paras that – 15.1. First, sub-section (1) of Section 16 does not create an unqualified right. It confers entitlement expressly “subject to such conditions and restrictions as may be prescribed”. The entitlement is therefore conditional at its very source. 15.2. Second, sub-section (2) opens with a non obstante clause and is cast in the negative form. It says that no registered person shall be entitled to credit unless the enumerated conditions are met. In our view, being a negatively worded non-obstante provision, it is mandatory in nature. The conditions in clauses (a), (aa), (b), (ba), (c) and (d) are cumulative and not alternative. Thus, satisfaction of five of them does not dispense with the sixth. The submission that compliance with clauses (a), (aa), (b) and (ba) by itself establishes entitlement, therefore, runs against the plain reading of the provision. If that submission were accepted, clause (c) would be rendered otiose. 15.3. Third, clause (c) does not operate in isolation. It is expressly made “subject to the provisions of section 41”. Section 41(2) requires reversal of credit where the supplier has not paid, and the proviso then permits the recipient to re-avail that very credit once the supplier discharges the liability. The denial under clause (c) is, therefore, contingent and reversible, not final and confiscatory. 15.4. Fourth, Section 155 places the burden of establishing eligibility squarely on the person claiming the credit. The claimant must prove entitlement. The revenue is not required to disprove it in the first instance. 16. Taken together, the provisions, ibid, indicate a deliberate allocation of risk. Parliament has chosen to place the risk of supplier default on the recipient, while simultaneously providing a mechanism for restoration of the credit once the default is cured. Whether that allocation is wise or harsh is a question of legislative policy. Most certainly, it is not a question of constitutional validity to be adjudicated by us in the absence of any material to the contrary. Particularly, in light of the fact that benefit of Input Tax Credit is a contingent statutory entitlement, rather than a vested right, much less a constitutional right. 17. Further, Section 155 of the Act places the burden of proving eligibility for ITC upon the person claiming such credit. Consequently, the Respondent was required to substantiate its claim by producing relevant and credible evidence demonstrating that the goods covered by the disputed invoices were actually supplied and received in the course of business. Under Section 155 the burden of proof is upon the claimant that he has completed all the transactions along with valid papers to claim ITC, which were not produced/uploaded by the respondent onto the portal to verify the same besides the instructions of tribunal. The Bench further observes that the issue relating to actual receipt of goods under Section 16(2)(b) is distinct from the issue pertaining to payment of tax to the Government under Section 16(2)(c). The latter condition cannot be treated as a substitute for the former. The Bench is of the considered view that the First Appellate Authority and the Adjudicating Authority are required to record specific and reasoned findings regarding the movement of goods, after proper examination and verification of the relevant documentary evidence, including tax invoices, e-way bills and other supporting documents. In the present case, the Respondent had specifically stated in its reply to the show cause notice that the documents relating to the movement of goods were available with it. It was further submitted that the relevant tax invoices and e-way bills had been uploaded on the portal and placed before the Adjudicating Authority. However, the Order-in-Original does not disclose any proper consideration of the said documents, nor does it assign cogent reasons as to why the documentary evidence furnished by the Respondent was found insufficient or unreliable to establish the actual movement of goods. The Adjudicating Authority invoked Section 74 of the UPGST Act, 2017, not only on the ground that the supplier firm was allegedly non-existent but also on the allegation that the purchasing firm had wrongly availed Input Tax Credit (ITC) without the actual movement of goods. Once such allegations were levelled, it was incumbent upon the Adjudicating Authority to examine the relevant documentary evidence and record findings, supported by reasons, regarding the genuineness of the transactions and the movement of goods. It is further observed that the First Appellate Authority, while passing the impugned Order-in-Appeal, appears to have confined its consideration primarily to the question of whether the GST registration of the supplier was active on the common portal. The said aspect, though relevant, could not by itself be determinative of the Respondent’s entitlement to ITC. The authority was also required to examine the tax invoices, e-way bills and other relevant material to ascertain whether the statutory conditions governing the availment of ITC had been fulfilled. The First Appellate Authority was, therefore, required to undertake an independent and comprehensive examination of the claim for ITC in the light of the applicable provisions of Section 16 of the CGST/UPGST Act, 2017, read with the other relevant statutory provisions. Although ITC is an integral feature of the GST framework, intended, inter alia, to avoid the cascading effect of taxation, its availment is subject to the fulfilment of the statutory conditions and requirements prescribed under the Act. Consequently, the claim for ITC must be examined on the basis of the applicable statutory provisions and the evidence available on record. Payment of tax to the Government and the existence or validity of the supplier’s registration are relevant considerations; however, the actual receipt of goods and the genuineness of the underlying transactions must also be examined in accordance with law. The movement of goods, wherever material to the dispute, is required to be assessed on the basis of the evidence available on record, including tax invoices, e-way bills, transportation documents and other corroborative material, as well as the judicial pronouncements of the Hon’ble High Courts referred to hereinabove. In the present matter, neither the Order-in-Original nor the Order-in-Appeal reflects an adequate and reasoned examination of the documentary evidence relating to the movement of goods and the fulfilment of the statutory conditions for availment of ITC. The material placed on record has not been dealt with in a manner that enables this Bench to ascertain whether the conclusions arrived at by the authorities below are legally sustainable. In view of the foregoing discussion, the impugned Order-in-Original and the Order-in-Appeal cannot be sustained in their present form. The matter, therefore, warrants reconsideration by the Adjudicating Authority after proper verification and evaluation of the relevant documentary evidence and after affording the Respondent an effective opportunity of hearing. 9. The authority shall also examine the fulfilment of the applicable statutory conditions for availment of ITC and record specific, clear and reasoned findings on the issues involved. (ii) The Respondent shall be afforded a reasonable and effective opportunity to produce all relevant documents and supporting evidence concerning the transactions and the movement of goods. The Respondent is directed to place such documents before the Adjudicating Authority for consideration during the fresh adjudication proceedings. (iii) The appeal stands disposed of in the aforesaid terms. The Order is pronounced in Open Court.

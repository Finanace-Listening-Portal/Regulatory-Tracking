AAR/2025-26/23 dated 25 March 2026, examined whether online coaching services provided through digital platforms qualify as Online Information and Database Access or Retrieval Services (OIDAR) under the Integrated Goods and Services Tax Act, 2017. The applicant, a coaching institute providing preparatory courses for competitive examinations such as JEE and NEET, offered online programmes involving live lectures, interactive doubt-solving sessions, mentoring, assessments, performance evaluation and continuous academic guidance. The principal question was whether the use of internet-based technology for delivering educational services automatically brought such coaching programmes within the scope of OIDAR. The applicant contended that the essential character of its supply remained educational instruction and coaching rather than automated digital content delivery. Faculty members actively conducted classes, explained concepts, answered questions, evaluated students and provided personalised academic guidance. The internet merely facilitated communication between teachers and students. The AAR examined the nature of these activities and the degree of human participation in delivering the services. It considered the distinction between faculty-led online coaching and technology-driven services involving automated content delivery. The applicant maintained that substantial human intervention was central to the educational process and that the digital mode of delivery did not alter the underlying character of the supply. While the mode of delivery of education has evolved, an important question under GST has remained unresolved: Does online coaching become an “Online Information and Database Access or Retrieval Service” (OIDAR) merely because it is delivered through the internet? The distinction is not merely academic. Classification as OIDAR carries significant implications for place of supply, taxability, registration requirements and compliance obligations, particularly in cross-border transactions. The Rajasthan Authority for Advance Ruling (AAR), in M/s Allen Career Institute Private Limited, Advance Ruling No. RAJ/AAR/2025-26/23 dated 25.03.2026, has examined this issue and provided important guidance on the distinction between online coaching services and OIDAR services. The ruling reinforces the principle that the use of technology as a medium of delivery does not automatically transform a service into OIDAR. What is relevant is the nature of the service and the degree of human intervention involved. Facts of the Case M/s Allen Career Institute Private Limited (“Allen”), one of India’s leading coaching institutes, provides coaching and preparatory courses for competitive examinations such as JEE, NEET and other entrance examinations. Apart from conventional classroom coaching, Allen also offers courses through digital platforms. Students enrolled in such programmes are provided access to live online classes conducted by faculty members, doubt-solving sessions, mentoring support, assessments, test series, performance evaluation, study material and continuous academic guidance. The coaching process involves substantial interaction between students and faculty members. Teachers conduct lectures, answer queries, explain concepts, evaluate performance and provide personalized guidance throughout the duration of the course. Allen approached the Rajasthan AAR seeking a ruling on whether such online coaching programmes would qualify as “Online Information and Database Access or Retrieval Services” (OIDAR) under Section 2(17) of the IGST Act, 2017. The principal contention of the applicant was that the essence of the service remained coaching and educational instruction. The internet merely served as a medium for delivery. Since the services involved extensive human participation and continuous faculty intervention, they could not be regarded as automated services falling within the scope of OIDAR. The core issue before the AAR was whether educational services delivered through online platforms, but involving substantial human interaction, could be classified as OIDAR merely because the classes were conducted digitally. Legal Framework Governing OIDAR Definition under Section 2(17) of the IGST Act Section 2(17) of the Integrated Goods and Services Tax Act, 2017 defines OIDAR services as: “services whose delivery is mediated by information technology over the internet or an electronic network and the nature of which renders their supply essentially automated and involving minimal human intervention and impossible to ensure in the absence of information technology.” The definition further provides illustrative examples such as: Advertising on the internet; Cloud services; Supply of e-books and digital content; Online gaming; Provision of databases; Music, films and software downloads; Automated distance teaching. The definition itself contains three important ingredients: 1. Delivery through Information Technology – The service must be supplied through the internet or an electronic network. 2. Essentially Automated – The service should be automated in nature. 3. Minimal Human Intervention – Human involvement should be negligible or insignificant in the actual delivery of the service. All three conditions must exist simultaneously. Why Human Intervention is the Key Test The defining characteristic of OIDAR is not online delivery but automation. A service does not become OIDAR merely because a laptop, mobile phone or digital platform is used. The legislature has consciously restricted OIDAR to services which are largely technology-driven and function without meaningful human involvement. The phrase “essentially automated” and “minimal human intervention” forms the heart of the statutory definition. Thus, the relevant question is: Whether the customer receives a service from a human expert through an electronic medium, or whether the customer receives a service generated and delivered automatically by a technological system. This distinction is crucial. What Qualifies as OIDAR in the Education Sector? The law itself specifically includes “automated distance teaching” as an example of OIDAR. Educational services may qualify as OIDAR where: Pre-recorded lectures are uploaded on a portal; Students independently access recorded content; Content delivery is completely automated; Assessments are auto-generated and auto-evaluated; No teacher interaction is available; Learning occurs through a self-paced digital system; Queries are addressed through automated mechanisms rather than human instructors. Examples may include: Recorded video course libraries; AI-driven self-learning platforms; Automated certification modules; Subscription-based digital learning repositories; Self-paced online educational content. In such cases, the educational content is delivered automatically through technology without substantial involvement of faculty members. The student is effectively interacting with a digital platform rather than a teacher. What Does Not Qualify as OIDAR? Online coaching involving active participation of teachers generally falls outside OIDAR. Examples include: Live online classes; Interactive webinars; Virtual classrooms; Faculty-led coaching sessions; Real-time doubt solving; Personal mentoring; Teacher evaluation and feedback; Interactive educational discussions. In such cases, technology merely facilitates communication between the teacher and the student. The actual service being supplied continues to be instruction, coaching and mentoring by human faculty members. The educational value arises from the teacher’s expertise and not from automation. Findings of the Rajasthan AAR The Rajasthan AAR examined the nature of Allen’s online coaching programmes and observed that: Continuous Faculty Participation – The courses involved live lectures delivered by faculty members. Interactive Learning Environment – Students could interact with teachers and seek clarifications during the course. Human-Centric Delivery Model – The service was dependent upon the knowledge, expertise and intervention of faculty members. Absence of Automation – The educational process was not automated in the manner contemplated under the OIDAR provisions. Coaching Remained the Essence of Supply – The internet functioned merely as a medium of communication and delivery. Based on these factors, the AAR concluded that the services supplied by Allen were not OIDAR services. The essential nature of the supply was coaching and educational instruction involving substantial human intervention. Practical Impact of the AAR on the Ed-Tech Industry The ruling provides important guidance for: Coaching Institutes Online classes conducted by teachers should ordinarily not be regarded as OIDAR where substantial faculty participation exists. Universities and Educational Institutions Virtual classrooms involving live instruction remain educational services rather than automated digital services. Ed-Tech Platforms Businesses must carefully analyse whether they are providing: Faculty-led coaching services; or Automated digital content delivery. The GST consequences may differ significantly. Hybrid Models Many platforms today combine: Recorded lectures, Live doubt solving, Faculty mentoring, AI-based learning tools. In such cases, the dominant nature of supply would require careful examination. Key Principles Emerging from the Ruling The following principles emerge from the Allen ruling: 1. Online delivery alone does not make a service OIDAR. 2. Human intervention remains the decisive test. 3. Faculty-led coaching is fundamentally different from automated content delivery. 4. The substance of the transaction prevails over the mode of delivery. 5. Technology acting merely as a communication channel does not convert coaching into OIDAR. 6. Live and interactive educational services generally remain outside the ambit of OIDAR. Conclusion The Rajasthan AAR’s ruling in Allen Career Institute is a welcome and pragmatic decision that restores focus on the true legislative intent behind the OIDAR provisions. The GST law seeks to tax as OIDAR only those services that are essentially automated, technology-driven and capable of being supplied with minimal human intervention. The mere use of digital infrastructure or online platforms does not satisfy this requirement. Where teachers actively conduct classes, interact with students, resolve doubts, evaluate performance and provide mentoring, the essence of the service remains education and coaching. The internet serves merely as a conduit for delivering the service and not as a substitute for the human element. The ruling therefore draws a clear and logical distinction between automated digital learning products and genuine faculty-led online coaching programmes. As educational institutions increasingly adopt hybrid and technology-enabled teaching models, this decision provides much-needed certainty and establishes that the presence or absence of meaningful human intervention remains the touchstone for determining whether a service qualifies as OIDAR under GST. Practical Notes: The ruling of the Rajasthan AAR in Allen Career Institute Pvt. Ltd. is significant not merely because it classifies a particular coaching programme as being outside the ambit of OIDAR, but because it reaffirms a fundamental principle that is often overlooked in the digital economy —the mode of delivery cannot override the true nature of the underlying service. In recent years, there has been a tendency to equate any service delivered through the internet with OIDAR. Such an approach ignores the statutory language of Section 2(17) of the IGST Act, which requires not only delivery through information technology but also that the service be essentially automated and involve minimal human intervention. The legislature has deliberately prescribed these additional conditions to distinguish between services that are merely delivered online and services that are generated and supplied through automated technological systems. The Allen ruling correctly places emphasis on the role of faculty members in the educational process. A student enrolling in a live coaching programme is primarily seeking the expertise, guidance and interaction of the teacher. The digital platform merely facilitates the communication between the instructor and the student. The value of the service is therefore derived from human knowledge and intervention rather than from technology itself. Consequently, treating such services as OIDAR would effectively render the statutory requirement of “minimal human intervention” redundant. The ruling may also have broader implications for other sectors where services are increasingly delivered through virtual platforms. Professional training, online tutoring, virtual consulting, legal advisory services, medical consultations and instructor-led corporate training programmes may all derive support from the reasoning adopted by the AAR. In each case, the decisive factor should be whether the recipient is interacting with a human expert or with an automated technological system. That said, the ruling should not be viewed as laying down a blanket proposition that all online educational services fall outside OIDAR. Modern Ed-Tech platforms frequently operate hybrid models combining recorded lectures, AI-assisted learning modules, automated assessments, digital content libraries and occasional faculty interaction. In such cases, the classification exercise may become considerably more nuanced and may require examination of the predominant nature of the supply, the extent of automation involved and the actual role played by human instructors. Another area that may continue to generate disputes relates to cross-border educational services. OIDAR occupies a unique position under the place-of-supply provisions of the IGST Act, particularly in transactions involving overseas recipients. Tax authorities may therefore continue to closely scrutinize online educational offerings to determine whether they are genuinely faculty-led services or digitally automated learning products. Consequently, educational institutions and Ed-Tech businesses should carefully document the extent of faculty participation, mentoring activities, doubt-resolution mechanisms and other human interventions forming part of their service model. From a policy perspective, the ruling reflects a balanced and commercially sensible interpretation of the law. The objective of OIDAR provisions is to tax digital and automated services supplied through technology, not to re-characterize the traditional services merely because technology has become the preferred medium of delivery. As businesses increasingly migrate towards virtual modes of operation, preserving this distinction will be critical to ensuring certainty and consistency in GST administration. Ultimately, the Allen ruling reinforces a simple but important principle: it is the nature of the service, and not the medium through which it is delivered, that determines whether a supply qualifies as OIDAR. Where human expertise remains central to the supply, the service should ordinarily fall outside the scope of OIDAR notwithstanding the use of sophisticated digital platforms.

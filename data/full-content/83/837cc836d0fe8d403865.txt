Summary: The UAE Corporate Tax regime has introduced significant tax and compliance obligations for Indian entrepreneurs establishing businesses in Dubai and other Emirates. Under the standard regime, taxable income up to AED 375,000 attracts a 0% rate, while income exceeding this threshold attracts Corporate Tax at 9%. Free-zone companies can benefit from a 0% rate on qualifying income only upon satisfying prescribed conditions relating to business substance, qualifying activities, transfer pricing, audited financial statements and the de minimis threshold. Eligible resident businesses with revenue not exceeding AED 3 million may elect for Small Business Relief, which has been extended to eligible tax periods ending on or before 31 December 2029. Corporate Tax registration, maintenance of financial records and timely return filing remain important even where no tax is payable. Indian investors must separately examine the taxation of salaries, dividends, capital gains and related-party transactions, along with Indian residential status, Place of Effective Management (POEM), permanent-establishment exposure and the India–UAE Double Taxation Avoidance Agreement. Compliance with FEMA, Overseas Direct Investment regulations, the Liberalised Remittance Scheme and foreign-asset reporting requirements is equally important. The article explains the distinction between taxable income and revenue, permissible business deductions, free-zone eligibility, participation exemption, withholding tax, foreign tax credit and VAT obligations through practical illustrations. It also provides a first-year compliance checklist and highlights common mistakes that Indian investors should avoid while establishing, managing and funding UAE companies. Genuine business operations, documented commercial decisions and disciplined accounting remain essential for sustainable cross-border tax planning. However, the statement that “Dubai has no tax” is no longer an accurate basis for making an investment decision. The UAE now operates a federal Corporate Tax regime. A company incorporated in a Dubai mainland jurisdiction or free zone may need to register, maintain proper accounts, file a Corporate Tax return and assess whether any tax is payable. The introduction of Corporate Tax has not removed the UAE’s commercial appeal. It has changed what responsible investors must do after incorporation. For Indian investors, the analysis has an additional layer. UAE Corporate Tax must be considered alongside Indian residential-status rules, place of effective management, foreign-asset reporting, transfer pricing, FEMA and the India–UAE Double Taxation Avoidance Agreement. A Dubai company should therefore be viewed as an operating business with continuing tax obligations—not merely as a trade license and bank account. How the UAE Corporate Tax regime works The UAE Corporate Tax Law applies to financial years beginning on or after 1 June 2023. For a business following the calendar year, the first tax period would ordinarily have begun on 1 January 2024. A company with a different financial year may have entered the regime on another date. The regime broadly covers: UAE-incorporated companies; Mainland companies; Free-zone companies; Certain foreign companies managed and controlled in the UAE; Foreign businesses with a UAE permanent establishment or other taxable nexus; Individuals conducting qualifying business activities in the UAE; and Other persons brought within the scope of the legislation. A company may be required to register and file a return even where it has no taxable profit or expects to benefit from a 0% rate. The standard Corporate Tax rates For an ordinary taxable person, the principal Corporate Tax rates are: 0% on the portion of taxable income up to AED 375,000; and 9% on the portion of taxable income exceeding AED 375,000. The AED 375,000 threshold applies to taxable income, not revenue. This distinction is important. A Dubai company may earn revenue of AED 2 million but, after allowable expenses and the prescribed tax adjustments, have taxable income below AED 375,000. Conversely, a consulting company with relatively limited expenses may cross the taxable-income threshold with substantially lower revenue. The threshold should not be multiplied by incorporating several entities for the same business. Artificial separation intended to obtain multiple 0% bands can be challenged under the applicable anti-abuse provisions. Large multinational groups may also be affected by separate minimum-tax rules. Those provisions are generally not relevant to an ordinary owner-managed Dubai company but should be examined where the investor forms part of a large international group. Corporate Tax is charged on taxable income, not gross receipts The starting point is generally the accounting net profit or loss shown in the company’s financial statements. That figure is then adjusted according to the Corporate Tax Law. Adjustments may relate to: Exempt income; Non-deductible expenditure; Related-party transactions; Interest-deduction limitations; Entertainment expenditure; Unrealised gains or losses, depending on the elected treatment; Tax losses; Reliefs and elections; Transactions with connected persons; and Income attributable to foreign or domestic permanent establishments. The amount appearing as profit in the company’s management accounts is therefore not automatically its final taxable income. Sound bookkeeping is essential because the tax computation begins with the accounting records. A practical calculation Assume an Indian investor owns a Dubai mainland consultancy company. For a particular tax period, the company records: Revenue of AED 1,800,000; Allowable business expenses of AED 1,200,000; and Accounting profit of AED 600,000. If there are no further tax adjustments, the first AED 375,000 of taxable income would fall within the 0% band. The remaining AED 225,000 would be subject to the 9% rate. The Corporate Tax liability would therefore be calculated on AED 225,000 rather than the complete AED 600,000 profit. If some expenses are disallowed, the taxable income and resulting liability may increase. Is every business expense deductible? A business expense is not deductible merely because it has been paid from the company’s bank account. Broadly, expenditure should be incurred wholly and exclusively for the business and should not be capital, personal or otherwise restricted. Indian owners of UAE companies should be particularly careful about mixing personal and corporate expenditure. Common problem areas include: Personal travel charged to the business; Family accommodation; Private vehicle expenses; Shareholder withdrawals recorded as business costs; Entertainment expenses; Undocumented management fees; Excessive salary or benefits paid to connected persons; Penalties and fines; Capital expenditure incorrectly expensed; and Payments unsupported by invoices or contracts. The company should be able to explain the commercial purpose of material expenses and connect them with proper documentation. Salary paid to the Indian owner A shareholder may also work as a director, manager or employee of the UAE company. A commercially reasonable salary paid for genuine services may be deductible, subject to the Corporate Tax rules and the arm’s-length principle. However, the arrangement should be supported by actual conduct and documentation. Relevant evidence may include: Employment or management agreement; Board or shareholder approval; Payroll records; UAE bank transfers; Details of duties performed; Work location; Visa and labour documentation, where applicable; and Comparable remuneration support. A large year-end charge described as “management salary” without regular payroll, defined responsibilities or evidence of services may attract questions. The Indian tax treatment of the recipient must be examined separately. A payment deductible in the UAE is not automatically exempt in India. Dividends received by the shareholder The UAE generally does not impose personal Corporate Tax on dividends received by an individual as personal investment income, provided the activity remains within the prescribed personal-investment exclusion. The position in India depends on the shareholder’s Indian residential status. An Indian resident is generally subject to Indian tax on worldwide income, which may include dividends received from a UAE company. A non-resident is ordinarily taxed in India on income received or deemed to be received in India and income accruing, arising or deemed to accrue or arise in India. The declaration and payment of dividends should be supported by: Approved financial statements; Board or shareholder resolution; Evidence of distributable profits; Dividend voucher or advice; Bank transfer record; and Appropriate disclosure in the recipient’s tax return. Moving money from a UAE company’s account to the owner’s personal account without identifying whether it is salary, dividend, loan repayment or reimbursement can create problems in both countries. Free-zone companies are not automatically tax-free Many Indian investors select a Dubai free zone after being told that the company will pay 0% Corporate Tax. That statement is incomplete. A free-zone company may benefit from a 0% rate on qualifying income only if it satisfies the conditions for being a Qualifying Free Zone Person. Income that does not qualify can be taxed at 9%. The 0% treatment is therefore conditional, not automatic. Conditions for Qualifying Free Zone Person status A free-zone company seeking the qualifying regime must generally satisfy requirements including: Maintaining adequate substance in the UAE free zone; Deriving qualifying income; Not electing to be subject to the ordinary Corporate Tax regime; Complying with the arm’s-length principle; Maintaining transfer-pricing documentation where required; Preparing audited financial statements; Staying within the permitted de minimis limit for non-qualifying revenue; and Meeting other conditions prescribed under the Corporate Tax framework. A company that fails a required condition may lose the benefit of the qualifying regime for the relevant period and potentially subsequent periods under the applicable rules. The trade license alone does not establish eligibility. What adequate substance means in practice Substance depends on the nature and scale of the business. A UAE company should ordinarily conduct its core income-generating activities in the free zone and maintain appropriate: Assets; Qualified employees; Operating expenditure; Premises; Management functions; and Commercial decision-making. Not every business needs a large office or extensive workforce. A holding company and an operating consultancy will naturally require different resources. However, a company with no genuine UAE activity, no decision-makers, no operational expenditure and all functions performed from India may struggle to demonstrate that its Dubai presence reflects the real business. Outsourcing may be permitted in appropriate circumstances, but the company should retain proper supervision and should document the outsourced functions. Qualifying income must be identified A Qualifying Free Zone Person does not receive 0% on all income merely because it operates from a free zone. The treatment depends on matters such as: Identity and status of the customer; Nature of the activity; Whether the activity is qualifying or excluded; Location and use of immovable property; Transactions with other free-zone persons; Dealings with mainland customers; Existence of a domestic permanent establishment; and Compliance with the prescribed conditions. Specified activities can be treated as qualifying, while other activities are excluded. Certain transactions with natural persons, banking, insurance, finance, leasing and immovable-property activities may require particular attention, subject to the detailed rules and exceptions. The company should classify each material revenue stream instead of applying one tax rate to its complete turnover. The de minimis test A Qualifying Free Zone Person may earn a limited amount of non-qualifying revenue without automatically losing its status. Broadly, non-qualifying revenue must not exceed the lower of: AED 5 million; or 5% of total revenue. This is a revenue test, not a profit test. A business that approaches the limit should monitor the position throughout the year. Discovering the breach only after the financial statements have been completed can leave limited scope for corrective action. A free-zone company does not receive the ordinary AED 375,000 band A common misunderstanding is that a free-zone company receives: 0% on qualifying income; 0% on the first AED 375,000 of non-qualifying income; and 9% only on the balance. That is not the general treatment of a Qualifying Free Zone Person. A qualifying free-zone company is subject to 0% on qualifying income and 9% on taxable income that is not qualifying income. It does not ordinarily receive the standard AED 375,000 0% threshold against its non-qualifying taxable income. This difference should be considered before assuming that the free-zone regime will always produce a better result than the ordinary Corporate Tax regime. Should a free-zone company elect for the standard regime? In some circumstances, a free-zone company may decide that the ordinary Corporate Tax regime is commercially more suitable. The decision may be relevant where the company: Primarily serves mainland customers; Earns significant non-qualifying income; Cannot maintain the required substance; Does not wish to bear the cost of qualifying-regime compliance; Expects taxable income to remain within the standard 0% threshold; or Operates a business model that does not fit comfortably within the qualifying-income rules. The election should not be made casually because it can apply for the prescribed period. A comparison should be prepared using realistic projections of revenue, expenses, customer categories and compliance costs. Small Business Relief Eligible resident persons with revenue not exceeding AED 3 million may elect for Small Business Relief, subject to the prescribed conditions. The relief can treat the eligible person as having no taxable income for the relevant tax period and can simplify certain compliance requirements. Under the current framework, the relief has been extended to eligible tax periods ending on or before 31 December 2029. Important points include: The AED 3 million test applies to revenue, not taxable profit; The threshold must be considered for the current and relevant earlier tax periods; The election must be made for the relevant tax period; A Qualifying Free Zone Person cannot elect for the relief; Certain members of large multinational groups are excluded; Registration and return filing may still be required; and Records supporting the election must be maintained. Small Business Relief is not the same as simply having no tax to pay under the AED 375,000 taxable-income band. Natural persons conducting business in the UAE An Indian individual conducting a business or business activity in the UAE can fall within Corporate Tax even without using a company. A natural person is generally subject to Corporate Tax on UAE business activities where total turnover from those activities exceeds AED 1 million in a Gregorian calendar year. The following categories are generally excluded from the business-turnover test when earned in the person’s private capacity and the applicable conditions are met: Employment income; Personal investment income; and Real-estate investment income. An employee receiving a Dubai salary does not become subject to Corporate Tax merely because the salary exceeds AED 1 million. Similarly, an individual holding shares personally and receiving ordinary investment dividends may be outside Corporate Tax for that income. The treatment can change where the person is carrying on a licensed or commercial investment activity rather than investing privately. UAE property income of an individual Real-estate investment income earned by an individual may fall outside Corporate Tax where the activity is conducted personally and is not carried on, or required to be carried on, through a license. However, a licensed property-management, holiday-home or real-estate operating business may not receive the same treatment. Investors should distinguish between: Personally owning and leasing investment property; Operating a licensed holiday-home business; Developing and selling properties as a business; Managing properties belonging to others; and Holding property through a company. VAT, land-department fees and the investor’s Indian tax position must also be considered separately. Corporate Tax registration A UAE company that is a taxable person must register with the Federal Tax Authority and obtain a Corporate Tax Registration Number within the applicable timeline. This obligation can apply even if the company: Has not started trading; Has no taxable profit; Expects to use Small Business Relief; Is incorporated in a free zone; Believes all income will qualify for the 0% rate; or Has made losses. The registration process is completed through the EmaraTax platform. Late registration can attract an administrative penalty. The company should not wait until its first tax return becomes due before addressing registration. Filing and payment deadline A Corporate Tax return is generally due within nine months from the end of the relevant tax period. Any Corporate Tax payable is ordinarily due within the same period. For example, a company with a financial year ending on 31 December 2026 would generally have a filing and payment deadline nine months after that year-end, subject to any applicable official extension or special decision. A company eligible for Small Business Relief still has to file the prescribed return and make the relevant election. The filing deadline should be planned backward. Accounts, related-party schedules and tax adjustments should be prepared well before the final month. Financial statements and accounting records Every investor should know the company’s financial year from the date of incorporation. The business should maintain records including: Sales invoices; Supplier invoices; Bank statements; Expense receipts; Payroll records; Fixed-asset register; Loan agreements; Shareholder-current-account ledger; Related-party contracts; Inventory records; Foreign-exchange workings; Ownership records; and Supporting tax computations. Accounting records should generally be maintained for the prescribed retention period. Records supporting a relief, exemption or tax position must be sufficiently detailed for the Federal Tax Authority to review. A bank statement is not a substitute for bookkeeping. Are audited financial statements required? The audit requirement depends on the company’s status, revenue, free-zone position and any separate requirement imposed by the licensing authority. A Qualifying Free Zone Person is required to maintain audited financial statements. Other taxable persons may also fall within a prescribed audit requirement, while a free-zone authority or bank may request audited accounts independently of the Corporate Tax Law. Indian investors should not choose a low-cost accounting package on the assumption that an audit will never be needed. The business structure and tax position should be reviewed first. Related-party transactions Transactions between a UAE company and its Indian shareholder, Indian group company or other related person must comply with the arm’s-length principle. Common cross-border related-party transactions include: Management fees; Technical services; Software support; Marketing services; Loans; Interest; Guarantees; Royalties; Cost allocations; Purchase or sale of goods; and Transfer of intellectual property. The company should be able to establish that: The service or funding was actually provided; The transaction had a commercial purpose; The amount was arm’s length; The allocation method was reasonable; Appropriate contracts and invoices exist; and The treatment is consistent in both countries. Transfer-pricing documentation requirements may apply when the prescribed conditions and thresholds are met. Even where a formal file is not mandatory, the arm’s-length rule continues to apply. Participation exemption for qualifying investments The UAE Corporate Tax regime contains a participation exemption that can exempt qualifying dividends and capital gains from certain ownership interests, subject to detailed conditions. The conditions can include matters such as: Minimum ownership interest or acquisition cost; Minimum holding period or intention to hold; Tax status of the investee; Rights attached to the ownership interest; and Composition of the investee’s assets. An Indian investor should not assume that every dividend or share-sale gain earned by a UAE holding company is automatically exempt. The investment structure, holding period and status of the underlying company should be reviewed before acquisition or disposal. Tax losses A company making a loss may be able to carry forward qualifying tax losses and use them against taxable income of later periods, subject to statutory restrictions. Continuity of ownership and business may affect the availability of losses following a major ownership change. Proper filing remains important during loss-making years. If the company fails to register, file or maintain supporting records, it may have difficulty establishing the loss when the business later becomes profitable. UAE withholding tax The UAE Corporate Tax regime currently applies withholding tax at a rate of 0% to prescribed categories of UAE-sourced income paid to non-residents. A 0% withholding rate should not be confused with a complete absence of tax analysis. A non-resident may still have UAE Corporate Tax exposure if it has a permanent establishment, taxable nexus or another basis of taxation in the UAE. The Indian recipient must also examine whether the income is taxable in India. Foreign tax credit Where the same income is taxed outside the UAE, foreign tax credit may be available against UAE Corporate Tax, subject to the prescribed conditions and limits. The credit is generally restricted and does not automatically produce a refund for foreign tax exceeding the UAE tax attributable to the income. The company should retain: Foreign tax return; Withholding certificate; Proof of payment; Income reconciliation; Tax computation; and Evidence linking the foreign tax to the relevant income. Foreign tax credit is calculated at the company level. Tax paid personally by a shareholder ordinarily cannot simply be claimed by the company. UAE Corporate Tax does not replace VAT Corporate Tax and VAT are separate regimes. Corporate Tax is imposed on taxable income or profit. VAT is generally imposed on taxable supplies of goods and services. A company may therefore: Be registered for both Corporate Tax and VAT; Be registered for Corporate Tax but remain below the VAT threshold; Have no Corporate Tax payable but still owe VAT; or Qualify for a 0% Corporate Tax rate while making standard-rated VAT supplies. Free-zone status also produces different results under the two regimes. A free zone recognised for Corporate Tax purposes is not automatically treated as a designated zone for every VAT purpose. The Indian place-of-effective-management risk For an Indian investor, the most serious cross-border issue may arise where a UAE company is managed from India. Under Indian tax law, a foreign company can become resident in India if its place of effective management is in India during the relevant period. The analysis broadly considers where the key management and commercial decisions necessary for conducting the business as a whole are made in substance. Risk indicators can include: The Indian owner approving all contracts from India; Online banking controlled exclusively from India; Strategic decisions made during meetings in India; UAE directors acting only on instructions; No operational management in Dubai; Employees reporting to decision-makers in India; Pricing and customer acceptance controlled from India; and Board minutes that do not match actual conduct. A Dubai trade license and UAE Corporate Tax registration do not prevent Indian corporate residence if the management facts point to India. Permanent-establishment exposure in India Even if the UAE company is not treated as an Indian resident, it may have an Indian permanent establishment. This risk can arise where the company: Maintains a fixed place in India; Uses an Indian office on a continuing basis; Sends employees to India for substantial service activities; Has a dependent agent acting for it; Negotiates or concludes contracts through India; or Performs core revenue-generating functions in India. Where a permanent establishment exists, India may tax the profits attributable to it. UAE Corporate Tax and the India–UAE treaty should then be considered together, including the possible availability of foreign tax credit. Indian tax on the investor Incorporating a company in Dubai does not automatically remove the Indian shareholder from Indian taxation. Depending on the investor’s residential status and the nature of the payment, India may tax: Salary from the UAE company; Directors’ fees; Dividends; Interest; Royalties; Capital gains; Benefits received from the company; and Income arising from transactions with the UAE entity. An Indian resident is generally exposed to tax on worldwide income. A non-resident’s position is narrower but must still be examined for Indian-source income and amounts received in India. The investor’s residence should be determined for every tax year rather than inferred from the UAE visa. FEMA and overseas-investment compliance Where an Indian resident invests in a UAE company, the transaction must also comply with India’s foreign-exchange and overseas-investment framework. Relevant matters may include: Permitted investment route; Liberalised Remittance Scheme, where applicable; Overseas direct investment classification; Remittance through an authorised dealer bank; Reporting of acquisition or incorporation; Valuation requirements; Annual Performance Report; Changes in capital or ownership; Loans and guarantees; Disinvestment; and Repatriation of amounts due to India. Corporate Tax registration in the UAE does not regularise a FEMA default in India. The ownership documents, remittance records and regulatory filings should be organised from the date of incorporation. Foreign-asset reporting in India An Indian resident may be required to disclose foreign shares, bank accounts, signing authority, financial interests and foreign income in the Indian income-tax return. The reporting obligation can apply even where: The UAE company has made no profit; No dividend has been received; The shareholding has a low value; The company is inactive; The account is used only for business; or UAE Corporate Tax has been properly filed. Foreign-asset reporting is separate from calculating taxable income. Incomplete disclosure can have consequences under India’s tax and foreign-asset framework. Practical example: Indian owner of a Dubai consultancy Assume an Indian investor incorporates a Dubai mainland consultancy company. The company earns AED 2.5 million in revenue and employs three people in Dubai. It maintains an office, enters into contracts with UAE customers and keeps proper accounting records. The owner participates in management from Dubai but also spends time in India. The company should consider: Corporate Tax registration; Preparation of financial statements; Deductibility of salaries and operating expenses; Related-party payments to India; Taxable-income calculation; Return filing within nine months; VAT registration and filing; Transfer-pricing compliance; and Retention of supporting records. The owner must separately examine: Indian residential status; Taxation of salary and dividends; Foreign-asset disclosure; FEMA reporting; Location of strategic decision-making; and Whether activities in India create a permanent establishment. The fact that the UAE company pays Corporate Tax does not settle the owner’s Indian obligations. Practical example: Dubai free-zone trading company Consider an Indian-owned free-zone company purchasing goods internationally and selling them to customers in the UAE and other countries. Before relying on the 0% free-zone rate, the company should classify: Nature of the goods; Location from which trading activities are conducted; Status of suppliers and customers; Whether transactions are with free-zone or mainland persons; Whether the activity falls within a qualifying category; Amount of non-qualifying revenue; UAE substance; Related-party transactions; Customs and VAT treatment; and Audit and transfer-pricing requirements. A sales invoice issued from a free zone is not enough to establish qualifying income. Common mistakes made by Indian investors Frequent mistakes include: Treating the UAE as a no-tax jurisdiction; Assuming free-zone income is automatically taxed at 0%; Confusing revenue with taxable income; Ignoring Corporate Tax registration because the company is inactive; Believing no tax payable means no return is required; Failing to file the Small Business Relief election; Applying the AED 375,000 band to non-qualifying income of a Qualifying Free Zone Person; Keeping no proper accounting records; Paying personal expenses from the company; Recording shareholder transfers without identifying their nature; Ignoring related-party pricing; Managing the company entirely from India; Overlooking Indian permanent-establishment exposure; Failing to report UAE shares and accounts in India; Ignoring FEMA reporting; and Reviewing tax only when the first return becomes due. A practical first-year compliance plan An Indian investor establishing a UAE company should complete the following steps: 1. Confirm the company’s first financial year and tax period. 2. Register for Corporate Tax within the applicable deadline. 3. Determine whether the company will follow the standard or qualifying free-zone regime. 4. Assess Small Business Relief eligibility where relevant. 5. Establish accounting records from the first transaction. 6. Separate personal and business expenditure. 7. Document shareholder funding as capital, loan or another identifiable item. 8. Prepare agreements for related-party transactions. 9. Review VAT registration independently. 10. Monitor free-zone qualifying and non-qualifying revenue. 11. Determine whether audited financial statements are required. 12. Review Indian POEM and permanent-establishment risks. 13. Complete FEMA and overseas-investment reporting. 14. Assess the owner’s Indian tax and foreign-asset disclosures. 15. Prepare the Corporate Tax return well before the nine-month deadline. Conclusion UAE Corporate Tax has moved Dubai business ownership into a more structured compliance environment, but the UAE remains commercially attractive for Indian investors who establish and operate their businesses properly. The standard regime offers a 0% rate on taxable income up to AED 375,000 and a 9% rate above that level. Eligible small businesses may benefit from Small Business Relief, while qualifying free-zone companies may access a 0% rate on qualifying income. Each benefit is subject to its own conditions. For Indian investors, the UAE tax return is only one part of the complete picture. The operating structure must also withstand Indian residence, POEM, permanent-establishment, transfer-pricing, FEMA and foreign-asset-reporting analysis. A Dubai company with genuine operations, disciplined accounting and documented decision-making is considerably easier to defend than an entity created only on paper. The most effective Corporate Tax planning therefore begins when the business is structured—not when the return deadline approaches.

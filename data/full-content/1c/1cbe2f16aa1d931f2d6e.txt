appeals without examining the dispute, but decided to rule on the substantive legal questions. It held that the administrator could not write down the AT1 bonds after the final reconstruction scheme came into force on March 13, 2020, because the government had removed from the approved scheme the provision expressly authorising the write-down. The Bench ruled that a general provision preserving existing contracts and instruments under the reconstruction scheme could not, by itself, confer specific authority on the administrator to extinguish the bonds. It described the write-down as an impermissible act beyond the administrator’s powe₹under the RBI’s Master Circular on Basel III Capital Regulations read with the reconstruction scheme. The court also rejected the argument that the administrator could write down the bonds at any point while the reconstruction process remained underway. It interpreted the RBI’s regulations to mean that the bonds had to be written down or converted before the reconstruction scheme took effect, not after its commencement. The Bench further held that the legality of the administrator’s action had to be assessed against the law and circumstances prevailing on March 14, 2020, when the write-down was ordered. Subsequent developments in Yes Bank’s financial position could not determine the validity of that decision. The court clarified that annual coupon payments on the bonds would remain unaffected by the outcome of the appeals and must be made in accordance with the relevant information memoranda. The parties were directed to bear their own costs. The dispute followed the RBI’s decision to place Yes Bank under a moratorium on March 5, 2020, amid concerns over its deteriorating financial position. The central bank subsequently framed a reconstruction scheme under the Banking Regulation Act, 1949, to revive the lender and facilitate the induction of new investors. On March 14, 2020, the bank’s administrator notified the stock exchanges that the outstanding AT1 bonds had been written down. The move was challenged by Axis Trustee Services Limited, representing debenture holders, along with individual investo₹and other bondholders. The challenge₹contended that the administrator could not extinguish their investments once the reconstruction scheme had taken effect. They argued that the draft scheme had expressly provided for an AT1 bond write-down, but the provision was removed from the final version approved by the Centre. The Bombay High Court accepted this argument, finding that Yes Bank had been reconstructed on March 13, 2020, when the final scheme came into force. The administrator, therefore, could not take a fresh decision to write down the bonds the following day without legal authority under the operative scheme. The High Court also found that neither the final reconstruction scheme nor the RBI had empowered the administrator to carry out the write-down. It rejected the objection that the writ petitions were not maintainable, observing that the bond conditions drew their statutory basis from the RBI’s Master Circular, issued under its regulatory powers. While setting aside the write-off, the High Court clarified that its ruling did not prevent the parties concerned from pursuing any further action permitted by law. Yes Bank and other parties subsequently challenged the judgment before the Supreme Court. The appeals centred on whether the administrator could extinguish the bondholders’ investments despite the deletion of the write-down provision from the final reconstruction scheme. In a further observation, the Supreme Court said Yes Bank had actively offered the high-risk AT1 instruments to individual investo₹despite RBI rules restricting their distribution to specified categories of investors. It said tighter scrutiny should have been exercised before such securities were sold to individuals. The court also criticised the handling of the bonds by Yes Bank, the administrator and the RBI, observing that investo₹had been exposed to avoidable risks because of administrative lapses. The appeals were dismissed, leaving the Bombay High Court’s decision intact. The Supreme Court clarified that its observations were confined to the appeals before it and would not influence the outcome of any pending proceedings. Siddharth Batra, Advocate on Record, Supreme Court said that Yes Bank’s decision to wipe out the AT-1 bonds cannot stand because the Administrator exercised a power reserved for the RBI. It gives substantial relief to bondholde₹by restoring their rights under the bonds, with coupon payments to be made according to the bond terms, he said. "The wider impact is that banks and regulato₹must strictly follow the prescribed legal process when imposing losses on investors, even during a financial rescue. AT-1 bonds remain high-risk instruments; the judgment does not protect them against a write-down lawfully ordered," he said. Karan Batura, Partner, Dhir & Dhir Associates said the remedies available to Yes Bank are limited and include a review petition on narrow grounds, followed, if necessary, by a curative petition. "The bank will also have to assess the accounting, capital adequacy and regulatory implications of restoring bondholders’ rights," he said. The judgment reinforces that regulatory intervention must remain within the bounds of statutory authority and contractual terms, said Tushar Kumar, Advocate, Supreme Court of India.

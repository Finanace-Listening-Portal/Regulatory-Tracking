Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); Vintek Control Systems Vs Commissioner of Central Tax (GSTAT Bengaluru) Summary: The Goods and Services Tax Appellate Tribunal considered an appeal by Shri Kariyappa Alahalli Manjappa, proprietor of M/s Vintek Control Systems, concerning input tax credit claimed for financial year 2018-19. Scrutiny disclosed a difference between credit availed in GSTR-3B and credit appearing in GSTR-2A, comprising IGST of Rs. 1,32,655 and CGST and SGST of Rs. 1,288 each. The appellant attributed the mismatch to purchases from M/s Naveen’s and M/s Classic Power Company and contended that a genuine purchaser should not lose credit because of supplier default. The adjudicating authority confirmed tax, interest and penalty; the first appellate authority dismissed the challenge. The Tribunal identified the central issue as whether the appellant had discharged the burden under Section 155 of proving compliance with Section 16(2)(c) of the CGST Act, including actual payment of tax by the suppliers. It examined the competing submissions concerning Suncraft Energy, Instakart Services, Bhandari Scrap Traders and other authorities discussed in the order. Applying its understanding of the Supreme Court’s later decision in Bhandari Scrap Traders, it held that Section 16(2)(c) remained applicable and could not be read down merely because the recipient claimed to be bona fide. It also acknowledged that a mismatch with GSTR-2A alone does not establish supplier non-payment for FY 2018-19. Circular No. 183/15/2022-GST prescribed a mechanism for resolving mismatches for the relevant period, including supplier confirmation where the difference attributable to a supplier did not exceed Rs. 5 lakh. The appellant produced neither a supplier certificate nor a tax-payment confirmation, supplier GSTR-3B or other satisfactory evidence, despite repeated opportunities. Although the original adjudication did not apply the circular and the appellant missed the scheduled hearings, the first appellate authority identified the required certificate and the Tribunal afforded another opportunity. The Tribunal found no demonstrated prejudice warranting remand and concluded that the statutory burden had not been discharged. The appellant also relied on a purchase order, supplier invoice, banking records and export documents to substantiate the alleged purchase and movement of a portable fire pump from Chennai. The Tribunal observed discrepancies between the supplier invoice and export documentation concerning classification and description, and the absence of e-way bills, transport receipts, freight evidence, goods-receipt records and stock entries. It did not find fraud or conclude that the exported goods were different; rather, it held that the documents did not establish the necessary connection between the claimed purchase and subsequent export. This discussion addressed the appellant’s own plea and was not treated as a fresh ground of demand. Accordingly, the Tribunal upheld the ITC demand of Rs. 1,35,231 and penalty of Rs. 33,266. It sustained interest liability under Section 50(3) but directed the proper officer to calculate interest only on credit wrongly availed and utilised, in accordance with Rule 88B(3), and furnish the computation before recovery. The order preserved any entitlement to re-avail credit if suppliers subsequently discharged tax, subject to applicable law, and directed transmission of information to the authorities having jurisdiction over the suppliers. The appeal was dismissed without costs. Cases Discussed Bhandari Scrap Traders v. Union of India & Ors. — SLP (C) No. 23931 of 2026; order dated 24 July 2026. ANALYSIS AND FINDINGS Issue (i): The scope of Section 16(2)(c) 26. Section 16(1) confers the entitlement to credit; Section 16(2) conditions it. Sub- section (2) opens with a non obstante clause and provides that no registered person “shall be entitled” to credit “unless” each of the listed conditions is satisfied. The conditions are cumulative. Clause (c), as it stood in 2018- 19, required that, subject to Section 41 or Section 43A, “the tax charged in respect of such supply has been actually paid to the Government”. Section 155 places the burden of proving eligibility on the claimant. It is settled that credit is a creature of statute and is available only on the conditions the statute prescribes as held by the Honourable Suprem e Court in the cases of Jayam & Co. v. Assistant Commissioner, (2016) 15 SCC 125 and ALD Automotive Pvt. Ltd. v. Commercial Tax Officer, (2019) 13 SCC 225. 27. In Instakart Services, decided on 09.02.2026, the High Court of Karnataka, following the Tripura High Court in Sahil Enterprises v. Union of India (decided on 06.01.2026), read down Section 16(2)(c) so that it would not control. A decision of the jurisdictional High Court ordinar ily binds this Bench. 28. The question has since been considered by the Supreme Court. In Bhandari Scrap Traders v. Union of India & Ors., SLP (C) No. 23931 of 2026, decided on 24.07.2026 [2026 LiveLaw (SC) 725], the Supreme Court dismissed the special leave p etitions against the judgment of the Gujarat High Court in Maruti Enterprise v. Union of India, which had upheld Section 16(2)(c) and declined to read it down. The dismissal was accompanied by reasons. The Supreme Court held that the Delhi Value Added Tax Act, 2004 and the CGST Act rest on different statutory schemes, so that the decisions under the Delhi statute protecting bona fide purchasers cannot be carried over. It observed that Sections 41, 73 and 74 provide a framework under which a purchaser may re – avail credit once the supplier discharges the tax. It found no ground to hold Section 16(2)(c) unconstitutional, and agreed with the High Court, which had refused to read it down. 29. An order dismissing a special leave petition does not ordinarily declare th e law. Where, however, the Supreme Court gives reasons, the law stated in those reasons is a declaration under Article 141 and binds all courts and tribunals as held by the Supreme Court in the case of Kunhayammed v. State of Kerala, (2000) 6 SCC 359. The High Court of Rajasthan has so understood Bhandari Scrap Traders in Sumetco Alloys Pvt. Ltd. v. Union of India Civil Writ Petition No. 9323 of 2026, decided on 13.08.2026), and declined to follow Sahil Enterprises and Instakart Services. 30. The High Court of Karnataka decided Instakart Services before the Supreme Court pronounced on the question, and did not have the benefit of that decision. The law declared by the Supreme Court now governs. With the utmost respect to the jurisdictional High Court, we a re bound by Article 141 to apply Section 16(2)(c) as enacted and upheld. 31. Suncraft Energy does not advance the Appellant’s case either. The Revenue’s special leave petition against that judgment was dismissed in limine. The view that a purchaser may be proc eeded against only in exceptional cases cannot be reconciled with the statutory scheme affirmed in Bhandari Scrap Traders. The same applies to the other decisions cited in the reply to the notice. 32. This does not leave an honest purchaser without remedy. For 2017- 18 and 2018-19, Circular No. 183/15/2022- GST enables the purchaser to establish the supplier’s payment by a certificate. The proviso to Section 41(2), in turn, permits the purchaser to re- avail the credit once the supplier pays the tax, subject to th e conditions and time limits prescribed by law. What the purchaser cannot do is retain the credit when nothing on record shows that the tax reached the Government. 33. Issue (i) is answered accordingly. Section 16(2)(c) applies as enacted and cannot be read do does not by itself satisfy the clause. The Appellant must prove that the suppliers paid the tax to the Government at least when the recipient was put on notice by the Revenue with the details of the supplier. Issue (ii): Proof of payment of tax by the suppliers 34. We accept the Appellant’s submission that, for 2018-19, a shortfall in GSTR – 2A does not by itself establish that the supplier failed to pay the tax. FORM GSTR-2A is a facilitator for self- assessment and not an instrument of assessment as held by the Honourable Supreme Court in the case of Union of India v. Bharti Airtel Ltd., (2022) 4 SCC 328. Neither Section 16(2)(aa) nor Rule 36(4) was in force during that year. It is for this reason that the Board issued Circular No. 183/15/2022- GST. Under paragraph 4, the proper officer is to verify that the recipient holds a tax invoice, has received the goods or services, and has paid the supplier. The recipient must then establish that the suppli er paid the tax. Where the difference in respect of a supplier exceeds Rs. 5 lakh, a certificate from a Chartered Accountant or Cost Accountant is required (paragraph 4.1.1 of the Circular); where it does not, a certificate from the supplier suffices (paragraph 4.1.2 of the Circular). In the present case, the difference in respect of each supplier is well below Rs. 5 lakhs. 35. The Circular does not dispense with Section 16(2)(c). Nor does it shift to the Department the burden that Section 155 places on the cla imant. It prescribes the manner in which the recipient may discharge that burden. That is a with the supplier, and a purchaser who has paid a supplier in full through the bank may reasonably be expected to obtain from that supplier a certificate of payment of tax. 36. The Appellant had ample opportunity to produce that certificate. The ASMT – 10 of 03.10.2023 raised the mismatch. The notice of 28.12.2023 stated the ground plainly and called for all the evidence relied upon. The Adjudicating Authority fixed three dates of hearing, and the Appellant attended none. On 30.05.2025, the First Appellate Authority held in terms that the certificate was required and had not been produced. The pres ent appeal was filed about eleven months thereafter, and written submissions were filed in September 2026. At the hearing on 17-09-2026, the learned Authorised Representative was specifically asked whether a certificate from either supplier, or any other p roof of payment of tax, could be produced. None was produced, and no time was sought. To a specific question the Authorised Representative for the Appellant categorically submits that they could not reach out to the supplier even after repeated attempt. 37. At no stage, therefore, has the Appellant produced a certificate from either supplier, a certificate from an accountant, a copy of either supplier’s GSTR – 3B, or even a letter to either supplier seeking confirmation. Nothing on record explains why a supplie r who was paid in full through the bank could not be approached. Neither the grounds of appeal nor the written submissions address the finding of the First Appellate Authority on this point. 38. In State of Karnataka v. Ecom Gill Coffee Trading Pvt. Ltd. Thirdly, even at this stage of the entire proceedings before this Tribunal as the final fact- finding authority, the Appellant has still produced nothing. In Abhimaani Structures, the taxpayer had no opportunity to comply; this Appellant had it repeatedly. A party complaining of a procedural lapse must show real prejudice as held in the case of State Bank of Patiala v. S.K. Sharma, (1996) 3 SCC 364. No prejudice is shown. 41. Issue (ii) is, therefore, answered against the Appellant. He has not proved that M/s Naveen’s or M/s Classic Power Compan y paid the tax. The condition in Section 16(2)(c) is not satisfied, and the lapse at the first stage has caused no prejudice. This finding by itself sustains the demand. Issue (iii): The plea of genuine purchase 42. Our answer to Issue (ii) decides the appeal on the ground stated in the notice. We nonetheless examine this issue because the Appellant’s defence of good faith, and his reliance on Ecom Gill Coffee Trading, rest on the assertion that the goods moved from C hennai to Dabaspet and were received by him. Having raised that plea, the Appellant must establish it. 43. Section 68 empowers the Government to require documents to accompany goods in transit. Rule 138 of the CGST Rules, substituted by Notification No. 12/2018-Central Tax dated 07.03.2018, made the e- inter- State movement of goods from 01.04.2018, the date notified by Notification No. 15/2018- Central Tax dated 23.03.2018. As the rule stood in June 2018, a registered person causing moveme nt of goods of consignment value exceeding fifty thousand rupees, inclusive of tax, was required to generate the e- way bill on the common portal before the movement commenced. It could be generated by the supplier, the recipient or the transporter. Where t he supplier or the transporter generated it, the particulars were made available to the recipient on the portal, with the option to accept or reject them (sub- rules (11) and (12)). Rule 138A required the conveyance to carry the e-way bill, and Section 129 provided for detention of goods moving without it. 44. Every condition of Rule 138 is attracted here. The supply was inter- State. The invoice is dated 27.06.2018. The consignment value was Rs. 14,00,000/-. The purchase order stipulated delivery “by vehicle”. An e- way bill was, therefore, mandatory, and the Appellant’s own written submissions state that e- way bills exist. 45. The e- way bill records the GSTINs of the parties, the invoice, the value, the places of dispatch and delivery, and the vehicle number. It is g enerated before the goods move. It is the contemporaneous record that connects the invoice with a vehicle carrying the goods to a stated destination. In Ecom Gill Coffee Trading, the Supreme Court held that physical movement is to be proved by, among other payment of freight charges, acknowledgement of taking delivery of goods”. The Appellant has produced none of these, beyond an unexplained handwritten endorsement on his own copy of the invoice. 46. The e- way bill was within the Appellant’s reach. If the supplier generated one, its particulars were available to the Appellant on the portal. If the supplier did not, the Appellant could have generated it himself. The Appellant asserts that e-way bills exist, yet has not produced them at any of the three stages, and offers no explanation. That unexplained omission weighs against the claim that the goods moved as the invoice states. 47. The subsequent movement is equally unrecorded. The Appellant states that the goods were received at Dabaspet and then exported, but the shipping bill was filed at Ahmedabad. Movement of goods worth Rs. 12,50,000/- from Dabaspet to Ahmedabad would itself have required an e- way bill, and none is produced. If the goods instead tra velled directly from Chennai to Gujarat on the Appellant’s direction, an e- way bill for that movement would show it. None is produced either. 48. The export documents do not fill the gap. To prove that M/s Naveen’s supplied the goods that were exported, the Ap pellant must connect the two sets of documents. As noted in paragraph 22, the invoice of M/s Naveen’s describes a fire pump under an apparel code, taxed at 12%. The shipping bill describes a “control panel” under 85371000, of Gujarat origin. The “corrected ” export transport record links them, and no explanation has been offered. We do not hold that the exported goods were different goods. We hold only that these documents, in the absence of explanation, do not establish the link. Although no application was made under Rule 112, we have examined these documents under Section 113(1) in the interest of justice. Even if they proved receipt, receipt would go only to Section 16(2)(b); it cannot establish that the supplier paid the tax. 49. This finding does not confirm the demand on a new ground, which Section 75(7) forbids. The demand rests on Section 16(2)(c), the ground stated in the notice; this finding only answers the Appellant’s own plea. Par agraph 4 of the very Circular on which the Appellant relies requires the proper officer to verify that the recipient has received the goods or services. Nor do we hold that the absence of an e- way bill defeats credit in every case, for Section 16(2) does n ot make it a condition. We hold that where a recipient relies on the genuineness of an inter-State purchase above the threshold value, the e- way bill is the primary evidence of movement; and if it is not produced and its absence is not satisfactorily expla ined, movement must be proved by other reliable contemporaneous evidence, such as lorry receipts, freight payments, goods receipt notes and stock entries. None has been produced. The plea of genuine purchase fails. Issue (iv): Tax, interest and penalty 50. Tax. The IGST of Rs. 1,32,655/- represents, on the Appellant’s own working, the tax charged in the invoice of M/s Naveen’s, less the excess credit relating to other months (paragraph 19). It is sustained for the reasons recorded under Issue (ii). No material has been produced in respect of M/s Classic Power Company, and the CGST and SGST of Rs. 1,288/- each are likewise sustained. The tax sustained is, therefore, IGST Rs. 1,32,655/-, CGST Rs. 1,288/- and SGST Rs. 1,288/-, in all Rs. 1,35,231/-. 51. Interest. Inte rest is compensatory and follows the tax. Section 50(3), substituted by the Finance Act, 2022 with effect from 01.07.2017, levies interest on credit wrongly availed and utilised, at eighteen per cent per annum (Notification No. 13/2017-Central Tax, as amen ded by Notification No. 9/2022- Central Tax). Rule 88B(3) prescribes the method of computation: interest runs from the date of utilisation to the date of reversal or payment, and credit is treated as utilised when, and to the extent that, the balance in the electronic credit ledger falls below the amount wrongly availed. The liability to interest is upheld. The Appellant’s grievance about its computation is, however, well founded. The Order-in- Original directs recovery “at applicable rates” without any working, and records no finding on utilisation. The proper officer shall compute the interest under Rule 88B(3) and furnish the working to the Appellant before recovery. 52. Penalty. Section 73(9) mandates a penalty of ten per cent of the tax or ten thousand rupees, whichever is higher, and Section 122(2)(a) is to the same effect. This penalty does not depend on fraud or intent to evade, which are the province of Section 74. The absence of any such finding, therefore, does not assist the Appellant. The table in para graph 20 shows that the Adjudicating Authority imposed the statutory minimum under each enactment. The penalty of Rs. 33,266/- is upheld. G. A WORD BEFORE WE PART 53. In upholding Section 16(2)(c), the Gujarat High Court in Maruti Enterprise was conscious of the predicament of an honest purchaser who pays the price and the tax, and then loses the credit through a default he can neither see nor prevent. The High Court observed that the Government should consider a technology-driven mechanism enabling recipients to verify, invoice by invoice and in real time, whether their suppliers have paid the tax. The Revenue may also recover the tax from the defaulting supplier under Sections 73 and 74, whereupon the purchaser may re-avail the credit. 54. In 2018, the Appellant could not see his suppliers’ GSTR- 3B or their payments of tax; the proper officer can. Nothing said here lightens the burden under Section 155, and our decision does not rest on this paragraph. Good administration, however, requires that the proper officer examine the the defaulting supplier with equal diligence. M/s Naveen’s is registered in Tamil Nadu, and M/s Classic Power Company may not fall within the Respondent’s jurisdictio n. We expect the Department to pass this information to the officers having jurisdiction over those suppliers, so that they may proceed in accordance with law. If the tax is recovered, the Appellant’s right to re-avail the credit under Section 41 as reaffirmed in Maruti Enterprise may then be exercised in accordance with law. H. ORDER 55.

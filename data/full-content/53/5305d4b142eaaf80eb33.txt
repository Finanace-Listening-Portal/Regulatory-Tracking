(NCLAT Delhi) Summary: The National Company Law Appellate Tribunal (NCLAT), Principal Bench, New Delhi, considered four connected appeals filed by Hindalco Industries Limited and H.I.W. Workers’ Union under Section 61 of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016 (IBC), challenging the order dated 22 December 2021 passed by the NCLT, Cuttack Bench. The dispute concerned the initiation of the Corporate Insolvency Resolution Process (CIRP) against Hirakud Industrial Works Limited on an application filed by Nandakini Contractors Pvt. Ltd. under Section 7 of the IBC, alleging default in repayment of a loan of ₹14,51,047. The appellants challenged the genuineness of the financial debt, the circumstances surrounding the initiation of insolvency proceedings, the constitution of the Committee of Creditors (CoC), the conduct of the Resolution Professional and the approval of the resolution plan submitted by Regus Impex Pvt. Ltd. The Tribunal examined the background of the dispute, which originated from the disinvestment of the shareholding held by Infrastructure Development Company Limited of the Government of Odisha in the corporate debtor. The entire shareholding was acquired by Varsha Fabrics Pvt. Ltd., Mudrika Commercial Limited and India Finance Pvt. Ltd. Pursuant to directions issued by the Odisha High Court, a tripartite agreement dated 2 June 2006 was executed to ensure payment of outstanding workers’ dues. However, the factory was subsequently closed in 2007, and the shareholding was transferred to Indo Wagon Limited without settlement of the workers’ outstanding claims. Following prolonged litigation and directions issued by the Supreme Court, the Labour Court at Sambalpur quantified the workers’ dues at ₹45,66,67,133 on 11 November 2016. As the concerned companies failed to discharge these liabilities, the Odisha High Court directed auction of the corporate debtor’s assets for recovery of the workers’ dues. Hindalco Industries Limited, being the lessee and sole user of the private railway siding belonging to the corporate debtor, had participated in the auction proceedings. However, while the process of auctioning the corporate debtor’s assets was underway, Nandakini Contractors initiated proceedings under Section 7 of the IBC. Both the purported financial creditor and the corporate debtor jointly requested an urgent hearing on 3 June 2019, following which the NCLT admitted the insolvency application on 4 June 2019 and imposed a moratorium under Section 14. The appellants contended that the insolvency proceedings were deliberately initiated to prevent the auction of assets and defeat the legitimate claims of workers. They further alleged that the purported financial creditor and corporate debtor belonged to the same group of companies and had acted in collusion to misuse the insolvency framework for purposes other than genuine insolvency resolution. While examining whether a genuine financial debt under Section 5(8) of the IBC existed, the NCLAT observed that the Section 7 application was not supported by any loan agreement, documentary evidence of disbursement, relevant bank account entries or ledger accounts of the purported financial creditor. The Tribunal noted that the financial creditor had failed to furnish the prescribed documentary evidence establishing the existence of debt and default under Form 1 of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy (Application to Adjudicating Authority) Rules, 2016. The application was substantially founded upon correspondence between the financial creditor and corporate debtor, including an admission of liability by the corporate debtor. The Tribunal held that such correspondence, considered alongside the absence of supporting financial records, did not satisfactorily establish the existence and disbursement of the alleged loan. It distinguished the Supreme Court’s ruling in Orator Marketing Pvt. Ltd. Vs Samtex Desinz Pvt. Ltd., observing that recognition of interest-free loans as financial debt could not establish the genuineness of an otherwise unsubstantiated or fraudulent transaction. The NCLAT also examined the failure of the Resolution Professional to comply with the NCLT’s order dated 28 February 2020 directing production of documentary evidence supporting the financial debts claimed by the purported financial creditors. Despite subsequent opportunities, the requisite evidence was not properly placed before the Adjudicating Authority. The Tribunal rejected the explanation that the documents had been forwarded through email to the NCLT Registry, observing that compliance with judicial directions required production of the relevant material before the Tribunal itself. Considering the absence of reliable evidence of financial debt, the relationship between the purported financial creditor and corporate debtor, the timing of the insolvency proceedings and the unusual haste in admitting the application, the NCLAT concluded that the Section 7 application had been fraudulently initiated in collusion with the corporate debtor. Accordingly, the circumstances attracted Section 65 of the IBC concerning fraudulent or malicious initiation of insolvency proceedings. Another significant issue concerned the constitution of the Committee of Creditors and the participation of entities allegedly related to the corporate debtor. The appellants produced detailed charts demonstrating common directorships, cross-shareholdings, common registered addresses and interconnected corporate ownership among the purported financial creditors, the corporate debtor and the successful resolution applicant. The Tribunal examined the ownership structure involving Indo Wagon Engineering Limited, Adishwar Nivesh Pvt. Ltd. and several interconnected companies. It also considered the presence of common directors in the holding company of the corporate debtor, the entities constituting the CoC and Regus Impex Pvt. Ltd. In determining the actual relationship between the entities, the NCLAT considered the definition of control under Section 2(27) of the Companies Act, 2013, the concept of persons acting in concert under the SEBI (Substantial Acquisition of Shares and Takeovers) Regulations, 2011, and judicial principles governing the lifting of the corporate veil. The Tribunal concluded that the interconnected entities could not be examined merely by reference to their separate corporate identities. The circumstances justified piercing the corporate veil to ascertain the actual nature of their relationships, common control and coordinated conduct. Relying upon the principles discussed in Phoenix ARC Pvt. Ltd. Vs Spade Financial Services Ltd. and ArcelorMittal India Pvt. Ltd. Vs Satish Kumar Gupta, the NCLAT examined whether the entities were effectively acting in concert with the corporate debtor. It found that the constitution of the CoC was vitiated by the participation of related parties exercising substantial influence over its decisions. The Tribunal further held that the constitution of the CoC without proper verification and admission of financial claims violated Regulation 12(3) of the CIRP Regulations and the applicable proviso to Section 21(2) of the IBC. Referring to Jayanta Banerjee Vs Shashi Agarwal, it held that decisions taken by an illegally constituted CoC could not be validated merely by invoking the principle of commercial wisdom. The NCLAT separately examined the conduct of the Resolution Professional, particularly his treatment of the workers’ outstanding claims. The Tribunal noted that the workers’ union had communicated details of their long-pending dues through a letter dated 14 December 2019, shortly before consideration and approval of the resolution plan by the CoC. Having regard to the statutory duties prescribed under Sections 18 and 25 of the IBC and Regulations 9A, 12, 12A and 13 of the CIRP Regulations, the Tribunal observed that the Resolution Professional ought to have advised the workers to submit their claims in the prescribed form and considered any delay in the appropriate factual context. Instead, the Resolution Professional displayed a casual and indifferent approach towards the workers’ claims while extending considerably greater consideration to the purported financial creditors. The Tribunal also deprecated his conduct in signing an affidavit made in the name of a representative of the successful resolution applicant concerning eligibility under Section 29A. These circumstances warranted further examination of the conduct and statutory responsibilities of the Resolution Professional. Regarding the eligibility of Regus Impex Pvt. Ltd. to submit a resolution plan, the NCLAT examined the shareholding relationships between the successful resolution applicant, Dahisar Traders Pvt. Ltd., Luni Holding and Developers Pvt. Ltd. and companies connected with the corporate debtor’s holding structure. The Tribunal found significant linkages indicating influence within the meaning of clauses (k) and (m) of Section 5(24) of the IBC. The successful resolution applicant argued that even if the alleged related-party financial creditors were excluded from the CoC, the approval of the resolution plan would remain unaffected because the remaining independent financial creditor had also approved the plan. Reliance was placed on Pratap Technocrats Pvt. Ltd. Vs Monitoring Committee of Reliance Infratel Ltd. However, the NCLAT distinguished the decision, observing that the present case involved fraudulent initiation of insolvency proceedings. Consequently, neither the approval of an independent financial creditor nor the commercial wisdom of the CoC could sustain a resolution plan founded upon an invalid and fraudulent CIRP. Having concluded that the insolvency proceedings were fraudulently initiated and that the subsequently constituted CoC was also tainted, the NCLAT set aside the admission order passed under Section 7 of the IBC. Consequently, the CIRP stood abated, the corporate debtor was released from the rigours of insolvency proceedings, including the effect of the moratorium, and the approval of the resolution plan submitted by Regus Impex Pvt. Ltd. was quashed. Exercising powers under Section 65 of the IBC, the Tribunal imposed a penalty of ₹50 lakh each upon Nandakini Contractors Pvt. Ltd. and Hirakud Industrial Works Ltd., aggregating to ₹1 crore. The penalties were directed to be deposited with the Pay and Accounts Officer, Ministry of Corporate Affairs, within one month. The Tribunal also directed that ₹40 crore paid by the successful resolution applicant to the financial creditor in implementation of the approved resolution plan be returned to Regus Impex Pvt. Ltd. within one month. Other payments, alienation of assets and creation of third-party rights pursuant to the approved resolution plan were also directed to be reversed within the stipulated period. Finally, considering the serious acts of omission and commission attributed to the erstwhile Resolution Professional, the NCLAT directed the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Board of India (IBBI) to investigate his conduct, including possible collusion with the corporate debtor, financial creditor and successful resolution applicant, as well as dereliction of statutory duties. The investigation was directed to be completed within three months, with appropriate action to be taken in accordance with its findings. The Tribunal also granted liberty to the H.I.W. Workers’ Union and workers to pursue recovery of their outstanding dues from the corporate debtor. The NCLAT thus held that insolvency proceedings initiated fraudulently, and decisions taken by a tainted Committee of Creditors, cannot acquire legal validity merely because a resolution plan has subsequently been approved.

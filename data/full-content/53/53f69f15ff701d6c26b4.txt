(Karnataka High Court) Summary: The Karnataka High Court examined a writ petition filed by M/s SS Exports under Article 227 of the Constitution of India challenging the order dated 16 September 2022 passed by the XIII Additional City Civil Judge, Mayo Hall, Bengaluru, in Execution Case No. 15180 of 2005. The dispute concerned the recognition and enforcement in India of a foreign arbitral award dated 21 February 2005 made in Singapore in favour of ARK Shipping Co. Ltd., a company incorporated in Korea. The principal question was whether the award could be resisted under Section 48 of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996, on grounds relating to the validity of the arbitration agreement, constitution of the arbitral tribunal, inadequate opportunity of hearing and public policy of India. The Executing Court had rejected the judgment-debtor’s objections and held the foreign award enforceable as a decree under Section 49. Aggrieved by that decision, SS Exports approached the High Court seeking to invalidate the execution order and prevent enforcement of the award. The underlying commercial transaction arose from a Charter Party dated 12 May 2004, under which ARK Shipping Co. Ltd. provided a vessel to SS Exports for transporting goods. A dispute subsequently arose over unpaid freight and demurrage charges. The shipping company claimed US$275,479.90 and invoked the arbitration clause contained in the Charter Party. It nominated Mr. Jaya Prakash as arbitrator and requested SS Exports to nominate its arbitrator. When SS Exports failed to make an appointment despite communications and opportunities, Mr. Jaya Prakash proceeded as sole arbitrator. The judgment-debtor did not participate in the arbitration, and an ex parte award was passed on 21 February 2005 allowing the shipping company’s claim, together with simple interest at 4% from 7 August 2004 until the date of the award and specified costs. ARK Shipping thereafter instituted execution proceedings in Bengaluru, seeking recognition of the award as a foreign award under Section 44 and its enforcement through attachment and sale of property or other legally available execution measures. In the execution proceedings, SS Exports filed objections challenging the validity and enforceability of the foreign award. It also filed IA No. 5 under Section 48 seeking suspension of the award and IA No. 6 seeking condonation of delay in filing that application. The Executing Court dismissed both applications, overruled the objections and recognised the award as enforceable under Section 49. Before the High Court, the petitioner contended that no valid Charter Party had been executed between the parties and that the alleged agreement did not bear its signature. It further argued that the amendment introducing Clause 19(A) had been made without its knowledge or consent. According to the petitioner, the amended clause did not prescribe a valid mechanism for appointing an arbitrator, making the constitution of the arbitral tribunal defective. It also alleged that insufficient time had been granted to respond to the arbitration proceedings and that the arbitrator had accepted the shipping company’s claim merely because it remained uncontested. The petitioner relied particularly upon Sections 48(1)(b), 48(1)(d) and 48(2)(b) of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996. It submitted that enforcement could be refused where a party had not received proper notice of the appointment of an arbitrator, had been unable to present its case, or where the composition of the arbitral tribunal was inconsistent with the parties’ agreement. It also argued that the award was contrary to the public policy of India. In support of these submissions, the petitioner referred to Renusagar Power Co. Ltd. v. General Electric Co., BGS SGS Soma JV v. NHPC Ltd. and Shagang South-Asia (Hong Kong) Trading Co. Ltd. v. Daewoo Logistics. The petitioner maintained that its failure to challenge the award in the country of arbitration did not automatically prevent it from raising statutory objections when enforcement was sought in India. Accordingly, it requested the High Court to exercise its supervisory jurisdiction and set aside the Executing Court’s decision. ARK Shipping opposed the petition, contending that SS Exports had been informed of the arbitration proceedings at every material stage and had deliberately failed to participate. It submitted that the communications exchanged between the parties expressly referred to the Charter Party and its arbitration clause. The petitioner had sought additional time to consult its lawyers and nominate an arbitrator but had not denied the contractual arrangement or challenged the arbitration clause at the relevant time. The decree-holder also emphasised the limited scope of the public-policy exception applicable to foreign awards. Relying upon Shri Lal Mahal Ltd. v. Progetto Grano SpA, Cruz City 1 Mauritius Holdings v. Unitech Ltd. and Avitel Post Studioz Ltd. v. HSBC PI Holdings (Mauritius) Ltd., it argued that Section 48 did not permit an Indian enforcing court to conduct a fresh adjudication of the commercial dispute or reassess the merits of the foreign award. The High Court first examined whether a valid arbitration agreement existed. It considered Section 7 of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, which recognises written arbitration agreements not only through documents signed by the parties but also through exchanges of communications evidencing their agreement. The Court noted that the original Charter Party dated 12 May 2004 had been produced before the Executing Court. More importantly, several communications exchanged between the parties expressly referred to that Charter Party and the arbitration arrangement. The Court found that SS Exports had not disputed the transportation of its consignment on the vessel provided by ARK Shipping. The correspondence also demonstrated that the petitioner was aware of the arbitration proceedings and had requested time to make arrangements through its lawyers. These circumstances established the existence and execution of the Charter Party. The Court therefore rejected the contention that no binding arbitration agreement existed between the parties. The Court next considered the objection concerning the appointment of the sole arbitrator. Clause 19 of the Charter Party originally provided for arbitration in London under English law and contained a mechanism for appointment of arbitrators by the parties. Rider Clause 38 amended the arrangement to provide for arbitration in Singapore while retaining the application of English law. The petitioner argued that the amended Clause 19(A) did not independently specify a procedure for appointment of arbitrators. The High Court rejected this interpretation, holding that Clause 19(A) had to be read together with the original Clause 19. The amendment changed the seat of arbitration but did not eliminate the existing appointment mechanism. Since the petitioner had been notified of the proposed appointment and had failed to nominate its arbitrator within the permitted period, the subsequent appointment of Mr. Jaya Prakash as sole arbitrator could not be invalidated on the grounds advanced. The Court also distinguished the English decision in Shagang South-Asia Trading, observing that the circumstances of that case were materially different. A substantial part of the judgment addressed the interpretation of the public-policy exception under Section 48. The High Court traced the development of Indian arbitration jurisprudence through Renusagar Power Co. Ltd., ONGC Ltd. v. Saw Pipes Ltd., Phulchand Exports Ltd. v. O.O.O. Patriot and Shri Lal Mahal Ltd. v. Progetto Grano SpA. It explained that although the expression “public policy” had received a broader interpretation in the context of setting aside certain arbitral awards under Section 34, the enforcement of foreign awards under Section 48 was governed by a narrower and more restrictive standard. The Court noted that Shri Lal Mahal had overruled the approach adopted in Phulchand Exports insofar as it extended the broader patent-illegality standard to foreign-award enforcement. The statutory amendments of 2015 further clarified that examining whether an award contravenes the fundamental policy of Indian law must not involve a review of the merits of the dispute. Accordingly, a mere error of law, inadequate reasoning or disagreement with the arbitrator’s assessment would not ordinarily justify refusal of enforcement. The High Court also examined Cruz City 1 Mauritius Holdings v. Unitech Ltd., Vijay Karia v. Prysmian Cavi E Sistemi SRL and the Supreme Court’s observations concerning the pro-enforcement approach underlying the New York Convention. It recognised that a party could raise objections falling within Section 48 even if those objections had not been pursued before the arbitral tribunal or the supervisory court. However, the party resisting enforcement was required to establish the relevant statutory grounds through evidence. The enforcing court also retained discretion in considering whether enforcement should actually be refused. Applying these principles, the High Court found that SS Exports had received sufficient notice of the arbitration proceedings and had been given an opportunity to present its case. Its failure to participate could not, by itself, establish a violation of natural justice. The Court further found no circumstances showing that enforcement would offend the fundamental policy of Indian law or the most basic notions of morality and justice. Finally, the Karnataka High Court held that the objections raised by SS Exports under Sections 48(1)(b) and 48(2)(b) were untenable and had rightly been rejected by the Executing Court. It observed that poor reasoning or assessment in an arbitral award was not sufficient to refuse its enforcement. Regarding IA No. 6, the Court noted that Section 48 did not prescribe a specific limitation period for raising objections to recognition and enforcement of a foreign award. Consequently, the application seeking condonation of delay under Section 5 of the Limitation Act, 1963, was unnecessary. IA No. 5 was likewise superfluous because the judgment-debtor had separately filed substantive objections to enforcement. Finding that the Executing Court had passed a reasoned order and that no supervisory interference was warranted, the High Court dismissed the writ petition and upheld the enforceability of the Singapore arbitral award dated 21 February 2005. Pending interlocutory applications were also disposed of. The ruling reinforces the limited grounds available for resisting foreign arbitral awards in India and the importance of demonstrating actual statutory defects rather than relying upon general allegations concerning procedural irregularities or public policy.

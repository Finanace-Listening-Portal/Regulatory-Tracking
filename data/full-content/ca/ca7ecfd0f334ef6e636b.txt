Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); Sukhbir Singh Vs Verbio India Private Limited (NCLAT Delhi) Summary: The National Company Law Appellate Tribunal, Principal Bench, New Delhi, comprising Justice N. Seshasayee (Member Judicial), Arun Baroka (Member Technical) and Indevar Pandey (Member Technical), dismissed the appeal filed by Sukhbir Singh, suspended director and shareholder of Farm2energy Private Limited, challenging the order dated 9 September 2024 passed by the NCLT Chandigarh Bench admitting an application under Section 9 of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016. The proceedings were initiated by Verbio India Private Limited, the operational creditor, seeking recovery of an outstanding advance of ₹1,19,53,864 arising out of an agreement for the supply of paddy biomass bales. The Appellate Tribunal, by judgment dated 6 July 2026, held that the existence of operational debt and default had been established and that the appellant had failed to demonstrate any genuine pre-existing dispute warranting rejection of the insolvency application. The dispute originated from an agreement dated 19 July 2022 under which Farm2energy Private Limited agreed to supply 20,000 tonnes of biomass bales to Verbio India Private Limited for a total consideration of ₹4.40 crore. The operational creditor paid two advances of ₹77 lakh each, aggregating ₹1.54 crore, representing 35% of the contractual consideration. The supply period was from 15 September 2022 to 15 December 2022. According to the operational creditor, goods worth only ₹34,46,136 were supplied, leaving ₹1,19,53,864 payable by the corporate debtor. After issuing a legal notice and serving a statutory demand notice under Section 8 of the IBC on 19 January 2023, the operational creditor initiated proceedings under Section 9 when the amount remained unpaid and no notice of dispute was received within the statutory period. The appellant challenged the admission of the insolvency petition on several grounds. It contended that the amount arose from a contractual supply arrangement and did not constitute operational debt, that the alleged claim was disputed, and that the operational creditor had failed to establish proper service of the statutory demand notice. It further alleged denial of natural justice because the NCLT had proceeded ex parte without granting sufficient opportunity. The appellant relied upon decisions including Macquarie Bank Limited Vs Shilpi Cable Technologies Limited, Transmission Corporation of Andhra Pradesh Limited Vs Equipment Conductors and Cables Limited, Mobilox Innovations Private Limited Vs Kirusa Software Private Limited and Innoventive Industries Limited Vs ICICI Bank. It also argued that initiation of CIRP would undermine the highly seasonal agricultural operations of the corporate debtor and adversely affect value maximisation. The operational creditor disputed these contentions and maintained that the corporate debtor had received the agreed advances but failed to supply the corresponding quantity of biomass. It produced evidence of service of the demand notice dated 17 January 2023 and contended that no genuine dispute had been raised within the prescribed ten-day period. The Resolution Professional informed the Tribunal that the CIRP remained under judicial supervision, that Verbio India Private Limited was the sole member of the Committee of Creditors with 100% voting rights, and that progress had been affected by non-cooperation from the suspended management and statutory auditors. The Resolution Professional had also initiated proceedings under Section 19(2) of the Code for necessary cooperation. The Appellate Tribunal first rejected the appellant’s objection regarding the non-service of the demand notice. It found that the notice under Section 8 dated 17 January 2023 had been delivered on 19 January 2023 and that proof of service, in accordance with Rule 5 of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy (Application to Adjudicating Authority) Rules, 2016, formed part of the NCLT record. The Tribunal also rejected the allegation of denial of natural justice. It observed that the corporate debtor had filed its reply and written submissions but failed to participate at the hearing despite sufficient opportunities. Accordingly, the ex parte proceedings could not be treated as an unlawful denial of opportunity merely because the corporate debtor had not availed itself of the opportunities provided. On the alleged pre-existing dispute, the Tribunal examined email correspondence produced by the appellant at the appellate stage, particularly an email dated 27 December 2022 from Nitin Jain of Farm2energy Private Limited. The communication expressly referred to arranging funds to refund the differential amount after adjusting the material supplied and to clearing Verbio India’s account by 31 March 2023. The Tribunal found that the correspondence acknowledged the outstanding liability rather than establishing a dispute concerning the debt. It further held that the appellant had failed to produce bills substantiating its assertion that goods worth ₹2.20 crore had been supplied. Consequently, the reliance placed on the principles governing genuine pre-existing disputes in Mobilox Innovations was found to be unavailing. The Tribunal also rejected the contention that the unpaid advance did not constitute operational debt. It found that the amount had been advanced for the supply of goods and that the corporate debtor’s failure to supply the full contracted quantity or refund the outstanding consideration supported the operational creditor’s claim. This approach is consistent with the Supreme Court’s recognition in Consolidated Construction Consortium Limited Vs Hitro Energy Solutions Private Limited that advances connected with supply of goods or services can give rise to operational debt. The Tribunal found no merit in the arguments relating to seasonal operations, prospective loss of business value or the absence of claims by banks and other financial institutions, since the relevant requirements for Section 9 admission were otherwise satisfied. Accordingly, the NCLAT held that the appellant had not established any pre-existing dispute and that the debt and default were clearly demonstrated. The appeal and all pending interlocutory applications were dismissed. The Tribunal further vacated its interim direction dated 6 February 2025 restraining consideration of any resolution plan without its permission. It directed the Resolution Professional to continue the CIRP and required the appellant to cooperate in the conduct of the proceedings. The judgment reinforces that an unpaid advance for contracted supply of goods may constitute operational debt and that unsupported allegations of contractual disagreement cannot defeat a properly established insolvency application. In such a situation, the arguments canvassed by the Appellant are not sustainable, and accordingly, such an argument is rejected. 54. The Appellant has also canvassed another argument that the alleged debt owed by the Corporate Debtor was pursuant to a supply agreement and not in the nature of any operational debt in default, and hence no Section 9 application could have been allowed. The Appellant also claims that there was a contract between the Appellant and the Respondent No.1. Since there was a breach of the contract, the Respondent was not having any right for the payment. At best it can seek damages for specific performance. We find such an argument to be unsustainable for the reason that an advance was provided to the Corporate Debtor for supply of goods, but it did not supply the full quantity of goods, for which the advance was provided. When the money was asked to be returned, it could not make payment, and therefore the Section 9 proceedings were initiated. The Operational Creditor filed a claim against the Corporate Debtor, and therefore Section 9 could very well be initiated. We do not find any infirmity in the order of the Adjudicating Authority in accepting such a petition. 55. Another argument presented by the Appellant that the initiation or continuation of the Corporate Insolvency Resolution Process (CIRP) against the Corporate Debtor would not lead to any maximization of value of the Corporate Debtor, since the operations of the Corporate Debtor pertain to collection of farm produce across the states of Punjab and Haryana, the operations are highly interpersonal to the Appellant, and cannot possibly be performed by any other person. Hence, the purpose of proceeding under the provisions of the Code is defeated. We find such an argument to be completely baseless. The Code prescribes the existence of debt and default, and if the above is established and there is no pre-existing dispute, then the Adjudicating Authority doesn’t have to go beyond that and therefore such an argument is totally untenable. 56. Another argument of the Appellant that the banks/financial institutions have not filed any claim in the CIR proceedings against the Corporate Debtor doesn’t provide any assistance to the Appellant for not taking up CIR proceedings. 57. In the facts and circumstances of the case, the judgments cited by the Appellant in the matters of Macquarie Bank Limited (supra), Transmission Corporation of Andhra Pradesh Limited (supra) and Mobilox Innovations Private Limited (supra) do not provide any assistance to the Appellant. 58. We have gone through the contentions of both sides. We find that the debt and default are clearly established there is no pre-existing dispute. Conclusions 59. The Appellant has failed to establish a pre-existing dispute basis these emails. Otherwise, debt and default are accepted and therefore we do not find any infirmity in the order of the Adjudicating Authority. Order 60. We dismiss the Appeal. All IAs are also disposed of. It is brought to our notice that the Appellant is not cooperating in the CIR Proceeding. In this appeal vide order dated 06.02.2025, we had directed that “no resolution plan shall be considered without the leave of this Tribunal”. This interim order automatically stands vacated. We further make it clear that our interim direction was a very limited one and, in any case, with the final order the Appellant is duty bound to provide assistance to the RP. RP should continue with the CIR Proceeding. The Appellant should provide assistance to the RP in the conduct of the CIRP.

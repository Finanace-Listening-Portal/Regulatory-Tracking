Articles 5 min read GFF 2026: 'Deregulation is the next big reform': Niti Aayog's Rajiv Gauba calls for sweeping overhaul of India’s regulatory maze India’s next generation of reforms must be driven by deregulation, with the government needing to dismantle the maze of licences, permissions and compliance requirements that continue to constrain businesses, NITI Aayog member Rajiv Gauba said at Global FinTech Fest 2026. Gauba also called for fintech regulation to remain principle-based, technology-neutral and proportionate to risk, while urging the sector to take digital finance beyond payments into credit, insurance and customised financial services. Advt ‘Next generation reform has to be defined by deregulation’Gauba said the next phase of India's reform agenda should focus less on headline structural changes and more on removing the regulatory friction businesses face in their day-to-day operations.“In one word, deregulation. The next generation reform architecture has to be defined by deregulation,” Gauba said.He said while major reforms such as GST, the insolvency framework and liberalisation of the FDI regime have already changed the economic landscape, a large number of permissions, registrations and renewal requirements continue to exist across central, state and local regulations.“The next generation reform has to break industry and the city government at large from this tightening web of rules and regulations,” he said.Gauba said the exercise would involve scrutinising the “maze of laws, regulations and rules” against the government's principle of trust-based governance.Under the framework being worked on, licences would be required primarily for national security or activities involving serious risks to human health or the environment. Where licences remain necessary, he said they should have perpetual or long-term validity and inspections should be significantly reduced. Advt State-level deregulation key to unlocking investmentGauba said a large part of the regulatory burden faced by businesses lies at the state and municipal level, covering areas such as land use, building permits, pollution consents and various sector-specific licences.He said the Centre is working with states to replicate deregulation measures and use incentives, including central funding, to encourage implementation.A key example is the proposed overhaul of building regulations. Gauba said changes to the national building framework could allow states and municipalities to liberalise requirements around site coverage, ground coverage and parking. Advt Such reforms, he said, could unlock significantly more built-up area and bring down the cost of developing hotels and other infrastructure.He also pointed to reforms in tourism and hospitality, where businesses continue to face multiple state-level permits and licences.“The role of central, state and local authorities is distinct and defined, but they have to work together,” Gauba said, adding that the Centre's role is to create model reforms, share templates and best practices and link incentives to implementation.The objective, he said, is to make deregulation a mechanism for attracting investment, accelerating growth and creating jobs. Advt Fintech regulation must not choke innovationGauba said the same principle needs to apply to fintech, an industry that has expanded rapidly on the back of India's digital public infrastructure.India now has more than 10,000 fintech firms and has attracted nearly $40 billion in investment over the last decade, he said, while highlighting the sector's role in expanding financial inclusion and transforming payments.“Much more important than the numbers is the fact that our fintech ecosystem has truly democratised access and financial inclusion. Payments have been revolutionised,” Gauba said.However, he said fintech's next phase needs to extend beyond payments, with greater access to credit, insurance, investment and other financial services.“I think there is a lot more to do. Access to credit can certainly be made more democratic and universal to the small shopkeeper and MSMEs. Insurance needs much greater penetration,” he said.Gauba also flagged the potential for AI to strengthen financial systems against fraud and make decisions by lenders, shopkeepers and individuals more nuanced and customised.‘Over-regulation will push innovation off’Gauba said India needs to avoid both excessive regulation and regulatory gaps as technology and fintech evolve rapidly.“Over-regulation will push innovation off. On the other hand, under-regulation can produce a crisis and a loss of control,” he said, arguing that the balance between innovation and consumer protection needs to remain dynamic rather than static.He called for fintech regulation to be principle-based, technology-neutral and proportionate to the level of risk involved.“Regulation has to be principle-based, technology-neutral, and scale the risk rather than being a private company,” Gauba said.He also argued that regulation should promote competition by allowing new entrants to enter and exit markets without unnecessary friction, while ensuring non-discriminatory access to underlying physical, digital and financial infrastructure.Greater coordination between regulators and reducing duplicate or conflicting KYC requirements would also be important to improve predictability for businesses, he said.Government needs to build technology capacityWith AI and other technologies evolving at breakneck speed, Gauba said the government itself needs to continuously upgrade its technological capabilities.He identified three priorities — closer collaboration with the private sector, capacity building within government and creating a more open system for bringing professional expertise into government.Gauba said technology adoption within government has already accelerated substantially, pointing to digital identity, government procurement, passports, e-visas and direct benefit transfers as examples.He said the government also needs to institutionalise continuous upskilling of civil servants as technology and skills rapidly become outdated.“The scale and speed of technology adoption in India by the government is not fully appreciated,” Gauba said, adding that partnerships with the private sector could help bring state-of-the-art expertise into government.For India's fintech ecosystem, the broader message from Gauba was that the next phase of digital transformation will depend as much on regulatory reform and institutional capacity as on technology itself — with deregulation, competition and risk-based oversight becoming critical to sustaining innovation.Click here to read our coverage of the

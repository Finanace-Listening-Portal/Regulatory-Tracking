Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); DCIT Vs Saurabh Agrawal (ITAT Ahmedabad) Sharp Rise in Share Value and Schedule FA Omission Alone Cannot Justify Section 68 Addition Where Share Transactions Are Documented: ITAT Ahmedabad Summary: The Ahmedabad Bench of the Income Tax Appellate Tribunal upheld the deletion of a ₹30,72,83,192 addition under Section 68 after the assessee substantiated the acquisition, sale and buyback of shares in a Singapore company. The Tribunal also upheld the CIT(A)’s allowance of the Section 54F claim of ₹19,97,40,203, relying on evidence that the assessee had constructed and used a residential house within the prescribed three-year period. The Revenue’s appeal was dismissed. Facts of the Case The assessee received 10,000 shares of Awlencan Innovations Pte., Singapore, by gift in June 2018. During the previous year relevant to AY 2021–22, he sold 6,298 shares to an existing shareholder at USD 320.48 per share. The company subsequently bought back 1,345 shares from him at USD 1,646.15 per share. The assessee declared long-term capital gains of ₹30.73 crore and claimed exemption under Section 54F for investment in a residential house. The Assessing Officer (AO) questioned the unregistered gift deed, the sharp rise in share value, the absence of the buyer’s US tax returns and the omission of the shares from Schedule FA. The AO treated the disclosed gains as unexplained cash credits under Section 68. He also questioned the assessee’s eligibility under Section 54F and made no separate disallowance because he had already added the entire gain under Section 68. The CIT(A) deleted the addition and allowed the exemption. Section 68 Addition Could Not Stand Against Documentary Evidence The Tribunal found that the gift deed, share certificate and Singapore corporate records supported the assessee’s ownership. The share purchase agreement, buyback resolution and bank inward credit advices established how the shares were transferred and the consideration received. The buyer already held a 30% stake in the company, and the CIT(A) had examined his profile and business interests in assessing his creditworthiness. An independent Singapore valuation supported the sale price, while transfer pricing documentation supported the buyback. The assessee explained the appreciation by reference to the company’s cryptocurrency business and the increase in the value of its underlying assets. The Tribunal observed that the AO had to examine whether Rule 11UA applied before invoking it to reject the valuation. The Revenue had produced no independent valuation or other material contradicting the assessee’s evidence. The Tribunal held that the test of human probabilities could not displace the documentary evidence on record without material to rebut it. The Revenue had also produced no tangible evidence that the sale and buyback proceeds represented the assessee’s own unaccounted funds routed back to him. Schedule FA Omission Did Not Establish a Sham Transaction The assessee had disclosed the share transactions in his return for the year under appeal and reported the buyback in Form 3CEB. The omission from Schedule FA could have consequences under the Black Money Act, and the Tribunal expressly left the Revenue free to proceed under that law. The Schedule FA omission alone did not establish that the share sale and buyback were sham transactions. Section 54F Relief Upheld on Evidence of Residential Construction The assessee relied on adjoining land purchases, related acquisition expenses and a ₹25 lakh deposit under the Capital Gains Account Scheme. The CIT(A) accepted that the investment related to one residential house. Before the Tribunal, the record included a bungalow plan, a photograph, a completion certificate dated 29 August 2023 and electricity bills showing substantial consumption from July 2023. The bills identified the property as Survey No. 11, one of the plots purchased by the assessee. The Revenue produced no site verification or other evidence contradicting the finding that the house had been completed and used within three years. Referring to the Karnataka High Court’s decision in CIT v. Sambandam Udaykumar, the Tribunal examined whether the assessee had actually invested in a residential house. It also discussed the separate ₹9 crore advance under a land agreement that was later cancelled. The assessee stated that he had offered the corresponding gains to tax in the subsequent year. The Tribunal observed that the effect of the cancellation and the subsequent tax treatment required examination under the applicable provisions. Its final finding upheld the CIT(A)’s allowance on the basis of qualifying investment and documented construction. Rule 46A Objection Rejected After AO Failed to Submit Remand Report The CIT(A) called for a remand report on the additional evidence and sent several reminders over more than two months. The AO neither submitted a report nor sought further time. Relying on the Gujarat High Court’s decision in Kamalaben Sureshchandra Bhatti, the Tribunal held that the AO had been given an opportunity to examine the evidence and dismissed the Revenue’s Rule 46A objections. Cases Discussed: CIT Vs Durga Prasad More (Supreme Court); (1971) 82 ITR 540. The Revenue relied on the principle that tax authorities may examine surrounding circumstances and apply the test of human probabilities. The Tribunal held that this principle could not substitute for evidence rebutting the assessee’s documentary record. Sumati Dayal Vs CIT (Supreme Court); (1995) 214 ITR 801. Cited by the Revenue in support of the human-probabilities test. The Tribunal found no tangible adverse material establishing that the disclosed foreign share transactions represented the assessee’s unaccounted money. CIT Vs Sambandam Udaykumar (Karnataka High Court); IT Appeal No. 175 of 2011; 15/02/2012. Read Judgment. The Tribunal considered the beneficial purpose of Section 54F and the importance of actual investment towards acquisition or construction of a residential house. Kamalaben Sureshchandra Bhatti (Gujarat High Court); (2014) 44 taxmann.com 459. The Tribunal relied on this decision to reject the Rule 46A objection where the CIT(A) had sought a remand report and afforded the AO an opportunity to examine additional evidence. We have carefully considered the rival submissions and perused the material available on record. Before we discuss the grounds taken by the Revenue on the merits of the case, we deem it proper to adjudicate the ground taken by the revenue on admission of additional evidences. The Revenue has contended that the Ld. CIT(A) had erred in admitting additional evidences submitted by the assessee without recording reason, which was in violation to Rule 46A(2) of the Income-tax Rules. Further that no sufficient opportunity was given to the AO to examine the additional evidences and submit a remand report under Rule 46A(3) of the Income Tax Rules. It is noted from the order of the Ld. CIT(A) that he had called for a remand report from the AO on the additional evidences filed by the assessee. However, no remand report was sent by the AO in spite of several reminders over a period of more than two months. Thus, the Ld. CIT(A) had allowed an opportunity to the AO to examine the evidences brought on record before him. Having not availed the opportunity to send the remand report, the Revenue now cannot take a plea that the AO was not allowed an opportunity to examine the fresh evidences and submit a remand report under Rule 46A(3) of the Income Tax Rules. No evidence has been brought on record by the Revenue that the AO had sought further time to examine the complex issues and submit his remand report, which was declined by the Ld. CIT(A). Hon’ble Gujarat High Court has held in the case of Kamalaben Sureshchandra Bhatti (44 taxman.com 459) (Gujarat) that where the CIT(A) had called for the remand report and permitted the AO to comment on the additional evidences, no error was committed by the CIT(A) and the admission of additional evidence was not a breach of requirement of Rule 46A of the Rules. 8. In the present case neither the AO had objected to admission of the additional evidence nor submitted any remand report on the fresh evidences filed by the assessee. It was the prerogative of the Ld. CIT(A) to admit the additional evidences. In the absence of any remand report, the Ld. CIT(A) had admitted the additional evidences, examined the same, drawn inferences and passed a speaking order. Once the Ld. CIT(A) had called for the remand report of the AO on the fresh evidences, it was imperative that he had admitted those evidences and provision of Rule 46A(2) is deemed complied. Otherwise also, as per provision of Rule 46A(4) of IT Rules, the Ld. CIT(A) had inherent power to direct the production of any document, or the examination of any witness, to enable him to dispose of the appeal, or for any other substantial cause. Regarding the evidentiary value of the fresh evidences filed before the Ld. CIT(A), we will consider this aspect while adjudicating the specific grounds taken by the Revenue. So far as the grounds raised by the Revenue in respect of admissibility of additional evidences and opportunity to the AO to examine them, the same are found to be devoid of merit and are, therefore, dismissed. Addition u/s 68 of the Act: 9. The first two grounds taken by the Revenue pertain to the addition of Rs. 30,72,83,192/- made by the AO u/s 68 of the Act. The AO had treated the LTCG disclosed by the assessee as bogus cash credit. The AO’s case rests upon the circumstances that the assessee had received the shares by way of gift deed, that the initial value of the shares was substantially lower than the subsequent sale/buyback value, that the valuation report was not under Rule 11UA of the Act, that the foreign purchaser’s income-tax returns were not furnished, that the financial statements of Awlencan Innovations Pte. Singapore were not audited/certified, and that the shares were not reflected in Schedule FA of the ITRs filed by the assessee. These circumstances may cast doubt about the transaction, but do not by themselves establish that the capital gain disclosed by the assessee represented unexplained cash credit u/s 68 of the Act. The assessee had furnished documentary evidence regarding acquisition of shares, subsequent transfer and buyback, identity of the purchaser, valuation of the shares, and the receipt of consideration. It is found that the Ld. CIT(A) has examined each of these aspects in quite detail. He has given a categorical finding that the shares received under the gift deed was transferred in the assessee’s name in the corporate records of Singapore. Further, the acquisition and subsequent transfer of shares were supported by documentary evidence and the transactions were duly disclosed by the assessee. 10. The assessee has brought on record a copy of gift deed in the paper book. Pursuant to the gift deed, 10,000 ordinary shares of Awlencan Innovations Pte. Singapore were duly registered in the name of the assessee as on 29.06.2018 and a copy of the share certificate in the name of the assessee has been filed. A copy of the Share Purchase Agreement between Shri Saurabh Agarwal, Shri RahulDev Ravi Pagidipati and Awlencan Innovations Pte. Singapore dated 31.08.2020 has also been brought on record, as per which 6,298 shares were sold by the assessee to Shri RahulDev Ravi Pagidipati at the rate of USD 320.48 per share for a total consideration of USD 20,18,377. This transaction was done on the basis of the valuation report of YTK, a copy of which has been filed. It is evident from the report and the financial statement that AIPS was operating a cryptocurrency trading platform with revenue streams coming from cryptocurrency sales and membership fees. The company had investments in seven subsidiaries across four countries and the basis of valuation of shares was well documented in the valuation report. In fact, Shri RahulDev Ravi Pagidipati was an existing shareholder of AIPS having 30% shareholding in the company and, therefore, his identity was not in doubt. The assessee has also brought on record a copy of inward credit advice for receipt of USD 4,07,945 on 02.10.2020 and USD 16,10,392 on 15.12.2020 from Shri RahulDev Ravi Pagidipati. A copy of the resolution for buy-back of shares by AIPS has also been brought on record, as per which the company had decided to buy back a total of 4,035 ordinary shares from Shri Saurabh Agarwal, Shri Sandeep Ashok Goenka and Shri MahendraKumar Rambabu Gupta. The total consideration paid was USD 2214071.75, to each of the three shareholders for 1345 shares held by them at the rate USD 1646.15 per share. The evidence for receipt of USD 22,14,042 by the assessee from AIPS was also brought on record in the form of inward credit advice of IndusInd Bank. 11. The objection of the AO regarding non-registration of the gift deed does not establish that the transaction was fictitious. The assessee had submitted that the gift deed was not required to be registered as per Singapore law. The assessee has brought on record evidence to the effect that the shares were appearing in his name as per the ACRA profile (Government of Singapore official website for corporate affairs) under the Singapore law. The gift deed was executed in Singapore, which was duly recognised by the Singapore authorities, and the shares were transferred in the name of the assessee. If the AO had any doubt about the genuineness of the gift deed and the pursuant sale of shares of AIPS, a reference should have been made to the Singapore authorities through the FT&TR Division of the CBDT to enquire into the matter. Having not made any such reference, the AO was not correct in rejecting the evidences brought on record by the assessee on mere suspicion. The AO’s rejection of the evidences filed by the assessee is found to be based on mere surmises and conjectures. The shares were reflected in the assessee’s name in the relevant corporate records of Singapore and no contrary material has been brought on record by the Revenue to establish that the assessee did not acquire those shares or that the entries in the foreign company’s records were fabricated. 12. The AO has also placed much reliance on non-reporting of the foreign shares in Schedule FA of the ITRs filed by the assessee. Such non-reporting of foreign assets in the ITR may have consequences under the applicable Black Money Act, but such omission cannot automatically lead to the conclusion that the subsequent sale and buyback transactions were sham transactions. The fact that the transactions were disclosed in the ITR for the year under consideration and the fact that the buyback was reported in Form 3CEB has not been controverted by the Revenue. The Revenue has contended that reporting a transaction in Form 3CEB or Balance Sheet disclosures under domestic heads, does not cure the statutory defect of non-reporting foreign assets under Schedule FA. The Revenue is at liberty to proceed against the assessee under the provisions of Black Money Act for non-reporting the foreign assets under Schedule FA of ITRs. Merely because the assessee had not declared the assets in the Foreign Assets Schedule of the IT Returns of earlier years doesn’t negate the fact that the assessee had acquired the shares of AIPS through gift deed and had subsequently sold them, resulting in LTCG during the year. The assessee has brought on record evidences to substantiate the acquisition of shares and subsequent sale of them, and the Revenue has not brought on record any material to controvert these evidences. Neither has the Revenue pointed out any FEMA violation by the assessee. Further no TP reference was made to determine the correct arm’s length price of the share transactions, if the AO had any doubt about the valuation of shares. Thus, the assessee’s transaction of sale and buyback of shares was accepted by the Revenue in principle. 13. The AO had referred to the abnormal increase in the value of the shares of Awlencan Innovations Pte. Singapore. As already discussed earlier, there was substantial difference between the values attributed to the shares at different points of time. The assessee had explained the reason for the same, which was not controverted by the Revenue. Further, a substantial increase in value, by itself, cannot be treated as conclusive evidence that the transaction was fictitious. The assessee’s explanation that Awlencan Innovations Pte. Singapore was connected with the cryptocurrency business, the value of which had increased substantially during the relevant period, was not controverted by the Revenue. The sale of shares on 31.08.2020 was supported by an independent valuation report prepared by a Singapore-based valuer. The subsequent buyback on 02.01.2021 was also supported by transfer pricing documentation, which was not doubted by the AO, as he didn’t make any TP reference. If the Revenue had any doubt in respect of valuation of the shares, the matter should have been referred to an independent valuer. In the absence of any material brought on record by the Revenue to demonstrate that the valuation adopted by the independent valuer was erroneous or that the transaction was a sham transaction; we find no basis to doubt the valuation adopted by the assessee. Rule 11UA of the Income-tax Rules cannot be mechanically applied to reject a valuation report, without first examining whether the particular provision is applicable to the transaction under consideration and whether the valuation adopted by the assessee is false. The valuation adopted by the Singapore-based valuer was based on the financial and business characteristics of the company. The Revenue has not brought on record any independent valuation demonstrating that the shares could not have been worth the consideration at which they were transferred. Merely because the value of the shares increased substantially within a short period cannot be considered as conclusive evidence of a sham transaction. The assessee had explained the appreciation in the value of the shares by reference to the underlying cryptocurrency business and increase in the value of the assets. The principle of valuation is a technical exercise and cannot be reduced to a mere mathematical decision. The AO’s objection to the valuation does not establish any fundamental error in the valuation which would render the underlying transaction as non-genuine. 14. The AO has also questioned the creditworthiness of Shri RahulDev Ravi Pagidipati on the ground that the assessee had not furnished his US tax returns and income proof. The assessee had brought on record evidence to establish the identity of Shri RahulDev Ravi Pagidipati, who was a high-net-worth individual and a venture capitalist. The Ld. CIT(A) has considered the profile of Shri RahulDev Ravi Pagidipati, Chairman of ZebPay-India’s biggest Bitcoin exchange. The Ld. CIT(A) also discussed in detail the nature of business interests of Shri RahulDev Ravi Pagidipati and recorded a finding that he was an investor in various unicorn entities having substantial net worth. The AO has not brought on record any material demonstrating that the purchaser was a fictitious person or that the consideration received by the assessee was not actually received from him pursuant to the stated sale transactions. 15. The AO has also placed reliance on the principle of human probabilities. It is true that the surrounding circumstances of a transaction can be examined to determine its real character. However, the test of human probabilities cannot substitute evidence, where the assessee has produced documentary evidence establishing the existence and movement of shares and the receipt of sale consideration. In the present case, the AO has not brought on record any material to dislodge the evidences filed by the assessee. Under the circumstances, the reliance on the principle of human probabilities by the AO was otiose. 16. In the present case, the Revenue has failed to bring on record any direct or tangible material to establish that the consideration received by the assessee was his own unaccounted money routed back through the share transactions. The AO’s conclusion is based merely on an inference drawn from the unusual appreciation in the value of the shares, which was duly explained by the assessee. Once the assessee furnished evidences regarding acquisition and transfer of shares, valuation thereof, receipt of consideration, and disclosure of capital gain in the ITR, the Revenue was required to bring some cogent adverse material on record to establish that the apparent transactions as claimed by the assessee, were not real. In the absence of any such material, we are in agreement with the findings of the Ld. CIT(A) that the assessee had discharged the burden cast upon him by furnishing documentary evidences and the AO has not brought any tangible material to dislodge the explanation and the evidence furnished by the assessee. We find that the Ld. CIT(A) has examined each of the objection raised by the AO and recorded his findings based on the documents and explanations available on record. We do not find any perversity in those findings warranting our interference. Accordingly, we uphold the order of the Ld. CIT(A) deleting the addition of Rs.30,72,83,192/- made u/s 68 of the Act and the ground Nos. 1 and 2 taken by the Revenue are dismissed. Deduction u/s. 54F of the Act 17. The next issue for consideration relates to the assessee’s claim of deduction of Rs.19,97,40,203/- under section 54F of the Act in respect of investment made towards acquisition of land and construction of a new residential house. The AO, having treated the entire capital gain as unexplained credit under section 68 of the Act, did not separately examine or make any disallowance of the claim u/s 54F of the Act. However, the AO had recorded certain adverse observations regarding the eligibility of the assessee’s claim. According to the AO, the assessee had not furnished documentary evidence such as the approved building plan, details of payments made to contractor towards construction, completion report and evidence regarding utilisation of the property within the prescribed period. The AO had also observed that the lands purchased by the assessee were not contiguous and, therefore, a single residential house could not have been constructed thereon. 18. The assessee had submitted that he had purchased various adjoining parcels of land for construction of one residential house; incurred stamp duty, brokerage and other expenses of Rs.64,84,640/-; deposited Rs. 25,00,000/- in the Capital Gains Account Scheme; and subsequently completed the construction of the residential house. The details of investment made towards purchase of land, aggregating to Rs.19,07,55,563/-, are as under: Survey number of land purchased Cost Date 11 (Old survey No. 604) 4,54,54,545/- 22/02/2022 76 (old survey No. 616) 1,78,95,086/- 22/02/2022 77 (Old survey No. 613) 9,00,00,000/- 07/03/2022 79 (Old survey No. 605) 1,18,85,790/- 22/02/2022 80 (Old survey No. 602) 2,55,20,142/- 22/02/2022 Total 19,07,55,563/- 19. On examination of the sale deed dated 22.02.2022 relating to purchase of land bearing Survey No. 11 (Old Survey No. 604), it is noticed that the assessee had made payments of Rs.3,00,00,000/- on 24.08.2021, Rs.1,00,00,000/- on 22.10.2021 and Rs.50,00,000/- on 02.11.2021 through banking channels, while the balance amount of Rs.4,54,545/- represented TDS deducted. Similarly, in respect of land bearing Survey No. 76 (Old Survey No. 616), the assessee had made payments of Rs.50,00,000/- each on 01.10.2021, 04.10.2021 and 05.10.2021 and Rs.27,16,135/- on 07.02.2022, while the balance amount of Rs.1,78,951/- represented TDS. In respect of Survey No. 79 (Old Survey No. 605), payments of Rs.1,00,00,000/- on 01.10.2021 and Rs.17,55,832/- on 07.02.2022 were made, while the balance amount represented TDS. Likewise, for Survey No. 80 (Old Survey No. 602), payments of Rs.2,00,00,000/- on 23.08.2021, Rs.50,00,000/- on 28.08.2021 and Rs.2,54,840/- on 09.02.2022 were made, while the balance amount represented TDS. Apart from the aforesaid investments, the assessee had claimed ancillary expenditure of Rs.64,84,640/- towards stamp duty, brokerage, franking and other legal expenses connected with the purchase of land. The assessee had also deposited Rs.25,00,000/- in the Capital Gains Account Scheme. 20. The AO had objected to the assessee’s claim on the ground that most of the payments towards acquisition of land had been made after the due date of filing the return of income. According to the AO, in terms of section 54F of the Act, the amount of capital gain which had not been appropriated towards purchase of a new residential house or utilised for construction thereof before the due date of filing the return, was required to be deposited in the Capital Gains Account Scheme. Since the assessee had not deposited the entire unutilised amount in the said scheme, the AO considered the assessee ineligible for deduction under section 54F of the Act. On the other hand, the Ld. Senior Counsel submitted that merely because the payments towards acquisition or construction of the new residential property were made beyond the due date of filing the return, the assessee could not be denied the benefit of section 54F of the Act. In this regard he has relied upon certain judicial decisions. In the case of Sambandam Udaykumar (19 taxman.com 17) (Kar.), the Hon’ble Karnataka High Court has considered the issue of delay in investment in the new property and had observed as under: “11. Section 45 of the Act makes it very clear that any profits or gains arising from the transfer of a capital asset effected in the previous year shall, save or otherwise provided in sections 54, 54B, 54D, 54E, 54EA, 54EB, 54F, 54G and 54H is chargeable to income tax under the head ‘capital gains’ and shall be deemed to be income of the previous year in which the transfer took place. The aforesaid sections which form part of section 54 of the Act are cases where capital gain on transfer of capital asset not to be charged in those cases. Section 54F of the Act is a beneficial provision of promoting the construction of residential house. Therefore, the said provision has to be construed liberally for achieving the purpose for which it was incorporated in the statute. The intention of the Legislature was to encourage investments in the acquisition of a residential house and completion of construction or occupation is not the requirement of law. The words used in the section are ‘purchased’ or ‘constructed’. For such purpose, the capital gain realized should have been invested in a residential house. The condition precedent for claiming benefit under the said prevision is the capital gain realized from sale of capital asset should have been parted by the assessee and invested either in purchasing a residential house or in constructing a residential house. If after making the entire payment, merely because a registered sale deed had not been executed and registered in favour of the assessee before the period stipulated, he cannot be denied the benefit of section 54F of the Act. Similarly, if he has invested the money in construction of a residential house, merely because the construction was not complete in all respects and it was not in a fit condition to be occupied within the period stipulated, that would not disentitle the assessee from claiming the benefit under section 54F of the Act. The essence of the said provision is whether the assessee who received capital gains has invested in a residential house. Once it is demonstrated that the consideration received on transfer has been invested either in purchasing a residential house or in construction of a residential house even though the transactions are not complete in all respects and as requited under the law, that would not disentitle the assessee from the said benefit.” 21. The aforesaid decision lays down that section 54F of the Act, being a beneficial provision intended to encourage investment in residential housing, requires consideration of the actual investment made by the assessee towards purchase or construction of a residential house. The essential requirement is that the capital gain realised should have been invested in the acquisition or construction of a residential house. Merely because the sale deed is not registered or the construction is not fully completed within the stipulated period, the benefit cannot automatically be denied, provided the requisite investment has been established. In the present case, the payments towards acquisition of certain parcels of land were made during the period of the COVID-19 pandemic. Having regard to the peculiar circumstances prevailing during that period and the fact that the payments were made through banking channels, the delay in making such payments cannot, by itself, be a conclusive ground for denying the benefit of section 54F of the Act. However, the aforesaid judicial proposition does not dispense with the requirement of establishing that the assessee had actually invested the capital gain towards acquisition or construction of a residential house. In the present case, the details furnished by the assessee primarily establish investment towards purchase of land. Whether any further amount was actually deployed towards construction of the residential house and whether the construction was completed are, therefore, material aspects requiring examination. The assessee has furnished deployment of funds towards purchase of land only, on which new property was proposed to be constructed. The crux issue to be considered here is whether any amount was deployed towards cost of construction of the new residential property and whether the same was constructed. We will examine this aspect after considering the other objections of the AO. 22. Another objection raised by the AO relates to the amount of Rs.9 crores paid towards purchase of land bearing Survey No. 77 (Old Survey No. 613). According to the AO, the said land was not ultimately purchased by the assessee and, therefore, the amount of Rs.9 crores could not be considered as eligible investment u/s 54F of the Act. It is, however, noticed that the assessee had made payment of Rs.9 crores on 07.03.2022 pursuant to an agreement dated 07.03.2022. The assessee explained that the said agreement was subsequently cancelled on account of certain title and documentation issues and that the capital gains attributable to the cancelled transaction were voluntarily offered to tax in the subsequent financial year, with the tax payment having been made on 26.05.2022. It was accordingly contended that disallowance of the said amount in the year under consideration would result in double taxation. In this regard, it is relevant to note that section 54F itself provides for taxation of the unutilised amount deposited in the Capital Gains Account Scheme in the year in which the prescribed period of three years from the date of transfer of the original asset expires, subject to the conditions specified therein. Therefore, the effect of cancellation of the agreement and the subsequent tax treatment adopted by the assessee would also require examination in accordance with the applicable provisions of law. 23. The AO had also raised objections regarding the non-contiguity of certain survey numbers, the cancellation of the agreement relating to the additional parcel of land, the amount deposited in the Capital Gains Account Scheme and the absence of supporting evidence regarding construction and utilisation of funds. The assessee, on the other hand, had contended that the various parcels of land purchased by him were adjoining and formed part of a single residential property, upon which one residential house had been constructed. The assessee had also furnished details of ancillary expenditure aggregating to Rs.64,84,640/- towards stamp duty, brokerage, franking and legal expenses, besides furnishing details of the amount of Rs.25 lakh deposited in the Capital Gains Account Scheme and its stated utilisation towards construction. The Ld. CIT(A), after examining the material placed before him, had accepted the assessee’s explanation that the relevant survey numbers were adjacent and that the investments related to one residential property. The Ld. CIT(A) had further recorded a finding that the construction had been undertaken within the prescribed statutory period and that the assessee had complied with the conditions applicable for claiming deduction u/s 54F of the Act. 24. We have considered the rival submissions and examined the materials on record. It will be relevant to first reproduce the findings of the Ld. CIT(A) on this issue, which is as under: “7.4 The detailed submission of the appellant vide submission dated 10.06.2024 (Para 5.2 ) has been examined by me. In the present case appellant chose to construct house within 3 years utilizing the consideration received on account of transfer of shares. Since the all the above-mentioned conditions were satisfied in the case facts, the appellant had also claimed deduction under section 54 F of the Act with reference to the expenses incurred towards such house under the referred section. Also, under the provisions of said section it is open to the appellant to construct the residential house within a period of 3 years from the date of transfer. As a matter of fact, the appellant has purchased land bearing survey nos. 604 (new survey no 11), 616 (new survey no 76), 605 (new survey no 79), 602 (new survey no 80) and agreement to sale was executed for buying survey no 613 (new survey no 77), 601 (new survey no 91) and 603 (new survey no 10). These lands are adjacent to each other as duly evidenced by the copy of purchase deed of the plot for construction of new house which was submitted during assessment and during the proceedings. Additional expenses relating to stamp duty, brokerage, franking and other legal expenses of Rs. 64,84,640/- were also made in February 2022. Also, an amount of Rs. 25,00,000/- was deposited in capital gain account scheme which was duly utilized for the purpose of construction of property and additional sum was also expended in F.Y. 2021-22 towards construction and development of house. Further, all the lands having survey no 10,11, 76, 77, 79, 80 & 91 for which appellant has claimed deduction are adjacent to each other and this fact can also be verified from the analysis mentioned by the Ld. Thus, appellant has constructed new residential property on an adjacent land only and the deduction claimed under section 54F of the Act pertains to only one new residential house constructed by the appellant within the time limit as prescribed under the said provision. Also, since investment has been made before the return so appellant was eligible for claiming deduction under section 54F of the Act as all the conditions pertaining to same are complied in the case facts. Thus the claim of Rs. 19,97,40,203/-as made under section 54 F of the Act was justified in facts as well as in law. Ld. Assessing officer has denied the deduction under section 54F of the Act on the ground that the Assessee has not given any evidence of depositing the entire consideration in the CGAS account. The appellant has made elaborate documentary evidence that shows that the assessee has completed the construction of the residential house. The contention of the appellant wrt claim of deduction u/s 54 F of the Act has been established in the eyes of law n following grounds. Taking into consideration the facts above mentioned facts, it can be seen that appellant has constructed new residential property on an adjacent land only and the deduction claimed under section 54F of the Act pertains to only one new residential house constructed by the appellant within the time limit as prescribed under the said provision. Also, since investment has been made before the return filed, so appellant was eligible for claiming deduction under section 54F of the Act as all the conditions pertaining to same are compiled in the case facts. Considered the material on record, the arguments and written submission of the appellant, the impugned order of the AO and the facts of the case. In view of above discussion the claim of deduction under section 54F of the Act as claimed by the appellant vide ground no 2 is allowed and this ground of appeal is decided in favor of the appellant and against the Revenue.” 25. We find that the finding of the CIT(A) is essentially a finding of fact based upon the documentary evidences produced before him. The Revenue has not brought before us any material to demonstrate that the finding regarding completion and use of the residential house is factually incorrect. The fact that the assessee had incurred expenditure of Rs.19,07,55,563/- towards purchase of land and additional expenditure of Rs.64,84,640/- towards stamp duty, brokerage, franking and other legal expenses in connection with the acquisition of the aforesaid land, is not under dispute. The assessee had brought on record a certificate for completion of bungalow issued by BSB Interior LLP, the professional engaged for construction of the house at Rancharda village, near Bunny Farm, Taluka Kalol, Dist. Gandhinagar. As per the certificate dated 29.08.2023 issued by this entity, the construction of the property was complete in all respect. The assessee had provided a copy of the bungalow plan and photograph of the property. Further the bills for electricity connection were also brought on record, from which it is evident that the property was constantly in use since July 2023 onwards. The address appearing in the electricity bills in the name of the assessee is that of Survey number 11, Ranchandra Village, which is evident of the fact that the bungalow was constructed on the land purchased by the assessee. The electricity bills for the July’23 to August’23 on record, reflect substantial electricity consumption which substantiate that the property was being used for residential purpose, within the time limit of three years as stipulated u/s 54F of the Act. The Revenue has been unable to controvert the findings brought on record by the assessee. Neither any remand report was sent to the Ld. CIT(A) in spite of specific directions nor any adverse material has been brought on record by the Revenue before us. No enquiry was made from the engineer, no physical verification was done and no material was produced by the Revenue to substantiate the allegation that the property was not constructed. Since the Ld. CIT(A) has allowed the deduction on the basis of actual qualifying investment and completed construction supported by evidence, we find no reason to interfere with his findings. On an overall consideration of the material on record, we find that the Ld. CIT(A) has examined the objections raised by the AO and has recorded reasoned findings in respect of the claim u/s 54F of the Act. Therefore, the finding of the Ld. CIT(A) allowing the deduction u/s 54F of the Act on the basis of the qualifying investment and construction of the residential house, is upheld. The grounds taken by the Revenue in this respect are dismissed. 26. In the result, the appeal of the Revenue is dismissed. Order pronounced in the Court on 18/11/2026 at Ahmedabad.

Summary: An initial public offering (IPO) can comprise a fresh issue, an offer for sale (OFS), or both. In a fresh issue, the issuer creates and allots new equity shares, and the company receives the issue proceeds after relevant expenses. In an OFS, existing shareholders sell their shares to public investors and ordinarily receive the sale proceeds; the company does not raise fresh equity from that component. The distinction affects capital availability, dilution, ownership distribution and how investors should assess the transaction. Investors should examine the offer document for the split between the two components, objects and estimated deployment of fresh-issue proceeds, selling shareholders, pre- and post-issue shareholding, promoter participation, risk factors, financial statements and valuation. A large OFS is not inherently negative, just as a large fresh issue is not automatically beneficial. The commercial significance depends on pricing, the company’s funding needs, cash generation, capital allocation and governance. This article provides a structured way to read the disclosures and compare issue structures without treating the IPO format as an investment recommendation. Its outstanding equity share count rises, and proceeds flow to the company, subject to issue expenses and disclosed purposes. In an offer for sale, existing shareholders transfer existing shares to successful applicants. The outstanding number of shares ordinarily remains unchanged solely because of that OFS; consideration belongs to selling shareholders, subject to expenses and applicable arrangements. Investors should read the objects of the issue alongside the timetable, estimated costs and assumptions. A stated intention to reduce debt is not equivalent to an immediate reduction in debt on the listing date. OFS: Liquidity for Existing Shareholders OFS proceeds normally go to the selling shareholders, which may include promoters, private equity investors or other eligible holders. Selling can reflect portfolio rebalancing, liquidity needs or planned exits; it should not automatically be interpreted as a negative signal. Examine who is selling, how much is being sold and the stake each shareholder will retain. How Fresh Issues Change Shareholding and Dilution A fresh issue generally dilutes the percentage ownership of shareholders who do not acquire additional shares, even though their absolute share count remains unchanged. Dilution is not itself evidence of value destruction: whether the capital raise is beneficial depends on the price paid, the quality of investments made with the funds and the returns generated. Illustration: New Shares Versus Existing Shares Assume a company has 100 lakh shares before an IPO. It issues 25 lakh new shares at ₹100 per share. Its post-issue share count becomes 125 lakh, and gross fresh-issue proceeds are ₹25 crore before expenses. A pre-issue shareholder with 10 lakh shares moves from 10% ownership to 8%, assuming no participation or other changes. If instead 25 lakh existing shares are sold through an OFS, the company still has 100 lakh shares outstanding and does not receive ₹25 crore from the sale. These figures are illustrative and ignore taxes, costs and other corporate actions. Evaluating Promoter Sales and Post-Issue Ownership Review the identities of the selling shareholders, the percentage of holdings sold and the promoter group’s post-issue stake. Consider whether a controlling shareholder retains substantial economic exposure and whether the disclosures describe relevant transactions with related parties. An OFS may help satisfy public-shareholding objectives, but investors must examine the actual issue and listing requirements applicable to the issuer. Also examine lock-in disclosures under the applicable SEBI framework. Lock-in requirements can differ according to the category of holding and the regulatory conditions; investors should not assume that all promoter and pre-issue shares become freely tradable on the same date. Reading the Prospectus Before Applying Issue Composition and Objects Locate the size of the fresh issue and OFS separately, the offer price or price band, total issue size and estimated net proceeds. Cross-check the objects of the issue with outstanding borrowings, capital projects, historical cash flows and disclosed funding gaps. Note which uses are firm commitments and which remain subject to future business decisions. Financial Statements and Cash Generation Study revenue trends, operating margins, cash flow from operations, capital expenditure, debt maturities and working-capital requirements. Profits alone do not establish the company’s ability to fund expansion or service borrowings. Compare the proposed capital raise with the issuer’s existing balance sheet and the cash it can realistically generate. Risk Factors, Litigation and Governance Read the issuer’s prospectus and offer disclosures for customer concentration, related-party dealings, contingent liabilities, regulatory proceedings, use-of-proceeds monitoring and material contracts. The summary of an offer document cannot replace the complete disclosures. IPO Valuation Matters More Than the Fresh Issue–OFS Label A company may have an attractive expansion plan but still be offered at an expensive valuation. Conversely, an OFS-heavy issue may involve an established business with healthy cash generation. Compare valuation metrics such as earnings multiples, enterprise value relative to operating performance, return on capital and peer comparisons, while accounting for differences in growth, leverage and accounting policies. When the fresh issue is substantial, calculate valuation using the post-issue equity base where relevant. For an OFS-only IPO, distinguish the offer price from the proceeds available to the business: the seller receives the consideration, not the issuer. Avoid treating a headline subscription multiple or grey-market quotation as evidence of fundamental value. Regulatory and Tax Considerations for Indian IPO Investors Indian public issues are subject to the applicable provisions of the Companies Act, 2013, SEBI (Issue of Capital and Disclosure Requirements) Regulations, 2018, and other relevant securities laws, as amended. Exact eligibility, disclosures, lock-in and public-shareholding requirements depend on the issuer and issue structure. Refer to current SEBI regulations and the final offer document rather than assuming that an older IPO precedent applies unchanged. For individual investors, allotment, subsequent sale and holding period can affect tax reporting and capital-gains consequences. The tax position depends on the applicable law for the relevant financial year, transaction facts and any securities transaction tax conditions. The company’s receipt of share capital and a selling shareholder’s sale proceeds are separate events; their respective tax treatment should not be conflated. Practical Due-Diligence Checklist for IPO Applicants Identify the gross issue size and split between fresh issue and OFS. Read the proposed utilisation, implementation schedule and estimated issue expenses. Identify every material selling shareholder and compare pre- and post-issue stakes. Calculate dilution and consider the post-issue share count. Review operating cash flows, debt, profitability and related-party transactions. Compare offer valuation with relevant listed peers and business risks. Read lock-in, litigation, governance and risk-factor disclosures. Retain the final prospectus and relevant investment records for future reference. Frequently Asked Questions on Fresh Issues and OFS Does a company receive money from an OFS? Normally no. The consideration for existing shares sold through an OFS goes to the selling shareholders, not the issuer company. Does every fresh issue dilute existing shareholders? Issuing additional shares increases the total share count and generally reduces the percentage holdings of existing shareholders who do not participate, subject to the complete capital structure. Is an IPO with a large OFS necessarily risky? No. The percentage being sold is one factor. Assess seller identity, retained holdings, valuation, operating performance and governance before reaching a conclusion. Can one IPO contain both a fresh issue and an OFS? Yes. Many public issues combine newly issued shares with a sale of existing shares. The offer document identifies each component. How can investors verify the use of fresh-issue proceeds? Review the objects of the issue, proposed deployment schedule, monitoring disclosures where applicable and subsequent company reporting. Does an OFS increase the total number of company shares? Not by itself. An OFS transfers existing shares. A simultaneous fresh issue or another corporate action can separately increase outstanding shares. Should investors compare valuations on a pre-issue or post-issue basis? Use a consistent, appropriate equity base. For measures based on shares outstanding, consider the post-issue count and disclose assumptions. Are lock-in restrictions identical for all pre-IPO holders? No. The applicable conditions depend on the regulatory classification of shares and holders. Consult the final offer document and current SEBI rules. Key Takeaways for IPO Investors The essential distinction is the recipient of funds: fresh-issue proceeds finance the company, while OFS proceeds go to selling shareholders. But investment quality is determined by much more than issue composition. Assess capital deployment, dilution, seller motivations, governance, cash generation, valuation and the final offer disclosures together.

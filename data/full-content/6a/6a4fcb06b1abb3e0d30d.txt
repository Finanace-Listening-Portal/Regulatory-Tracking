Vs Commissioner of Central Excise & ST (CESTAT Chandigarh) Summary : Summary: The Chandigarh Bench of the Customs, Excise and Service Tax Appellate Tribunal (CESTAT), in five connected appeals filed by M/s National Refrigeration & Air Conditioning Engg., set aside service tax demands aggregating to ₹1,25,43,246 and penalties aggregating to ₹1,92,63,601 imposed in relation to composite contracts for supplying air-conditioning machinery, equipment and parts, together with erection, commissioning, installation and associated civil works. The appellant executed contracts for Government departments and authorities, including the Military Engineering Services (MES), Ordnance Factories, Railways, Airports, Government hospitals and the State of Jammu & Kashmir. The contracts specified the machinery and materials to be supplied, while the appellant paid VAT/CST on materials and the recipients deducted works contract tax. The five appeals covered different periods, including October 2003 to September 2008, 2010–11 and 2013–14. The principal appeal concerned a demand of ₹73,15,931, besides interest and penalties under Sections 76, 77 and 78 of the Finance Act, 1994. In the first round of litigation, CESTAT, by Final Order No. ST/158/2011-Cus DB dated 24 February 2011, had remanded the matter after observing that the adjudicating authority had not properly examined the work orders, particularly the separate labour charges for erection, commissioning and testing. The Tribunal had also considered the Military Engineering Services letter dated 20 February 2008, which reproduced a Ministry of Finance clarification concerning repair and maintenance services connected with buildings or civil structures not used for commercial activities. It had specifically observed that services rendered to MES during the relevant period were not liable to service tax, that the appellant was entitled to cum-service-tax benefit where applicable, and that the taxable value had to be examined with reference to the labour charges for erection, installation and commissioning. However, the adjudicating authority, through its order dated 1 October 2012, substantially reiterated its earlier demand of ₹73,15,931 and imposed penalties, taking the view that the 5% labour component mentioned in one work order did not represent the entire service portion of the composite contracts. The appellant contended that the adjudicating authority had exceeded the scope of remand and disregarded the Tribunal’s earlier directions. It produced a Chartered Accountant’s certificate and supporting contracts showing aggregate receipts of ₹19,90,42,973 during October 2003 to September 2008. Of this amount, ₹16,35,58,494 represented the supply of goods, ₹54,22,876 related to civil works, ₹50,58,543 represented services rendered, and ₹2,50,03,060 related to work executed in Jammu & Kashmir. The certificate further identified receipts of ₹14,00,54,298 from MES, ₹1,96,39,742 from Ordnance Factory, ₹32,13,165 from Railways, ₹12,32,077 from Airports and ₹99,00,630 from Government hospitals. The appellant argued that the goods component had been subjected to VAT/sales tax and could not again be included in the service-tax value; services rendered to MES and Government bodies were not taxable or were exempt; services performed in Jammu & Kashmir were outside the relevant levy; and the amount of ₹50,58,543 shown as service receipts had already suffered service tax. It also disputed the application of the 67% abatement method under Notifications Nos. 19/2003-ST dated 21 August 2003 and 1/2006-ST dated 1 March 2006, maintaining that the actual value of goods and services was identifiable from the contracts. The Revenue defended the adjudication by relying on the composite nature of the contracts and the fact that several work orders separately mentioned supervision, erection, commissioning and testing charges. It argued that the service component could not be restricted to the 5% labour charge in one particular work order, since the value of service also included supervision, testing at different stages, erection and other related expenses. The adjudicating authority had also rejected the MES clarification on the ground that it concerned repair and maintenance services rather than erection, commissioning, installation or works contract services. On valuation, it relied on Section 67 of the Finance Act, 1994, Notification No. 19/2003-ST and Rule 2A of the Service Tax (Determination of Value) Rules, 2006, taking the position that the value of the taxable service included labour, subcontractor charges, planning and designing, machinery hire, consumables, establishment expenses and profit attributable to labour and services. The appellant separately challenged the extended limitation period, alleging absence of wilful suppression or intent to evade tax, and disputed simultaneous penalties under Sections 76 and 78. CESTAT examined the actual receipts and documentary evidence, including the Chartered Accountant’s certificate and contracts placed in Annexure D of the appeal paper book. It noted that the Commissioner had allowed a notional 67% deduction towards goods from the gross receipts, whereas the appellant had produced evidence identifying the actual goods component of ₹16,35,58,494. Relying on Commissioner of Central Excise & Customs, Kerala Vs Larsen & Toubro Ltd., 2015 (39) S.T.R. 913 (S.C.), the Tribunal held that the goods portion, where identifiable and subjected to sales tax, could not be subjected to service tax through a notional valuation. It further noted that works contract service became taxable only from 1 June 2007 and that, before the introduction of a specific charging provision for works contracts, indivisible composite contracts could not be subjected to service tax under the earlier taxable-service entries. The Tribunal also accepted the appellant’s submissions concerning services rendered to Government authorities and departments, the exempt nature of work performed in Jammu & Kashmir, and the exclusion of the goods component. It distinguished the MES clarification issue from the separate question of taxability under erection, commissioning and installation services, while ultimately agreeing with the appellant on the relevant non-taxability claims. On limitation and penalties, the Tribunal observed that the legal position concerning composite and works contracts remained unsettled during the relevant period and was clarified by the Supreme Court in Larsen & Toubro. The show-cause notice for the principal period had been issued on 22 April 2009, invoking the extended period. In the circumstances, the appellant could not be said to have deliberately suppressed facts or intended to evade tax merely because of a disputed interpretation of the service-tax provisions. The Tribunal also considered that the supplies were predominantly made to Government departments and authorities and that the appellant was not shown to have deliberately enriched itself through tax evasion. It concluded that the demands were unsustainable on the facts and law and that the consequential penalties could not survive. Accordingly, all five appeals were allowed with consequential relief, by Final Order Nos. 60592–60596/2026 pronounced in open court on 4 November 2026. Cases Discussed Northern Operating Systems Pvt.

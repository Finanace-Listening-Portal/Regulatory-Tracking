(Supreme Court of India) The Supreme Court held that courts must exercise considerable restraint while interfering with tender decisions relating to major infrastructure projects, particularly those financed through foreign sovereign funding arrangements. In National High Speed Rail Corporation Limited Vs Montecarlo Limited & Anr., the Court examined whether the Delhi High Court was justified in overturning the rejection of a technical bid submitted for the Mumbai–Ahmedabad High Speed Rail Project. The Supreme Court emphasised that judicial review of contractual and tender decisions is confined principally to examining the legality and fairness of the decision-making process, rather than substituting the court’s assessment for the technical and commercial evaluation undertaken by competent authorities. The Court allowed the appeal filed by National High Speed Rail Corporation Limited (NHSRCL), set aside the Delhi High Court’s judgment and dismissed the original writ petition. Background of the dispute: NHSRCL, a government company established with equity participation from the Government of India and the Governments of Gujarat and Maharashtra, issued a tender notice on 22 October 2020 for Bid Package No. MAHSR-8. The package concerned the design and construction of civil and building works for the Sabarmati depot under the Mumbai–Ahmedabad High Speed Rail Project. The proposed works included site formation, retaining walls, roadbeds, culverts, roads, drainage systems, water supply arrangements, fire-fighting infrastructure, inspection sheds, maintenance facilities and associated construction activities. The project was supported by concessional financing from the Japan International Cooperation Agency (JICA), and the bidding process was governed by the applicable tender documents and procurement requirements associated with that financing arrangement. The technical bids were opened on 19 February 2021. Montecarlo Limited was among five bidders whose bids were rejected at the technical evaluation stage. NHSRCL communicated the rejection through the technical proposal evaluation summary uploaded on 27 April 2021 and its subsequent communication dated 28 April 2021. The bid was treated as technically non-responsive because the bidder had not signed Form CON 2.0 relating to pending litigation and Form CON 3.0 relating to litigation history in the physically submitted bid documents. According to NHSRCL, these omissions constituted material non-conformities with the bidding requirements. The evaluation had been undertaken through the technical consultancy mechanism associated with JICA, reviewed by the tender committee and concurred in by the funding agency. Proceedings before the Delhi High Court: Aggrieved by the rejection, Montecarlo approached the Delhi High Court through Writ Petition (C) No. 5127 of 2021. It argued that the relevant forms contained the necessary information and had been stamped, although they had inadvertently remained unsigned. The bidder contended that this was a curable defect and did not justify disqualification. It further alleged discriminatory treatment because certain other bidders had been permitted to clarify or rectify deficiencies in their documents. By its judgment dated 23 August 2021, the Delhi High Court accepted the challenge, quashed the rejection communications and directed NHSRCL to proceed with further examination of Montecarlo’s bid. The High Court considered the omission to be capable of substantial compliance and questioned the operation of tender provisions restricting disclosure of evaluation details before the award of the contract. Arguments advanced by NHSRCL: Before the Supreme Court, NHSRCL submitted that the project was a major national infrastructure initiative funded through a concessional Japanese loan arrangement. The procurement documents had been prepared in accordance with JICA’s standard bidding documents and procurement guidelines. The technical evaluation had been undertaken by the consultant and approved through the prescribed process. NHSRCL argued that it could not independently relax essential conditions stipulated under the financing and procurement arrangements. It maintained that the failure to sign the relevant litigation declaration forms was a material deviation and that permitting subsequent correction would undermine the prescribed evaluation process. It also submitted that the other bidders who had been asked for clarifications had passed the preliminary administrative evaluation stage and were therefore not similarly situated. NHSRCL further contended that courts should be especially cautious in interfering with procurement decisions relating to foreign-funded infrastructure projects. Such projects involve international financial commitments, technical requirements, bilateral cooperation and strict implementation schedules. Judicial interference at an intermediate stage of tender evaluation could delay interconnected construction packages, increase costs and potentially affect future financing arrangements. The appellant relied upon established Supreme Court decisions limiting judicial review in contractual matters, including Tata Cellular v. Union of India, Afcons Infrastructure Limited v. Nagpur Metro Rail Corporation Limited, Michigan Rubber (India) Limited v. State of Karnataka and Central Coalfields Limited v. SLL-SML. Arguments advanced by Montecarlo: The respondent maintained that foreign funding could not place a tender process entirely beyond judicial scrutiny. It submitted that the financing provided by JICA was a loan ultimately repayable from public resources and that the project therefore retained a substantial public-law character. Montecarlo argued that the tender conditions were required to be applied fairly and uniformly to all participating bidders. According to the respondent, the absence of signatures on two otherwise completed and stamped forms was a minor omission that could have been rectified through clarification. It contended that other bidders had received opportunities to clarify deficiencies and that denying a similar opportunity to Montecarlo was arbitrary and discriminatory. The respondent relied upon the doctrine of substantial compliance and maintained that the High Court had correctly directed reconsideration of its technical bid. Supreme Court’s analysis: The Supreme Court examined the nature of the high-speed rail project, the JICA financing arrangements, the procurement framework, the technical evaluation process and the respective functions of NHSRCL, the consultant and the funding agency. The Court observed that the project involved significant international cooperation and concessional financing intended to support infrastructure development in India. Such arrangements required adherence to the terms and conditions governing procurement and implementation. The Court recognised that foreign-funded projects may involve considerations extending beyond ordinary domestic public works contracts, particularly because changes to procurement conditions or prolonged litigation could affect the financing arrangement and the timely execution of the project. The Court reiterated that the scope of judicial review in tender matters is limited. Courts are concerned with whether the decision-making process is arbitrary, irrational, mala fide, discriminatory or contrary to public interest. They do not ordinarily sit in appeal over technical evaluations made by competent authorities. The employer or tendering authority is best placed to interpret the tender documents, determine the significance of particular conditions and decide whether a bid satisfies the prescribed requirements. Judicial interference is warranted only where the decision is legally unsustainable under the established standards of review, rather than merely because another interpretation or evaluation might also be possible. In examining the rejection of Montecarlo’s technical bid, the Supreme Court considered the failure to sign the prescribed litigation declaration forms and the evaluation mechanism applicable to all bidders. The Court accepted that the relevant tender requirements had been applied through the prescribed technical evaluation process and that the bid had been treated as non-responsive on account of material non-conformities. The Court found no sufficient basis for the High Court to substitute its own assessment of substantial compliance for the evaluation made by the competent technical authorities. The distinction between bidders rejected at the initial administrative stage and those who had qualified for subsequent stages was also material when considering the allegation of unequal treatment. Confidentiality and disclosure of reasons: The Supreme Court also considered the tender provisions dealing with confidentiality of bid evaluation and disclosure of reasons to unsuccessful bidders. Clauses ITB 28.1 and 42.5 contemplated that evaluation information would remain confidential until communication of the contract award and that unsuccessful bidders could thereafter seek a debriefing explaining why their bids had not been selected. The Court considered these provisions in the context of the need to preserve the integrity and continuity of the procurement process. The restrictions did not mean that an unsuccessful bidder would be permanently deprived of legal remedies. Rather, the procedure was designed to avoid disruption of the ongoing tender process while preserving an opportunity to seek reasons after the award. Importance of public interest: The Supreme Court stressed that major infrastructure projects serve significant public purposes and that delays caused by avoidable litigation can have substantial financial and administrative consequences. In the context of the Mumbai–Ahmedabad High Speed Rail Project, the Court noted that the works were divided into interconnected packages and that delay in one package could affect the implementation of others. It observed that prolonged judicial interference in foreign-funded development projects could increase project costs, disturb implementation schedules and affect future investment or financing. Courts were therefore required to balance the need to prevent arbitrary administrative action against the wider public interest in the timely completion of nationally important infrastructure projects. The Court referred to the principles laid down in Tata Cellular v. Union of India, including judicial restraint in administrative matters, the limited competence of courts to reassess technical decisions, the contractual nature of tender conditions, the freedom of government authorities in commercial decision-making and the need to prevent arbitrariness, bias and mala fides. It further cautioned High Courts to be particularly circumspect when entertaining challenges or granting interim orders that could delay major public projects. The Court observed that, in appropriate circumstances, a litigant whose unsuccessful challenge causes delay may be put on notice of potential liability for resulting damages. These observations were made as guidance concerning the exercise of judicial discretion and protection of the larger public interest. Final decision: The Supreme Court concluded that the Delhi High Court had exceeded the permissible scope of judicial review by interfering with the technical evaluation and rejection of Montecarlo’s bid. The Court held that the High Court’s judgment dated 23 August 2021 was unsustainable and accordingly quashed and set it aside. The appeal filed by NHSRCL was allowed, and the original writ petition instituted by Montecarlo before the Delhi High Court stood dismissed. No order as to costs was made, and pending applications were disposed of. The judgment reinforces the principle that courts should not substitute their own commercial or technical judgment for that of competent tendering authorities in the absence of established illegality, arbitrariness, mala fides or other recognised grounds for judicial interference, especially where nationally important and foreign-funded infrastructure projects are concerned.

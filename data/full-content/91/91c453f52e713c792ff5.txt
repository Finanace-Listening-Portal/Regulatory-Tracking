(GSTAT Ghaziabad) Summary : The Goods and Services Tax Appellate Tribunal (GSTAT), State Bench Ghaziabad, dismissed the State Revenue’s appeal against the order of the First Appellate Authority allowing transitional credit claimed by M/s Systematic Conscom Ltd. under Form GST TRAN-1. The dispute concerned ₹99,63,376 claimed as transitional credit, comprising ₹97,73,630 described by the taxpayer as refundable tax deducted at source (TDS) under the Uttar Pradesh Value Added Tax Act, 2008, and ₹1,89,746 claimed as purchase-related input tax credit. The VAT return reflected only ₹607, which the taxpayer described as a clerical error. The proper officer treated VAT TDS as distinct from input tax credit and confirmed tax of ₹99,62,769, interest of ₹26,39,998 and penalty of ₹9,96,277 under Section 73 of the GST law. The First Appellate Authority subsequently cancelled the demand and recognised transitional credit of ₹97,74,237. The Revenue challenged that appellate relief. The Revenue argued that TDS deducted from works-contract receipts constituted payment towards the taxpayer’s output tax liability rather than input tax on inward supplies. Consequently, it contended that the amount could not be transferred to the GST electronic credit ledger under Section 140(1), read with Sections 2(62) and 2(63). The taxpayer maintained that the TDS amount had already been deposited with the Government and remained available for adjustment or refund under the UP VAT Act. It relied on Sections 15 and 34 of that Act and decisions including D.M.R. Constructions, Subhash Singh Choudhary and Magma Fincorp. The taxpayer also pointed out that its legacy VAT assessments remained pending, that its entitlement had not been finally reconciled, and that the original notice did not specifically dispute the transitional eligibility of the TDS component. The Tribunal examined Notice No. 320 dated 7 August 2018 and found that it raised two objections: a discrepancy between the ₹607 reflected in the VAT return and the ₹99,63,376 claimed in TRAN-1, and non-furnishing of Form C particulars relating to inter-State sales. However, the proper officer subsequently accepted the Form C particulars and did not sustain that objection. The remaining demand was founded substantially on a different legal proposition: that TDS arising from outward works-contract receipts did not constitute eligible input tax. The Tribunal held that this distinct ground had not been formulated in the show cause notice. Relying on the principles recognised in Commissioner of Central Excise v. Brindavan Beverages and Oryx Fisheries v. Union of India, it emphasised that a notice must disclose the substantive allegations sufficiently to permit an effective defence. A general reference to Section 140 and Rule 117 could not substitute for a specific charge. Sections 75(6) and 75(7) further required reasoned determination within the grounds identified in the notice. The Tribunal also found that neither the proper officer nor the First Appellate Authority had adequately reconciled the separate TDS and purchase-credit components. The Revenue had not produced a completed VAT assessment, refund-disposal order or other reliable reconciliation establishing whether the claimed legacy balance had been exhausted, adjusted or rejected. The Tribunal declined to presume that the taxpayer’s claim was invalid merely because the VAT authorities had not completed the relevant proceedings. It further held that the interest demand lacked a verifiable computation identifying the applicable provision, period, rate and actual utilisation of credit. The penalty could not be restored when the underlying tax determination itself was unsustainable. The Revenue’s additional reliance on decisions concerning strict interpretation of tax credit provisions did not overcome the defects in the notice and adjudication. Importantly, the Tribunal did not determine the general question whether unadjusted VAT TDS is eligible for transition into the GST electronic credit ledger. It expressly left that substantive issue open, including the differing approaches adopted by the Madras, Jharkhand and Kerala High Courts. Its decision rested instead on the defective show cause notice, inadequate component-wise determination, absence of essential supporting evidence and unsupported consequential demands. Holding that remand merely to construct a fresh charge beyond the original notice would not cure the proceedings, the Tribunal dismissed the Revenue’s appeal, confirmed the First Appellate Authority’s order dated 15 October 2022 and maintained cancellation of tax of ₹99,62,769, interest of ₹26,39,998 and penalty of ₹9,96,277. This appeal by the State Revenue challenges the order dated 15 October 2022 passed by the First Appellate Authority in Appeal No. 86/19. This departmental appeal, APL/D46/GZB/2026, concerns the transition of tax deducted at source (TDS) from the respondent’s works-contract receipts under the UP VAT Act into the GST electronic credit ledger through Form GST TRAN-1. The respondent, M/s Systematic Conscom Ltd., carries on trading and executes works contracts in Uttar Pradesh. It states that its contractees deducted tax from its bills under the Uttar Pradesh Value Added Tax Act, 2008 (the UP VAT Act). It also engaged subcontractors and deducted tax from payments made to them. According to the respondent, the tax deducted from its receipts exceeded its final VAT liability. It states that refunds had been allowed in earlier years. Notice No. 320 dated 7 August 2018 was issued under Section 73. It identified a TRAN-1 claim of Rs. 99,63,376 against only Rs. 607 shown as input tax credit carried forward in the VAT return for June. It also raised an objection about particulars of Form C against inter-State sales. The notice required a written explanation on 13 August 2018 and proposed disallowance, interest under Section 50 and penalty under the applicable provisions. The adjudication order records the respondent’s explanation that Rs. 607 was a clerical error and that the correct input-credit figure was Rs. 1,89,746. It separately identified Rs. 97,73,630 as refundable tax deducted at source, claimed in Form 52 for 2016-17. The respondent stated that the VAT assessments for 2016-17 and the relevant part of 2017-18 were pending when TRAN-1 was filed. The supplementary reply, reproduced in the adjudication order, stated that Rs. 26,60,305 of the claimed credit had been used before the notice. Challans for that amount and Rs. 1,17,783 towards interest were produced. Hence at the cost of repetition we reiterate that issue of TDS which subsequently became basis of adverse finding by the proper officer is not discernable from SCN . It does not expressly allege that an identified TDS balance is legally incapable of transition. The later order, however, rejects the claim by treating TDS as outside the definitions of input tax. A mere reference to the section and rule cannot, without examining the actual charge, be treated as notice of every possible disqualification. Our conclusion concerns the combined defects i.e the notice did not formulate TDS as distinct legal ground and the Form C ground was dropped. Even the final demand issued by the proper officer did not adequately reconcile the TDS figure and ITC figure, which is against Section 75(6), which requires the order to state the relevant facts and its basis. Section 75(7) confines confirmation to the grounds in the notice and the prescribed monetary limits. The adjudicating order could assess the explanation within the notice’s scope; it could not repair a missing substantive charge by introducing it for the first time. 8 . Returns and the unascertained components and its impact on the matter. 8.1. Prior to giving an adverse finding it was necessary for the adjudicating authority to distinguish the amount declared, by the respondent in the Return the amount carried forward under the legacy law, the amount claimed as TDS, the purchase-credit component and any amount already adjusted or refunded. An arithmetic difference between two entries does not answer all these questions. The adjudication order refers to the filed June return, showing Rs. 607 and records the respondent’s explanation of clerical error, its alternative figure, and its Form 52 claim. But from perusal of the documents filed by the Revenue, we find that the State produces no completed VAT assessment report /order rejecting or exhausting the stated refund balance. In absence of final assessment order by VAT officer, one would not be able to know actual status of refund / balance status. Obviously there is no denial by the Revenue that Returns were not filed by the Respondent, hence we cannot blame Respondent for above mention state of affairs . We must not forget that matter is of year 2017-18. There is a specific TDS/refund figure of Rs. 97,73,630 in the respondent’s explanation. However, we should keep in mind that TDS is not part of SCN. Hence any adverse finding beyond SCN would be legally unsustainable. Section 75(7) prevents confirmation on grounds other than those specified in the notice and confines the determination within the prescribed monetary limits. 9. Why a general ruling on TDS and TRAN-1 is unnecessary As we know, the relevant transitional provision for State VAT is section 140(1) of the UPGST Act. The proviso and the applicable return requirements are necessary legal requirement for just determination of present issue of transition of balance from VAT regime to GST regime. However, application of those conditions requires identification of the amount and its character. There is no separate, reasoned finding in the OIA reconciling the precise TDS component with the entire disputed demand. We therefore need not decide whether TDS can, in principle, be included in TRAN-1. This appeal can and should be decided on the validity of the notice, the reasons for adjudication and the proof of the demand sought to be restored. 10 . Absence of final assessment order and the period of waiting. The present situation at hand is very much similar to the Mangalore Chemicals. In Mangalore Chemicals & Fertilisers Ltd. v. Deputy Commissioner of Commercial Taxes, 1992 Supp (1) SCC 21, Hon’ble Supreme Court distinguished substantive conditions from procedural requirements. The taxpayer’s entitlement and eligibility for adjustment were undisputed, but the Revenue had withheld the required permission without justification. The Court did not allow that inaction to defeat relief. The analogy here is limited: departmental delay cannot, without identifying a taxpayer default, be treated as proof against the taxpayer. The proper officer recorded the respondent’s statement that the relevant VAT assessments were pending. The Revenue neither asserts their subsequent completion, nor produces a completed assessment or refund-disposal order. We cannot presume an unproduced decision rejecting, reducing or exhausting the legacy balance. The practical question remains: why should the respondent wait, and for how long? If ascertainment of the balance depends on departmental action, the Department must explain the delay and identify any outstanding requirement attributable to the respondent. An established entitlement cannot fairly be left unresolved indefinitely because the Department has not completed its own work. 11. Interest and Penalty 11.1. Now we take up question of Interest. Interest must have a statutory basis and a verifiable computation. In a case governed by section 50(3), the inquiry concerns credit wrongly availed and utilised. Rule 88B supplies the relevant computation framework. If the claim is instead under section 50(1) for unpaid output tax, the liability and period of default must be identified. Those distinct bases cannot be interchanged without reasons.The SCN and demand expressly refer to section 50. The material before us does not supply the dates, rate, ledger-based utilisation and period supporting interest of Rs. 26,39,998. The respondent’s statement that Rs. 26,60,305 had been used before the notice is not a substitute for that working. Section 75(9) does not dispense with proof of the facts required to establish and compute interest. Hence we find that determination and demand of interest is unsustainable. 11.2. The respondent’s submission about absence of fraud must also be kept in its proper place. Section 73 concerns cases other than fraud, wilful misstatement or suppression of facts to evade tax. Absence of fraud alone does not exclude a liability or penalty otherwise arising under that section. Here, however, the tax determination supporting the penalty has not been sustained. In Union of India v. Rajasthan Spinning & Weaving Mills, (2009) 13 SCC 448, the Supreme Court explained that the statutory conditions for the penalty provision under consideration must first exist. Mandatory consequences do not dispense with those conditions. Applying that limited principle, we find no basis to restore the penalty of Rs. 9,96,277 when the Revenue has failed to sustain the demand on which it rests. 12.

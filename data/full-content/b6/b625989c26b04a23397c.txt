Vs ACIT (ITAT Surat) Summary: The Surat Bench of the Income Tax Appellate Tribunal, comprising Judicial Member Shri Pawan Singh and Accountant Member Dr. Arjun Lal Saini, partly allowed the appeal filed by Huber Group India Pvt. Ltd. (formerly Micro Inks Pvt. Ltd.) Vs ACIT for Assessment Year 2011-12. The appeal arose from the final assessment order dated 4 December 2015, passed under Sections 144C(13) and 143(3) of the Income Tax Act, 1961, following directions issued by the Dispute Resolution Panel, Mumbai. The proceedings involved substantial transfer pricing adjustments relating to corporate guarantees and royalty payments, along with disputes concerning deductions under Sections 80-IB and 10B, foreign exchange fluctuation losses, interest on income-tax refunds and additional grounds concerning dividend distribution tax. The assessee, a wholly owned subsidiary of MHM Holding GmbH, Germany, was engaged in manufacturing and selling printing inks, intermediates and allied products. It operated manufacturing units at Vapi, Daman, Silvassa-I, Silvassa-II and a 100% export-oriented unit. In its return for AY 2011-12, the assessee claimed deductions under Section 80-IB(4) for the Silvassa-II unit and Section 10B for the export-oriented unit. The Transfer Pricing Officer examined the international transactions reported in Form 3CEB and proposed an upward adjustment of ₹89.18 lakh for corporate guarantee commission and ₹15.42 crore for royalty payments. The Assessing Officer incorporated these adjustments and made further additions and disallowances, which the DRP substantially sustained. On the corporate guarantee issue, the assessee had provided a guarantee to the Bank of India in connection with borrowings of its US subsidiary. The guarantee amount was USD 10 million. The assessee contended that extending the guarantee was a shareholder activity or quasi-equity support, undertaken to facilitate its subsidiary’s operations in the American market, and therefore did not require a separate arm’s length commission. The TPO rejected this contention and determined a guarantee commission of 2%, resulting in an adjustment of ₹89.18 lakh. Before the Tribunal, the assessee accepted that the commission could be restricted to 0.5%, relying on the Bombay High Court decision in CIT Vs Everest Kento Cylinders Ltd., reported at 58 taxmann.com 254. Following that precedent, the Tribunal directed the Assessing Officer/TPO to restrict the corporate guarantee adjustment to 0.5%, partly allowing the relevant ground. The principal transfer pricing controversy concerned royalty of ₹25.70 crore paid to the associated enterprise for technical know-how, trademarks and brand-related rights. The assessee benchmarked the transaction using the Transactional Net Margin Method (TNMM), contending that no sufficiently comparable uncontrolled transaction was available. The TPO rejected the combined benchmarking approach, selected the Comparable Uncontrolled Price (CUP) method and relied on an agreement involving Altair Nanotechnologies. Applying a royalty benchmark of 2%, the TPO proposed an upward adjustment of ₹15,42,01,392. The assessee disputed the comparability of the Altair agreement, pointing out differences in technology, business activities, geographical coverage and rights to use trademarks and brand names. The assessee alternatively argued that the royalty rates were within the limits permitted under the applicable RBI and government approval framework. It relied on decisions including DCIT Vs DIC India Ltd., CIT Vs SGS India Pvt. Ltd. and ACIT Vs Dow Agrosciences India Pvt. Ltd., in which approved royalty rates had been considered relevant for transfer pricing benchmarking. The Tribunal examined these authorities and accepted the assessee’s position concerning the royalty payments. It consequently granted relief against the royalty transfer pricing adjustment of ₹15.42 crore. The ruling turned on the facts and regulatory framework applicable to the relevant assessment year and should not be read as establishing that RBI-permitted rates automatically constitute arm’s length prices in every case. With respect to the deductions under Sections 80-IB and 10B, the Assessing Officer had reduced the eligible profits of the Silvassa-II and export-oriented units on account of inter-division transfers. The Tribunal considered the treatment adopted in the assessee’s earlier assessment years and directed reconsideration consistently with the applicable coordinate Bench decisions. The Tribunal also examined the exclusion of scrap-sale receipts from eligible business profits under Sections 80-IB and 10B, and the assessee’s contention that only the net amount of export incentives should be excluded when determining eligible profits. These grounds were addressed separately, having regard to the nature of the receipts and the treatment adopted in earlier proceedings. Another issue concerned the disallowance of ₹6,05,115 arising from foreign currency fluctuation on a term loan. The assessee argued that the foreign currency borrowing arrangement was intended to reduce financing costs and that the exchange fluctuation should be treated as revenue expenditure. The Tribunal considered decisions dealing with Sections 37(1) and 43A and the distinction between capital and revenue exchange differences. Following the later Madras High Court ruling in CIT Vs Continuum Wind Energy (India) Pvt. Ltd., it directed that the fluctuation loss be adjusted against the cost of the relevant capital asset, with consequential depreciation allowed in accordance with law. The ground seeking revenue deduction was dismissed. The assessee also challenged the inclusion of ₹8,26,042 as interest income on an income-tax refund relating to AY 2007-08. It contended that the interest had subsequently been withdrawn following assessment proceedings and therefore had not finally accrued as income. The Tribunal directed the Assessing Officer to verify the factual position and grant consequential relief if the interest had been withdrawn. The additional grounds concerning a reduced dividend distribution tax rate under the India-Germany tax treaty were rejected as inadmissible because the relevant facts did not emerge from the orders of the lower authorities. The separate additional ground seeking deduction of education cess was dismissed as not pressed. Overall, the Tribunal partly allowed the appeal, granting material relief on the transfer pricing issues while issuing specific directions on the remaining disputed adjustments. The assessee along with its return of income furnished Audit report under Form-3CEB reporting various international transactions with its Associates Enterprise (for short as to “AE”). The assessee claimed deduction under section 80IB (4) @ 30% for its Unit at Silvassa-II and claimed deduction under section 10B at 100% in EOU. Since the assessee reported international transactions of more than prescribed limit, therefore assessing officer made a reference under section 92CA(1) for computation of Arm’s Length Price (for short as to “ALP”) to Transfer Pricing Officer (for short as to “TPO”). The TPO examined various international transactions reported in assessee’s audit reporting in Form-3CEB and out of 44 transactions reported by assessee, the TPO selected two transactions i.e., Corporate guarantee and Royalty payment transaction to its AE for computation of their APL’s. The TPO after giving show cause notice, considering the reply of assessee suggested upward adjustment of Rs.15.42 crores on account of payment of royalty. The Assessing Officer on receipt of report of TPO passed draft assessment order dated 23.02.2015. We have heard the submissions of both the parties. Before us the Ld. AR for the assessee submits that TPO wrongly rejected the TNMM as most appropriate method. The TPO adopted CUP method as appropriate method, which is not in accordance with Rule 10B (2) of the ITAT Rules, 1963. The assessee is in the manufacturing of Ink. The Altair Technology is an extraction and related product. The assessee’s parent company has name and trade mark included, however, in case of Altair Nanotechnology has name and trade-name are not included. There is geographical difference as per agreement of assessee. The assessee has agreement in India and rest of the world other than USA. The Altair Nanotechnology has agreement for USA. Altair Nanotechnology is not in manufacturing of printing. The Ld. AR for the assessee further submits when exact comparable is not available for benchmarking royalty, CUP is not most appropriate method. It has been held so by Mumbai Tribunal in the case of Cabot Indi Ltd. Vs. DCIT, in ITA No.6622/Mum/2009 dated 31.05.2011 and Pune Tribunal in the case of Kirloskar Ebara Pumps Ltd. vs. DCIT (2011) 12 taxmann.com 241 (Pune-Trib.) and Mumbai Tribunal in the case of DCIT-LTU vs. CLSA India Ltd. (2013) 33 taxmann.com 260 (Mumbai-Trib.). The Ld. AR for the assessee further submits that TNMM is the most appropriate method for benchmarking royalty as held by Jaipur Bench in the case of ACIT, Circle-2, Alwar vs. Sakata Inx (India) Ltd. (2015) 54 taxmann.com 106 (Jaipur-Trib.). 23. In alternative submission, the Ld. AR for the assessee submits that amount of royalty paid during the year is within permissible limit under Foreign Exchange Management Act (FEMA) and Reserve Bank of India (RBI) guidelines and hence the same be considered as valid comparison as per CUP method.

Summary:The article examines the conceptual inconsistency between the terminology used for callable and non-callable fixed deposits in Indian banking and conventional fixed-income market terminology. Traditionally, a call option gives the issuer the right to redeem an instrument before maturity, whereas a put option gives the investor the right to demand premature repayment. Since the premature-withdrawal facility in a bank fixed deposit is exercised by the depositor rather than the bank, the author argues that such deposits are conceptually closer to puttable instruments. The article discusses the RBI’s regulatory framework governing premature withdrawal, the economic rationale for offering higher interest rates on deposits without premature-withdrawal facilities, and the importance of liquidity management. Drawing a parallel with the RBI’s historical realignment of Repo and Reverse Repo nomenclature with international market practices, the author recommends reconsidering existing fixed-deposit terminology. The proposed alternative is to classify deposits as term deposits with premature-withdrawal facilities (puttable) and term deposits without premature-withdrawal facilities (non-puttable). The article emphasises that such a change would improve transparency, financial literacy and consistency without necessarily altering existing contractual arrangements or deposit products. An accompanying annexure explains the differences between callable and puttable bonds, their contractual characteristics and their conceptual relationship with bank fixed deposits. Show all 16 sections Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); A familiar banking product, an unfamiliar contradiction Everyone who has invested in a bulk bank fixed deposit (FD) understands two basic things: the interest the deposit will earn and the period for which the money will remain invested. There is, however, a third equally important consideration: can the depositor get the money back before maturity if an unexpected financial need arises? In India, ordinary fixed deposits generally allow premature withdrawal, subject to the bank’s applicable conditions, interest-rate penalty and recalculation, wherever applicable. Banks also offer deposits without a premature-withdrawal facility, particularly for larger deposits, often at a higher rate of interest. The distinction appears simple. One deposit offers flexibility; the other requires the depositor to remain invested until maturity, except for specified deposit policy exceptions. But look at the terminology used to describe these products, and an interesting inconsistency emerges. Indian banking practice refers to deposits that permit premature withdrawal as callable deposits and those that do not as non-callable deposits. For anyone familiar with conventional bond-market terminology, this raises a rather fundamental question: Is the terminology being used correctly? More importantly, who actually holds the right to call or put the money? Who holds the right – the issuer or the investor? In conventional financial-market terminology, the distinction between a call and a put is quite straightforward. A call option gives the issuer or borrower the right to repay an instrument before its stipulated maturity, subject to the certain contractual conditions. A put option, on the other hand, gives the investor or lender the right to demand early repayment from the issuer. In simple terms, a call belongs to the borrower, while a put belongs to the lender. Let us apply this understanding to a bank fixed deposit. When a customer places money in an FD, the bank is effectively borrowing money from the depositor. If the depositor has the contractual right to demand repayment before maturity, that right is conceptually similar to a put option. The depositor, and not the bank, holds the early-exit right. On the other hand, if the depositor is not ordinarily permitted to withdraw the money before maturity (in any manner), the deposit can be described as non-puttable. It is said depositor has surrendered the early-repayment option. Consider a simple example. A customer places ₹25 lakh in a three-year FD at 7% p.a interest. After one year, market interest rates rise to 8%. The customer may prefer to withdraw the existing deposit, subject to the bank’s conditions and applicable penalty, and reinvest the money at the higher rate. The early-exit facility is therefore an option available to the depositor. Such withdrawal need not be for reinvesting. Now reverse the situation. Suppose market interest rates fall to 5%. Can the bank unilaterally compel the depositor to close the existing FD paying 7%, merely because it now finds the contracted rate expensive? Ordinarily, it cannot. The RBI’s published clarification on term deposits also recognises this distinction: a term deposit is a contract for a definite period and cannot be prematurely paid at the bank’s own option; premature repayment takes place at the customer’s request, subject to standard conditions. If we apply the conventional financial-market understanding, the early-withdrawal right in an ordinary FD is therefore closer to a put option than a call option. How did Indian banking arrive at this terminology? This is not merely a terminology adopted by individual banks. The RBI has used and institutionalised this convention. The RBI circular dated October 26, 2023, expressly refers to “non-callability of deposits” and explains it as the non-availability of the premature-withdrawal option except in rare situations like depositor’s death, bankruptcy, court orders, or regulatory directions. Under the RBI’s deposit interest-rate framework, domestic term deposits accepted from individuals, singly or jointly, for amounts of ₹1 crore and below are required to carry a premature-withdrawal facility. So non-callable FDs are generally offered single deposit of above ₹1 crore (some banks set higher minimums, such as ₹3 crore). Banks may also offer term deposits without this facility. The relevant provisions also extend to NRE and NRO deposits. Consequently, the terminology has become embedded in the banking system. Banks use it because it is part of the established regulatory vocabulary. It would therefore be unfair to suggest that an individual bank is necessarily misclassifying its product by following the prescribed convention. However, regulatory acceptance of a terminology does not automatically make it the most appropriate terminology. Perhaps, from the perspective of an ordinary depositor, the expression “calling back” one’s own money appears instinctive/in-built/intuitive. But financial terminology cannot be judged only by its everyday interpretation. It must also be consistent with the underlying contractual rights and the established language of financial markets. This becomes particularly relevant in India, where banking, money markets and bond markets are increasingly interconnected, and where financial-market participants, students and investors frequently move between different segments. The economic rationale behind non-callable deposits There is, of course, a sound economic rationale for offering a higher interest rate on deposits without a premature-withdrawal facility. A bank accepting deposits needs a reasonable degree of certainty about the availability and duration of its funding. When a depositor retains the flexibility to withdraw money before maturity, the bank faces some uncertainty in managing its liabilities and liquidity. When that flexibility is surrendered, the bank obtains greater certainty about the period for which funds will remain available. The depositor, in turn, may receive a higher interest rate as compensation for giving up liquidity. For instance, consider two hypothetical offers for a ₹2 crore deposit: Deposit A offers 7% p.a. interest and permits premature withdrawal, subject to applicable conditions. Deposit B offers 7.25% p.a. interest under non-callable deposit (does not* permit premature withdrawal). The additional 0.25 percentage point translates into ₹50,000 a year on a simple-interest basis, before tax. The depositor must decide whether this additional return adequately compensates for surrendering the flexibility to access the money. This is a legitimate commercial arrangement. The issue being raised here is not the economic justification for such deposits, but the language used to describe the rights attached to them. It is also important to remember that even an FD permitting premature withdrawal does not necessarily offer a cost-free exit. The applicable interest may be recalculated based on the period for which the deposit actually remained with the bank, along with contracted interest rate penalty stipulated. A precedent already set by RBI: The Repo and Reverse Repo terminology Interestingly, the Reserve Bank of India has already demonstrated that established financial terminology can be revisited when a change improves consistency with international market practice. When the Liquidity Adjustment Facility was introduced in June 2000, the RBI initially defined Repo and Reverse Repo from its own balance-sheet perspective. Under that terminology, Repo was associated with liquidity absorption, while Reverse Repo was associated with liquidity injection. Commercial Banks were using term Ready Forward (Repo) from their prospective of liquidity. For borrower of funds it was Repo and same from lender of fund’s perspective, was a Reverse Repo. For clarity/transparency such perspective should be understood in the same manner by all concerned. This was contrary to the terminology conventionally followed in international financial markets, where the transactions were understood from the central bank’s perspective. In October 2004, the RBI changed the nomenclature to align it with international practice. Repo thereafter represented liquidity injection by the RBI, while Reverse Repo represented liquidity absorption. The change was subsequently incorporated into the RBI Act through the Reserve Bank of India (Amendment) Act, 2006, effective January 9, 2007. This was a significant example of a regulatory institution recognising that established terminology need not remain unchanged merely because the market has become accustomed to it. The comparison with fixed deposits is not exact. Repo transactions and depositor withdrawal rights are different financial concepts. However, the underlying principle is relevant: when established terminology creates an avoidable disconnect between domestic usage and accepted financial-market understanding, a regulator should be willing to examine whether the terminology deserves correction. I feel: If the RBI could realign Repo and Reverse Repo terminology with international convention, why should the same willingness to introspect not be applied to fixed-deposit terminology? What could be a more appropriate approach? The objective should not be to replace one unfamiliar technical expression with another and leave ordinary depositors equally confused. A simple, transparent description would be more useful. Banks could describe the two categories as: 1. Term Deposit with Premature-Withdrawal Facility (Puttable). 2. Term Deposit without Premature-Withdrawal Facility (Non-Puttable). The plain-language description should appear prominently, while the corresponding financial-market terminology can be mentioned in brackets for the benefit of informed investors and market participants. The same descriptions could be incorporated into deposit application forms, deposit receipts, product disclosures and online deposit-booking platforms etc. For example, a deposit without an early-withdrawal facility could carry a clear statement: “This deposit does not ordinarily permit premature withdrawal at the depositor’s request, except* under the circumstances specified in the applicable terms and conditions. Please assess your liquidity requirements before investing.” Similarly, a deposit carrying an early-withdrawal facility could clearly disclose that premature closure is subject to interest recalculation, applicable penalties and other contractual conditions. Such a change need not alter the existing deposit products, their interest rates, their maturity periods or the underlying contractual arrangements. It is essentially a question of describing the rights accurately and consistently and correct the situation before it gets deep rooted. During any transition, banks could retain the existing RBI terminology in brackets to avoid disruption and ensure continuity in regulatory reporting. The larger question RBI should ask itself At first glance, this may appear to be a minor linguistic/semantic issue concerning two expressions used in the banking industry. I believe it deserves a wider examination. India’s financial markets have developed considerably. Retail participation is increasing, financial literacy is receiving greater attention, and market participants are expected to understand products across banking, debt and money markets and their consistent usage of terms is important. In such an environment, consistency in financial terminology is not merely an academic preference. It is an important element of market education, transparency and investor understanding. A fixed income (bond) student/investor learning about callable and puttable bonds should not encounter an entirely different interpretation of the same concepts when studying bank deposits. Nor should a market participant familiar with international fixed-income conventions have to mentally reverse the meaning of an established financial expression while interpreting Indian banking products (as was being done in case of repo prior to year 2007). The RBI, as the country’s central banking and regulatory institution, has a particularly important role in ensuring that the terminology it prescribes is clear, consistent and aligned, wherever practicable, with accepted financial-market conventions. The question is not whether Indian banks have followed RBI’s prescribed terminology. Instead of taking terminology with RBI they chose and adopt. The more fundamental question is whether the prescribed terminology itself merits reconsideration. Summing up An interest rate tells a depositor what the money may earn. A premature-withdrawal clause tells the depositor when the money can be accessed. Both are important elements of a deposit contract and deserve equal clarity. In conventional financial-market terminology, the right to demand early repayment belongs to the investor and is understood as a put option. The right to redeem an instrument, if any available, early belongs to the issuer and is understood as a call option. Indian banking’s established use of callable and non-callable terminology for fixed deposits does not reflect this distinction in sync with the global financial conventional sense. Hence usage of term non-callable fixed deposits needs by banks and RBI needs reconsideration. This is an opportunity for the RBI to revisit an established convention, just as it did in the case of Repo and Reverse Repo, and consider a more transparent and internationally consistent approach. Financial markets evolve, products evolve and regulations evolve. Should the language used to describe them remain frozen merely because it has been in use for some time? Perhaps it is time for the RBI to give this seemingly small but meaningful issue the attention it deserves. It gives readers who are unfamiliar with bond-market terminology a little more background without interrupting the main article. Understanding Callable and Puttable Bonds 1. What is a Callable Bond or Debenture? A callable bond or debenture is a debt instrument that gives the issuer (the borrower) the right, but not the obligation, to redeem the instrument before its stated maturity, subject to the conditions specified at the time of issue. For example, a company issues a 10-year debenture carrying an annual interest rate of 9%, with a provision allowing it to redeem the debenture after five years. If market interest rates fall to 6% after five years, the company may find it beneficial to repay the existing debenture and raise fresh funds at the lower rate. By exercising its call option, the company can reduce its borrowing cost. The investor, however, loses the opportunity to continue earning the original 9% interest for the remaining five years. This creates what is known as reinvestment risk: the investor may have to reinvest the repaid amount at a lower prevailing interest rate. 2. What is a Puttable Bond or Debenture? A puttable bond or debenture gives the investor (the lender) the right to demand repayment before the instrument’s stated maturity, subject to the specified contractual conditions. For example, a company issues a 10-year debenture with an option allowing investors to demand repayment at the end of the fifth year. An investor who exercises this option receives repayment according to the issue terms. Alternatively, the investor may choose not to exercise the option and continue holding the debenture. Here as there is no scope to exercise call option, the company cannot reduce its borrowing cost and replay the bond/debenture. The important distinction is that the early-repayment right belongs to the investor, not the issuer. 3. Investor, as per contract Issuer, as per contract Typical economic motivation Issuer wants to refinance at a lower rate/Does not need borrowing etc Investor wants an early exit or protection against changing conditions 4. Can these options be exercised at any time? No. Relating to Bond/debentures neither a call nor a put option necessarily permits unrestricted early repayment. The issue terms specify the permitted exercise dates, redemption price, notice period and other applicable conditions. Both parties are bound by these contractual provisions. 5. How does this relate to bank fixed deposits? A conventional bank FD allowing premature withdrawal gives the depositor an early-exit facility, subject to the bank’s applicable conditions. Conceptually, this resembles an investor-held open put option. A deposit without a premature-withdrawal facility removes that option from the depositor, except for specified exceptions. Conceptually, it is non-puttable. It is important to recognise that an FD withdrawal facility is not identical to a formally embedded bond-market put option. The contractual conditions, interest recalculation, penalties, mandatory regulatory directions are applicable to fixed deposits and continue to govern the depositor’s rights. The underlying principle, however, remains the same: the party holding the right to initiate early repayment determines whether the option is a call or a put. In case of Bank FDs it is always Puttable or Non-Puttable. Issuer/Borrower of funds i.e bank has no right to call or pre-pay.

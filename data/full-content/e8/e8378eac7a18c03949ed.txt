(Supreme Court of India) Summary: The Supreme Court dismissed the Special Leave Petition filed by the Revenue in ACIT Vs Tungabhadra Minerals Pvt. Ltd., observing that there was a gross delay of 411 days in filing the petition, which had not been satisfactorily explained. The Court further held that, even otherwise, there was no good ground to interfere with the Bombay High Court’s impugned order. Consequently, the Special Leave Petition was dismissed on the grounds of both delay and merits, leaving the High Court’s decision undisturbed. The Supreme Court’s brief order did not independently examine the detailed reassessment issues or lay down separate reasons concerning Sections 147 and 148 of the Income Tax Act, 1961. The underlying dispute arose from reassessment proceedings initiated against iron ore mining and exporting concerns in Goa for various assessment years, including AYs 2009-10, 2010-11 and 2011-12. The Assessing Officers issued notices under Section 148 alleging escapement of income on account of under-invoicing of iron ore exports, commission payments to foreign agents, income arising from allegedly illegal mining operations and failure to disclose material facts. The Revenue principally relied upon the Justice M.B. Shah Commission Report, information received from the Directorate of Revenue Intelligence (DRI), Mumbai, and the Supreme Court’s observations concerning the expiry of mining leases in Goa. The assessees challenged the notices and the orders rejecting their objections before the Bombay High Court. The assessees contended that the reassessment proceedings were founded on borrowed information without independent application of mind by the Assessing Officers. They submitted that the DRI material concerned customs valuation and export-duty issues, whereas the Assessing Officers had failed to establish any rational connection between that information and escapement of taxable income. They further argued that the Shah Commission’s findings were merely expressions of opinion and could not independently justify reassessment. Regarding the alleged illegality of mining operations after November 2007, the assessees maintained that the Supreme Court’s relevant determination in Goa Foundation Vs Union of India was delivered only on 21 April 2014 and that they could not have disclosed a legal position that had not been established when the relevant returns were filed. The Bombay High Court accepted these submissions and held that reassessment cannot be sustained merely by reproducing information received from another government department or an inquiry commission without independently establishing a live link between the material and the alleged escapement of income. The Court observed that the Assessing Officers had substantially copied the DRI findings without explaining how the alleged customs-duty discrepancies affected the income originally disclosed. Similarly, the third report of the Justice M.B. Shah Commission lacked the finality and authoritativeness necessary to justify reopening in the absence of independent supporting material. Relying upon judicial precedents concerning the formation of a genuine reason to believe, the High Court concluded that the statutory jurisdictional requirements had not been satisfied. The High Court also examined reassessment notices issued beyond four years and reiterated the requirement to establish the assessee’s failure to disclose fully and truly all material facts, where the applicable statutory conditions required it. It held that an assessee could not be accused of withholding facts of which it had no knowledge at the relevant time. The Court further rejected the Revenue’s attempt to rely upon subsequent material concerning proceedings before the Singapore High Court, holding that recorded reassessment reasons could not subsequently be supplemented through affidavits or additional evidence. Accordingly, the Bombay High Court quashed the reassessment notices and the orders rejecting the assessees’ objections. The Supreme Court’s subsequent dismissal of the Revenue’s SLP left this relief intact, although the dismissal should not be described as an independent, detailed affirmation of every proposition in the High Court’s reasoning. The Bombay High Court examined the validity of reassessment proceedings initiated under Sections 147 and 148 of the Income Tax Act, 1961, in a group of petitions involving iron ore mining and export businesses in Goa. Reassessment Based on Borrowed Information The High Court held that information obtained from the Directorate of Revenue Intelligence (DRI), Mumbai, could not automatically justify reopening an income tax assessment. The Assessing Officer was required to independently examine the material and establish a rational connection between the information received and the alleged escapement of taxable income. The Court found that the Assessing Officers had substantially reproduced information received from the DRI without recording independent reasons explaining how the alleged under-invoicing of iron ore exports and commission payments to overseas agents resulted in escapement of income. Justice M.B. Shah Commission Report The High Court observed that the third report of the Justice M.B. Shah Commission contained expressions of opinion that lacked finality and authoritativeness. Such findings, without independent supporting material and application of mind by the Assessing Officer, could not constitute sufficient grounds for reopening completed assessments. Failure to Disclose Material Facts The Revenue alleged that the assessees had failed to disclose that mining leases in Goa had expired in November 2007 and that subsequent mining operations were illegal. The High Court noted that the Supreme Court’s decision in Goa Foundation Vs Union of India, concerning the expiry of mining leases, was delivered on 21 April 2014. The Court held that an assessee could not be treated as having failed to disclose facts that were not within its knowledge when the relevant income tax returns were filed. Additional Material Cannot Supplement Recorded Reasons The Revenue sought to rely upon subsequent information concerning proceedings before the Singapore High Court. However, the Bombay High Court held that the validity of reassessment proceedings must be determined on the basis of the reasons originally recorded by the Assessing Officer. Subsequent affidavits, explanations or additional evidence could not be used to supplement or substitute the reasons recorded when the reassessment notices were issued. Final Decision of Bombay High Court The Bombay High Court concluded that the Assessing Officers had failed to satisfy the statutory requirements for reopening the completed assessments. The Court accordingly allowed the writ petitions and quashed the reassessment notices and the orders rejecting the assessees’ objections. Effect of Supreme Court Order The Supreme Court dismissed the Revenue’s Special Leave Petition on account of an unexplained delay of 411 days and also found no good ground to interfere with the Bombay High Court’s order. Consequently, the Bombay High Court’s decision quashing the reassessment proceedings remained undisturbed. The Supreme Court’s short dismissal order did not separately elaborate upon the legal principles discussed by the High Court.

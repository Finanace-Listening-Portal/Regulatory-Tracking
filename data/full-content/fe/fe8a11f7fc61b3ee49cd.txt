Summary: Corporate gifting during Diwali raises important questions concerning the applicability of output GST and eligibility for input tax credit under the CGST Act, 2017. The tax treatment depends on whether the recipient is an employee, client, dealer or vendor, whether the transfer constitutes a supply under Section 7 and Schedule I, and whether the procurement credit is restricted under Section 17(5)(h). Gifts given by employers to employees not exceeding ₹50,000 in a financial year are specifically excluded from the scope of supply under the proviso to Schedule I, Entry 2, although ITC on goods disposed of as gifts is generally blocked. Where the prescribed threshold is exceeded, the implications of deemed supply, valuation under Rule 28, output GST and corresponding ITC eligibility require examination. A separate distinction arises between voluntary festive gifts and contractual employee perquisites, with CBIC Circular No. 172/04/2022-GST clarifying the GST treatment of benefits provided under employment agreements. Gratuitous gifts distributed to unrelated clients, customers and vendors generally do not attract output GST but may result in blocked input credit. Conversely, sales-linked dealer incentives and promotional schemes require examination of their contractual obligations, commercial consideration and supporting evidence. Advance rulings involving Biostadt India Limited, Surfa Coats (India) Pvt. Ltd. and Orient Cement Limited illustrate the different approaches to gifts and business promotion incentives. The article also examines whether the ₹50,000 employee-gift threshold operates as an exemption limit or an incremental deduction, the documentation required to distinguish gifts from promotional expenditure, and the importance of correct reporting in GSTR-3B. Recommendations made at the 57th GST Council Meeting on 8 October 2026 concerning ITC on free samples are also considered, while recognising that recommendations must be distinguished from enacted statutory amendments. Beyond the Hamper: Unraveling Corporate Diwali Gifting under GST In the Indian corporate ecosystem, the festive season anchored by Diwali witnesses extensive corporate gifting to employees, business associates, clients, distributors, and vendors. While intended to cultivate commercial goodwill and employee motivation, these gratuitous distributions trigger intricate indirect tax implications under the Goods and Services Tax (GST) regime. The primary tax issues center around two fundamental questions under the Central Goods and Services Tax Act, 2017 (CGST Act): first, whether the distribution constitutes an outward “supply” under Section 7that attracts output GST; and second, whether the tax paid on procurement can be claimed as Input Tax Credit (ITC) under Section 16(1)or is barred under Section 17(5)(h). This is because the term “gift” is not defined in the CGST Act, courts and tax authorities rely on general legal principles treating a gift as a voluntary transfer of property made without consideration or contractual obligation. Under the GST laws, an employer and employee are deemed “related persons” by virtue of Explanation (i) to Section 15 of the CGST Act 2017. Consequently, any transaction between them without consideration normally attracts the deeming fiction of Section 7(1)(c) read with Schedule I (Entry 2), which treats transfers between related persons in the course of business as taxable supplies. However, the legislature carved out an explicit safe harbor via the proviso to Entry 2 of Schedule I: Statutory Provision — Proviso to Schedule I, Entry 2 of the CGST Act: “Provided that gifts not exceeding fifty thousand rupees in value in a financial year by an employer to an employee shall not be treated as supply of goods or services or both.” When voluntary, occasional gifts to an employee remain upto ₹50,000 in a financial year, the distribution is not treated as a supply. Therefore, no output GST is payable by the employer. However, input credit is blocked as it is governed by Section 17(5)(h): Statutory Provision — Section 17(5)(h) of the CGST Act 2017: “Notwithstanding anything contained in sub-section (1) of section 16 and sub-section (1) of section 18, input tax credit shall not be available in respect of: … (h) goods lost, stolen, destroyed, written off or disposed of by way of gift or free samples.” Because the gifts are disposed of without output tax, the inward tax paid on purchasing them is strictly blocked under Section 17(5)(h). As a result, during procurement GST becomes an unrecoverable operational cost for the employer. Illustration 1 (Gifts Within ₹50,000): An IT company purchases Diwali hampers for its staff at ₹8,000 plus ₹1,440 GST (18%) each. The total gifts given to each employee during the financial year remain well below the ₹50,000 ceiling. Output GST: Nil. The distribution is exempt from supply status under the proviso to Schedule I, Entry 2. Input Tax Credit: Blocked under Section 17(5)(h). The ₹1,440 GST paid per hamper must be absorbed as an overhead expense. Situation 2. Employee Gifting Exceeding ₹50,000 in a Financial Year 3. When voluntary gifts to an employee exceed ₹50,000 in a financial year, the protection of the safe-harbor proviso ends. The distribution reverts to the operative rule of Schedule I, Entry 2, qualifying as a deemed taxable supply without consideration between related persons. In this situation, the employer must discharge output GST on the transaction. The taxable value is determined based on the Open Market Value (OMV) under Rule 28 of the CGST Rules, 2017. Because the distribution is now treated as an outward taxable supply, the statutory block under Section 17(5)(h) does not apply, and ITC on the inward procurement becomes available under Section 16(1), provided the goods are not blocked under other clauses such as Section 17(5)(b). Illustration 2 (Gifts Breaching ₹50,000): An enterprise gifts a premium electronics package valued at ₹65,000 plus ₹11,700 GST (18%) to a senior executive during Diwali. Output GST: Payable by the employer on the OMV as a deemed supply under Schedule I, Entry 2. • Input Tax Credit: Eligible under Section 16(1). Because the gift is treated as a taxable supply, the inward GST of ₹11,700 can be claimed as ITC to set off against output tax liability. 3. Validity of Charging GST on the Full Value vs. Incremental Excess A key debate in corporate taxation is whether crossing ₹50,000 triggers output GST on the entire amount(e.g., ₹65,000) or only on the excess amount above ₹50,000(e.g., ₹15,000). Legal Position Statutory Interpretation Departmental vs. Assessee Stance Tax on Full Amount (Revenue Position) The proviso reads: ‘gifts not exceeding fifty thousand rupees… shall not be treated as supply.’ This is a conditional threshold, not a slab deduction. Once the aggregate exceeds ₹50,000, the statutory immunity ceases entirely for the whole amount. Revenue View / Low Audit Risk: Field formations and audit teams routinely demand GST on the entire value once the threshold is crossed. Tax on Incremental Excess (Assessee Contention) Assessees argue by analogy to basic exemptions (such as Section 56(2)(x) under Income Tax) that the legislative intent was to grant relief up to ₹50,000, taxing only the incremental surplus. High Litigation Risk: The statute omits qualifying terms like ‘to the extent of’ or ‘on the excess’. Under strict interpretation principles (Dilip Kumar & Co. [SC]), this stance is prone to litigation. 4. Contractual Perquisites vs.Discretionary Gifts:CBIC Circular No.172/04/2022-GST In order to address widespread confusion regarding employee benefits, the Central Board of Indirect Taxes and Customs (CBIC) issued Circular No. 172/04/2022-GST (dated July 6, 2022). The Circular clarified that the interaction between employment is a perquisites read with Schedule III: CBIC Circular No. 172/04/2022-GST, Paragraphs 5.1 & 5.2: “Schedule III to the CGST Act provides that ‘services by employee to the employer in the course of or in relation to his employment’ will not be considered as supply of goods or services and hence GST is not applicable on services rendered by employee to employer pursuant to employment agreement entered into between them.” “It is pertinent to mention here that perquisites provided by the employer to the employee in terms of contractual agreement entered into between the employer and the employee, will not be subjected to GST when the same are provided in terms of the contract between employer and employee.” This distinction is extremely crucial. If a festive benefit or bonus is an enforceable contractual right in the employment agreement / CTC, it is treated as employment compensation under Schedule III (Entry 1) and falls outside the GST net entirely, irrespective of value. In contrast, purely voluntary, discretionary Diwali gifts remain governed by Schedule I and are subject to tax on the full value once the ₹50,000 threshold is breached. 5. Gifts to Clients, Customers, Dealers, and Vendors In business it is general practice that Diwali gifts are frequently sent to external third parties such as key clients, distributors, and vendors. Because these are independent third parties and not related persons, Schedule I does not apply. Furthermore, because these gifts are voluntary and made without consideration, they do not qualify as a “supply” under Section 7(1)(a). Consequently, no output GST is payable. However, input tax credit on such procurement is strictly blocked under Section 17(5)(h). Various Advance Rulings have firmly affirmed this position: M/s. Biostadt India Limited (Maharashtra AAR, 2019): The Authority ruled that gold coins and gifts distributed to channel partners under customer schemes without consideration were items disposed of as gifts; therefore, ITC was blocked under Section 17(5)(h). M/s. Surfa Coats (India) Pvt. Ltd. (Karnataka AAR, 2020): The AAR confirmed that consumer electronics and appliances distributed to dealers under marketing schemes without separate consideration were gifts, and ITC was rightly denied under Section 17(5)(h). Illustration 3 (Client Gifting): A manufacturing company distributes premium confectionery hampers costing ₹5,000 plus ₹900 GST (18%) each to 50 key commercial clients. Output GST: Nil. The distribution is not a supply under Section 7. Input Tax Credit: Blocked under Section 17(5)(h). The total GST paid of ₹45,000 cannot be claimed as ITC and must be absorbed as a business entertainment cost. 6. Promotional Items and Sales Schemes vs. Gratuitous Gifts It is absolutely vital to distinguish between a gratuitous gift and sales promotion or marketing expenditure. Items given to promote business visibility such as branded diaries, pens, calendars, and marketing merchandise are business development expenses incurred in the course or furtherance of business under Section 16(1). Similarly, when festival rewards (e.g., gold coins or white goods) are given to dealers for achieving specific sales targets, the distribution is not a gratuitous gift. The dealer’s sales performance serves as enforceable contractual consideration. This distinction was recognized in recent jurisprudence: M/s. Orient Cement Ltd. (Telangana AAR, Order No. 20/2023):The Authority held that gold coins and household items distributed to dealers upon achieving agreed sales targets under promotional schemes did not constitute ‘gifts’. Because there was mutual commercial consideration, Section 17(5)(h) did not apply, and ITC was eligible under Section 16(1). Illustration 4 (Target-Linked Dealer Scheme vs. Gift): A company promises a 10-gram gold coin to any distributor who achieves a 25% sales growth during the pre-Diwali quarter. Characterization: This is aTarget-linked commercial incentive, not a gratuitous gift. GST Treatment: Following the Orient Cement ruling, ITC is available under Section 16(1), and the restriction under Section 17(5)(h) does not apply. 7. Latest Position on Blocked Credit & The 57th GST Council Meeting The Tax discussions frequently refer to reforms recommended in the 57th GST Council Meetingregarding blocked credits under Section 17(5)(h). It is vital to clarify the exact scope of the recommendations with reference to this reform: a) Focus on Free Samples, Not Corporate Gifts: The GST Council’s recommendation to ease credit restrictions under Section 17(5)(h) is specifically aimed at legitimate business distribution of free samples(such as pharmaceutical samples to doctors or FMCG trial packs to prospective consumers). b) No General Relaxation for Gifts: The Council has not extended this relief to voluntary festival gifts or corporate hampers. The statutory restriction on ‘gifts’ under Section 17(5)(h) remains fully operative. c) Distinction Remains Vital: Distributing a company’s own manufactured products as genuine test samples to drive future orders is legally distinct from gifting festive hampers or consumer goods. Gifting third-party sweets, dry fruits, or electronics continues to face mandatory ITC blockage. 8. Practical Compliance and Audit Preparedness (In Simple Terms) 9.To avoid tax disputes, penalties, and audit notices during festive gifting, taxpayers shall implement four straightforward, practical touch points so that they are in line with legislative obligations: a) Track Gift Values per Employee: Keep a simple, centralized check of all gifts given to each employee across the financial year. If an employee’s cumulative gifts stay at or below ₹50,000, no GST is payable. If the total crosses ₹50,000, it has to be flagged immediately to charge output GST on the deemed supply and claim the eligible input credit. b) Separate Gift Bills from General Office Purchases: The vendors may be advised to bill corporate gifts and festival hampers on separate, dedicated invoices rather than bundling them with regular office supplies or IT equipment. This prevents blocked tax credits from accidentally mixing with routine business credits. c) Put Dealer and Vendor Incentive Schemes in Writing: If the taxpayer is offerig festival rewards (like gold coins, appliances, or travel vouchers) to dealers or vendors, they have to be linked clearly to measurable targets (such as sales quotas or early order bookings). Documentation of the scheme terms before the festive period starts is critical. When backed by targets, the rewards qualify as business promotion incentives with valid credit rather than blocked gifts. d) Accurately Report Blocked Tax in Monthly Returns: When filing monthly GST returns (Form GSTR-3B), it has to be ensured that the GST paid on gifts to clients and sub-₹50,000 employee gifts is correctly declared as ineligible credit in Table 4(B)(1). Claiming credit on these items and failing to reverse it is one of the easiest red flags for automated audit notices. 9. Before bidding adieu……. 10. Every corporate Diwali gifting reflects genuine commercial goodwill and cultural tradition, yet it requires careful navigation of the GST legal framework. The indirect tax implications depend on three clear factors: (i) nature of the recipient, (ii) statutory monetary threshold, and (iii) underlying contractual intent. 11.For employee gifting, the law provides a clear dividing line. Gratuitous festival gifts up to ₹50,000 per financial year remain safe from output tax under the proviso to Schedule I, Entry 2, with the inward procurement GST absorbed as an unrecoverable business expense under Section 17(5)(h). When that ceiling is breached, the full value becomes a deemed outward supply under Rule 28, balanced by the ability to claim inward credit under Section 16(1). Meanwhile, contractual perquisites outlined in employment contracts remain shielded from GST under Schedule III and CBIC Circular No. 172/04/2022-GST. For corporate clients, vendors, and channel partners, voluntary gifts without consideration attract no outward GST, but input tax credit remains permanently blocked. However, as recognized in modern advance rulings like Orient Cement, where festive distributions are tied to measurable commercial performance, they qualify as legitimate sales incentives eligible for full credit. 12. Finally, the discussions from the 57th GST Council Meeting emphasize that while relief is proposed for promotional free samples, the statutory bar on corporate gifts under Section 17(5)(h) remains firmly intact. Taxpayers that maintain disciplined accounting records, separate promotional incentives from personal gifts, and implement simple tracking protocols can celebrate the festive season with complete tax certainty, without ambiguity.

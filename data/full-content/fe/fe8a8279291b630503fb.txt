Commissioner of Customs, CUSAA 132/2022, decided on 6 November 2026, examined whether a penalty of ₹10 lakh imposed under Section 112(b)(i) of the Customs Act, 1962, upon a Customs House Agent (CHA) was legally sustainable. The proceedings arose from the import of two containers under Bill of Lading No. OOLU2023101170 dated 4 August 2016, declared to contain bicycle parts. During examination by the Directorate of Revenue Intelligence (DRI) at Inland Container Depot, Tughlakabad, on 5 September 2016, Chinese-made firecrackers were allegedly discovered concealed behind a small quantity of bicycle parts and pumps. The appellant, proprietor of M/s Krishna Clearing & Forwarding Agency, Ludhiana, had acted as CHA for M/s Rahul Traders and M/s Kirat Sales. The Department alleged that he knew about the misdeclaration, arranged meetings with Delhi-based customs agents and facilitated misuse of the KYC documents of M/s Kirat Sales. Based on statements recorded during investigation and shipping-line correspondence, the adjudicating authority imposed a penalty of ₹10 lakh, although it specifically found that the appellant’s role in the actual import of the seized consignment had not been proved. The appellant argued that he neither filed the Bill of Entry nor handled clearance of the consignment at ICD Tughlakabad and that the goods had been seized before filing of the Bill of Entry. He challenged the penalty as being founded on third-party statements without permitting cross-examination of Ajay Sagar, Purshottam and Jaskirat Singh. He also relied upon earlier CESTAT orders dated 11 February 2020 and 17 August 2021, whereby penalties imposed on co-noticees Saurabh Aggarwal and Rahul Sehgal had been set aside. The Department, however, maintained that the appellant’s own statement dated 13 October 2016, statements of the Delhi-based CHAs and correspondence of the shipping line established his knowledge of the proposed import, his role in arranging meetings and his facilitation of the use of M/s Kirat Sales’ KYC documents. It contended that non-filing of the Bill of Entry did not absolve him and that the evidence had to be examined collectively. On examining the adjudication record, the High Court found that there was no independent evidence establishing the appellant’s involvement in the alleged improper importation. The adjudicating authority had itself concluded that his role in importing the seized consignment was not proved and had imposed the penalty principally on the basis of alleged knowledge of misdeclaration, arranging a meeting and the appearance of his office telephone number in the contact details of M/s Rahul Traders. The allegation that M/s Rahul Traders was the actual importer also lost its foundation when CESTAT, by order dated 17 August 2021, set aside the penalty imposed on Rahul Sehgal. Further, the persons with whom the appellant allegedly arranged the meeting were not proceeded against for any role in the importation. The Court considered that the penalty against another co-noticee, Saurabh Aggarwal, had also been quashed by CESTAT on 11 February 2020, in the absence of evidence beyond the appellant’s statement. In these circumstances, the Department could not sustain a different treatment of the appellant without independent supporting material. The High Court also found a material factual error in the CESTAT order concerning cross-examination. The appellant had specifically requested cross-examination of Ajay Sagar, Purshottam and Jaskirat Singh, whose statements were relied upon against him, but the adjudicating authority rejected the request on the grounds that it would serve no useful purpose and cause delay. CESTAT proceeded on the incorrect premise that no such request had been made. The Court clarified that cross-examination is not an absolute right, but it falls within the principles of natural justice and is consonant with Section 138B of the Customs Act, 1962. Considering the absence of independent evidence, the setting aside of penalties against the co-noticees and the denial of the requested cross-examination, the Court answered the substantial question of law in favour of the appellant. It accordingly set aside both the impugned CESTAT order dated 4 May 2022 and the penalty order, and allowed the appeal. FULL TEXT OF THE JUDGMENT/ORDER OF DELHI HIGH COURT 1. The present appeal is filed under Section 130 of the Customs Act, 1962 (for short ‘the Act’) against the order dated 04.05.2022 passed by the Customs, Excise and Service Tax Appellate Tribunal (CESTAT). 2. The facts in brief are that the appellant is the proprietor of M/s Krishna Clearing & Forwarding Agency at Ludhiana. The appellant was operating as a Customs House Agent (CHA) for M/s Rahul Traders and M/s Kirat Sales, a concern of Rahul Sehgal and Jaskirat Singh respectively. 2.1 The litigation originates from the import of two containers under Bill of Lading No. OOLU2023101170 dated 04.08.2016. The consignment was declared to contain bicycle parts. On 05.09.2016, the Directorate of Revenue Intelligence (DRI) examined the containers at the Inland Container Depot (ICD), Tughlakabad, New Delhi, Chinese-made firecrackers (hereinafter ‘firecrackers’) concealed behind a small quantity of bicycle parts and pumps were allegedly found and the goods were seized. On 06.09.2016, the DRI officers searched the office premises of the appellant at Ludhiana and seized documents, a mobile phone and a laptop. 2.2 During the investigation, the statement of appellant, Manish Srivastava, Deputy Manager (Imports), Orient Overseas Line (hereinafter referred to as ‘Shipping Line’) India Pvt. Ltd. and Ajay Sagar and Purshottam, CHA based in Delhi, were recorded. The Department relied on these statements to hold that the appellant knew about the misdeclared goods, arranged meetings with Delhi-based CHA and facilitated the import by misusing the IEC/KYC documents of M/s Kirat Sales. The show cause notice (SCN) dated 02.03.2017 was issued to the appellant. 2.3 The Additional Commissioner of Customs, ICD Tughlakabad (Import), New Delhi, passed the Order-in-Original (OIO) dated 28.02.2018, holding that the import of prohibited goods by misusing the KYC documents of M/s Kirat Sales had been facilitated by the appellant. A penalty of Rs.10,00,000/- under Section 112 (b)(i) of the Act was imposed on the appellant. 2.4 The appellant challenged the imposition of penalty and the CESTAT by the impugned order dated 04.05.2022, dismissed the appeal. Hence, the present appeal. 3. The substantial question of law framed by this Court on 24.08.2023 is as under: “a) Whether the appellant was rightly and justifiably penalized under Section 112(b)(i) of the Customs Act, 1962.” 4. Learned counsel for the appellant submits that the appellant was working as CHA at Ludhiana and not at Delhi, neither the appellant filed the Bill of Entry (BOE) nor handled clearance of the consignments at ICD, Tughlakabad. It is contended that there is no evidence to establish the involvement of the appellant in the import of the misdeclared goods. 4.1 It is contended that the penalty of Rs.10 lakh was imposed on the basis of third-party statements, without granting an opportunity of cross-examination. The rejection of the request for cross-examination of Ajay Sagar, Purshottam and Jaskirat Singh is violative of the principles of natural justice. 4.2 Learned counsel relies on the orders of the CESTAT dated 11.02.2020 and 17.08.2021 setting aside the penalties imposed on co-noticees Saurabh Aggarwal and Rahul Sehgal, respectively and prays that the impugned order dated 04.05.2022 be set aside. 5. Per Contra the appellant was the CHA for M/s Rahul Traders and M/s Kirat Sales. The statement of the appellant dated 13.10.2016, the statements of the Delhi-based CHA and the correspondence of shipping-line establish three things, that the appellant knew of the proposed import; arranged a meeting with the Delhi-based CHA; and facilitated the use of M/s Kirat Sales’ KYC documents. 5.1 The contention is that non-filing of BOE does not absolve the appellant and the liability must be judged on the entire evidence. 5.2 The argument is that the cross-examination was rightly refused to avoid delay. The statements relied upon are corroborated by statement of the appellant and the shipping-line correspondence and together the evidence establishes the involvement of the appellant. ANALYSIS 6. The proceedings commenced with drawing of a panchnama on 05/06.09.2016 at ICD, Tughlakabad where the customs authorities found the firecrackers concealed behind bicycle parts and pumps. The goods were seized prior to the custom gate and before filing of the BOE. The consignment was covered by the Bill of Landing no.OOLU2023101170 dated 04.08.2016 in the name of M/s Kirat Sales. The SCN alleged that Rahul Sehgal, proprietor of M/s Rahul Traders in connivance with Saurabh Aggarwal and the appellant planned to import firecrackers in the guise of freely importable goods i.e. bicycle parts. The adjudicating authority (hereinafter ‘AA’) relied upon the deposition of Deputy Manager (Imports), Shipping Line who stated that the consignment was initially booked in the name of M/s Rahul Traders and later the name of the consignee was changed to M/s Kirat Sales. Further that in second week of August, 2016 a phone call made on landline number was to the appellant who informed to be the CHA of M/s Rahul Traders. The material collected during the search of the premises of Rahul Sehgal coupled with failure to appear during investigation was treated as substantial evidence that Rahul Sehgal was the importer and by mis-declaration attempted to get the firecrackers cleared from ICD. The goods were ordered to be confiscated and a penalty of Rs.41,16,424/- under Section 112(b)(i) of the Act was levied on Rahul Sehgal. 7. The AA imposed a penalty of Rs.5 lakhs on Saurabh Aggarwal under Section 112(b)(i) of the Act. The AA noted that Saurabh Aggarwal was named by the appellant. The statement of Purshottam disclosed that Purshottam and Ajay Sagar met Rahul Sehgal along with the appellant and two other persons in a restaurant in Ambala in January, 2016. During the meeting a proposal was made to the accompanying persons to clear the consignment by mis-declaration but it was not accepted. The AA inferred that Saurabh Aggarwal failed to join the investigation despite repeated summons and this indicates active participation in the unlawful import of the consignment. 8. Jaskirat Singh gave the statement that the KYC documents of M/s Kirat Sales were given to the appellant who was working as a CHA for both M/s Kirat Sales and M/s Rahul Traders. Further that the KYC documents of M/s Kirat Sales were handed over by the appellant to M/s Rahul Traders for misuse. The investigation against Jaskirat Singh was dropped. 9. The appellant was penalised for Rs.10 lakhs under Section 112(b)(i) of the Act. After considering the evidence on record, the AA held that role of the appellant in the import of the seized consignment was not proved but he had the knowledge of mis-declaration of goods and has arranged the meeting between Rahul Sehgal, Saurabh Aggarwal and the two CHAs, namely, Ajay Sagar and Purshottam, based in Delhi. Further that contact number of the office of the appellant was mentioned in the contact details of M/s Rahul Traders as given in correspondence through e-mail provided by the shipping line. M/s Rahul Traders misused the KYC documents of M/s Kirat Sales. 10. The request of the appellant for cross-examination of Ajay Sagar, Purshottam and Jaskirat Singh whose statement were used against the appellant was denied holding that the respective depositions clearly disclose the roles of Saurabh Aggarwal, the appellant and Jaskirat Singh. Further that the main noticee-Rahul Sehgal deliberately failed to join the investigation. No purpose would be served by cross-examination except causing delay moreover, Rahul Sehgal has been held to be the importer of the seized consignment. 11. The CESTAT while dismissing the appeal noted that the penalties imposed on the other persons allegedly involved in the import of firecrackers by mis-declaration were set aside and the proceedings against Jaskirat Singh were dropped during adjudication. 12. The AA held albeit, the role of the appellant in import of the seized consignment was not proved but penalty was leviable for: (i) the appellant having knowledge of mis-declared goods and (ii) for arranging meeting with Rahul Sehgal, Ajay Sagar and Purshottam and two other persons. 13. There was no evidence against the appellant except the statements of the co-noticees. The persons with whom the appellant had arranged the meeting were not proceeded against. In other words no role was attributed to them in the import of the consignment. The knowledge of misdeclaration to the appellant was inferred from the statement of Jaskirat Singh and the fact that in the contact details of M/s Rahul Traders the phone number of the appellant was mentioned. The case set up by the Department that M/s Rahul Traders was the actual importer fell flat when Rahul Sehgal succeeded before the CESTAT whereby vide order dated 17.08.2021 the penalty was quashed. The foundation of the allegations against the appellant for proceeding under Section 112(i)(b) of the Act were eroded by quashing of penalty against Rahul Sehgal. 14. Another aspect is that the appellant made a specific request for cross-examination of Ajay Sagar, Purshottam and Jaskirat Singh whose statements were being relied upon against the appellant. The request was denied stating that no useful purpose would be served and the matter would be delayed and in view of the fact that Rahul Sehgal was held to be the importer of the seized consignment. However, the CESTAT proceeded on a wrong factual premise that the appellant had not sought cross-examination of these three persons. 15. There cannot be quarrel with the proposition that the cross-examination is not an absolute right yet falls withing the ambit of principle of natural justice and is in consonance with Section 138(B) of the Act. 16. Another aspect is that the penalty against another co-noticee Saurabh Agarwal was also quashed by the CESTAT vide order dated 11.02.2020 by holding that apart from the statement of the appellant, there was no evidence against Saurabh Agarwal. The penalty imposed on the appellant was upheld despite the fact that the goods were seized before filing of the BOE and no documentary evidence directly indicating the role of the appellant in import of the goods came in the possession of the Department. In absence of independent evidence against the appellant the different treatment of the appellant from co-noticees cannot be sustained. 17. In view of the above discussions, the substantial question of law is answered in favour of the appellant and the impugned order and the penalty order are set aside. 18. The appeal is allowed.

ceiling of the RBI’s tolerance band, strengthening the case for further tightening. The central bank delivered its first interest rate hike in nearly four years last month. The combination of rising prices and borrowing costs is “a double whammy for India,” said Apoorva Javadekar, head of research at Shriram Research Pvt Ltd. “It is going to impact consumption, especially in rural areas, as agricultural prospects are worsening and inflation there is higher than in urban areas.”Oil BillDuring the previous tightening cycle in 2024-25, cheaper oil and easing food prices gave policymakers more room to maneuver. This time, its ability to cushion the economy is more constrained. The Middle East conflict has kept crude elevated, swelling India’s import bill and adding to the government’s subsidy burden. Figures Friday showed India’s trade deficit widened to a one-year high in October. Food is another source of strain, with severe El Niño conditions threatening global harvests while deficient rains have hurt crops at home. Javadekar expects the strain on public finances to persist and price pressures to prove more stubborn than previously expected. “We are not out of the woods yet,” he added. Aditi Nayar, an economist at ratings agency ICRA Ltd. expects inflation “to remain above the 6% upper bound of the RBI’s tolerance band over the next couple of months,” making a December rate hike “almost certain,” she said. Global monetary policy could add another complication. Further Federal Reserve tightening would narrow the yield advantage of Indian assets, potentially accelerating capital outflows and weakening the rupee. A return to rates above 6% could also create a political challenge for Prime Minister Narendra Modi’s government. Public discontent has become more visible this year, with protests at their highest in a decade, according to ACLED, a nonprofit that tracks political violence and protests globally. Tens of thousands joined a rally led by India’s main opposition parties in New Delhi earlier this month to protest a contentious overhaul of voter rolls, with some demonstrators also voicing frustration over inflation and unemployment. Higher rates risk adding to that economic unease by squeezing household demand. “The intended and unintended consequences of tighter macro-financial conditions will be demand destruction, particularly as supply-side pressures that are beyond the RBI’s control, continue to play spoilsport,” said Madhavi Arora, an economist at Emkay Global Financial Services. A few things are working in the RBI’s favor for now. Gross domestic product expanded 7.8% in the April-June quarter and private consumption rose 7.1%. With full-year growth on track to exceed 7%, economists say the central bank has room to sacrifice some demand to bring prices under control. RBI policymakers have signaled they are prepared to do so. High inflation is a “tax on everyone,” Governor Sanjay Malhotra said last month. Besides, foreign investors are returning to Indian assets after pulling out more than $30 billion this year through October. The rupee has also recovered after hitting a record low near 97 per dollar last month, helped by measures from the central bank. Still, net outflows remain nearly $23 billion, compared with less than $1 billion in 2024. And the rupee remains vulnerable to swings in crude prices and global capital flows. “I have my sympathies with what the central bank is doing because when you have both an oil and a food shock together, then the spillover impact can be more meaningful and large,” said Pranjul Bhandari, chief India economist at HSBC Holdings Plc. Bhandari expects an outsized 50-basis-point hike at the RBI’s next meeting in December.

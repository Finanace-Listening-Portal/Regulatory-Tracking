Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); Royal Enfield Vs Commissioner of GST and Central Excise (CESTAT Chennai) Summary: The Customs, Excise and Service Tax Appellate Tribunal (CESTAT), Chennai, in Royal Enfield Vs Commissioner of GST and Central Excise, allowed three appeals challenging service tax demands raised under the reverse charge mechanism on payments connected with overseas advertising, trade mark renewals, transportation, foreign exhibitions and other activities. The Division Bench comprising Judicial Member Ajayan T.V. and Technical Member Vasa Seshagiri Rao held that foreign remittances cannot automatically be treated as consideration for taxable services received in India. The Tribunal found that the show cause notices failed to identify the relevant taxable service classifications under Section 65(105) of the Finance Act, 1994, and that the adjudicating authorities had impermissibly introduced their own classifications while confirming the demands. The appellant, a motorcycle manufacturer selling its products in India and abroad, had incurred expenditure relating to promotional and other activities undertaken in foreign markets. An internal audit covering April 2006 to December 2008 identified payments made in convertible foreign currency towards advertisements, renewal of brand names, transportation and arrangements for overseas exhibitions. The audit also identified an alleged short payment of service tax of Rs.1,02,058 on consulting engineering services due to exclusion of certain expenses from taxable value. The Department issued five show cause notices covering different periods between April 2006 and March 2012. The first notice dated 19 March 2010 proposed service tax of Rs.16,67,332 on foreign remittances aggregating Rs.1,35,15,349, besides Rs.1,02,058 relating to consulting engineering services. The subsequent notices proposed additional demands of Rs.38,725, Rs.85,857, Rs.48,709 and Rs.3,20,472. The adjudicating authorities classified the disputed transactions as business auxiliary services or consulting engineering services and confirmed the demands with applicable interest and penalties. The Commissioner (Appeals) upheld the orders, reasoning that the appellant had paid for the services, benefited from them and used them for promoting products manufactured in India. The appellate authority also relied on Circular No.111/05/2009 dated 24 February 2009 and judicial decisions concerning imported services. Royal Enfield challenged these findings, contending that the foreign dealers independently purchased and resold motorcycles on a principal-to-principal basis, undertook advertising and promotional activities in their own markets, and received only partial reimbursement of expenditure from the manufacturer. The Tribunal first examined the validity of the show cause notices. It observed that Section 66A did not impose tax merely because money was remitted abroad. Taxability depended upon identifying a service specified under Section 65(105), determining its correct classification under Section 65A and applying the relevant conditions under Rule 3 of the Taxation of Services (Provided from Outside India and Received in India) Rules, 2006. Different categories of services were subject to different territorial tests, including the location of immovable property, the place of performance and the receipt of services for business or commerce. Consequently, identification of the taxable service was essential to determine whether a transaction attracted service tax in India. None of the notices specified the relevant sub-clause of Section 65(105), the applicable clause of Section 65(19) or the particular limb of Rule 3. Relying on the Supreme Court decisions in CCE Vs Brindavan Beverages Pvt. Ltd. and Commissioner of Service Tax Vs 3i Infotech Ltd., the Tribunal held that a show cause notice forms the foundation of adjudication and that the Department cannot confirm a demand on a classification not alleged in the notice. The defect was particularly significant because services such as foreign exhibitions, technical testing, inspection and transportation were governed by specific provisions of the Import Rules, some of which required performance in India. The adjudicating authorities could not bypass those requirements by treating all expenditure as business auxiliary services. The Tribunal therefore held that the demands falling within the normal limitation period were unsustainable for want of valid notices. Independently examining the nature of the transactions, the Tribunal distinguished between the person who pays towards an expense and the person who actually receives a service. The evidence included a letter dated 5 May 2008 under which Royal Enfield agreed to share 50% of a foreign dealer’s advertising expenditure, subject to a ceiling of USD 100 per motorcycle sold and production of supporting invoices. The advertising agencies contracted with, invoiced and received payment from the dealer. Royal Enfield merely contributed towards the dealer’s expenditure. The Tribunal held that sharing promotional expenditure incurred by an independent foreign dealer did not make the Indian manufacturer the recipient of an advertising service. Since the dealer promoted motorcycles that it had already purchased and owned, the arrangement also did not constitute promotion of goods belonging to a client within the meaning of business auxiliary service. The Tribunal further held that trade mark registration and renewal fees paid to foreign registries represented statutory levies arising from sovereign functions rather than consideration for taxable services. Similar reasoning applied to payments made to foreign authorities for vehicle approvals and safety-standard reviews. Purchases of automobile magazines and reprints were not shown to involve taxable services. Transportation, exhibitions and inspection or certification activities performed wholly abroad were also examined under the applicable territorial provisions of the Import Rules. The Tribunal clarified that Section 66A required actual receipt of a taxable service in India and that the location of the payer or the ultimate commercial benefit could not, by itself, establish such receipt. Services contracted, performed and consumed abroad in connection with goods already sold to foreign dealers could not be taxed merely because the manufacturer contributing towards their cost was situated in India. On limitation, the Tribunal rejected the Department’s invocation of the extended period under the proviso to Section 73(1). Royal Enfield was registered under service tax, regularly filed returns, maintained books of account and discharged reverse charge tax on other imported services. The disputed remittances were recorded in its financial records and were discovered from documents available during audit. The Tribunal held that mere detection during audit or non-payment arising from a disputed interpretation of law did not establish wilful suppression or an intention to evade tax. It also considered the revenue-neutral character of the transactions, since tax paid on eligible imported services could have been available as CENVAT credit. Accordingly, the demands relating to April 2006 to September 2008 were held to be time-barred. Regarding the separate consulting engineering issue, the Tribunal recorded that Royal Enfield had already paid service tax of Rs.1,02,058 on 30 January 2009, before issuance of the notice, and interest of Rs.25,759 on 28 April 2010. In the absence of fraud, collusion, wilful misstatement or suppression with intent to evade, the confirmation of that amount as a demand under Section 73(2) could not survive. However, the Tribunal clarified that the payments would remain a discharge made by the appellant on its own ascertainment under Section 73(3), and the order did not itself confer any right to their refund. The penalties imposed under Sections 76, 77 and 78 were set aside, together with the interest demands that depended on the unsustainable tax liabilities. In conclusion, the Tribunal allowed all three appeals and set aside Order-in-Appeal Nos.228, 229 and 230/2016 dated 23 March 2016, subject to its specific directions concerning the tax and interest already paid on the consulting engineering issue. Royal Enfield was held entitled to refund of amounts deposited during the pendency of proceedings and other consequential relief available under law. The Tribunal also emphasised the professional obligation to disclose relevant adverse precedents and cautioned against excessive citation of judgments without identifying the authorities genuinely necessary to resolve the dispute. The decision reinforces the principles that service tax liability must arise from an identified taxable service, adjudication must remain within the show cause notice, and the extended limitation period requires evidence of deliberate evasion rather than mere audit detection. Applied: courts should not decide unnecessary questions. FULL TEXT OF THE CESTAT CHENNAI ORDER These three appeals by M/s. Royal Enfield, a unit of Eicher Motors Limited (the appellant), call into question the common Order in Appeal Nos. 228, 229 and 230/2016 (STA-I) dated 23.03.2016 passed by the Commissioner of Service Tax (Appeals-I), Chennai (the impugned order), by which three orders in original confirming demands of service tax under the reverse charge mechanism were upheld in their entirety. The appeals arise from a common set of facts and raise common questions. They were heard together and are disposed of by this common order. Brief facts 2. The appellant manufactures motorcycles at Tiruvottiyur, Chennai, and sells them in India and abroad through a network of dealers. It holds service tax registrations for, among others, Goods Transport Agency, Business Auxiliary, Renting of Immovable Property and Intellectual Property services, and was discharging service tax under the reverse charge mechanism on certain services imported by it, notably consulting engineering services procured from engineering firms abroad. During an internal audit of its accounts for the period from April 2006 to December 2008, the officers noticed, from the balance sheet, trial balance, ST-3 returns, TR-6 challans and invoices, that the appellant had remitted amounts in convertible foreign currency to persons abroad for what the audit described as advertisement, renewal of brand name, transportation and arrangements for foreign exhibitions towards the sale of its motor vehicles in foreign markets. The audit also noticed that the appellant had not included certain expenses, described as freight charges, in the value of the consulting engineering services on which it was paying tax. 3. Show Cause Notice No. 75/2010 dated 19.03.2010 was issued by the Additional Commissioner for the period from April 2006 to December 2008. It proposed a demand of Rs.16,67,332/- on remittances aggregating Rs.1,35,15,349/- (which we shall call Issue 1) and a further demand of Rs.1,02,058/- for short payment of tax on consulting engineering services by reason of the exclusion of the said expenses from the taxable value (Issue 2). The notice invoked the proviso to Section 73(1) of the Finance Act, 1994 (the Act) and proposed interest under Section 75 and penalties under Sections 76, 77 and 78. Four further notices followed for the later periods, each confined to Issue 1 and each issued within the normal period proposing demands for the amounts as stated below, under Section 73(1), proposing interest and a penalty under Section 76 alone. These were Show Cause Notice No. 27/2010 dated 13.04.2010 for January to March 2009 proposing Rs.38,725/-, the notice taking up the period from where the first notice left off although its quantification begins only from February 2009, Show Cause Notice No. 74/2010 dated 30.09.2010 for April 2009 to March 2010 proposing Rs.85,857/-, Show Cause Notice No. 67/2011 dated 18.10.2011 for April 2010 to March 2011 proposing Rs.48,709/-, and Show Cause Notice No. 44/2012, though not bearing any date on its face, for April 2011 to March 2012 proposing Rs.3,20,472/-. Order in Original No. 45/2014 describes it as dated 19.10.2012, and the endorsement of its receipt on 22.10.2012 is consistent only with its issue on or before that day. We therefore take it as dated 19.10.2012. 4. All five notices share one feature that lies at the heart of these appeals. Each describes the remittances in the words used by the audit, extracts Section 66A of the Act and Rule 2(1)(d)(iv) of the Service Tax Rules, 1994, and concludes that the payments made towards services “in the form of Advertisement, renewal of Brand Name, Transportation and arrangements for foreign exhibitions” are chargeable to service tax under those provisions. None of the notices identifies the sub clause of Section 65(105) of the Act under which any of the services is said to fall. None refers to Section 65(19), which defines business auxiliary service, or to any of its clauses. None says which of the three clauses of Rule 3 of the Taxation of Services (Provided from Outside India and Received in India) Rules, 2006 (the Import Rules) is attracted. The later notices go no further than reproducing the audit narrative of the first. The extended period was invoked in Show Cause Notice No. 75/2010 on the premise that the receipt of the services was not disclosed in the ST-3 returns and that, but for the audit, the non payment would not have come to light. Reply and orders of adjudication 5. The appellant replied that the expenses were incurred on services received and consumed outside India, that the renewal of trade marks abroad was not a consulting engineering service, that the entire tax of Rs.1,02,058/- on Issue 2 had been paid on 30.01.2009, before the notice, with interest of Rs.25,759/- paid on 28.04.2010, that the demand for the earlier period was barred by limitation, and that the exercise was revenue neutral since any tax so paid would be available to it as CENVAT credit. At the hearing it explained that the remittances related to brand renewals, advertisement and exhibitions arranged through its dealers abroad and to magazines distributed to those dealers, and it later produced agreements and invoices. 6. By Order in Original No. 151/2011 dated 25.11.2011, the Additional Commissioner grouped the payments into three heads, namely activities aimed at promotion of sales, technical assistance including testing, and miscellaneous expenses such as packing and transport. He held the technical assistance received from Fujitech Inc., Japan, Vepro Ltd., U.K. and others to be consulting engineer service, and the promotional and miscellaneous items to be business auxiliary service, both falling, in his view, under Rule 3(iii) of the Import Rules. Meeting the appellant’s objection on trade marks, he observed at paragraph 19 that the show cause notice had not proposed to classify those charges as consulting engineer service, and that it had proposed to demand tax on “the charges paid in convertible foreign currency for the services received from the service providers located outside India”. He confirmed Rs.17,69,390/- under Section 73(2), appropriated the sums of Rs.1,02,058/- and Rs.25,759/- already paid, demanded interest, imposed a penalty of Rs.17,69,360/- under Section 78 with the option of a reduced penalty, desisted from imposing a penalty under Section 76, and imposed Rs.1,000/- under Section 77. 7. By Order in Original No. 105/2011 dated 28.12.2011, the Assistant Commissioner adjudicated Show Cause Notice Nos. 27/2010 and 74/2010 and confirmed Rs.1,24,582/- as tax on business auxiliary service, with interest and a penalty under Section 76. By Order in Original No. 45/2014 dated 01.05.2014, Show Cause Notice Nos. 67/2011 and 44/2012 were adjudicated. The adjudicating authority there reproduced the whole of Section 65(19), held that the services were aimed at promoting the appellant’s products and fell under business auxiliary service, rejected a plea of limitation as to the last notice on the ground that the return for April to September 2011 had been filed on 19.10.2011, and confirmed Rs.48,709/- and Rs.3,20,472/-with interest and penalties under Section 76. 8. The Commissioner (Appeals) dismissed all three appeals. He read Section 66A with Rule 2(1)(d)(iv) to mean that service received by any person in India is taxable, and held that because the appellant paid the consideration, was the beneficiary of the services and the services were meant for its products in India, the services were received and consumed in India. He drew support from Circular No. 111/05/2009 dated 24.02.2009 for the proposition that the place where the benefit accrues is decisive, and from the decision in Glyph International Ltd. v. Union of India, 2012 (25) S.T.R. 209 (All.). He affirmed the classification of trade mark renewal as business auxiliary service, observed that the demand on Issue 2 had not been contested and had been paid with interest, upheld the extended period because the facts surfaced only in audit, relying on Aircel Digilink India Ltd. v. CCE, 2006 (3) S.T.R. 386 (Tri.-Del.), S.K. Jalendra and Associates v. CCE, 2012 (26) S.T.R. 135 (Tri.-Del.) and Shilpa Printing Press v. CCE, 2013 (297) E.L.T. 417 (Tri.-Mumbai), and held the penalty under Section 76 to be mandatory on the authority of Syndicate Bottles v. CST, 2014 (33) S.T.R. 379 (Tri.-Chennai). Submissions on behalf of the appellant 9. Mr. M. Sivarajan, learned Consultant appearing for the appellant, argued that the demand concerns motorcycles already exported and sold abroad by independent dealers who buy them on a principal to principal basis. The dealers advertise in their own markets, participate in exhibitions there, arrange local transport and subscribe to periodicals. The appellant only shared part of such expenses, as the letter dated 05.05.2008 addressed to Classic Motor Works, U.S.A., shows, by which it agreed to bear half the dealer’s advertisement and publicity cost, against proof, subject to a ceiling of USD 100 for each motorcycle sold. The advertising agencies contracted with and were paid by the dealers, and the appellant had no privity with them. A dealer selling its own goods does not render any business auxiliary service to the manufacturer, as held in Charanjeet Singh Khanuja v. CST, 2016 (41) S.T.R. 213 (Tri.-Del.), and a sharing of advertisement cost is not consideration for any service, as held in G.R. Thanga Maligai v. Commissioner of GST and Central Excise, Chennai South, 2026-TIOL-656-CESTAT-MAD. He urged that trade mark registration and renewal fees paid to foreign registries and charges for vehicle approval in the United Kingdom and Canada are statutory levies and not consideration for any service, relying on Sidmak Laboratories (India) Pvt. Ltd. v. CCE and ST, 2023 (11) TMI 614 – CESTAT New Delhi and Zydus Lifesciences Ltd. v. CCE, 2024 (6) TMI 1265 – CESTAT Ahmedabad. Transport abroad is not a goods transport agency service, and if it were, only a quarter of its value would be taxable; business exhibition service was specifically taxable under Section 65(105)(zzo) and falls under Rule 3(ii). Item wise, he contended that the Fujitech deliverable was a performance test report, that the Xenophia payment was a pure reimbursement of air fare and visa charges which cannot be included in value after Intercontinental Consultants and Technocrats Pvt. Ltd. v. Union of India, 2013 (29) S.T.R. 9 (Del.), affirmed in Union of India v. Intercontinental Consultants and Technocrats Pvt. Ltd., 2018 (10) G.S.T.L. 401 (S.C.), and that the Vepro work under the agreement dated 15.02.2006 was completed before 18.04.2006. 10. An examination of the effect of limitation on the first appeal is necessary before we proceed, because it determines the span of the demand on which the merits arise. Under Section 73(6)(i)(a) of the Act, the relevant date, where a periodical return is filed, is the date on which it is filed. The normal period under Section 73(1) was then one year; it became eighteen months only from 28.05.2012. Paragraph 21 of Order in Original No. 151/2011 records that the return for October 2008 to March 2009 was filed on 24.04.2009, a finding the adjudicating authority recorded on the return that the appellant had enclosed as Annexure 2 to its reply, and which we accept. Show Cause Notice No. 75/2010 dated 19.03.2010 was thus within the normal period for the months of October to December 2008. Annexure I to that notice shows, for those months, payments towards trade mark renewals in November 2008 and an advertisement expense in December 2008. Annexure II, on the other hand, shows short payment on Issue 2 only against invoices of 2006, 2007 and January 2008. The merits of Issue 1 therefore arise for the whole of the second and third appeals and for the October to December 2008 portion of the first. The balance of the first appeal, covering April 2006 to September 2008 on both issues, depends on the extended period, which we take up at its place. The first question, a demand without a classification 15. The charge under Section 66A is not a charge on remittances. It is attracted only where “any service specified in clause (105) of section 65” is provided by a person located abroad and is “received by a person … who has his place of business, fixed establishment, permanent address or usual place of residence, in India”. Such a service is then to be “treated as if the recipient had himself provided the service in India, and accordingly all the provisions of this Chapter shall apply”. Section 65A(1) requires that taxable services be classified according to the terms of the sub clauses of Section 65(105) and, where a service is prima facie classifiable under two or more sub clauses, Section 65A(2)(a) prefers the sub clause providing the most specific description. 16. The Import Rules, notified under Sections 93 and 94 read with Section 66A by Notification No. 11/2006-ST dated 19.04.2006, then sort the taxable services into three groups, each identified by its sub clause of Section 65(105). Services in clause (i) are taxable as imports when they relate to immovable property in India. Services in clause (ii) are taxable only if performed in India, wholly or in part. Every other service falls in clause (iii) and is taxable when “received by a recipient located in India for use in relation to business or commerce”. Among the services placed in clause (ii) as notified were business exhibition service under sub clause (zzo), technical testing and analysis under sub clause (zzh), technical inspection and certification under sub clause (zzi) and goods transport agency service under sub clause (zzp). Advertising agency service under sub clause (e) and business auxiliary service under sub clause (zzb) fell in clause (iii). The Import Rules were amended several times during the period in dispute, by Notification Nos. 31/2007-ST, 6/2008-ST, 21/2008-ST, 22/2009-ST, 37/2009-ST, 16/2010-ST, 13/2011-ST, 23/2011-ST and 37/2011-ST. Those amendments moved sub clauses between the lists, inserted provisos and redefined India, but none of them touched the concluding words of clause (iii), on which the second question turns. Two of them bear on the present matter. Notification No. 6/2008-ST, with effect from 01.03.2008, inserted a second proviso to clause (ii) by which services under sub clauses (zzg), (zzh) and (zzi), provided through the internet or an electronic network in relation to goods or immovable property situated in India, are treated as performed in India. Notification No. 13/2011-ST, with effect from 01.04.2011, substituted the list in clause (ii), from which sub clauses (zzh) and (zzp) were dropped; technical testing and goods transport agency services therefore passed into clause (iii) from that date, and Notification No. 23/2011-ST correspondingly omitted (zzh) from the second proviso. Business exhibition service and technical inspection and certification service remained in clause (ii) throughout. 17. Classification is therefore not a mere label under this scheme. It decides which of the three different tests of receipt in India governs the transaction. A business exhibition held abroad or an inspection and certification carried out abroad would, if properly classified, be outside the levy altogether, since neither is performed in India, and until 31.03.2011 the same was true of a test performed abroad or goods carried by road abroad. Only when the sub clause is known, can the assessee know whether the question is one of the location of property, the place of performance or the location and use of the recipient. A notice that withholds the sub clause withholds the very test the assessee is called upon to meet. 18. That is exactly what has happened here. Each notice spoke of services “in the form of Advertisement, renewal of Brand Name, Transportation and arrangements for foreign exhibitions” and stopped there. The classification was supplied for the first time by the adjudicating authorities. Order in Original No. 151/2011 classified some items as consulting engineer service and the rest as business auxiliary service. The later orders treated everything as business auxiliary service, Order in Original No. 45/2014 doing so after reproducing all seven clauses of Section 65(19) without saying which of them applied. The Commissioner (Appeals) affirmed the result. Paragraph 19 of Order in Original No. 151/2011, which answered the appellant’s objection by observing that the notice had not classified the trade mark charges as consulting engineer service and had sought tax on the charges paid in foreign currency, is the clearest measure of the defect. The Revenue sought to tax money that had left the country, not a service identified by the statute. 9. The law on the point is settled. The Supreme Court held in Brindavan Beverages, supra, that the show cause notice is the foundation on which the Department has to build its case, and that allegations which are vague, lacking in detail or unintelligible deny the noticee a proper opportunity to meet them. In Commissioner of Service Tax v. 3i Infotech Ltd., 2023 INSC 711: 2024 (388) ELT 30 (SC), the Supreme Court, dealing with a service tax notice that had adopted a wrong classification, held at paragraph 10 that “Elementary principles of natural justice required that the adjudication on the basis of show cause notice should be made only on the basis of classification stated in the show cause notice.” The Principal Bench in Balaji Enterprises, supra, following Swapnil Asnodkar, supra, held that the Department must specify which of the seven clauses of Section 65(19) is alleged, failing which the demand cannot be confirmed. This Tribunal applied the same principle to a reverse charge demand under Rule 3(iii) in Sanmar Shipping, supra, relying also on CCE, Nagpur v. Ballarpur Industries Ltd., 2007 (215) E.L.T. 489 (S.C.) and CCE v. Gas Authority of India Ltd., 2008 (232) E.L.T. 7 (S.C.), and again in Trade Line, supra, where the order travelled beyond the allegation in the notice. 20. The present notices are weaker than the one that failed before the Supreme Court in 3i Infotech, supra. There the Revenue had at least named a service, albeit the wrong one. Here no service was named at all. In confirming the demands under a classification never put to the appellant, the adjudicating authorities did not decide the case the Revenue had made; they built one for it. In doing so they also swept services which the Import Rules expressly assign to the performance test of clause (ii) into the residual clause (iii), contrary to Section 65A(2)(a). We say, with restraint, that this is not adjudication within the four corners of a notice; it is levy by inference. Article 265 of the Constitution does not permit a tax to be raised in that manner, and a charge must rest on the Act and not on the conjecture of the officer applying it. The Commissioner (Appeals) ought to have noticed that the orders before him had no foundation in the notices, and did not. 21. We accordingly hold that the demands on Issue 1, for the whole of the second and third appeals and for October to December 2008 in the first appeal, cannot be sustained for want of a valid notice. This finding is sufficient to dispose of the merits of those demands. The second question, receipt in India and the scope of Rule 3(iii) 22. We are conscious that the first finding disposes of the demands on merits. We nevertheless take up the second question, for three reasons. The same question governs every period from April 2006 to March 2012 covered by these appeals. It was argued at length on both sides. And the reasoning of the impugned order on it, which equates the payer and the beneficiary with the recipient, is apt to be repeated in proceedings of this kind unless it is examined. 23. The impugned order rests on three steps, namely that the appellant paid the consideration, that it was the beneficiary of the services and that the services were meant for its products made in India. The flaw lies in the first two steps. Section 66A asks two distinct questions. One is whether the recipient has its place of business or fixed establishment in India. The other is whether the service was “received by” that person. The location of a person is not the same thing as receipt by that person, and the place where a service is performed may differ from the place where it is received. Rule 3 opens with the words “Subject to section 66A” and cannot dispense with the second question. 24. Who, then, receives a service? Service tax is levied on a transaction of service, and a transaction is defined by its contract. The recipient is ordinarily the person who contracts for the service, who is liable to pay for it and who can enforce its performance, even if a third person ultimately benefits. The mere bearing of part of the cost by someone else does not make that person the recipient. The Supreme Court in CST v. Vodafone India Ltd., (2025) 33 Centax 152 (S.C.), decided on 06.05.2025, considered a large batch of Revenue appeals under the Export of Services Rules, 2005 in which the Revenue’s central contention was that services contracted and paid for by persons abroad should be denied export status because the beneficiaries were in India. The Court declined to interfere with the decisions of this Tribunal, holding that the Tribunal had rightly analysed the activity in each case. The import rules mirror the export rules, and the same reasoning applies with equal force in the reverse direction. An Indian manufacturer who reimburses part of an expense incurred by a foreign dealer for a service the dealer contracted and received does not, by that act alone, become the recipient of that service. In C.C., C.E. and S.T., Bangalore v. Northern Operating Systems Pvt. Ltd., 2022 (61) G.S.T.L. 129 (S.C.), the Supreme Court likewise determined who was the recipient on an examination of the arrangement between the parties, and this Tribunal applied that approach in Renault Nissan Technology and Business Centre India Pvt. Ltd. v. CST, Chennai III, 2025 (10) TMI 680 – CESTAT Chennai, by reading the clauses of the contracts together. 25. Applied to the record, these principles leave little of the demands. The letter dated 05.05.2008 to Classic Motor Works is, in substance an offer by the appellant to participate in the dealer’s own advertisement campaign for April 2008 to March 2009 and to bear half its cost, against the dealer’s invoices from its advertising agencies, the materials published and proof of the dealer’s payment to the agencies, subject to USD 100 for each motorcycle sold. The record does not show that the dealer signed and returned it. The Revenue has never disputed, however, that the parties acted on its terms, and Order in Original No. 151/2011 itself proceeds on the basis that these activities were carried out through the dealers. The agencies were contracted with, billed and were paid by the dealer. What the appellant paid was a share of the dealer’s cost, measured by the motorcycles the dealer sold, which is a trade incentive and not consideration for a service rendered to the appellant. The dealer, having bought the motorcycles, was promoting goods that belonged to it; clause (i) of Section 65(19) speaks of goods “belonging to the client”. The Principal Bench held in Charanjeet Singh Khanuja, supra, that distributor reselling goods it has bought is not promoting goods belonging to its client. The wide words of a definition do not alter that result. In Coal Handlers Pvt. Ltd. v. CCE, 2015 (38) S.T.R. 897 (S.C.), the Supreme Court, setting aside the very decision of the Tribunal that was cited before us in another context, held that “11. From the reading of the definition contained in the aforesaid provision, together with its dictionary meanings contained in Legal and Commercial dictionaries, it becomes apparent that in order to qualify as a C&F Agent, such a person is to be found to be engaged in providing any service connected with ‘clearing and forwarding operations’. Of course, once it is found that such a person is providing the services which are connected with the clearing and forwarding operations, then whether such services are provided directly or indirectly would be of no significance and such a person would be covered by the definition.” Therefore, it is beyond any doubt that the expressions such as “directly or indirectly” and “in any manner” in a definition of a taxable service cannot be isolated from the core activity which the definition names, and that a service falls within the definition only if it partakes of that activity. The words “in relation to” in Section 65(19) are no different. They cannot carry into business auxiliary service an activity which is not the promotion or marketing of goods belonging to the client. The arrangement also bears every feature that led this Tribunal, in G.R. Thanga Maligai, supra, to hold that a fixed share of media expenses borne by one party, released only on production of the advertising vendor’s invoices, in a joint effort to promote a product in which both have a stake, is a sharing of cost and not consideration for a service, there being no identifiable provider and recipient between the parties. That decision drew support from Hindustan Construction Company Ltd. v. Commissioner of CGST, Navi Mumbai, (2025) 27 Centax 299 (Tri.-Bom.), affirmed in (2025) 27 Centax 300 (S.C.). There, the Revenue sought to treat the party receiving the contribution as the provider of a service; here, it seeks to treat the party making the contribution as the recipient of one. The principle cuts both ways. The appellant and its dealer shared the cost of advertisement procured by the dealer from a third party, with a common interest in selling more motorcycles, and the appellant received nothing from the dealer in return for its share. A coordinate Bench at Chennai, considering a car manufacturer’s reimbursements to overseas distributors for warranty services performed abroad, held in Hyundai Motor India, supra, that “Section 66A applies only where the service is received in India”, and the Supreme Court declined to interfere with that decision on 09.12.2019. 26. The trade mark payments stand on a different but equally untenable footing. Fees for the registration and renewal of trade marks paid to foreign registries are levies exacted in the discharge of a sovereign function. They are not consideration for a service. Payments to foreign regulators for product approvals were held not to be exigible in Sidmak Laboratories, supra, followed in Zydus Lifesciences, supra, and the same reasoning governs the charges for vehicle approval in the United Kingdom and for review of safety standard documents in Canada. Order in Original No. 151/2011 also records that the renewals were handled by an agency abroad through M/s. Mohan Associates, Chennai. Whether any element of professional fee was involved, and whether it was rendered from abroad at all, was never examined in the notices. We say no more on it, since the Revenue made no case on it. 27. The remaining items do not advance the Revenue’s case either. Subscriptions to and reprints of automobile magazines distributed to dealers are purchases of publications, and no taxable service has been identified in them. Transportation abroad, participation in exhibitions abroad and inspection or certification by foreign authorities are, if they are services at all, services of the kind that Rule 3(ii) taxes only when performed in India. For transportation, that is so until 31.03.2011. For the months from April 2011, goods transport agency service fell in clause (iii), but the transport in question was of goods already exported, carried abroad for the dealer, and it is answered by what we say below on the reach of that clause. Nothing in the notices or the orders suggests that any part of these services was performed in India, or that any inspection or certification was rendered through the internet or an electronic network in relation to goods situated in India so as to attract the second proviso to clause (ii). None of them could be pulled into clause (iii) by calling it business auxiliary service. Intas Pharmaceuticals, supra, and Coforge Smartserve, supra, are to the same effect. 28. This brings us to the question whether, even where an Indian manufacturer does itself contract for a service abroad, the words “for use in relation to business or commerce” in Rule 3(iii) reach services contracted, performed and consumed abroad in relation to goods already sold abroad. On a literal reading, the words “in India” do not follow “business or commerce”. Coordinate Benches have nonetheless read them so. In Tech Mahindra, supra, the Mumbai Bench held at paragraph 25 that “A plain reading would make it apparent that the services referred to must be for pursuit of business or commerce in India.” In Genom Biotech, supra, it held that advertising services rendered abroad in relation to goods after their arrival in Ukraine were not imported into India. A coordinate bench of this Tribunal in 3i Infotech Ltd. v. CST, Mumbai -II, 2017 (51) STR 305 (Tri-Mumbai), held at paragraph 13 that the reference to business or commerce in Rule 3(iii) is restricted to business and commerce in India. A different note has been struck in Gujarat Borosil Ltd. v. CCE and ST, 2014 (36) S.T.R. 808 (Tri.-Ahmd.), a single Member decision, and in Prithvi Information Solutions Ltd. v. Commissioner of Central Tax, 2025-TIOL-641-CESTAT-HYD, where it was held that Section 66A imposes no requirement that the service be consumed in India. 29. We would not read into a delegated rule words that its framers did not use merely because the result appears desirable. The qualifier that the Mumbai Bench discerned does not, however, depend on reading words into Rule 3(iii). It flows from the Act under which the rule is made. Section 64(1) extends Chapter V of the Act to the whole of India except Jammu and Kashmir, and the levy is on services in the taxable territory. Section 66A brings an imported service within the Chapter by a fiction, treating the recipient as if he had himself provided the service in India. That fiction presupposes a real receipt in India. Rule 3 is expressly subject to Section 66A, and a rule made under Sections 93 and 94 cannot enlarge a charge that the Act itself confines. A charge under a taxing statute can be laid only by the Act and not by the rules (State of Kerala v. Madras Rubber Factory Ltd., (1998) 1 SCC 616 at paragraph 20; Bimal Chandra Banerjee v. State of Madhya Pradesh, (1970) 2 SCC 467). The constitutional position points precisely in this direction. In GVK Industries Ltd. v. ITO, (2011) 4 SCC 36 : 2017(48) STR 177 (SC), a Constitution Bench held that Parliament may legislate for extra territorial aspects or causes only where they have an impact on or nexus with India, and the Allahabad High Court in Glyph International, supra, repelled the challenge to Section 66A on the ground of extra territoriality on the footing of that very nexus. A service contracted by a foreign dealer, performed abroad, consumed abroad and related to goods that have left India and belong to the dealer has but one Indian element, namely the source of the money. That is not receipt in India. To tax it would be to tax a cross border remittance, which the Act does not contemplate. 30. Read in this way, Gujarat Borosil, supra, and Prithvi Information Solutions, supra, are not in conflict with the view we take. In each, the Indian assessee itself contracted for and paid for the service, and used it for business carried on from India, whether to raise a loan for its Indian operations or to execute its own contracts. The nexus with India was real. Our conclusion accords with Tech Mahindra, supra, Genom Biotech, supra, 3i Infotech, supra, of the Mumbai Bench and Hyundai Motor India, supra. Since we do not differ from any coordinate Bench, there is no occasion to refer the question to a Larger Bench, which judicial discipline would otherwise have required (Collector of Central Excise, Kanpur v. Matador Foam (P) Ltd., 2005 (179) E.L.T. 257 (S.C.); Mary Pushpam v. Telvi Curusumary, 2024 INSC 8). 31. Neither of the authorities on which the impugned order rests supports it. Circular No. 111/05/2009 dated 24.02.2009 was issued to clarify the export of services. The test of accrual of benefit it adopted is not found in the Rules, and a circular cannot create a charge. Turning an export clarification inside out to impose a tax on imports is wholly impermissible. The Tribunal in Microsoft Corporation (I) (P) Ltd. v. CST, 2014 (36) S.T.R. 766 (Tri.-Del.) noticed that the expression accrual of benefit was not used in the Rules at all. Glyph International, supra, decided the constitutional validity of Section 66A. On the facts, it said no more than that the services were prima facie taxable, and it xpressly left the matter to adjudication (paragraphs 43 to 45). Even that prima facie view rested on the finding that the foreign entity procured work that the petitioner executed at its facility in Noida, a nexus with India that is wholly absent here. 32. We therefore hold, on the second question as well, that the remittances in issue were not consideration for taxable services received in India by the appellant within Section 66A read with Rule 3 of the Import Rules. We make it clear that this conclusion rests on the record of these appeals. It does not foreclose a proper case in which an Indian person itself contracts for and receives a service for use in its business in India. The third question, extended period of limitation 33. Show Cause Notice No. 75/2010 invoked the proviso to Section 73(1) on two grounds. The first is that the receipt of the services was not disclosed in the ST-3 returns. The second is that, but for the audit, the non payment would not have come to notice. Neither ground satisfies the proviso. The Supreme Court held in M.M. Ltd., supra, that the notice must put the assessee on notice of the specific default alleged and that an intention to evade cannot be inferred automatically. In Uniworth Textiles, supra, following Pushpam Pharmaceuticals Co. v. CCE, 1995 (78) E.L.T. 401 (S.C.), it held that mere non payment is not suppression, that the proviso occupies a “smaller, specific and more serious niche”, and that a positive act betraying a deliberate intent to evade is a mandatory prerequisite. In Larsen and Toubro Ltd. v. CCE, supra, the Court refused the extended period where the question was debatable and had been answered differently by different Benches. In Northern Operating Systems, supra, the Supreme Court held the extended period untenable in a reverse charge case even while upholding the levy itself. 34. Here, the appellant was a registered assessee, filing returns and paying tax under the reverse charge mechanism on other imported services. Every remittance was recorded in its books, and the notice itself says the audit gathered the facts from the balance sheet, trial balance, returns, challans and invoices. The taxability of payments of this kind was, and remains, a contested question on which coordinate Benches have differed, as our discussion of the second question shows. The appellant is a manufacturer, and any tax paid under Section 66A on services used in its business would have been available to it as credit; Rule 5 of the Import Rules only bars treating such services as output services. As this Tribunal held in Renault Nissan, supra, revenue neutrality does not extinguish a liability that has otherwise arisen, but it bears directly on whether an intention to evade can be attributed, as the Supreme Court in Nirlon, supra, and the Chennai Bench in Hyundai Motor India, supra, have recognised. Nothing in the notice points to any positive act of concealment. 35. The decisions relied on by the Commissioner (Appeals) do not assist the Revenue. Aircel Digilink, supra, concerned a provider who failed to declare its own taxable sales. K. Jalendra, supra, and Shilpa Printing Press, supra, turned on their own facts, and none of them survives Uniworth Textiles, supra, as authority for the proposition that detection in audit is, by itself, proof of suppression. The impugned order observed that “it is not the case of the appellant that they brought relevant facts to the notice of the department on their own”. That observation is misconceived as that inverts the burden. It is for the Revenue to establish the ingredients of the proviso, not for the assessee to prove a negative. An appellate authority that answers a plea of limitation by placing the burden on the assessee does not decide the plea at all. 36. We hold that the extended period was not available. The demand in the first appeal for April 2006 to September 2008, on both Issue 1 and Issue 2, is therefore barred by limitation. Nothing on record suggests that the returns for the half years ending on or before 30.09.2008 were filed after 19.03.2009, and the notice itself based the demand for that span solely on the proviso. The demand for October to December 2008, being within the normal period, has already failed on merits for the reasons recorded on the first and second questions. 37. Having held the demand for April 2006 to September 2008 to be time barred, we do not examine the contentions confined to that span, namely those relating to the Vepro agreement predating the levy, the Fujitech test report, the inclusion of the Xenophia air fare in the light of Intercontinental Consultants, supra, the abatement for transport and the valuation on Issue 2. This is not a case where limitation strikes at the proceedings at the threshold and leaves nothing to be decided. Limitation under Section 73 runs for each period from its own relevant date, and here it removes only a part of the demand, while the questions common to all three appeals have been answered on merits for the normal period and in the second and third appeals. What remains are questions particular to a demand that has already failed, and no answer to them can alter the result. A court “should always be careful not to cover ground which is strictly not relevant for the purpose of deciding the petition before it”, as a Bench of nine Judges of the Supreme Court cautioned in Naresh Shridhar Mirajkar v. State of Maharashtra, (1966) 3 SCR 744 : AIR 1967 SC 1, and a time barred demand affords no occasion to enter upon its merits (CCE v. Monsanto Manufacturer Pvt. Ltd., 2014 (35) S.T.R. 177 (All.)). We therefore express no opinion on these contentions. Leaving them open causes no prejudice to either side, since the demand to which they relate fails on limitation. 38. Issue 2 requires one further observation. The tax of Rs.1,02,058/- was paid on 30.01.2009, before the notice, and the interest of Rs.25,759/-on 28.04.2010. Section 73(3) of the Act provides that where tax is paid before service of notice and the officer is informed, no notice shall be served in respect of the amount so paid, and Section 73(4) withholds that protection only in cases of fraud, collusion, wilful misstatement, suppression or contravention with intent to evade, none of which has been established. Explanation 2 to Section 73(3), inserted with effect from 08.05.2010, declares that no penalty shall be imposed in respect of such payment and the interest thereon. The appellant did not contest the taxability of Issue 2 before the Commissioner (Appeals) and presses before us only the penalty and the time bar. The confirmation of the amount as a demand under Section 73(2) cannot stand. The amounts paid, however, shall stand as a discharge made by the appellant on its own ascertainment, and this order does not by itself entitle the appellant to their refund. The fourth question, penalty and interest 39. The penalty of Rs.17,69,360/- under Section 78 rests on the same ingredients as the extended period and falls with it. The penalty of Rs.1,000/- under Section 77 was imposed for failure to furnish information in the returns; the demand to which that information related has not survived, and no penalty can rest on a notice that has failed (M.M. Ltd., supra). The penalties under Section 76 in the second and third appeals presuppose a failure to pay tax that was payable. Since no tax was payable, the question whether Section 76 left any discretion, on which Syndicate Bottles, supra, was cited, does not arise. No interest is payable on demands that have not been sustained, save that the interest already paid on Issue 2 stands as part of the appellant’s own discharge. The plea under Section 80 was urged in the alternative; it does not arise, and we express no opinion on it. Conclusion 40. We sum up our discussions as follows. The demands on Issue 1 within the normal period fail because the notices identified no taxable service, and the adjudicating authorities could not supply a classification the Revenue never alleged. Independently, the remittances were not consideration for taxable services received in India by the appellant. Some were shares of costs incurred by foreign dealers for services they contracted and received, some were statutory fees paid to foreign authorities, and the rest were performance based services rendered wholly abroad. The extended period was not available in the first appeal, so the demand for April 2006 to September 2008 on both issues is barred. Its merits are, by binding authority, not examined. The penalties and interest fall with the demands. The amounts paid by the appellant on Issue 2 before the notice stand as its own discharge. The impugned order cannot be sustained. 41. Before parting, we are constrained to observe that more than fifty decisions were cited for the appellant, with a compilation running to well over two hundred pages. Fourteen were cited on the single point that Rule 3(iii) contemplates business in India, twelve on the specificity of the show cause notice, and some fifteen on revenue neutrality, limitation and penalty, with Genom Biotech, 3i Infotech, Intas Pharmaceuticals, Coforge Smartserve and IPCA Laboratories recurring under more than one head. Yet the submissions did not mention Gujarat Borosil or Prithvi Information Solutions, which take the contrary view on Rule 3(iii), nor that the decision of the Tribunal in Coal Handlers had been set aside by the Supreme Court, as noticed in paragraph 25. In New India Assurance Co. Ltd. v. Dolly Satish Gandhi, 2026 INSC 498, the Supreme Court has restated that counsel must bring to the Court’s notice judgments that do not aid their case as well as those that do, while the Court has an independent duty to apply the correct law even if it is not cited, to ensure consistency with precedent and to avoid decisions per incuriam. That duty is served by a few apt decisions, the adverse ones included, and not by a multitude on one point. The practice of piling up precedents was deprecated in Rashmi Metaliks Ltd. v. Kolkata Metropolitan Development Authority, (2013) 10 SCC 95, and Kanwar Natwar Singh v. Directorate of Enforcement, (2010) 13 SCC 255 holds that a court need not consider decisions placed in a compilation but not cited at the Bar. This Tribunal said as much in Wiegand India (P) Ltd. v. CCE, New Delhi, 1995 (78) E.L.T. 331 (Tribunal), and has reiterated it in Shree Vijayalakshmi Charitable Trust v. Commissioner of GST and Central Excise, Coimbatore, 2025 (6) TMI 519 – CESTAT Chennai, Exide Industries Ltd. v. Commissioner of GST and Central Excise, Salem, 2025 (6) TMI 1960 – CESTAT Chennai, and Surya Prakash Bhandari v. Commissioner of Customs, Chennai, 2025 (11) TMI 389 – CESTAT Chennai : (2026) 38 Centax 341 (Tri. -Mad.). We have examined the adverse authority ourselves and confined our discussion to the decisions that decide the questions before us, without explaining, one by one, why the rest do not advance the matter. 42. In the result, Service Tax Appeal Nos. 41208, 41209 and 41210 of 2016 are allowed. The impugned Order in Appeal Nos. 228, 229 and 230/2016 (STA-I) dated 23.03.2016 is set aside, subject to our holding that the sums of Rs.1,02,058/- and Rs.25,759/- paid by the appellant on 30.01.2009 and 28.04.2010 towards Issue 2 shall stand as a discharge made by the appellant on its own ascertainment under Section 73(3) of the Finance Act, 1994. No penalty shall be levied in respect of them, and this order does not by itself entitle the appellant to their refund. The appellant shall be entitled to the refund of any amount deposited during the pendency of these proceedings, as well any other consequential relief in law, if any.

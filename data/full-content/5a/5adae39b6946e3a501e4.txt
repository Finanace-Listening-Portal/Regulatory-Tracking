Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); Harshil Mathur & Anr. Vs Union of India (Karnataka High Court) Summary: The Karnataka High Court, in Harshil Mathur & Anr. Vs Union of India, Writ Petition No. 16078 of 2026 (GM-RES), decided on 4 November 2026 by Justice M. Nagaprasanna, rejected a petition by two Razorpay directors seeking to quash proceedings initiated on a complaint by the Serious Fraud Investigation Office (SFIO). The petitioners, arrayed as accused Nos. 8 and 9 in Special C. No. 324 of 2026 arising from PCR No. 33 of 2025, challenged the complaint, the cognizance order dated 5 March 2026 and consequential summons. The proceedings arose from an SFIO investigation ordered on 9 September 2022 into Jilian Consultants India Private Limited and 32 other companies. One investigated entity, Mad Elephant Network Technology Private Limited (MENTPL), was alleged to have participated in predatory digital lending and coercive recoveries, generating wrongful gains of approximately ₹56.63 crore. The complaint alleged deficiencies in Razorpay’s merchant onboarding, know-your-customer checks, due diligence and transaction monitoring in relation to MENTPL. The petitioners argued that Razorpay was not among the companies named for investigation under Section 212 of the Companies Act, 2013; that the company itself had not been made an accused; and that its directors could not be subjected to criminal liability merely on allegations of negligence under Section 166. They also raised limitation, absence of mens rea and nexus with MENTPL, allocation of KYC responsibilities, and an objection to the cognizance procedure under Section 223(1) of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023. The SFIO countered that Section 212(14) extended to persons directly or indirectly connected with the affairs of the investigated companies, and alleged that Razorpay had processed approximately ₹320 crore in MENTPL transactions. These figures and allegations were presented as part of the prosecution case, not as independently adjudicated findings of wrongdoing. The Court noted the scope of Section 212(1) and (14), including the possibility of prosecution of other persons connected directly or indirectly with a company’s affairs. It held that whether the directors had discharged their fiduciary responsibilities under Section 166 and conducted adequate due diligence raised seriously disputed questions of fact that could be considered at trial. The omission to arraign Razorpay itself did not justify intervention at this preliminary stage. Relying on Kaptan Singh v. State of Uttar Pradesh and Muskan v. Ishaan Khan (Sataniya), the Court reiterated that inherent powers under Section 528 BNSS could not be used to conduct a mini-trial or weigh disputed evidence. It declined interference and rejected the writ petition. The ruling leaves the underlying accusations to be tested at trial; it does not record a conviction or a final finding of guilt. FULL TEXT OF THE ORDER OF KARNATAKA HIGH COURT The petitioners/accused Nos.8 and 9 in Special C.No.324 of 2026 pending before the LIX Additional City Civil and Sessions Judge, Bengaluru arising out of P.C.R.No.33 of 2025 are at the doors of this Court calling in question initiation of proceedings by the respondent/Serious Fraud Investigation Office (hereinafter referred to as ‘SFIO’ for short). 2. Facts, in brief, germane are as follows: – The petitioners are the office bearers of Razorpay Private Limited (hereinafter referred to as ‘Razorpay’ for short), an intermediary. 1st petitioner is the whole time Director and the 2nd petitioner is the Managing Director of the payment aggregator platform/Intermediary – Razorpay. It is the averment that Razorpay is regulated by the Reserve Bank of India and is engaged in the business of providing intermediary payment aggregation services and implementing payment solutions to third party merchants across diverse sectors. The genesis of the proceedings in the case at hand is on a confidential report of the Registrar of Companies, which is said to have uncovered a network of shell companies with dummy directors created by one Jilian Consultants India Private Limited (‘JCIPL’), a wholly owned subsidiary of a company named Fininty Private Limited. Based on the affairs of the said company and 32 other companies, the Government of India by an order dated 09-09-2022 directs investigation into the affairs of the said company under Section 212 of the Companies Act, 2013 (hereinafter referred to as ‘the Act’ for short). The investigation report is placed before the Government on 31-07-2024 by the respondent/ SFIO. The investigation report by the SFIO forms the sole foundation for the impugned complaint. 3. As per the complaint, one Mad Elephant Network Technology Private Limited (‘MENTPL’), which was one of the 33 companies under investigation, was incorporated on 15-11-2019 through JCIPL, allegedly arranging dummy directors completely controlled by Chinese entities. MENTPL subsequently entered into an arrangement with X10 Financial Services Limited, a non-banking financial company, as a service provider for lending operations through digital platforms and mobile applications. The allegations against MENTPL is that unethical methods were followed for recovery, which was predatory and coercive and generated wrongful gains to MENTPL to the tune of Rs. 56.63/- crores. Insofar as the petitioners are concerned, they are arrayed as accused Nos.8 and 9. The allegations qua the petitioners in the impugned complaint pertain to deficiencies and negligence in merchant onboarding processes, including alleged lapses in know your customer (‘KYC’) verification of MENTPL, non-conduct of due diligence and non-monitoring of transactions of MENTPL by Razorpay. The impugned complaint further alleges that the petitioners being Directors of Razorpay failed to exercise their duties as Directors with due and reasonable care, skill and diligence and exercise of independent judgment and, therefore, were liable to be charged under Section 166 of the Act. 4. The statement of the 1st petitioner was recorded on 16-08-2023, wherein it is said that the 1st petitioner explained that Reserve Bank of India guidelines were then evolving and onus of doing a full KYC was not on the petitioners. The petitioners challenge the said proceedings in P.C.R.No.33 of 2025 on manifold scores inter alia the absence of statutory obligation and imaginary duty cast upon payment aggregators like the Razorpay. They seek complete quashment of proceedings against them – accused Nos.8 and 9. 5. Heard Sri Sandesh J. Chouta, learned senior counsel appearing for the petitioners and Sri K. Arvind Kamath, learned Additional Solicitor General of India appearing for the respondent. 6.1. The learned senior counsel Sri Sandesh J. The petitioners are not the officers of any company under investigation. Mere provision of payment gateway cannot constitute an action in connection with the affairs of a company under Section 212 of the Act. 6.2. The submission of the learned senior counsel is that Section 166 is wholly inapplicable and misconceived. Negligence per se would not become an offence is the contention of the learned senior counsel. Amplifying the submission that Razorpay, which is allegedly negligent at the time of onboarding, is not an accused. Without there being any specific allegation against the petitioners, the Director or the Managing Director of Razorpay cannot be dragged in as there is no concept of vicarious liability in criminal jurisprudence. 6.3. Since the complaint is registered after three years, it would be hit by Section 468 of the Cr.P.C. Learned senior counsel would contend that the allegation is non-adherence to KYC obligation. KYC obligation is the duty of the Bank and not an intermediary like Razorpay. In the alternative, learned senior counsel submits that cognizance taken by the concerned Court after the onset of BNSS is contrary to law and therefore, must be obliterated and remitted back to the hands of the concerned Court for fresh consideration in terms of Section 223 (1) of the BNSS. Per contra, the learned Additional Solicitor General of India Sri Arvind Kamath would vehemently refute the submissions to contend that the petitioners are Directors of Razorpay, a payment aggregator. The offence against the Directors is the one punishable under Section 166 of the Act. The accusations against the petitioners are based upon statutory investigation under Section 212 of the Act. The backdrop of the allegations is that Chinese controlled illegal digital lending operation have made wrongful gains of about Rs.57/- crores through usurious interest at 30% upfront deduction at the time of payment and coercive recovery. Section 212 of the Act has a wide amplitude and it necessarily extends to persons and entities through whom the affairs were conducted and through whom funds were routed. The routing of money has taken place through fake companies. Razorpay was precisely a conduit carrying Rs.320/- crores of MENTPL’s transactions. The prosecution of the petitioners flows from the mandate of the statute under Section 212 of the Act, which empowers the Government of India to take action. Insofar as the order of taking cognizance, it cannot be said that a rowing inquiry or a mini-trial must be conducted at the time of taking of cognizance. He would, therefore, seek dismissal of the petition. 8. I have given my anxious consideration to the submissions made by the respective learned counsel and have perused the material on record. 9. The submission of the learned Additional Solicitor General of India is that, Section 212 of the Act deals with investigation into the affairs of the company and would include the entities through whom company affairs would be conducted and the funds were routed. It is by now too well settled that this Court while exercising its jurisdiction under Section 528 of the BNSS cannot embark upon a mini-trial and adjudicate upon disputed questions of fact by entering into the merits of allegations. 12.1. The Apex Court in the case of KAPTAN SINGH v. STATE OF UTTAR PRADESH1, has held as follows: “ …. …. …. 9.1. At the outset, it is required to be noted that in the present case the High Court in exercise of powers under Section 482 Cr.P.C.,, has quashed the criminal proceedings for the offences under Sections 147, 148, 149, 406, 329 and 386 IPC. It is required to be noted that when the High Court in exercise of powers under Section 482 Cr.P.C.,, quashed the criminal proceedings, by the time the investigating officer after recording the statement of the witnesses, statement of the complainant and collecting the evidence from the incident place and after taking statement of the independent witnesses and even statement of the accused persons, has filed the charge-sheet before the learned Magistrate for the offences under Sections 147, 148, 149, 406, 329 and 386 IPC and even the learned Magistrate also took the cognizance. From the impugned judgment and order [Radhey Shyam Gupta v. State of U.P., 2020 SCC OnLine All 914] passed by the High Court, it does not appear that the High Court took into consideration the material collected during the investigation/inquiry and even the statements recorded. If the petition under Section 482 Cr.P.C.,, was at the stage of FIR in that case the allegations in the FIR/complaint only are required to be considered and whether a cognizable offence is disclosed or not is required to be considered. However, thereafter when the statements are recorded, evidence is collected and the charge-sheet is filed after conclusion of the investigation/inquiry the matter stands on different footing and the Court is required to consider the material/evidence collected during the investigation. Even at this stage also, as observed and held by this Court in a catena of decisions, the High Court is not required to go into the merits of the allegations and/or enter into the merits of the case as if the High Court is exercising the appellate jurisdiction and/or conducting the trial. As held by this Court in Dineshbhai Chandubhai Patel [Dineshbhai Chandubhai Patel v. State of Gujarat, (2018) 3 SCC 104 : (2018) 1 SCC (Cri) 683] in order to examine as to whether factual contents of FIR disclose any cognizable offence or not, the High Court cannot act like the investigating agency nor can exercise the powers like an appellate court. It is further observed and held that that question is required to be examined keeping in view, the contents of FIR and prima facie material, if any, requiring no proof. At such stage, the High Court cannot appreciate evidence nor can it draw its own inferences from contents of FIR and material relied on. It is further observed it is more so, when the material relied on is disputed. It is further observed that in such a situation, it becomes the job of the investigating authority at such stage to probe and then of the court to examine questions once the charge-sheet is filed along with such material as to how far and to what extent reliance can be placed on such material. 9.2. In Dhruvaram Murlidhar Sonar [Dhruvaram Murlidhar Sonar v. It is further observed that inherent jurisdiction under Section 482 Cr.P.C.,, though wide is to be exercised sparingly, carefully and with caution, only when such exercise is justified by tests specifically laid down in the section itself. The main issue that arises for our analysis is whether the High Court was right in quashing the criminal proceedings against the private respondents primarily on the ground that the earlier complaints did not mention the two specific incidents dated 22.07.2021 and 27.11.2022, which were later on added in the FIR and whether the same would not amount to conducting a ‘mini trial’ which is clearly prohibited under the scheme of Section 482 of the Cr. P.C. …. …. …. 22. On the aspect of the powers of the Courts under Section 482 of the Cr. P.C., it is settled that at the stage of quashing, the Court is not required to conduct a mini trial. Thus, the jurisdiction under Section 482 of the Cr. P.C. with respect to quashing is somewhat limited as the Court has to only consider whether any sufficient material is available to proceed against the accused or not. If sufficient material is available, the power under Section 482 should not be exercised. 23. This Court in the case of State of Odisha Pratima Mohanty10 held that: “8.2. It is trite that the power of quashing should be exercised sparingly and with circumspection and in rare cases. As per the settled proposition of law while examining an FIR/complaint quashing of which is sought, the court cannot embark upon any enquiry as to the reliability or genuineness of allegations made in the FIR/complaint. Quashing of a complaint/FIR should be an exception rather than any ordinary rule. Normally the criminal proceedings should not be quashed in exercise of powers under Section 482 CrPC when after a thorough investigation the charge-sheet has been filed. At the stage of discharge and/or considering the application under Section 482 CrPC the courts are not required to go into the merits of the allegations and/or evidence in detail as if conducting the mini-trial. As held by this Court the powers under Section 482 CrPC are very wide, but conferment of wide power requires the court to be more cautious. It casts an onerous and more diligent duty on the Court.” 24. Further in the case of Central Bureau of Investigation Aryan Singh11, this Court held that at the stage of Section 482 of the Cr. P.C., the High Court is not required to conduct a mini trial. “6. From the impugned common judgment and order [Aryan Singh v. CBI, 2022 SCC OnLine P&H 4158] passed by the High Court, it appears that the High Court has dealt with the proceedings before it, as if, the High Court was conducting a mini trial and/or the High Court was considering the applications against the judgment and order passed by the learned trial court on conclusion of trial. As per the cardinal principle of law, at the stage of discharge and/or quashing of the criminal proceedings, while exercising the powers under Section 482 CrPC, the Court is not required to conduct the mini trial. The High Court in the common impugned judgment and order has observed that the charges against the accused are not proved. This is not the stage where the prosecution/investigating agency is/are required to prove the charges. The charges are required to be proved during the trial on the basis of the evidence led by the prosecution/investigating agency.” (emphasis supplied) 25. Further, this Court in the case of State of Telangana v. Habib Abdullah Jeelani12 held that: “13. There can be no dispute over the proposition that inherent power in a matter of quashment of FIR has to be exercised sparingly and with caution and when and only when such exercise is justified by the test specifically laid down in the provision itself. It surprises us in the extreme that the High Court thought that in the exercise of its inherent powers under Section 482 of the Code of Criminal Procedure, it could quash a first information report. We are of the view that the High Court has erred in law by embarking upon an enquiry with regard to credibility or otherwise of the allegations in the complaints and the FIR. Normally, for quashing an FIR, it must be shown that there exists no prime facie case against the accused persons. In the present case, from the conjoint reading of the complaints and the FIR, it can be seen that prime facie allegations of harassment and demand of dowry are made out, despite that the High Court quashed the FIR against the private respondents primarily on the ground that the earlier two complaints that were filed by the appellant did not mention the specific instances that happened on 22.07.2021 and 27.11.2022 and the same were later on mentioned in the FIR only as an afterthought and was a counterblast to the legal notice sent by respondent no. 1/husband to the appellant as she was not coming back to her matrimonial home.

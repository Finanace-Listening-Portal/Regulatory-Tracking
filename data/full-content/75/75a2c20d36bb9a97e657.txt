(Supreme Court of India) The Supreme Court of India, in Committee of Creditors of Essar Steel India Limited Vs Satish Kumar Gupta & Ors., delivered a landmark judgment concerning the commercial wisdom of the Committee of Creditors (CoC), distribution of resolution proceeds among different classes of creditors, the jurisdiction of the National Company Law Tribunal (NCLT) and National Company Law Appellate Tribunal (NCLAT), and the constitutional validity of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (Amendment) Act, 2019. The dispute arose from the corporate insolvency resolution process of Essar Steel India Limited, which commenced following applications filed by Standard Chartered Bank and State Bank of India under Section 7 of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016. After several rounds of litigation concerning eligibility under Section 29A, ArcelorMittal India Private Limited submitted a revised resolution plan, which was approved by the CoC on 25 October 2018 with a voting majority of 92.24%. The resolution plan contemplated substantial payments to financial creditors and specified treatment for operational creditors, together with fresh investment for revival of the corporate debtor. The controversy intensified when the NCLAT, by its judgment dated 4 July 2019, substantially modified the distribution mechanism approved by the CoC. The Appellate Tribunal directed that financial and operational creditors should receive broadly equal treatment and redistributed resolution proceeds so that creditors would receive approximately 60.7% of their admitted claims, subject to the classifications adopted in its judgment. It also held that security interests were irrelevant for determining distributions during corporate insolvency resolution, questioned the authority of the CoC to determine allocations among creditors, directed distribution of profits earned during the insolvency process and permitted consideration of certain additional claims. The CoC and other appellants challenged these directions, contending that the NCLAT had exceeded its statutory jurisdiction by substituting its own commercial assessment for the decision of the financial creditors. Standard Chartered Bank and certain operational creditors defended the NCLAT’s approach, arguing that the proposed distribution was discriminatory and that the resolution process required equitable treatment of creditors. The Supreme Court held that the commercial wisdom of the CoC occupies a central position under the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code. The CoC is entitled to evaluate the feasibility and viability of a resolution plan and determine the manner of distribution among creditors, subject to the mandatory requirements of the Code and applicable regulations. The Court explained that equitable treatment does not necessarily require identical payments to all creditors. Financial creditors and operational creditors constitute distinct classes, and differences in security interests, priorities and the nature of claims may justify differential treatment. Consequently, the NCLAT could not impose a uniform recovery percentage merely because creditors had admitted claims against the same corporate debtor. Judicial review under Sections 30 and 31, and appellate scrutiny under Section 61, remain confined to the statutory grounds. The NCLT and NCLAT cannot replace a commercially approved resolution plan with a distribution formula of their own choosing. The Supreme Court further examined the amendments introduced in 2019, particularly the provisions concerning minimum payments to operational creditors and dissenting financial creditors. It upheld the legislative framework permitting the CoC to consider the priority and value of security interests while distributing resolution proceeds. The reference to Section 53 in Section 30(2)(b) was explained as a mechanism for calculating statutory minimum entitlements, rather than a requirement to reproduce the liquidation waterfall in every resolution plan. Regarding the amended 330-day period under Section 12, the Court recognised the importance of time-bound insolvency resolution but held that mandatory liquidation solely because the period had expired could produce arbitrary results where delays were attributable to judicial proceedings. Accordingly, extensions beyond 330 days could be permitted in exceptional circumstances. The Court also emphasised the importance of finality of claims under an approved resolution plan, so that a successful resolution applicant could revive the corporate debtor without facing unexpected liabilities arising from unresolved pre-resolution claims. On the facts of Essar Steel, the Supreme Court set aside the NCLAT’s interference with the commercial distribution approved by the CoC and restored the primacy of the resolution framework adopted by the creditors, subject to the directions contained in its judgment. The decision established that the objective of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code is resolution and value maximisation rather than compulsory equality of recovery among differently situated creditors. It remains a foundational precedent on the limited scope of judicial review of resolution plans, protection of commercial decision-making, classification of creditors, treatment of security interests and the need for certainty in corporate insolvency resolution.

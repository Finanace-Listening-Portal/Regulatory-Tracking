Summary: Rule 18 of the Goods and Services Tax Appellate Tribunal (Procedure) Rules, 2025 governs the presentation and number of appeals required before the GSTAT. While Section 112 of the CGST Act provides the statutory right of appeal, Rule 18 determines how an appeal must be structured and whether one or multiple appeals must be filed. Rule 18(1) requires electronic filing, a proper cause title, consecutively numbered factual paragraphs, complete party particulars and consistent numbering of parties. Rule 18(2) clarifies that multiple show cause notices, refund claims or demands covered by a single appellate order do not, by themselves, require separate appeals. However, Rule 18(3)(a) mandates separate appeal forms where one Order-in-Appeal covers multiple Orders-in-Original, while Rule 18(3)(b) prohibits joint appeals by different aggrieved persons. These distinctions have financial implications because each appeal attracts the applicable fee under Rule 110(5) of the CGST Rules and statutory pre-deposit requirements under Section 112(8), wherever applicable. Incorrect appeal counting may result in Registry objections, additional expenses, delays and limitation disputes. Taxpayers and professionals should therefore identify the underlying Orders-in-Original, determine the number of aggrieved persons, calculate separate filing obligations and prepare consistent grounds before approaching the GSTAT. In reality, the first hurdle often arises before the Tribunal examines a single legal argument. When an appeal reaches the Goods and Services Tax Appellate Tribunal (GSTAT), the Registry first checks whether it has been filed in the prescribed manner, whether the required documents have been attached, and most importantly, whether the correct number of appeals has been filed. A mistake at this stage can lead to objections from the Registry, additional costs, procedural delays, and in some cases, disputes over limitation. That is where Rule 18 of the GSTAT (Procedure) Rules, 2025 assumes practical significance. Rule 18 works alongside Section 112 of the CGST Act, which grants the statutory right of appeal, and Rule 110 of the CGST Rules, which prescribes Form GST APL-05 and the applicable filing fee. While Section 111 empowers the Tribunal to regulate its own procedure, that power remains subject to the Act and the Rules. Accordingly, Rule 18 does not create or restrict the right of appeal. Its role is more practical: it tells an appellant how an appeal should be presented and how many appeals must be filed. In simple terms, Rule 18 answers two fundamental questions: How should an appeal be drafted and structured? When a dispute involves multiple notices, orders, or parties, how many separate appeals are required? Getting these questions right at the outset can save considerable time, expense, and procedural complications later. Part 1: Drafting the GSTAT Appeal Properly Rule 18(1) requires every appeal to be filed electronically through the GSTAT Portal and lays down certain basic drafting requirements. (a) The Cause Title The appeal must be presented under the heading “In the Goods and Services Tax Appellate Tribunal” and should clearly identify the order under challenge. As a matter of good drafting practice, the appellant should mention not only the Order-in-Appeal (OIA) being challenged but also the underlying Order-in-Original (OIO). Since the GSTAT functions as a second appellate forum, presenting the entire chain of proceedings at the very beginning allows the Tribunal to understand the dispute immediately. For additional procedural guidance, see Practical Guide to Appealing Before the GST Appellate Tribunal. (b) One Fact, One Paragraph Facts should be stated in separately numbered paragraphs, with each paragraph dealing with a single fact or issue. Although it may appear to be a minor drafting formality, this requirement has a practical purpose. When the Department files its reply or the Tribunal refers to particular facts during the hearing, everyone can identify the relevant portion of the record quickly and accurately. A clear structure today often prevents confusion tomorrow. (c) State the Parties Once, and Clearly The opening portion of the appeal should contain complete details of every party, including name, GSTIN, legal status, and address. For individual taxpayers, parentage should also be stated. For example: “M/s ABC Traders, a proprietary concern of Shri Ramesh Patil, son of Shri Suresh Patil, GSTIN 27AAAAA0000A1Z5, Pune.” Once these details form part of the main appeal, they need not be repeated in every subsequent application filed in the matter, such as stay applications or condonation petitions. (d) Party Numbers Must Remain Consistent Each party is assigned a number and retains that number throughout the proceedings. This may appear administrative, but it becomes important where parties are deleted, substituted, or represented through legal heirs. For instance, if an appellant dies during the pendency of proceedings, the legal representatives are added under sub-numbers such as 1(a) and 1(b). They continue the existing appeal rather than initiating a fresh one. This approach aligns with Section 93 of the CGST Act, which contemplates continuation of liability through legal representatives. Part 2: Multiple Notices Do Not Mean Multiple GSTAT Appeals One of the most taxpayer-friendly aspects of Rule 18 is found in sub-rule (2). The rule clarifies that the number of show cause notices, refund applications, declarations, demands, or other proceedings covered by an order is irrelevant for determining the number of appeals. What matters is the order being challenged. Consider a taxpayer who receives separate show cause notices for FY 2018-19, FY 2019-20, and FY 2020-21. If all three notices culminate in a single Order-in-Original, which is then affirmed or modified through a single Order-in-Appeal, only one appeal is required before the GSTAT. The same principle applies to refund matters where multiple refund periods are disposed of through a common order. In practical terms, Rule 18(2) prevents unnecessary multiplication of proceedings and allows connected issues arising from a single adjudication order to be challenged through one appeal. The distinction is also explained in Practical Guideline for Filing Appeals before GSTAT. Part 3: Multiple Orders-in-Original Require Separate Appeals The position changes significantly under Rule 18(3)(a). Where a common Order-in-Appeal has been passed in respect of multiple Orders-in-Original, the appellant must file a separate GSTAT appeal for each Order-in-Original that remains in dispute. This is the provision that often surprises taxpayers. The key principle is straightforward: Show cause notices are not the unit of appeal. Orders-in-Original are. Imagine a situation where three separate OIOs were issued for three tax periods and three first appeals were filed. The appellate authority subsequently disposes of all three first appeals through a common Order-in-Appeal. Although there is only one OIA, three separate appeals must be filed before the GSTAT because there are three underlying OIOs. The consequences are not merely procedural. They are financial. Separate GSTAT Filing Fees and Pre-Deposit Obligations Each appeal attracts a separate filing fee. Under Rule 110(5) of the CGST Rules, the fee is calculated at ₹1,000 per ₹1 lakh of disputed amount, subject to a minimum of ₹5,000 and a maximum of ₹25,000 per appeal. Likewise, the statutory pre-deposit under Section 112(8) must be computed separately for each appeal. For detailed guidance on these requirements, see GSTAT Second Appeal Filing Procedure 2026: Section 112, APL-05, Pre-Deposit and Fees. For a taxpayer already dealing with a significant tax demand, the distinction between one appeal and three appeals can mean a substantial increase in immediate compliance costs. The phrase “so far as the appellant is concerned” is equally important. A person need only appeal against those Orders-in-Original by which they are actually aggrieved. If relief has already been granted for one of the orders, no appeal is required in respect of that order. Part 4: No Joint GSTAT Appeals by Different Persons Another frequent misconception is that persons affected by the same order can challenge it through a common appeal. Rule 18(3)(b) expressly rejects this approach. Where a single order affects multiple persons, each aggrieved person must file an independent appeal. Joint appeals are not maintainable. For example, if an order confirms tax and penalty against a company and separately imposes penalty on its director under Section 122(1A), both the company and the director must file their own appeals. Each appeal carries its own procedural requirements, filing fee, and pre-deposit obligations. Similarly, in alleged fake invoice investigations involving suppliers, recipients, brokers, or other participants, every affected person must independently approach the Tribunal. The rationale is simple. Although the facts may overlap, the legal rights and liabilities of each person remain distinct. The broader appellate framework, including separate appeal requirements and documentation, is discussed in Compendium on Operation, Procedure and Legal Architecture of GSTAT. Practical Takeaways Before Filing a GSTAT Appeal Before filing an appeal before the GSTAT, practitioners should pause and answer three questions: How many Orders-in-Original are involved? How many persons are aggrieved by the order? What will be the separate fee and pre-deposit obligations for each appeal? A careful review at this stage can prevent costly procedural errors later. Connected appeals should be linked and requested to be heard together. If there is any delay, a separate condonation application should ordinarily accompany each appeal. Most importantly, arguments taken across related appeals should remain consistent, as contradictory positions weaken the appellant’s overall case. For a detailed discussion of limitation, filing requirements and procedural compliance, refer to GSTAT Second Appeal Filing Procedure 2026. Conclusion: Correct Appeal Counting Is Essential Rule 18 may appear procedural, but it has significant practical consequences. It determines not only how an appeal is drafted but also how many appeals must be filed, how much the appellant pays in fees and pre-deposits, and whether the matter proceeds smoothly through the Registry. In essence, Rule 18(1) governs the form of the appeal, Rule 18(2) tells us that the number of notices is largely irrelevant, and Rule 18(3) reminds us that the real counting exercise lies in identifying the number of Orders-in-Original and the number of aggrieved persons. For taxpayers and professionals alike, getting that count right is often the first step towards a successful appeal. #Happy reading !

(Supreme Court of India) Judicial Custody Beyond 48 Hours Triggers Deemed Suspension: Supreme Court Restores Bail but Questions Reposting to Same Post Introduction An order granting bail concerns the liberty of an accused. The administrative consequences of arrest and detention raise a separate question. This distinction emerges from the Supreme Court’s order in Vishwajeet Singh @ Bishwajeet Ramanand v. State of Bihar & Others, arising out of SLP (Criminal) No. 13108 of 2026, decided on 6 November 2026. The Bench comprising Justice J.B. Pardiwala and Justice Manmohan restored the bail granted to a hospital administrator accused of demanding a bribe. At the same time, it expressed serious concern over his return to the same hospital and the same post after revocation of suspension. Background of the Case The appellant was serving as Manager/Administrator at Sadar Hospital, Samastipur, which the Court understood to be a government hospital. He allegedly demanded ₹4,000 for clearing the complainant’s outstanding wages of ₹36,421. An FIR was registered at the Vigilance Police Station as Case No. 29 of 2025, alleging an offence punishable under Section 7(b) of the Prevention of Corruption Act, 1988. The appellant was arrested and subsequently released on bail by the Sessions Court. The complainant challenged the grant of bail before the Patna High Court. By its order dated 1 July 2026, in Criminal Miscellaneous No. 74033 of 2025, the High Court cancelled bail and directed that the appellant be taken into custody. The appellant approached the Supreme Court against that order. Small Bribe Amount Cannot Determine Entitlement to Bail The Supreme Court noticed that the High Court had interfered with the bail order almost one year after the appellant’s release. From the tenor of the High Court’s order, the Bench understood that considerable weight had been placed on the Sessions Court’s description of the bribe amount as paltry. The Supreme Court accepted that the High Court could be right in observing that the quantum of the bribe was not a relevant factor for determining whether bail should be granted. However, it held that the High Court could have corrected that observation while allowing the appellant to continue on bail. The distinction is significant. Disapproval of a particular reason recorded by the court granting bail does not necessarily require cancellation of the bail itself. The Supreme Court considered restoration appropriate in the circumstances before it. Bail Restored Having Regard to the Nature of the Case The Bench observed that, ordinarily, in cases of this nature, bail should be granted having regard to the maximum punishment that could ultimately be imposed upon conviction. It added that criminal antecedents or particularly gross facts could justify declining to exercise discretion in favour of an accused. These observations must be read in their factual setting. The order does not declare an unconditional entitlement to bail in every corruption prosecution. It records the Court’s approach to cases of this type and identifies circumstances that may warrant a different conclusion. The Supreme Court accordingly set aside the High Court’s order and restored the original bail order passed by the Sessions Court. This relief concerned pretrial liberty; the Court did not determine whether the bribery allegation was proved. Judicial Custody Beyond 48 Hours and Deemed Suspension The Court then addressed what it described as a disturbing feature. Following his arrest, the appellant had remained in judicial custody for approximately 15 days. The Bench observed that when a public servant is arrested and remains in judicial custody for more than 48 hours, deemed suspension follows. It further stated that no specific suspension order was required for that consequence. The State’s counsel informed the Court that the appellant had, in fact, been suspended. Subsequently, however, the suspension had been revoked, and he had been posted back to the very same hospital in the very same position. The Court expressed strong disapproval of that development and required the State to examine it at the earliest and take necessary action in accordance with law. Scope of the Direction to the State The operative direction requires careful reading. The Supreme Court did not itself dismiss the appellant, direct his conviction, or expressly set aside the order revoking suspension. It required the State to look into his reposting and act lawfully. Likewise, the order does not undertake a detailed examination of the applicable service rules governing revocation of suspension or subsequent posting. Accordingly, the decision should not be presented as laying down an absolute prohibition against every reinstatement during a pending criminal case. Its immediate administrative concern was the appellant’s return to the same institution and post in the circumstances recorded. Author’s Comments The decision demonstrates that personal liberty and administrative accountability require separate consideration. Restoration of bail does not resolve the service consequences of detention or establish entitlement to a particular posting. Equally, a corruption allegation remains an allegation until adjudicated. Administrative authorities must act under the applicable rules, while criminal courts decide guilt on evidence. The practical message is clear: correcting an inappropriate observation in a bail order and cancelling bail are distinct exercises. Meanwhile, authorities must independently examine suspension and posting decisions, particularly where an accused public servant returns to the position connected with the alleged misconduct. FULL TEXT OF THE ORDER OF SUPREME COURT OF INDIA 1. Leave granted. 2. This appeal arises from the order passed by the High Court of Judicature at Patna dated 01.07.2026 in Criminal Miscellaneous No.74033 of 2025 by which the Criminal Miscellaneous Application preferred by the respondent no.2-the de facto-complainant, praying for cancellation of regular bail granted to the appellant before us, came to be allowed, and thereby the appellant was asked to be taken in custody. 3. It appears from the materials on record that a First Information Report came to be lodged with the Vigilance Police Station bearing Case No. 29 of 2025, against the appellant for the offence punishable under Section 7(b) of the Prevention of Corruption Act, 1988 (for short, “the Act, 1988”). 4. The materials on record indicate that the appellant is serving as a Manager/Administrator in a Hospital by name Sadar Hospital, Samastipur. This hospital appears to be a Government Hospital. The allegations against the appellant are that he demanded a bribe of the amount of Rs.4,000/- for the purpose of clearing the wages of the de facto complainant to the tune of Rs.36,421/-. 5. In connection with the FIR referred to above, the appellant came to be arrested. Later he was ordered to be released on bail by the Sessions Court. 6. The de facto complainant, being dissatisfied with the order passed by the Sessions Court releasing the appellant on bail, challenged the same before the High Court. The High Court thought fit to interfere with the order passed by the Sessions Court and accordingly cancelled the bail granted to the appellant. 7. In such circumstances, the appellant is here before us with the present appeal. 8. We heard Mr. Awanish Sinha, the learned counsel appearing for the appellant, Mr. Karan Verma, the learned counsel appearing for the de facto complainant (respondent no.2), Ms. Aditi Tripathi, the learned counsel appearing for the State and Mr. Parth Shekhar, the learned counsel appearing for the respondent no. 4, respectively. 9. We take notice of the fact that after a period of almost one year from the date of release of the appellant on bail by the Sessions Court, the High Court thought fit to disturb the order and cancel the bail. 10. Prima facie, it appears from the tenor of the impugned order that what weighed with the High Court was the observation of the Sessions Court, while releasing the appellant on bail that the bribe amount was paltry. 11. Ordinarily, in cases of this type, having regard to the maximum punishment that the trial court would impose, ultimately, if the accused is held to be guilty, bail is to be granted. It is only when there are criminal antecedents or the facts are very gross that the court may decline to exercise its discretion in favour of the accused. 12. The High Court may be right in observing that the quantum of the bribe amount is not a relevant factor for the purpose of determining whether bail should be granted or not. However, High Court could have observed this much and allowed the appellant accused to remain on bail. 13. In such circumstances, referred to above, we are of the view that we should set aside the impugned order passed by the High Court and restore the original bail order passed by the Sessions Court. We order accordingly. 14. However, there is one disturbing feature which we should not ignore. It appears, that upon arrest, the appellant accused was in judicial custody for almost a period of 15 days. Once a public servant is arrested and remains in judicial custody for more than 48 hours, there is a deemed suspension. He being a public servant, he could be said to have been placed under deemed suspension. No specific order is required to be passed in this regard. 15. We are informed by the learned counsel appearing for the State that in fact the appellant was placed under suspension but later, the suspension order came to be revoked, and, shockingly, he has been once again posted in the very same hospital and that too on the very same post. This aspect shall be looked into by the State at the earliest. 16. We expect the State to do the needful in the matter in accordance with law. 17. With the aforesaid, the appeal stands disposed of. 18. Pending application(s), if any, shall stand disposed of. 1. Leave granted. 2. The appeal is disposed of in terms of the signed order. 3. The relevant part of the order is as under:- “13…we are of the view that we should set aside the impugned order passed by the High Court and restore the original bail order passed by the Sessions Court. We order accordingly.” 4. Pending application(s), if any, shall stand disposed of.

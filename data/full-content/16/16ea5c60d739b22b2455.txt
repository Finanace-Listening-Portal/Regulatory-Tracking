Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); Mahesh Reddy Vs PCIT (ITAT Bangalore) Summary: The Bangalore Bench of the Income Tax Appellate Tribunal (ITAT) allowed two appeals filed by Mahesh Reddy for Assessment Years 2017-18 and 2018-19, challenging revisionary orders passed by the Principal Commissioner of Income Tax (PCIT) under Section 263 of the Income-tax Act, 1961. The Tribunal held that the PCIT could not invoke revisionary jurisdiction merely because further or more detailed enquiries were considered desirable, particularly when the Assessing Officer (AO) had already examined the relevant claim during scrutiny assessment. It emphasised that an assessment order cannot be set aside for a fresh enquiry without establishing that it is both erroneous and prejudicial to the interests of the Revenue. The dispute arose from the assessee’s claim of exemption amounting to ₹4,80,33,694 under Section 54F in connection with capital gains arising from the sale of six flats during Financial Year 2016-17. The aggregate sale consideration was stated to be ₹9,85,81,022. The assessee claimed to have invested in a new residential property or villa, with the records referring to investment figures exceeding ₹6 crore. The return for AY 2017-18 declared income of ₹2,52,16,420, and the assessment was completed under Section 143(3) on 28 December 2019, accepting the returned income. The assessment had been selected for complete scrutiny under CASS, including examination of the substantial residential-property exemption claimed by the assessee. The PCIT subsequently examined the assessment records and concluded that the AO had allowed the exemption without conducting adequate enquiries. According to the PCIT, the AO had failed to properly verify the purchase or construction of the new residential property, its address, purchase deed, approved plans, construction permissions, completion certificate and supporting bills or vouchers. The PCIT also questioned whether expenditure on kitchen fittings, doors, windows, decorative work, railings, painting and air-conditioning could qualify as eligible investment in a residential house. It was alleged that several expenses appeared to relate to renovation or refurbishment rather than acquisition or construction. The PCIT further noted inconsistencies concerning the claim under Sections 54 and 54F and the amounts disclosed in the assessee’s submissions. Accordingly, the assessment order was treated as erroneous and prejudicial to Revenue, and the AO was directed to conduct fresh enquiries and reassess the exemption claim. Before the Tribunal, the assessee contended that the PCIT had incorrectly treated the matter as one involving absence of enquiry. The AO had issued notices during the original assessment proceedings, including a notice dated 6 September 2019 seeking details of the properties sold, sale and purchase deeds, transfer expenses and investment in the new residential property. The assessee furnished replies and supporting particulars, including submissions dated 27 September 2019 and 21 December 2019. Further information concerning investment in the residential property had also been submitted. The assessee argued that these enquiries demonstrated application of mind by the AO and that the PCIT could not substitute her own assessment of the extent of verification undertaken. The assessee additionally alleged inadequate opportunity of hearing, stating that the Section 263 notice dated 15 March 2022 was received on 19 March 2022 and required compliance by 22 March 2022, leaving very limited effective time to respond. The Revenue defended the revisionary orders on the ground that the AO had failed to conduct the enquiries necessary to establish the eligibility of the exemption. It maintained that the PCIT was justified in directing fresh examination of the claim, particularly considering the substantial amount involved and the nature of the expenses claimed. Examining Section 263 and Explanation 2, the Tribunal observed that the jurisdictional requirements of an erroneous assessment order and prejudice to Revenue must be satisfied. Explanation 2, inserted by the Finance Act, 2015 with effect from 1 June 2015, identifies circumstances in which an assessment order may be deemed erroneous and prejudicial, including where necessary enquiries or verification have not been made. However, the Tribunal emphasised that the provision does not confer unrestricted or arbitrary powers on the revisionary authority. The discretion must be exercised judicially, on the basis of the assessment records and material establishing the alleged error. The Tribunal distinguished between lack of enquiry and inadequate enquiry. Relying on judicial principles laid down in Malabar Industrial Co. Ltd. v. CIT, CIT v. Sunbeam Auto and CIT v. Gabriel India Ltd., it observed that an assessment cannot ordinarily be revised merely because the PCIT considers that the AO should have undertaken more extensive verification. Where the AO has examined an issue and adopted a legally sustainable view, the PCIT cannot substitute a different opinion simply because another approach is possible. The Tribunal also noted that the AO is not required to provide elaborate reasoning on every deduction or exemption allowed in the assessment order, provided the record demonstrates that the relevant issue was considered. The Tribunal further discussed the responsibility of the revisionary authority to establish the error before setting aside an assessment. Referring to PCIT v. Delhi Airport Metro Express Pvt. Ltd., ITO v. D.G. Housing Projects Ltd. and DIT v. Jyoti Foundation, it observed that where the PCIT believes an existing enquiry was insufficient, the authority must undertake the necessary examination and record a clear finding demonstrating why the assessment is unsustainable. A direction to the AO to undertake fresh enquiries cannot replace the statutory requirement of establishing error and prejudice. The Tribunal also considered decisions explaining that Explanation 2 to Section 263 does not authorise revision of every assessment merely because additional verification might have been undertaken. Applying these principles, the Tribunal found that the AO had examined the assessee’s claim during scrutiny proceedings. The record disclosed notices, replies and particulars concerning the flats sold and investment in the residential property. The Tribunal also referred to bills, vouchers and investment details furnished during the assessment proceedings, including those considered in the connected assessment records. Significantly, the PCIT’s own order acknowledged that the assessee had submitted details of investment amounting to ₹6,66,50,852, against which a deduction of ₹4,64,71,744 had been referred to. These circumstances were inconsistent with the allegation that the exemption had been allowed without enquiry. The Tribunal concluded that the Section 263 proceedings were essentially intended to secure deeper verification and reconsideration of material already available during assessment. The PCIT had not brought sufficient independent material on record establishing that the AO’s conclusion was erroneous and had caused prejudice to Revenue. The revisionary jurisdiction had therefore been exercised without satisfying the statutory conditions. Accordingly, the Tribunal quashed the PCIT’s revision order for AY 2017-18 in ITA No. 1379/Bang/2024. For AY 2018-19, involving ITA No. 936/Bang/2024, the Tribunal found that the facts and basis of revision were similar. Applying its findings for AY 2017-18, it also quashed the revisionary order for AY 2018-19. Both appeals were allowed. The decision establishes that the PCIT must demonstrate a legally sustainable basis for revision under Section 263 and cannot order reassessment merely to conduct further enquiries into an issue already examined by the AO. The ruling concerns the validity of the revisionary proceedings; it does not constitute an independent adjudication that every item of expenditure claimed towards the residential property necessarily qualifies for exemption under Section 54 or Section 54F. The memorandum explaining the amendment reads as under: 6.2 The pre-amended provisions contained in sub-section (1) of section 263 of the Income-tax Act provides that if the Principal Commissioner or Commissioner considers that any order passed by the assessing officer is erroneous in so far as it is prejudicial to the interests of the Revenue, he may, after giving the assessee an opportunity of being heard and after making an enquiry pass an order modifying the assessment made by the assessing officer or cancelling the assessment and directing fresh assessment. The interpretation of expression “erroneous in so far as it is prejudicial to the interests of the revenue” has been a contentious one. In order to provide clarity on the issue it is proposed to provide that an order passed by the Assessing Officer shall be deemed to be erroneous in so far as it is prejudicial to the interests of the revenue, if, in the opinion of the Principal Commissioner or Commissioner,— a. the order is passed without making inquiries or verification which, should have been made; b. the order is passed allowing any relief without inquiring into the claim; c. the order has not been made in accordance with any order, direction or instruction issued by the Board under section 119; or d. the order has not been passed in accordance with any decision, prejudicial to the assessee, rendered by the jurisdictional High Court or Supreme Court in the case of the assessee or any other person.

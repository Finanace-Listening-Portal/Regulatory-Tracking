Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); Avanti Finance Private Limited Vs Our Food Private Limited (NCLT Amaravati) Summary: The NCLT Amaravati admitted Avanti Finance Private Limited’s petition against Our Food Private Limited under Section 7 of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016, concerning a claimed default of ₹8,42,87,387. The financial creditor had disbursed ₹7 crore under a loan agreement dated 31 May 2021. The corporate debtor disputed limitation, the creditor-debtor relationship and the relevance of payments and communications relied upon by the creditor, contending that certain transactions concerned separate farmer loans guaranteed by its CEO. On limitation, the Tribunal examined the bank statements and found that the corporate debtor’s last repayment was on 27 July 2022. It declined to consider the ₹1 lakh payment dated 22 August 2024 because that payment was not made by the corporate debtor. Since the July 2022 repayment fell within three years of the default date advanced by either party, the Tribunal held that fresh limitation extended until 26 July 2025. Although the present petition was filed on 27 March 2026, the original petition had been filed on 19 June 2025 and subsequently returned over registry defects. Following the earlier Rule 63 proceedings permitting withdrawal and fresh filing, the Tribunal considered the original filing date for determining limitation and held the petition timely. On merits, the admitted ₹7 crore borrowing, bank records and NeSL record established financial debt and default exceeding the ₹1 crore threshold. Differences between the NeSL outstanding amount and the petition claim did not, by themselves, undermine that conclusion. The earlier Partner Engagement Agreement did not displace the subsequent loan agreement. The Tribunal also held that a demand notice was not a condition precedent to a financial creditor’s Section 7 petition, and repayment of other lenders or continued operations did not negate the default in question. Relying on Catalyst Trusteeship Ltd. v. Ecstasy Realty Pvt. Ltd., it admitted the petition, declared a Section 14 moratorium, appointed Kantipudi Venkata Raju as interim resolution professional and directed an initial ₹5 lakh deposit towards CIRP expenses. REPLY FILED BY THE CORPORATE DEBTOR: 4. The Corporate Debtor vide Diary No.1712 dated 08.09.2026, filed its reply dated 05.09.2026 contending that the Petition is not maintainable on the following grounds: (i) There is no Creditor-Debtor relationship between the parties. The Petitioner is neither a Creditor much less a ‘Financial Creditor’ for the purpose of sustaining its claim. The parties are in fact partners by virtue of a Partner Engagement Agreement dated 25.09.2020 executed between the parties. The Petition is filed with a mala fide intention to harass and coerce the Corporate Debtor into paying a time barred claim. The Petition is barred by limitation. (ii) A working capital loan has been sanctioned by the Financial Creditor to the Corporate Debtor vide Agreement and Deed of Guarantee executed on 31.05.2021 in terms of which the loan is repayable in T+90 days, where T refers to the actual date of disbursal of the loan. The date of default as mentioned in the Petition is 01.10.2021. Therefore, in terms of the loan agreement dated 31.05.2021, the Petitioner would have to initiate steps for recovery of the loan after the expiry of 90 days from 01.10.2021, i.e. after 29.12.2021. The emails issued by the Financial Creditor to the Corporate Debtor and the personal guarantor dated 20.12.2021, 21.12.2021 and 01.06.2022 refer to a demand notice issued by the Financial Creditor dated 28.02.2022 and a loan recall notice dated 01.06.2022 in respect of the working capital loan extended by it. Therefore, the limitation of three years for the Petitioner to take steps to recover the amounts due would commence from 01.06.2022 and end on 31.05.2025. The rest of the email communications and WhatsApp messages exchanged between the Financial Creditor and the personal guarantor after 01.06.2022 pertain to the repayment of the equipment finance/ asset finance farmer loans sanctioned by the Financial Creditor to the eligible farmers in the States of Andhra Pradesh, Telangana and Maharashtra, to which the CEO of the Corporate Debtor stood as a personal guarantor in his personal capacity since a perusal of these mails would show that they do not refer to the working capital loan at all, but to retail portfolio and institutional repayment of loans sanctioned to eligible farmers. The alleged acknowledgment mails and WhatsApp messages do not acknowledge liability or repayment due of any ascertained amounts or confirm the amount mentioned in the demand notice issued by the Financial Creditor dated 01.06.2022. There has been no request on the part of the CEO of the Corporate Debtor seeking any statement of outstanding dues, so that the working capital loan account could be settled. In the absence of the aforementioned, the emails and WhatsApp messages cannot be construed as an acknowledgement by the Corporate Debtor of the working capital loan due and payable by it. Therefore, the present Petition filed by the Financial Creditor on 19.06.2025 admittedly is barred by limitation. (iii) In so far as particulars mentioned at Part-IV are concerned, while it is true that Our Food Private Limited had availed financial assistance of Rs.7,00,00,000/- by virtue of a loan agreement dated 31.05.2021 for meeting the procurement cost of toor dal supply under the National Federation of Farmers’ Procurement, Processing and Retailing Cooperatives of India Ltd (hereinafter referred to as the “NACOF”) agreement, the rest of the particulars regarding the amount of default and the date on which it allegedly became due are denied as false. Further, the particulars pertaining to personal guarantee of the CEO of the Corporate Debtor and other particulars mentioned in Part-V of the Petition are true, but the other particulars mentioned therein regarding record of default and alleged acknowledgment of debt are false. (iv) On one hand, the Petitioner admitted the date of default as 01.10.2021, while on the other hand, the amount of default is calculated till 05.11.2024 and the record of default issued by NeSL in Form D was generated on 28.08.2023. The Financial Creditor is put to strict proof of the contention that the amount became due and payable on 01.10.2021. (v) Section 5 of the Limitation Act, 1963 would not apply to the present proceedings. Section 5 of the Limitation Act would apply to an appeal or a review/ revision application, which is submitted after the prescribed period. An application under Section 5 of the Limitation Act would then be filed by the appellant or the Petitioner to satisfy the Tribunal that he had sufficient cause for not preferring the appeal or making the application within such period. Thus, this provision cannot be made applicable to the proceedings initiated under IBC. The term “Application” would include a Petition, however, these are not referable to original applications/ petitions/ proceedings filed before this Adjudicating Authority. (vi) The Financial Creditor miserably failed to satisfy that the Petition is within the period of limitation and that limitation is saved in terms of Section 18 of the Limitation Act. (vii) The Hon’ble Supreme Court of India in Suo Moto Writ Petition (C) No.3 of 2020 had clearly laid down that in respect of cases, where the limitation had expired during the period of Covid-19, then the interregnum period between 15.03.2020 and 28.02.2022 would stand excluded for the purpose of limitation under any general or special laws in respect of all judicial or quasi proceedings and where the limitation expired as on 28.02.2022, a further leeway of 90 days was given with effect from 01.03.2022. Thus, this principle will apply only to cases, where the limitation period had expired during the interregnum period i.e. between 15.03.2020 to 01.03.2022 and a further period of 90 days thereon was given. However, the said judgement has no application to the facts of this particular case, as the date of default is 01.10.2021, in which event the limitation starts from that date and it would not get excluded or extended. (viii) The acknowledgement made on 26.07.2022 and part payments made on 22.08.2024, 22.01.2025, 24.02.2025 are towards the institutional repayment of retail portfolio of the farmers, who have obtained loans from the Financial Creditor towards equipment/ asset finance for establishing micro processing units on their agricultural lands. The personal guarantor had issued these emails in his personal capacity and are certainly not for and on behalf of the Corporate Debtor. There are separate loans in respect of which these emails were addressed. There is no admission as alleged by the Financial Creditor. The commitment was in respect of the personal guarantor in his personal capacity. The letters per se do not satisfy the requirements of Section 18 of the Limitation Act. Alternatively, the same was by the personal guarantor in his personal capacity and further the same relate to other loan transactions and not the subject loan. (ix) The judgements cited by the Financial Creditor are of no avail to it. In Re. JC Budhraja Vs Chairman, Orissa Mining Corporation Ltd & Ors., it can be seen that Section 18 (1) was invoked by the appellant contractor at the appeal stage and in respect of an acknowledgment of ascertained sum due in a pending claim. The alleged acknowledgment mails and WhatsApp messages do not acknowledge liability or repayment due of any ascertained amounts or confirm the amount mentioned in the demand notice issued by the Financial Creditor dated 01.06.2022. There has been no request on the part of the CEO of the Corporate Debtor seeking any statement of outstanding dues, so that the working capital loan account could be settled. In the absence of the aforementioned, the emails and WhatsApp messages cannot be construed as an acknowledgement by the Corporate Debtor of the working capital loan due and payable by it. Therefore, the judgment cannot come to the aid of the Financial Creditor. (x) The Financial Creditor filed a Petition under Section 95 of the Code against the personal guarantor before the NCLT, Hyderabad. The application was not filed before the Hon’ble NCLT Hyderabad as a bona fide mistake seeking the same relief. Therefore, the question of invocation of the provisions of Section 14 of the Limitation Act does not arise, since in order to invoke the provisions of Section 14, the Petition before the wrong court/ Tribunal has to be based on the same cause of action, same relief and as against the same parties. These aspects are not satisfied in the instant case. (xi) The three years limitation period would not commence from 01.03.2022 in terms of judgement of the Hon’ble Supreme Court of India in Suo Moto Writ Petition(C) No.3 of 2020. The period would commence from 01.10.2021 and would expire by 30.09.2024 and had actually expired by 30.09.2024. Upon a perusal of the demand notices issued by the Petitioner dated 28.02.2022 and 01.06.2022, it can be seen that the Petitioner alleges that no payment towards the working capital loan was received by it. Without admitting to the contentions of liability alleged by the Petitioner, even if one were to calculate the limitation in terms of the demand notices issued by the Petitioner dated 28.02.2022 and 01.06.2022, the period to take steps would end on 31.05.2025. The present Petition having filed on 19.06.2025 is beyond the period of limitation. (xii) The limitation period of three years would not expire on 28.02.2025, as is being contended by the Financial Creditor herein, but had expired on 30.09.2024. The emails, WhatsApp messages are issued by the personal guarantor in his personal capacity that too relating to the other loan facility. In any event, this cannot be treated as an acknowledgement in terms of Section 18 of the Limitation Act in as much as it was issued after the period prescribed under the limitation i.e. 30.09.2024, moreover, the ingredients specified in Section 18 are missing in the instant case- namely, ascertainment of debt due by the guarantor, or request for statement of account of liability or balance confirmation etc. (xiii) The Financial Creditor has not made a case to condone the delay of 122 days. Even otherwise, the delay is wrongly computed, as it cannot be computed from 28.02.2025, but it has to be computed from 30.09.2024. Computing from that date, the delay would be 543 days, and the delay is inordinate, unexplained and therefore, the Petition is liable to be dismissed. Even otherwise, an application under Section 5 of the Limitations Act is not maintainable for the initiation of the proceedings under Section 7 of the IBC. (xiv) The Financial Creditor in the Petition stated that there has been sanction and disbursement of a loan of Rs.7,00,00,000/- to the Corporate Debtor on 31.05.2021 with its Director as a personal guarantor, who is not a party to present proceedings. This amount, as per the terms of the Loan Agreement dated 31.05.2021 is payable in 4 months’ time inclusive of the grace period of one month. Thus, the same would come into effect on 30.09.2021. The present Petition is filed on 27.03.2026, which is more than four years after the due date/ cause of action arose. (xv) The notice of demand dated 13.11.2024 is only addressed to the personal guarantor and not to the Corporate Debtor herein admittedly. The Petition filed by the Financial Creditor is liable to dismissed on the preliminary ground of want of demand notice upon this Corporate Debtor and barred by limitation. There is no legally enforceable debt as against the Corporate Debtor. (xvi) During Covid-19, the Corporate Debtor submitted a proposal to the Ministry of Food Processing Industries, Government of India with their machinery prototype, which would provide employment opportunities to 1 lakh youth in rural areas, if they avail the low-cost food processing equipment manufactured by the Corporate Debtor. As a result, NBFCs, including the Financial Creditor started approaching the Corporate Debtor proposing to sanction loans to the farmers, provided the CEO of the Corporate Debtor identified and stood as personal guarantor for them. Accordingly, during the period between December 2020 and August 2021, the Financial Creditor herein provided loans to 1383 farmers, wherein the CEO of the Corporate Debtor stood as personal guarantor. The working capital loan (loan account number A100L000032) was sanctioned by the Financial Creditor to the Corporate Debtor on 31.05.2021 towards meeting procurement cost for the supply of Toor Dal under the NACOF agreement for a period of 90 days with a clear understanding that it has to be repaid by the Corporate Debtor within 90 days with a grace period of 30 days. The loan terms include submission of the goods receipt note/ dispatch order issued by the buyer/ recipient/ NACOF. These documents have not been filed by the Petitioner. As such, it is not entitled to the relief sought by it. Moreover, the lis is evidently not in respect of the working capital loan, but in respect of the default on the part of the farmers, to which the CEO of the Corporate Debtor stood as guarantor. This can be clearly seen from the communication issued by NeSL in Form D, which on one hand states that the date of default of the loan account A100L000032 is 01.10.2021, while on the other hand, the date of last repayment is shown as 26.07.2022, which is self-contradictory. The Corporate Debtor denies having paid any amount to the Financial Creditor towards the working capital loan on 26.07.2022, much less, an amount of Rs.50,00,000/. The statement of ledger account filed by the Financial Creditor cannot be taken into consideration by this Adjudicating Authority, as it is generated internally by the Financial Creditor, therefore, its veracity and accuracy is questionable. Thus, the Corporate Debtor is not liable to pay the Financial Creditor any amount and much less a sum of Rs.8,42,87,387/- and the Petition is liable to be dismissed. (xvii) The plea of acknowledgment of debt by the personal guarantor is also of no avail to the Financial Creditor, because (a) the acknowledgment has not been made by or on behalf of the Corporate Debtor herein in writing, but by the personal guarantor in respect of retail portfolios and institutional repayments, where he stood as a guarantor but not in respect of the working capital loan and (b) it has not been signed on behalf of the Corporate Debtor, which are the two ingredients necessary for the Financial Creditor to rely in terms of the Section 18 of the Limitation Act, 1963. (xviii) In fact, in none of the communications, be it emails or WhatsApp messages dated 29.07.2022, 22.08.2024, 22.01.2025, 24.02.2025 referred to the working capital loan, but only retail portfolio and institutional repayments, which makes it clear that the debt discussed was not with reference to working capital, but to other debts. (xix) As both parties were aware that the debts owed by the farmers were the point of discussion, the original Petition filed by the Petitioner before the NCLT, Hyderabad was under Section 95 and against the personal guarantor alone, precisely why the Petitioner filed CP (IB) No.106/95/HDB/2023 before the Hon’ble NCLT, Hyderabad against Mr. Bala Reddy in his personal capacity. The Petitioner was very much aware and conscious that the mails, WhatsApp correspondence related to the issue of farmer loans. However, the Hon’ble NCLT, Hyderabad refused to adjudicate the matter on account of lack of territorial jurisdiction. The Petitioner has now filed the present Petition against the Corporate Debtor, while relying upon the same pleadings/ aspects, which were set out against Mr. Bala Reddy in his personal capacity in the proceedings before the Hon’ble NCLT, Hyderabad. (xx) The Corporate debtor is a going concern and is able to discharge its liabilities satisfactorily. (ii) As per Part IV of Form-1, the Financial Creditor has stated the amount claimed to be in default as Rs.8,42,87,387/-, comprising the principal outstanding and accrued interest, and has stated the date of default as 01.10.2021. In this regard, we may note the settled legal position that for admission of an application under Section 7 of the Code, the adjudicating authority is only required to examine and satisfy itself that a financial debt exists and there is default in relation thereto. In this context, the observations of this Court in Innoventive Industries Limited vs. ICICI Bank and another (2018) 1 SCC 407 are of relevance and are extracted hereunder: ‘30. On the other hand, as we have seen, in the case of a corporate debtor who commits a def9ault of a financial debt, the adjudicating authority has merely to see the records of the information utility or other evidence produced by the financial creditor to satisfy itself that a default has occurred. It is of no matter that the debt is disputed so long as the debt is “due”i.e. payable unless interdicted by some law or has not yet become due in the sense that it is payable at some future date. It is only when this is proved to the satisfaction of the adjudicating authority that the adjudicating authority may reject an application and not otherwise.’ Thus, the concept of a pre-existing dispute, which may be a stumbling block for admission of an application filed under Section 9 of the Code by an operational creditor, has no bearing on an application filed by a financial creditor under Section 7 of the Code.” (ix) However, before admission, this Adjudicating Authority has to satisfy that the Petition is complete and there are no disciplinary proceedings pending against the proposed Interim Resolution Professional (hereinafter referred to as the “IRP”).

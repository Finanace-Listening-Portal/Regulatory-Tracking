Summary: The death of a sole proprietor raises important questions about GST registration, unutilised input tax credit, business stock and outstanding tax liabilities. Under Section 18(3) of the CGST Act, 2017, read with Rule 41 and CBIC Circular No. 96/15/2019-GST dated 28 March 2019, unutilised ITC may be transferred to a legal heir or successor who continues the business, subject to the prescribed conditions and transfer of liabilities. The successor must arrange appropriate GST registration or amendment, complete pending compliance, file FORM GST ITC-02 and secure acceptance of the transferred credit before applying for cancellation of the deceased proprietor’s registration through FORM GST REG-16. Where the business continues as a going concern, the treatment of transferred stock must be examined alongside Schedule II and Section 29(5). Where the business closes, Section 29(5) and Rule 44 govern the liability relating to stock and capital goods, while remaining credit generally cannot be claimed as a cash refund merely because of closure. Sections 85 and 93 determine responsibility for the deceased proprietor’s outstanding tax, interest and penalties, with different consequences depending on whether the business continues or is discontinued. Proper succession documents, timely filings, accurate stock disclosure and coordinated GST compliance are essential to preserving eligible credit and protecting the legal heirs. A trader who built his business over decades passes away, and within days his family, his accountant, and sometimes even a fellow officer want to know what becomes of the GST registration, the stock lying in the godown and the input tax credit sitting in the electronic credit ledger. Is all of it lost? Does the stock suddenly become taxable? Can a son or daughter who already holds a GSTIN simply take the business over? The questions are natural because a sole proprietorship has no separate legal identity. The proprietor is the business, and the GST registration is granted against his personal PAN. When he dies, the registration loses its owner overnight, while the shop, the stock, the debtors and the credit remain very much alive. Fortunately, the Central Goods and Services Tax Act, 2017 and the identical State laws, including the J&K Goods and Services Tax Act, 2017, answer each of these questions. The answers are spread across Sections 18, 22, 29, 85 and 93 and Schedule II of the Act, Rule 41 of the Rules, and Circular No. 96/15/2019-GST issued by the Central Board of Indirect Taxes and Customs on 28 March 2019. This article brings them together and walks through them with examples drawn from ordinary business life. GST Registration After the Death of a Sole Proprietor A common belief is that Section 29 cancels the registration on death. It does not. Section 29(1) says that the proper officer “may”, on his own motion or on an application filed by the registered person “or by his legal heirs, in case of death of such person”, cancel the registration where the business has been discontinued or “transferred fully for any reason including death of the proprietor”. Death, in other words, is a ground on which cancellation may be ordered. The GSTIN continues to exist until the officer passes an order in FORM GST REG-19, and until then return obligations keep falling due. It is the legal heir who sets the process in motion by filing FORM GST REG-16 and choosing “death of sole proprietor” as the reason. Once that application is filed, Rule 21A treats the registration as suspended, so no further returns under Section 39 are required while it is pending. Equally important, Section 29(3) makes it clear that cancellation never wipes out the liabilities of the period before it. Can Unutilised GST Credit Be Transferred to a Legal Heir? Every case of a proprietor’s death turns on a single fact: will anyone carry the business forward? Circular No. 96/15/2019-GST clarifies that the death of a sole proprietor amounts to a “transfer or change in the ownership of business” for the purposes of Section 18(3), Section 22(3), Section 85(1) and Rule 41(1). If the business is continued by a successor, the unutilised credit can travel to him. If the business is closed, it cannot. Two examples show how differently the two roads run. Example 1: Son Continues the Deceased Proprietor’s Business Mr. ABC ran a hardware store in Budgam for nearly three decades. When he died last winter, his electronic credit ledger showed ₹1,70,000 of Central Tax and ₹1,70,000 of State Tax credit, and the shop held stock worth about ₹18 lakh. His son Mr. XYZ already runs a sanitary-ware business in Srinagar under his own GSTIN, also registered in Jammu and Kashmir. Mr. XYZ’s mother and sister are happy for him to take over the hardware store. Legal Heir Documentation and Access to GST Registration The first step is not a tax form but a family document. Under personal law the business devolves on all the heirs, while GST needs one identifiable successor. Mr. XYZ’s mother and sister therefore sign a relinquishment deed in his favour. With that deed, his father’s death certificate and the legal heir certificate in hand, Mr. XYZ visits the jurisdictional officer. Nobody can log in on a deceased person’s behalf, so, following the procedure explained in the GSTN’s FAQs on the subject, the officer adds Mr. XYZ’s as an authorised signatory on his father’s registration. Mr. XYZ’s now files the returns his father could not, and clears any tax due up to the date of death. Registration or Amendment by the Successor Next comes his own registration. Section 22(3) makes a successor liable to be registered from the date of succession. Had Mr. XYZ not been registered, he would have applied in FORM GST REG-01 within thirty days, selecting “death of the proprietor” as the reason, exactly as the Circular directs. Since he already holds a GSTIN in Jammu and Kashmir, no second registration is required. Section 85(2) asks a registered transferee who carries on the transferred business to seek amendment of his registration, so Mr. XYZ files FORM GST REG-14 under Rule 19, adding the Budgam shop as an additional place of business. If he preferred to keep the hardware store separate, he could take a separate registration for it under Rule 11 and use that GSTIN instead. Transfer of Input Tax Credit Through FORM GST ITC-02 Now the credit moves. Section 18(3) permits the unutilised credit in the electronic credit ledger to be transferred where the business changes hands “with the specific provisions for transfer of liabilities”, and Rule 41(1) prescribes FORM GST ITC-02 for any “change in the ownership of business for any reason”. Rule 41(2) requires a certificate from a practising chartered accountant or cost accountant confirming that the transfer carries the liabilities with it. Mr. XYZ therefore gives a written undertaking to clear all GST dues of his father, and the accountant certifies on that basis. Logging in to his father’s GSTIN, Mr. XYZ files ITC-02 showing his own GSTIN as the transferee and ₹3,40,000 as the credit to be transferred. He then logs in to his own GSTIN and accepts it. Under Rule 41(3), the credit lands in his electronic credit ledger the moment he accepts. Cancellation of Deceased Proprietor’s GSTIN and Final Return Only after this does Mr. XYZ file REG-16 on his father’s GSTIN, citing death of the sole proprietor and quoting his own GSTIN as the transferee, so that the two registrations stand linked. The sequence is not a technicality. Paragraph 3(d) of the Circular requires ITC-02 to be filed before the application for cancellation, and Rule 20 expects REG-16 within thirty days of the event. In practice, therefore, the whole exercise should be completed within the first month. Once the officer passes the cancellation order in REG-19, Mr. XYZ files the final return in FORM GSTR-10 on his father’s GSTIN within three months, as Section 45 and Rule 81 require. GST Treatment of Stock Transferred as a Going Concern This is where families worry most, and needlessly. Paragraph 4(c) of Schedule II deems the business assets of a person who ceases to be a taxable person to have been supplied by him, but expressly not where “the business is transferred as a going concern to another person” or “the business is carried on by a personal representative”. Mr. XYZ’s case falls squarely within the exception. Even if one were to treat the passing of a running business as a supply, Serial No. 2 of Notification No. 12/2017-Central Tax (Rate) exempts the transfer of a going concern. Rule 41(4), for its part, simply asks the transferee to account for the inputs and capital goods so transferred in his books, which shows that the law expects the stock to travel alongside the credit. Mr. XYZ records the ₹18 lakh of stock in his books and pays tax when he sells the goods, as any trader would. One caution deserves mention. Section 29(5) asks every person whose registration is cancelled to pay an amount equal to the credit on stock in hand, and it contains no express exception for a transfer of business. The better and widely followed view, reading it together with Section 18(3), Rule 41(4) and Schedule II, is that nothing is payable where stock passes to a successor as a going concern, because the goods remain within the tax chain and will be taxed on sale. The prudent course is full disclosure: show the stock as on the date of death in REG-16 and GSTR-10, with a note that it has passed to the transferee GSTIN in terms of Circular No. 96/15/2019-GST. If Mr. XYZ shifts some of the goods to his Srinagar shop, the movement is not a supply, but the goods should still travel on a delivery challan under Rule 55 and, above the prescribed value threshold, with an e-way bill under Rule 138. Liability for GST Dues of the Deceased Proprietor Suppose that six months after the takeover a scrutiny of Mr. ABC’s returns reveals a short payment of ₹45,000 for an earlier year. Mr. XYZ cannot say the debt was his father’s alone. Section 93(1)(a) makes the legal representative or any other person who continues the business liable for the deceased’s tax, interest and penalty, whether determined before or after the death, and Section 85(1) makes the transferor and the transferee jointly and severally liable for dues up to the date of transfer. The Circular confirms the position. The notice, however, must be addressed to Mr. XYZ and not to his late father. The family should hear it plainly from the outset: taking the credit means taking the past. Example 2: Business Closes After the Proprietor’s Death Mr. TP ran an electronics showroom in a neighbouring district. After his death, his wife and two daughters, both settled in other professions, decide to close it. Here no business is transferred, so no credit can be transferred either. One of the heirs is added as authorised signatory, files the pending returns and applies in REG-16 citing death of the sole proprietor. GST Liability on Closing Stock Under Section 29(5) The stock now attracts Section 29(5). Suppose the goods on the shelves were purchased for ₹5,00,000 and carried input tax credit of ₹90,000 at 18 per cent, while their market value today is only ₹4,00,000, on which output tax would be ₹72,000. The law requires the higher of the two, so ₹90,000 is payable. Rule 44 explains the computation: credit on inputs is worked out proportionately from the purchase invoices, credit on capital goods is reduced pro rata over a useful life of five years, and where invoices are missing the amount is estimated on the prevailing market price. If the ledger holds ₹30,000 of credit, it is set off against this amount and the remaining ₹60,000 is paid in cash. Whatever credit is left after all dues are settled simply lapses, because Section 54(3) allows refund of unutilised credit only for zero-rated supplies and inverted duty structures, not on closure. The final return in GSTR-10 follows within three months of the cancellation. Protection of Legal Heirs Against Personal Liability There is, however, an important protection for the family. Under Section 93(1)(b), when the business is discontinued, the legal representative pays the deceased’s dues only out of the estate and only to the extent the estate can bear them. If an old demand later surfaces against Mr. TP, the daughters’ personal savings are not at stake beyond what they inherited from him. The example carries a further lesson. Had one of the daughters wished to sell the remaining goods through her own registered shop, closure would have been the expensive choice. Tax would have been paid on the stock at the time of cancellation and then charged again on resale, with no credit for the first payment, because she would hold no tax invoice for those goods. Where an heir intends to deal in the inherited stock, structuring the matter as a genuine continuation of the business keeps the credit chain intact. Practical Guidance for GST Officers and Tax Practitioners For tax officers, the most helpful thing is to act promptly when a legal heir arrives with a death certificate and a legal heir certificate, because nothing else can move until the heir is added as an authorised signatory. Where access to the portal is not possible, FORM GST REG-16 itself provides that, in the case of death of a sole proprietor, the application may be made manually by the legal heir or successor before the tax authority. For tax practitioners, three things matter most. The first is the sequence: the heir’s access to the deceased’s GSTIN, pending returns, the successor’s registration or amendment, ITC-02 and its acceptance, REG-16, and finally GSTR-10. The second is the Rule 41(2) certificate, supported by the family’s settlement or relinquishment deed and the successor’s undertaking on liabilities. The third is honest disclosure of the stock in every form that asks for it. Aijaz Hussain Malik is a JKAS officer, presently posted as State Taxes Officer, Circle Budgam.

The next phase will depend on how much liquidity is absorbed naturally and how much the central bank removes through market operations, experts said.The banking system's liquidity surplus fell to around Rs 4.45 lakh crore on September 22 from a peak of Rs 11.16 lakh crore on September 6, a decline of about 60%. The RBI's focus is now shifting from managing an exceptional liquidity surplus to calibrating the remaining cash without creating an abrupt tightening of financial conditions. Advt The Reserve Bank of India has sharply reduced excess liquidity in the banking system through open market sales, variable rate reverse repo operations and foreign exchange operations. The surplus fell to around Rs 4.45 lakh crore on September 22 from Rs 11.16 lakh crore on September 6. The weighted average call rate has also moved close to the 5.25% repo rate, indicating that the immediate pressure from excess liquidity on overnight rates has eased.The liquidity build-up followed large foreign currency inflows under the FCNR(B) deposit mobilisation scheme and subsequent RBI foreign exchange operations that released rupee liquidity into the banking system. Government spending, including salaries and pensions, also added to liquidity.The RBI has announced Rs 1 lakh crore of open market sales for September. It sold Rs 50,000 crore of government securities on September 17 and another Rs 25,000 crore on September 21. A further Rs 25,000 crore sale is scheduled for September 28. These operations remove liquidity more durably because banks and other investors pay the RBI for government securities.The central bank has also used variable rate reverse repo operations to absorb surplus cash temporarily. On September 23, the RBI absorbed Rs 75,026 crore through an overnight VRRR operation at a weighted average rate of 5.24%.From liquidity glut to normalisation Advt The fall in surplus liquidity has already changed conditions in the overnight money market. The weighted average call rate was around 5.24% on September 21, close to the 5.25% repo rate.This is important for monetary policy transmission. When the banking system has a very large cash surplus, overnight rates can remain below the policy rate despite the RBI's stated stance. As the surplus declines, the repo rate becomes a more effective anchor for short term market rates.The RBI therefore does not need to drain every rupee of surplus liquidity immediately. The objective is to keep money market conditions orderly while preventing an excessive surplus from weakening the transmission of monetary policy. Advt What happens after the September OMO sales?The September 28 OMO will take the RBI's announced bond sales for the month to Rs 1 lakh crore. After that, the central bank has several options for dealing with any remaining durable surplus, including further market operations and foreign exchange transactions.Some liquidity can also be absorbed naturally. Tax payments, currency in circulation and stronger credit growth can reduce the cash surplus available with banks without requiring an equivalent amount of RBI intervention.The size of the durable component, however, depends on the methodology used to separate temporary and structural liquidity. Market estimates have varied, so the current Rs 4.45 lakh crore surplus should not be treated as equivalent to the amount of durable liquidity that the RBI needs to remove. Advt One estimate cited by Business Standard put the durable surplus at around Rs 4 trillion and suggested that absorption could extend into the second quarter of FY28. This is an economist estimate rather than an RBI projection.Bond yields face another testThe liquidity withdrawal comes as the government securities market prepares for a heavier supply of bonds in the second half of FY27. Government securities and state development loans are estimated to total around Rs 12.5 lakh crore in H2 FY27, compared with around Rs 9.2 lakh crore in the first half.The 10 year government bond yield was around 7.05% on September 21. Market participants have indicated that the yield could move towards 7-7.25% as the market absorbs government borrowing, additional RBI bond sales and other supply pressures.The additional supply of government securities matters because the RBI's OMO sales add to the amount of bonds that the market has to absorb even as the central bank withdraws liquidity.For banks, the impact is mixed. Lower surplus liquidity reduces the pressure to deploy excess funds at very low short term rates, while higher government bond yields can affect the valuation of banks' bond portfolios and their market funding costs.Will credit conditions tighten?The fall in surplus liquidity does not automatically mean a sharp increase in bank lending rates. The RBI's immediate task is to bring overnight rates into alignment with the repo rate rather than create a shortage of system liquidity.The current call rate of around 5.24% against a 5.25% repo rate suggests that this part of the adjustment has so far taken place without a significant tightening in overnight funding conditions.The next phase will depend on credit growth, government spending, currency demand and tax flows. If these absorb a significant portion of the remaining surplus, the RBI may need fewer additional operations. If surplus liquidity remains high, further durable absorption could be required.

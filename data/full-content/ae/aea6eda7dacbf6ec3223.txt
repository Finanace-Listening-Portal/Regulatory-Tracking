Summary : Revenue growth does not necessarily mean a business is becoming stronger. A company may increase sales while its profit margins shrink, customer payments slow, inventory rises and cash flow deteriorates. Sustainable growth depends on retaining value from every additional sale rather than pursuing revenue at any cost. The experience of businesses such as Webvan illustrates the danger of expanding before the underlying economics are proven. Three common growth traps are chasing sales through excessive discounts and poor contract terms, serving customers whose costs exceed their contributions, and expanding before the business is financially ready. To assess whether growth is healthy, business owners should monitor five vital signs: gross margin, contribution margin, cash conversion, customer concentration and break-even revenue. Other useful indicators include operating cash flow, receivable and inventory days, and revenue per employee. Practical measures include evaluating customer profitability after discounts and service costs, testing expansion plans against realistic downside scenarios, improving collection terms and aligning sales targets with margins. Businesses may deliberately invest ahead of profits, but successful growth requires a credible route to profitability, sound unit economics and sufficient funding. Indian businesses must also account for statutory payment timelines when dealing with micro and small enterprise suppliers and the related income-tax implications of delayed payments. The objective is not to build the largest business possible, but the strongest sustainable business—one that converts demand into lasting profit, profit into cash and cash into long-term value. A quarter later, the bank balance tells a different story. Money is tight. Costs have grown. Customers are slower to pay. Margins have never been thinner. If sales are climbing, why does the business feel weaker than before? Bigger is not the same as stronger The reason is straightforward: growth tells you how much a business sells — not how much value it holds on to. Revenue is the most applauded figure in business. It fills headlines, investor decks and award citations. Everyone understands it, and it’s easy to boast about. It’s also the easiest figure to buy. Discounts buy revenue. Generous credit terms buy revenue. Heavy advertising buys revenue. Taking every order, however poor the margin, buys revenue. None of these proves the business is getting stronger. Size brings attention. Financial strength brings staying power. What history teaches: the frog and the ox In one of Aesop’s fables, told for more than two millennia, a frog sees an ox and tries to make himself just as big. He puffs himself up, again and again, until he bursts. The story has lasted because the temptation never goes away. Businesses puff themselves up as well — more branches, more people, more products, more buyers — without asking whether their foundations can carry the weight. Two businesses, one question Picture two firms in the same city. Business A earns ₹1 crore a year, with healthy margins, firm collections and tight cost control. Business B earns ₹2 crore — but on thin margins, with long customer credit, surplus stock and suppliers it struggles to pay. Which is the stronger business? Revenue alone can’t say. From outside, Business B looks more successful. Business A is far better placed to survive a downturn, seize an opportunity, pay its owner and rest easy. A case from the public record In the late 1990s, the American online grocer Webvan raised massive sums from investors and the stock market. It built costly automated warehouses and moved swiftly into new cities before showing it could deliver groceries profitably in the first ones. Orders rose; losses rose faster. Webvan went bankrupt in 2001. Years later, online grocery delivery grew into a large, successful industry. The idea had been sound. The order was wrong: scale arrived before the economics were proven. Growth magnified losses rather than profits. The same pattern turns up everywhere — in startups that subsidise every order, in traders chasing volume on wafer-thin margins, in manufacturers taking any job just to keep machines busy. The three growth traps Trap 1: Pursuing sales whatever the cost Discounts, rich commissions and poor contract terms can lift revenue while eroding profit. A 10% discount on a product earning a 20% margin removes half the profit on that sale — and teaches customers to wait for the next discount. Fix: Measure the real margin on every major product, customer and channel. Revenue that doesn’t cover its costs deserves examination, not applause. Trap 2: Customers who take more than they give Not every customer is worth having. Some insist on constant discounts, absorb sizeable amounts of staff time, pay late and dispute invoices. Fix: Work out customer profitability after discounts, service costs, credit risk and payment delays. Your largest customer may not be your most profitable — and now and then it turns out to be your least. Trap 3: Expanding before the business is ready New branches, extra staff, bigger offices and larger stock commitments all add fixed costs. Fixed costs remain even when demand doesn’t arrive. Fix: Test the economics of expansion first. Model the expected revenue, extra costs, working capital, payback period and a realistic downside. Prove the model in one location before repeating it in ten. Quick recap The short version: growth adds value only when each extra sale brings in more than it costs — in margin, cash and management time. The Five Vital Signs of Healthy Growth A doctor checks vital signs before judging a patient’s health. An owner should check five before celebrating growth. 1. Gross margin — is each sale worth having? Revenue less direct costs, as a share of revenue. Healthy growth: gross margin steady or rising. Warning: margin falling as sales climb — typically because of discounting, rising input costs or a shift towards cheaper products. 2. Contribution margin — does each sale help fund the business? Revenue less every variable cost, including delivery, commissions, payment fees and returns. Healthy growth: every major product and customer makes a positive contribution. Warning: “profitable” lines that turn negative once all variable costs are included. 3. Cash conversion — does growth become money? How fast sales turn into collected cash, typically tracked through receivable days and inventory days. Healthy growth: collections keep up with sales. Warning: receivables and stock rising faster than revenue — the business is effectively lending its growth to buyers and warehouses. 4. Customer concentration — could one loss sink you? The share of revenue coming from your largest customers. Healthy growth: a widening customer base. Warning: one customer accounting for more than you could survive losing — many advisers treat 20–25% from a single customer as a level to watch, depending on the sector. 5. Break-even revenue — how far are you from safety? The sales required to cover all fixed costs. Healthy growth: actual revenue moving further above break-even. Warning: expansion lifting break-even faster than it lifts sales. Taken together, these answer a more significant question than “How fast are we growing?” They answer: “Is growth making us stronger?” Signs of progress Healthy growth reveals up in several numbers at once. Check them each quarter: Gross margin stays level or improves as revenue increases. Operating cash flow rises broadly in step with profit. Receivable and inventory days don’t grow faster than sales. Customer concentration falls — no single customer becomes dangerously dominant. Revenue per employee improves, showing that growth is more than extra people doing the same work. If revenue rises while two or more of these deteriorate, growth is eating the business instead of building it. The decade ahead Acquiring buyers through digital advertising keeps getting more costly, and investors no longer reward growth at any price. At the same time, platforms, quick commerce and AI-based pricing make it easier than ever to grow revenue fast — and to lose money fast. The companies that endure will question every new rupee of revenue: does it bring margin, cash and a customer who comes back? Next steps for each role Owner or CEO: stop praising revenue on its own in meetings. Ask to see margin and cash next to every sales figure. CFO or accountant: produce a one-page report on the Five Vital Signs for the past eight quarters. Sales head: list the ten largest customers by profit rather than revenue, and discuss the bottom three. Operations head: identify the product or service line where growth adds the most complexity and cost. A smaller-scale example Take a hypothetical bakery that expands from one outlet to four in two years. Revenue doubles. The owner is proud — and permanently short of cash. A quick review explains why. Two new outlets sit in high-rent areas with little weekday footfall. Wastage has climbed from 4% to 11% because baking is planned centrally without daily sales data. A corporate catering contract, won with a steep discount, pays after 75 days. The owner closes one outlet, moves production to daily orders from each branch and renegotiates the catering deal to 30-day terms with a smaller discount. Revenue drops by 15%. Profit triples and the overdraft is cleared within a year. A smaller business, but a healthier one — and finally ready to grow again on firm ground. Frequently asked questions “Is growing at a loss ever justified?” Sometimes — for a defined period, with a clear route to profit and enough funding. Growth without that route silently drains cash. “How can we tell which buyers lose us money?” Work out margin after discounts, delivery, service and payment delays for your main customers. The results are frequently surprising. “Should we slow down to fix the basics?” If two or more vital signs are weakening, yes. Strengthening the base first typically makes later growth quicker and cheaper. Where things stand (October 2026): Indian businesses buying from micro and small enterprises must pay them within the MSMED Act’s time limits (no over 45 days under a written agreement), and since FY 2023-24 late payments can lose their income-tax deduction until actually paid — a rule carried into the Income-tax Act, 2025. Growth that relies on stretching small suppliers now carries a tax cost as well as a reputational one. The toughest pushback — and a reply The pushback: “Many of the world’s best firms lost money for many years while they grew. Amazon reported losses for many years after its listing. If we fixate on margins, we will never build anything large.” The reply: Some firms do invest deliberately in growth ahead of profit — but the ones that succeed typically understand their unit economics, have patient capital and can show how each customer will become profitable over time. Amazon, for instance, stressed free cash flow and operating discipline to its shareholders even in loss-making years. The real distinction is between investing in growth that will pay back and buying revenue that never will. The first is strategy. The second is a slow route to running out of money. If you can’t explain when and how a customer becomes profitable, you’re in the second camp. Common mistakes to avoid Setting sales targets with no margin or collection targets beside them. Paying salespeople on revenue alone. Taking every order because “the machines have to keep running.” Opening the next location before the last one makes money. Celebrating revenue milestones without checking the cash. Try this today (30 minutes) Take your ten largest buyers by revenue. For each, note: 1. Last year’s revenue. 2. Gross margin earned. 3. Average discount given. 4. Average days taken to pay. 5. A rough estimate of the staff time they use. Then rank them by profit contribution, not revenue. The majority of owners who try this find at a minimum one surprise: a “star” customer silently losing money, or a small one silently holding the business up. Choose one action for each surprise — reprice, change terms, invest more or let go. A better definition of success A strong business does more than win customers, raise invoices or open locations. It turns demand into lasting profit, profit into cash, and cash into long-term value. Growth should make a business better able to cope with uncertainty — not more dependent on borrowing, late payments or ever-rising targets. Before you celebrate your next revenue milestone, ask one question: If sales stopped growing tomorrow, would my business still be financially healthy? If the answer is no, the priority may not be more sales. It may be better margins, leaner costs, quicker collections and firmer discipline. Key takeaways Revenue measures what you sell; it doesn’t measure what you keep. Three traps turn growth into weakness: chasing sales at any price, serving unprofitable buyers and expanding too early. Watch the Five Vital Signs — gross margin, contribution margin, cash conversion, customer concentration and break-even revenue. Investing in growth that will pay back is strategy. Buying revenue that never will is a slow decline. Rank customers by the profit they contribute, not by their sales — the answer frequently changes decisions. Aim for the strongest business you can sustain, not the largest one you can afford to run.

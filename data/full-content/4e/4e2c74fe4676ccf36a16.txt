Summary: Indian companies acquiring shares in existing businesses in Dubai or elsewhere in the United Arab Emirates must evaluate the transaction under both the UAE regulatory framework and India’s Foreign Exchange Management Act, 1999. Unlike incorporating a new subsidiary, acquiring an existing UAE company involves purchasing an established business along with its operating history, contracts, employees, banking relationships, liabilities and regulatory obligations. Investments in unlisted UAE entities will ordinarily qualify as Overseas Direct Investment (ODI) under the Foreign Exchange Management (Overseas Investment) Rules, 2022, the Overseas Investment Regulations, 2022 and applicable Reserve Bank of India directions. Indian acquirers must examine eligibility, pricing, financial commitment, ownership structures, overseas subsidiaries and reporting requirements before finalising the transaction. A central consideration is the valuation of the target company. Its commercial value may substantially exceed its nominal share capital because of established customer relationships, brands, licences, contracts and operational infrastructure. The acquisition price should satisfy applicable arm’s-length pricing requirements and be supported by appropriate documentation. Indian companies must also evaluate their aggregate overseas financial commitment, including equity investments, permitted loans and guarantees. Under the applicable automatic-route framework, the generally prescribed financial commitment limit is 400% of the Indian entity’s net worth, subject to statutory conditions and exceptions. UAE legal and tax due diligence is equally important. A share acquisition generally leaves the target’s historical Corporate Tax, VAT, employment, banking and contractual obligations within the acquired company. UAE free-zone status does not automatically guarantee a 0% Corporate Tax rate; eligibility depends on satisfying the conditions applicable to a Qualifying Free Zone Person. Indian buyers should also examine cross-border transfer pricing, financing arrangements, beneficial ownership reporting, deferred consideration, shareholder protections, future dividends and exit taxation. The article explains how an acquisition should be structured from preliminary valuation and FEMA feasibility through due diligence, documentation, AD-bank coordination, UAE ownership transfer and subsequent ODI reporting. Its central message is that an Indian company purchasing a UAE business acquires both its commercial opportunities and its historical risks. Proper transaction planning therefore requires an integrated assessment of commercial value, Indian overseas investment regulations and UAE legal, tax and operational compliance. A running UAE company may already have customers, employees, commercial contracts, licences, banking relationships, supplier arrangements and a track record that could take years to build organically. This makes acquisitions increasingly relevant for Indian groups entering sectors such as trading, technology, logistics, professional services, manufacturing, healthcare and regional distribution. But acquiring shares in a UAE company is not simply a Dubai corporate transaction. From the Indian company’s perspective, it is an overseas investment. That means the transaction needs to work under two systems at the same time: the commercial and regulatory framework of the UAE and India’s foreign exchange rules under FEMA. The most successful acquisitions are usually those where both sides are planned together from the beginning. Buying a UAE Company Is Different From Incorporating One There is an important practical distinction between establishing a new UAE subsidiary and buying an existing UAE company. When an Indian company incorporates a new entity in Dubai, it generally subscribes to newly issued shares. When it acquires an existing business, however, the Indian buyer may purchase shares from: a UAE resident shareholder; another foreign shareholder; an overseas corporate group; or several existing shareholders. The transaction therefore involves not only an overseas investment but also valuation, due diligence, transfer documentation and, in many cases, negotiation over historic liabilities. The Indian buyer is not merely acquiring a UAE trade licence. It is acquiring the corporate history attached to that entity. That difference is fundamental. The Investment Will Commonly Fall Within ODI Where an Indian company acquires equity in an unlisted UAE entity, the investment will ordinarily have the characteristics of Overseas Direct Investment under India’s current Overseas Investment framework. The Indian company therefore needs to examine the transaction under: FEMA; the Overseas Investment Rules, 2022; the Overseas Investment Regulations, 2022; and the applicable RBI directions. This should happen before the share purchase agreement becomes unconditional. One of the least desirable situations is for an Indian buyer to sign a binding UAE acquisition agreement and only afterwards discover that its proposed funding structure does not fit comfortably within the Indian overseas investment framework. Regulatory feasibility should form part of the transaction planning itself. Why Dubai Acquisitions Appeal to Indian Companies There are several reasons an Indian business may prefer buying an existing UAE company rather than forming a new subsidiary. Immediate market access A functioning Dubai business may already have active customers and suppliers. Existing commercial infrastructure The target may hold an office, warehouse, employees and operational systems. Established banking relationships Although ownership changes can trigger fresh bank due diligence, an existing corporate banking history may still have commercial value. Licences and approvals Some businesses require regulatory approvals or activity-specific licences that take time to obtain. Regional expansion A Dubai acquisition can provide an Indian group with a platform to serve the wider GCC, Middle East, Africa and international markets. Brand and customer relationships The value of the target may lie more in its commercial network than in its physical assets. These advantages can justify paying considerably more than the nominal capital of the company. That is where valuation becomes important from both commercial and FEMA perspectives. Valuation Should Be Taken Seriously Consider an Indian company proposing to acquire 70% of a Dubai trading business. The UAE target has: annual revenue of AED 25 million; established customer contracts; supplier credit arrangements; employees; inventory; and a recognised regional brand. Its nominal share capital may be only AED 100,000. The acquisition price could nevertheless be AED 8 million. That does not automatically make the transaction unusual. The economic value of an operating company rarely equals its registered share capital. However, the Indian buyer should be able to demonstrate that the price has a reasonable commercial basis. The overseas investment framework requires pricing to be determined on an arm’s-length basis in accordance with applicable requirements. For a meaningful acquisition, a proper valuation exercise can therefore serve several purposes. It helps management decide whether the transaction makes commercial sense. It supports the remittance process. It provides a defensible record for the Indian AD bank. It also becomes valuable later if the UAE company is sold, restructured or brought into a larger transaction. A poorly documented acquisition price can become much harder to explain several years after the deal. Financial Commitment Must Be Considered Before Agreeing the Deal Size Indian companies planning UAE acquisitions should not examine only the purchase price. The wider funding requirement matters. Suppose an Indian company agrees to acquire a Dubai company for AED 10 million. After acquisition, it also intends to: inject AED 5 million of additional equity; lend money for working capital; and provide a guarantee for a UAE banking facility. The Indian company’s overseas financial exposure is therefore larger than the original AED 10 million acquisition. For an Indian entity, total financial commitment under the overseas investment regime is generally subject to the applicable limit linked to its net worth, along with other conditions. The commonly encountered ceiling under the automatic-route framework is 400% of the net worth based on the last audited balance sheet, subject to applicable rules and exceptions. Management should therefore model the entire UAE investment requirement, not merely the purchase consideration. This is particularly important in capital-intensive businesses such as manufacturing, contracting, logistics, warehousing and commodity trading. Acquiring 100% Is Not Always Necessary A UAE acquisition does not necessarily mean purchasing the entire company. An Indian company may acquire: a minority strategic interest; 51% control; 75%; or the entire equity. The percentage should follow the commercial objective. For example, an Indian manufacturer entering the UAE may prefer acquiring 60% of an established local distributor while allowing the original founders to retain 40%. The UAE founders continue contributing market knowledge and customer relationships, while the Indian company obtains control and regional access. Such a structure can be commercially attractive. But minority and joint-venture acquisitions create their own issues. The shareholders’ agreement becomes particularly important. It should address matters such as: board control; reserved matters; dividend policy; future funding; transfer restrictions; deadlock; non-compete obligations; exit rights; and dispute resolution. FEMA compliance tells the Indian company whether and how it can make the overseas investment. It does not replace proper UAE shareholder protection. Due Diligence in Dubai Should Go Far Beyond the Trade Licence One of the biggest acquisition mistakes is treating a valid UAE licence as evidence that the company itself is healthy. A trade licence establishes that the entity is authorised to conduct specified activities. It does not tell the buyer whether the company has undisclosed liabilities. Before acquiring shares, the Indian buyer should normally examine areas such as: corporate ownership records; constitutional documents; licence status; regulatory approvals; material contracts; litigation; employees; visas; outstanding loans; bank facilities; guarantees; VAT compliance; Corporate Tax compliance; accounting records; customs exposure; related-party transactions; AML obligations where applicable; and beneficial ownership filings. The exact scope depends upon the business. Buying a Dubai consultancy company is different from buying a logistics operator with warehouses, vehicles and customs activity. Acquiring shares means inheriting the company. That includes its history. UAE Corporate Tax Has Made Tax Due Diligence More Important Historically, some investors approached UAE acquisitions with relatively limited corporate income-tax diligence. That approach is no longer appropriate. The UAE now has a federal Corporate Tax regime. A UAE-incorporated company will ordinarily need to consider Corporate Tax registration, taxable income, filing obligations and related compliance subject to the rules applicable to it. Therefore, when an Indian company acquires a UAE business, it should examine whether the target has properly addressed its Corporate Tax obligations. Management should understand: whether the entity is registered; which tax periods have arisen; whether returns have been filed where due; whether accounting records are adequate; whether related-party transactions exist; whether elections or reliefs have been claimed; and whether the company operates in a free zone. The acquisition agreement should allocate responsibility for pre-completion tax liabilities. A tax problem relating to the period before acquisition should not become an unexpected post-acquisition cost simply because the buyer failed to ask the right questions. Free Zone Status Requires Particular Attention Dubai contains numerous free zones, and Indian buyers frequently encounter acquisition targets established within them. A common mistake is assuming that a free-zone target automatically enjoys 0% Corporate Tax. The UAE Corporate Tax framework is more specific. A Qualifying Free Zone Person may benefit from a 0% rate on Qualifying Income if the relevant statutory conditions are satisfied. The acquisition itself does not guarantee that the company continues to qualify. An Indian buyer should therefore understand: the target’s actual activities; where its customers are located; the nature of its income; transactions with mainland entities; substance; transfer-pricing position; and compliance with the conditions applicable to Qualifying Free Zone Persons. A business should not be valued on the assumption of a permanent 0% tax position unless that assumption has been properly tested. The UAE Company’s Past VAT Position Can Follow the Buyer VAT is another important area. Suppose an Indian company acquires a Dubai trading business. The target has been operating for four years and appears profitable. After completion, the new owner discovers that: some taxable supplies were incorrectly treated; input VAT records are incomplete; historic returns contain errors; and supporting tax invoices cannot be located. Those liabilities belong to the company. Since the Indian buyer acquired the shares rather than merely purchasing selected business assets, the historic company remains the same legal entity. This is one reason share acquisitions require deeper tax diligence than simply forming a clean new subsidiary. Where material exposures are identified, the buyer may seek: price adjustment; warranties; indemnities; escrow; or resolution before completion. The right solution depends on the transaction. Transfer Pricing Becomes Relevant After Acquisition Once an Indian company acquires a UAE entity, the two businesses become part of the same group. That often creates cross-border related-party transactions. For example: Indian parent → UAE subsidiary The Indian parent may provide: management support; technical services; software; intellectual property; procurement assistance; financing; guarantees; or shared personnel. The UAE company may provide regional marketing, distribution or other services back to India. These transactions should not be priced casually. Both India and the UAE have transfer-pricing frameworks requiring related-party dealings to be evaluated on an arm’s-length basis. The acquisition model should therefore consider not only the purchase price but also how the two companies will transact after completion. A deal can be commercially successful and still develop tax problems if post-acquisition intercompany arrangements are poorly documented. Should the Acquisition Be Funded With Equity or Debt? This question becomes relevant particularly when the UAE target needs additional capital after the acquisition. The Indian parent might consider: subscribing for new shares; providing permitted debt; supporting third-party UAE borrowing; or using a combination of funding methods. Each route can have different FEMA, tax and accounting consequences. For example, a shareholder loan may appear attractive because repayment can be more flexible than returning share capital. But the overseas investment rules regulate debt financial commitment. The UAE company will also need to consider the Corporate Tax treatment of interest and related-party financing. Financing should therefore be designed as part of the acquisition structure rather than improvised when the UAE business later needs cash. UAE Banking Should Be Addressed Before Completion A share acquisition can trigger enhanced KYC review by the UAE company’s bank. Even where the company already has an active account, the bank will generally need to understand changes in ownership and ultimate beneficial ownership. The Indian acquirer should be prepared to provide information relating to: its corporate structure; shareholders; directors; audited financial statements; source of acquisition funds; nature of business; expected UAE transactions; and ultimate beneficial owners. This is especially relevant where the target’s operations depend heavily upon trade finance, credit lines or payment facilities. An acquisition should not be considered operationally complete until management understands whether the banking arrangements will continue satisfactorily after the ownership change. Acquisition Documents Should Reflect FEMA Conditions The share purchase agreement is normally governed by the commercial negotiations between buyer and seller. However, for an Indian buyer, FEMA compliance should also influence the transaction documentation. For example, completion may need to be conditional upon: satisfactory due diligence; required corporate approvals; regulatory approvals where applicable; AD-bank documentation; appropriate valuation; release of existing security; and satisfaction of UAE ownership-transfer requirements. The purchase agreement should also clearly identify the consideration. Problems can arise where side payments, deferred amounts, earn-outs or unusual payment arrangements are agreed without checking whether they can be implemented under the Indian foreign exchange framework. Commercial creativity should not create regulatory uncertainty. Deferred Consideration and Earn-Outs Need Careful Review Many acquisitions do not involve payment of the entire price on closing. The seller may agree to receive: part of the consideration immediately; another amount after 12 months; and an additional earn-out depending on future revenue. This can be commercially sensible because it protects the buyer against overpaying. But an Indian company should examine whether the proposed payment structure is permitted under the applicable FEMA framework and how it should be reported. Similarly, if the seller remains involved after completion, the distinction between: purchase consideration; salary; consulting fees; and earn-out payments should be clear. A transaction should not use multiple payment labels merely to achieve a preferred remittance outcome. Substance matters. Buying Assets and Buying Shares Are Not the Same Transaction Indian investors sometimes assume that acquiring a company’s business and acquiring the company itself are interchangeable. They are not. In a share acquisition, the Indian company acquires ownership of the UAE entity. The UAE company’s: contracts; employees; tax history; assets; liabilities; and licences generally remain with the same company, subject to relevant legal and contractual requirements. In an asset acquisition, the buyer acquires selected assets or business components. A new UAE entity may then operate those assets. The appropriate choice depends on the business. A share acquisition may preserve customer contracts and operating continuity. An asset acquisition may provide better protection from certain historical liabilities. However, transferring licences, employees, leases or contracts can make an asset acquisition considerably more complicated. The structure should be decided after due diligence, not before it. Step-Down Expansion Through Dubai Should Be Planned One reason Indian companies acquire UAE businesses is to use Dubai as their regional platform. An Indian group may ultimately operate: Indian Parent ↓ Dubai Company ↓ Saudi / Oman / European Subsidiaries This can make strong commercial sense. Dubai provides international connectivity, regional management infrastructure, professional services and access to a broad range of markets. But the Indian company should consider the intended wider structure when making the initial UAE investment. The overseas investment framework contains provisions relevant to foreign entities with subsidiaries and step-down subsidiaries. Accordingly, management should not assume that once funds reach Dubai, all future overseas investment by the UAE company becomes irrelevant from an Indian FEMA perspective. The regional plan should be mapped early. Dividends From the UAE Need Indian Tax Analysis A profitable UAE subsidiary may eventually distribute dividends to its Indian parent. At that stage, the analysis moves beyond FEMA. The Indian company should consider the Indian tax treatment of foreign dividends, applicable treaty provisions and foreign tax credit principles where relevant. The UAE Corporate Tax position of the operating company is separate from the taxation of the Indian shareholder. This distinction is important because promoters sometimes focus exclusively on the UAE’s tax environment. The economic return to the Indian parent depends upon the full chain: UAE operating profit → UAE Corporate Tax → dividend → Indian tax consequences A lower tax burden in Dubai does not automatically mean that the final group-level tax cost is the same. The transaction should therefore be modelled from profit generation through ultimate repatriation. Exit Planning Should Begin at Entry Suppose an Indian company acquires a Dubai technology business for AED 12 million. Five years later, a global investor offers AED 45 million for it. That is a successful commercial outcome. But the Indian company must then consider: FEMA rules governing transfer of the overseas investment; valuation and pricing; sale documentation; receipt and repatriation of consideration; Indian capital-gains taxation; UAE tax consequences; and any treaty implications. The future exit should not determine whether the acquisition is made, but it should be understood before closing. A clean investment and reporting history can make the eventual sale considerably easier. Practical Example: Acquiring a Dubai Distribution Company Consider an Indian consumer-products manufacturer looking to enter the UAE. It identifies a Dubai distributor with: AED 40 million annual turnover; 25 employees; a warehouse; retail relationships; established bank facilities; and operations across several Emirates. The Indian company proposes to purchase 80% of the shares while the UAE founder retains 20%. At a commercial level, the deal offers an immediate market presence. But the buyer should not begin with the share transfer alone. A disciplined process would examine: FEMA Can the Indian company make the proposed ODI and financial commitment? Valuation Does the purchase price have an arm’s-length commercial basis? Due diligence Are the target’s licences, contracts, taxes, employees and liabilities in order? UAE Corporate Tax Has the company met its registration and filing obligations? VAT Are historic returns and records reliable? Banking Will facilities remain available after the ownership change? Shareholder arrangements What rights will the 20% minority shareholder retain? Post-acquisition transactions Will the Indian parent supply goods or provide services to the Dubai subsidiary? Future GCC expansion Will the UAE company establish operations in Saudi Arabia or elsewhere? Each issue affects the same acquisition. That is why international M&A should not be divided into disconnected India and UAE exercises. A Smaller Acquisition Can Carry the Same Structural Risks The same principles apply even where the transaction is much smaller. An Indian software company may acquire a Dubai consultancy for AED 500,000. There may be: no warehouse; no external financing; four employees; and only a handful of customer contracts. The due diligence may be considerably narrower than in a AED 50 million acquisition. But the core questions remain: Is the investment FEMA-compliant? Does the price make sense? Does the company actually own what the buyer expects? Are there outstanding UAE tax or compliance obligations? Will the corporate bank account survive the ownership change? What liabilities remain inside the target? Small transactions should be proportionately reviewed, not blindly executed. The Commercial Licence Is Only One Part of the Value A frequent misconception in Dubai transactions is that an existing company is valuable simply because it has an old trade licence. Age can be useful. But it is not automatically value. An older UAE company may also carry: historic tax issues; unpaid liabilities; outdated shareholder arrangements; unresolved labour claims; poorly maintained accounting records; or dormant business activities. Conversely, a relatively young business may have excellent contracts, strong revenue and clean governance. An Indian acquirer should therefore pay for economic value—not merely the age of the licence. This sounds obvious, yet it can materially affect smaller Dubai acquisitions where transactions are sometimes negotiated primarily on the basis of “company history”. What Management Should Ask Before Acquiring the Shares Before committing to a UAE share acquisition, the Indian company’s management should be able to answer the following questions. Why are we buying rather than incorporating? Is the value in customers, licences, staff, contracts, technology or market access? What exactly are we acquiring? Shares, assets, intellectual property or a combination? What is the company’s real value? Has the acquisition price been properly assessed? What liabilities stay inside the UAE entity? Have tax, legal, employment and financial exposures been examined? Can the Indian company make the investment under FEMA? Does the transaction fall within the permitted ODI and financial-commitment framework? How will the purchase be funded? Equity, internal accruals, borrowing or another permitted source? Will additional funding be required after completion? This can materially affect the overseas financial commitment. What happens to the UAE bank account? Has the bank been informed of the change in ownership? Will the UAE company remain a standalone business or become a regional headquarters? This affects the long-term corporate structure. How will profits eventually return to India? Dividend and other intercompany flows should be understood early. How will the Indian company exit? A future disposal should be capable of being implemented cleanly under both jurisdictions. A Practitioner View: The Cheapest UAE Acquisition Can Become the Most Expensive An established Dubai company offered at an attractive price can be tempting. Perhaps the seller wants to exit quickly. Perhaps the licence has been operating for years. Perhaps the business already has customers. That can represent a genuine opportunity. But the purchase price is only one component of acquisition cost. Suppose an Indian buyer saves AED 300,000 by purchasing an older company rather than establishing and developing a new business. After completion, it discovers: weak accounting; tax corrections; an employee dispute; old receivables that are unlikely to be collected; and bank KYC issues caused by the ownership change. The supposed discount disappears quickly. An acquisition should therefore be evaluated on enterprise value after liabilities, not simply on the seller’s asking price. This becomes even more important for a foreign buyer because repairing UAE problems from India can consume both management time and professional fees. A Better Sequence for an India–UAE Acquisition A practical acquisition sequence can be summarised as follows. First, establish the commercial objective. Why does buying this UAE company create more value than incorporating a new one? Second, undertake preliminary valuation. Determine whether the asking price is commercially reasonable. Third, review FEMA feasibility. Confirm the Indian company can make the proposed overseas investment and financial commitment. Fourth, perform UAE due diligence. Review corporate, tax, financial, banking, employment and regulatory matters. Fifth, design the ownership and funding structure. Decide the percentage being acquired and how future capital will be provided. Sixth, negotiate the transaction documents. Use warranties, indemnities and completion conditions appropriate to the identified risks. Seventh, coordinate the Indian AD-bank and UAE transfer process. The regulatory and commercial steps should work together. Eighth, complete the UAE ownership change. Update the relevant corporate, licensing, beneficial ownership and banking records. Ninth, complete Indian ODI reporting. Keep the FEMA trail clean from the beginning. Finally, integrate the business. Align accounting, tax, transfer pricing, banking and management controls between India and the UAE. This approach requires more planning than simply signing a share-transfer document, but it considerably reduces post-acquisition surprises. Conclusion Acquiring shares in an existing UAE company can provide an Indian business with something that a fresh incorporation cannot immediately offer: operating history. The Indian acquirer may gain customers, employees, licences, contracts, regional relationships and an established presence in Dubai from the first day of ownership. That commercial advantage can be significant. But a UAE share acquisition should never be treated as merely purchasing a trade licence. For the Indian company, the investment must fit within the FEMA Overseas Investment framework. For the UAE business, the buyer must understand the company’s Corporate Tax, VAT, banking, employment, regulatory and commercial history. And after completion, the Indian parent and UAE subsidiary must operate within a proper framework for related-party transactions, funding and governance. The central principle is straightforward: When an Indian company acquires a UAE entity, it acquires both the opportunity and the history of that company. The opportunity is what justifies the purchase. The quality of due diligence determines how much of the history the buyer may eventually have to pay for.

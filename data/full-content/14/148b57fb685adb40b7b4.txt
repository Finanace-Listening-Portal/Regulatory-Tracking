Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); Shreeram Builders Vs Upendra Kantilal Thanawala And Others (Supreme Court of India) Arbitrators Cannot Rewrite Contract: Supreme Court Orders Refund with 9% Interest in Shreeram Builders Case Expanded Summary: The Supreme Court in Shreeram Builders v. Upendra Kantilal Thanawala and Others examined whether an arbitral tribunal, while resolving a dispute over a development agreement, could grant specific performance by departing from express contractual stipulations. The dispute arose from an agreement dated 6 August 2003 relating to the Thanawala family’s undivided 2/9th share in properties at Thane. The agreement contemplated development rights, an estimated consideration of ₹2,28,75,000 based on 25,000 square feet of floor space index (FSI), a schedule of instalment payments and eventual conveyance. Importantly, it made timely payment an essential contractual obligation and prohibited execution of the conveyance until all instalments and other sums due were paid. The consideration was also adjustable upon specified events, including changes in available FSI at the stage of sanction of the plan. After the landowners terminated the agreement by notice dated 9 April 2008, a three-member arbitral tribunal adjudicated the dispute. By majority award dated 10 December 2011, it declared the termination illegal and granted specific performance upon the builder depositing ₹10,91,30,164 within three months. In quantifying the amount, the majority increased the agreed rate of ₹915 per square foot to ₹5,490 per square foot, citing the passage of time and escalation in land prices, and made provision for alternative damages if the grant of specific performance were reversed. The Principal District Judge, Thane, ultimately upheld the majority award in proceedings under Section 34 of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996, but the Bombay High Court set it aside in appeals under Section 37. The builder challenged that decision before the Supreme Court. The Supreme Court explained that the character of a document depends on the rights and obligations it creates, not merely its title or the stamp duty paid. Thus, calling the instrument an “Agreement for Development” and paying stamp duty at a particular rate did not conclusively determine whether it operated as an agreement to sell. That classification, however, was not decisive: under either description, the arbitral tribunal remained bound to respect the parties’ bargain. The majority award had overlooked the contractual sequence requiring payment before conveyance and had treated the consideration as fixed despite provisions making it dependent on future events. The builder had not obtained the sanctioned plan contemplated by the agreement. Nor was there adequate reasoning or computation supporting the sixfold enhancement of the contractual rate. The Court also distinguished the landowners’ acceptance of the first six delayed instalments without protest from a general abandonment of the agreed payment schedule. Their conduct affected the treatment of those instalments but did not extinguish every subsequent deadline or the express stipulation that time was of the essence. Further defects included directions concerning excluded portions of the property and interests of persons who were not parties to the arbitration, and adjustments based on an earlier memorandum of understanding notwithstanding a clause superseding prior agreements. The alternative damages direction was also disapproved in the circumstances. These were not merely competing reasonable interpretations of an ambiguous contract; they amounted to material departures from the agreed terms, contrary to the obligation under Section 28(3) to take account of the contract. While reiterating the limited judicial review of arbitral awards under Sections 34 and 37, the Supreme Court held that deference to an arbitrator’s plausible interpretation cannot protect an award that substitutes a new bargain for the parties’ express obligations. The Court noted that the unamended Section 34 governed because the relevant applications had been filed before 23 October 2015. Accordingly, it did not restore the award granting specific performance. Nevertheless, the landowners were not entitled to retain the amounts they had received indefinitely after termination. Although Clause 54 contemplated repayment without interest in the specified circumstances, the landowners had failed to refund the money when the agreement was terminated. Exercising its power under Article 142 of the Constitution to do complete justice, the Court directed them to refund the amounts received with simple interest at 9% per annum from the respective dates of payment until actual refund. It also directed the return of the builder’s deposits lying with the courts, together with accrued interest, if any, and left the parties to bear their own costs. Facts of the Case The dispute arose from an agreement dated 6 August 2003 between Shreeram Builders and members of the Thanawala family concerning their undivided 2/9th share in properties situated at Thane. Although titled an “Agreement for Development”, the document contained several provisions relating to payment of consideration and eventual conveyance. The estimated consideration was ₹2,28,75,000, calculated with reference to projected Floor Space Index (FSI) of 25,000 square feet. The agreement prescribed a payment schedule, repeatedly stipulated that time was of the essence, and linked execution of the conveyance deed to payment of all instalments and other amounts due. It also excluded specified portions of the property, including a bungalow, gardens and a room in a chawl. The landowners terminated the agreement through a notice dated 9 April 2008. The dispute was thereafter referred to a three-member arbitral tribunal. Arbitral Award and Court Proceedings The majority award dated 10 December 2011 held the termination illegal and granted specific performance in favour of the builder, subject to deposit of ₹10,91,30,164 within three months. In arriving at this amount, the arbitrators increased the contractual rate from ₹915 to ₹5,490 per square foot, being six times the agreed rate, on account of passage of time and escalation in land prices. They also provided for alternative damages if the relief of specific performance was subsequently reversed. After an earlier remand, the Principal District Judge, Thane, upheld the majority award under Section 34 of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996. However, the Bombay High Court set it aside in appeals under Section 37. The builder approached the Supreme Court. Issues Before the Supreme Court The principal questions concerned whether the arbitral tribunal had permissibly interpreted the agreement or had rewritten it, and whether the High Court was justified in setting aside an award already upheld under Section 34. The Court also considered whether the landowners could retain the consideration after terminating the agreement. 1. Substance of the Agreement Prevails over Its Title The Supreme Court rejected the proposition that the document’s title and the stamp duty paid conclusively established its character. The High Court had relied partly on the payment of stamp duty at 1%, applicable to a development agreement, rather than the higher duty applicable to an agreement to sell. The Supreme Court explained that the rights and obligations created by an agreement determine its legal character. Its nomenclature and the stamp duty paid cannot, by themselves, settle that question. Ultimately, however, classifying the document was unnecessary to decide the validity of the award. Whatever its description, the parties remained bound by its express obligations and the sequence in which those obligations had to be performed. 2. Arbitrators Must Respect the Contractual Sequence The Court found that the majority award treated the dispute as an ordinary suit for specific performance while overlooking the contractual sequence governing the transaction. The agreement required payment of all amounts due before execution of the conveyance deed. The estimated consideration had not been paid in full within the stipulated period. Further, the actual consideration remained dependent on events contemplated by the agreement. Directing conveyance without giving effect to these conditions amounted to substituting a different arrangement for the parties’ bargain. The Court held that this violated the requirement under Section 28(3) to take account of the contractual terms. 3. Acceptance of Delayed Instalments Did Not Erase All Deadlines The landowners had accepted the first six instalments belatedly without protest and without issuing the contractual notice requiring the default to be remedied. The Supreme Court recognised the effect of that conduct concerning those accepted instalments. However, it held that such acceptance did not wipe out the payment schedule or the repeated stipulation that time was of the essence. It could not mean that the landowners were bound to accept every subsequent payment whenever the builder chose to tender it. 4. Consideration Could Not Be Treated as Finally Fixed The agreement linked consideration to several factors, including changes in FSI before sanction of the municipal plan, additional FSI arising from regularisation of certain land, and construction of basements. As the builder had not secured a sanctioned plan, the total consideration remained open to adjustment. The tribunal could not ignore these provisions and proceed as though the consideration had become finally determinable independently of the agreed events. The Court also disapproved the sixfold increase in the contractual rate. The arbitrators had provided neither a computation nor adequate reasoning to justify substituting ₹5,490 per square foot for the agreed ₹915. 5. Other Departures from the Agreement The Court identified further defects in the award. It found that the conveyance direction extended to portions of property excluded by the agreement and affected interests of persons who were not before the tribunal. It also held that adjustments relating to payments under an earlier memorandum of understanding disregarded the clause superseding prior agreements. The alternative damages direction was likewise disapproved in the circumstances of the case, including its operation upon a superior court reversing the specific-performance relief. These departures went beyond choosing between plausible interpretations of the contract. 6. Scope of Review under Sections 34 and 37 The Supreme Court reiterated that a reasonable contractual interpretation adopted by an arbitrator ordinarily deserves judicial deference. Courts examining an award do not exercise a regular appellate jurisdiction over its merits. That protection, however, does not permit arbitrators to replace express contractual terms with new ones. The Court also clarified that the unamended Section 34 applied, because the parties’ applications had been filed before 23 October 2015. Its discussion of public policy and patent illegality must therefore be read in that statutory context. Refund with Interest Despite the No-Interest Clause Although the Court found no ground to restore the award, it noted that the landowners had also failed to comply with the agreement. Clause 54 required return of the consideration, without interest, where the agreement ended in the circumstances specified and no construction had been carried out on their share. They had nevertheless retained the money after termination. The Court observed that the no-interest condition would have applied had the money been returned when the agreement was terminated on 9 April 2008. Having retained it thereafter, the landowners were directed, under Article 142, to refund the amounts received with simple interest at 9% per annum from each payment date until refund. The builder’s deposits lying with the courts were also directed to be returned with accrued interest, if any. The parties were left to bear their own costs. Practical Takeaway The judgment draws a clear boundary between interpreting a contract and rewriting it. An arbitral tribunal may resolve ambiguity, but it cannot disregard payment conditions, alter agreed rates without contractual foundation, or direct conveyance contrary to the agreed sequence. It also demonstrates that failure to obtain specific performance does not entitle the other party to retain refundable consideration indefinitely. In this case, the Supreme Court addressed that continued retention through its constitutional power to do complete justice. As noted earlier, the arbitration proceedings centred on the agreement dated 06.08.2003. One of the main issues considered by the arbitral tribunal was the status of this agreement, that is, whether it was a development agreement or an agreement to sell. The majority award held it to be an agreement to sell while the dissenting award held otherwise. The learned Principal District Judge, Thane, upheld the majority award and affirmed the finding therein that the agreement was an agreement to sell and was not just for development. However, in appeal under Section 37 of the Act of 1996, the Bombay High Court reversed that finding and opined that it was nothing more than a development agreement. Notably, it was recorded on the first page of the agreement that the agreement value was ₹2,28,75,000/- and that the document had suffered stamp duty of ₹2,29,000/-. The stamp duty, therefore, came to 1% of the agreement value. One of the factors that weighed with the High Court to hold that the agreement was a development agreement and not an agreement to sell was the fact that stamp duty of 1% was payable only on development agreements as an agreement to sell had to suffer stamp duty of 5% of the value. The High Court, accordingly, opined that, as the subject agreement had suffered only 1% stamp duty, it was clearly intended to be a development agreement only. 14. However, we are not persuaded to agree that this fact was sufficient to categorise the agreement as a development agreement. As is wont to happen, attempts are made by parties to a document to escape the rigour of paying higher stamp duty and in the course of that endeavour various measures are adopted, including portrayal of the document as something other than what it is actually intended to be. Naming the document as a development agreement and paying commensurate stamp duty may, thus, be a means of escaping the higher stamp duty applicable to an agreement to sell. The settled legal position is that the nomenclature of a document would not be decisive as to its nature. In Sasan Power Limited vs. North American Coal Corporation (India) Limited2, this Court held that it is the rights and obligations created under an agreement that determine its legal character. Thus, it is the contents of the agreement that would determine its actual nature. Hence, the stamp duty did not conclude the issue and the agreement had to be examined, not only for its import but also what it entailed in the context of the arbitration proceedings. 15. Significantly, the first paragraph of the said agreement, after naming the five Thanawalas, stated that they would be referred to as the ‘Vendors of the one part’. The appellant firm and its three partners, viz., Ramesh Amrutlal Mehta, Deepak Ramesh Mehta and Alpa Ketan Mehta, were referred to as the ‘Purchaser’, but the word ‘Purchaser’ was then cut out and, by hand, the word ‘Developers’ was inserted. However, this correction was not initialled by whoever had made the same. Further, in the rest of the document, the appellant and its partners continued to be referred to as the ‘Purchaser’ and no corresponding corrections were made. We then find that the title to Lands A, B and C was traced out in great detail, dating back to the initial sale deed executed on 23.05.1921, whereunder the two brothers, Govindlal and Maneklal, had purchased Land A. Details of the lineage of the Thanawalas and how the property devolved thereafter were also set out at great length. These details took up paragraphs ‘A’ to ‘Z’ of the agreement. Paragraph R noted that Kantilal and his sons, Upendra and Pankaj, had entered into a Memorandum of Understanding (MoU) on 15.03.2002 with Ramesh Mehta and Deepak Mehta, the appellant firm’s partners, in respect of their share in the property. As regards the five Thanawalas who constituted the ‘Vendors’, paragraph ‘W’ stated that Upendra and Pankaj had 8/90th share each and Vishal, Upendra’s son, was entitled to 2/90th share while Kunal and Niket, Pankaj’s sons, had 1/90th share each. In effect, their shares put together came to 20/90th or 2/9th undivided share in Lands A, B and C. 16. Paragraph ‘CC’ recorded that the ‘Purchaser’ had approached the ‘Vendors’ with an offer to develop the said property by constructing a shopping, commercial and residential complex thereon. Paragraph ‘DD’ recorded that the ‘Vendors’ had agreed to allow the ‘Purchaser’ to develop their share in the said property, excluding (1) the bungalow and the garden in front of and behind the bungalow and (2) Room No. 1 in Chawl No. 1 adjacent to the bungalow; and also the open space in front of and behind Chawl No. 1. Description of the area to be excluded was set out in the Second Schedule. The area and description of all the other structures on the property, leaving out the excluded area, were set out in the Third Schedule. The property, leaving out the excluded area but including all other structures and buildings thereon, was referred to as ‘the property agreed to be developed’. Paragraph ‘EE’ stated that the ‘Purchaser’ had agreed to take from the ‘Vendors’, the right to develop their share in the property agreed to be developed and the ‘Vendors’ had agreed to give to the ‘Purchaser’ the right to develop their share in the property agreed to be developed. Paragraph ‘FF’ stated that the ‘Vendors’ and the ‘Purchaser’ had surveyed the property and found that the undivided share of the ‘Vendors’ in the property agreed to be developed would measure 3,049.57 square meters or thereabouts. Paragraph ‘GG’ stated that the ‘Vendors’ and the ‘Purchaser’ had negotiated the terms and conditions for development of the share of the ‘Vendors’ in the property agreed to be developed and had agreed to enter into the said agreement. The terms agreed upon by the parties were set out thereunder from Clauses 1 to 64. Three schedules were appended to the agreement. 17. Clause 1 recorded that the ‘Vendors’ agreed to give and the ‘Purchaser’ agreed to take from them the right to develop their undivided 2/9th share in the total Floor Space Index (FSI) available for the property agreed to be developed at the time of sanctioning of the plan of the proposed project by the municipal authorities. The undivided share was computed at 3049.57 square meters or thereabouts and the ‘Vendors’ and ‘Purchaser’ jointly calculated the net FSI available for the 2/9th share of the ‘Vendors’ to be around 25,000 square feet. Clause 2 stated that the net available FSI of 25,000 square feet was arrived at after deducting the FSI that would be applicable for the excluded area. Clause 3 is of relevance and is extracted hereunder: – ‘3. That the Vendors and the Purchaser agree that the said development right shall be given to the Purchaser at the price/consideration of Rs. 915/- (Nine Hundred and Fifteen) per square feet. That as per the same the total consideration for these presents is Rs.2,28,75,000/- (Rupees Two Crores Twenty Eight Lakhs and Seventy Five Thousand Only). That the said total consideration is calculated on the basis of the total available F.S.I. on the undivided 20/90th share of the property agreed to be developed being 25,000 square feet. That in the event of any additional F.S.I. being available on account of any change in the rules, laws and regulations for computing F.S.I. at the time of the sanctioning of the plan or on account of any arithmetical error in calculating the said F.S.I., the consideration payable will proportionately increase and in the event of there being any decrease in the F.S.I. being available at the time of the sanctioning of the plan on account of any change in the rules, laws and regulations for computing F.S.I. or on account of any arithmetical error in calculating the said F.S.I., the consideration payable will proportionately decrease. The Purchaser and the Vendors agree, that any change in the relevant rules, regulations and laws for computing the F.S.I. available after the sanctioning of the plan shall operate to the benefit or prejudice of the Purchaser and the consideration for these presents shall not be affected thereby and the Vendors shall not claim any benefit therefrom.’ 18. Clause 4 stated that, in the event of the ‘Purchaser’ constructing basements in the proposed project, the ‘Purchaser’ shall pay to the ‘Vendors’ a further sum of ₹5,00,000/- as a one time and full and final consideration for the same, over and in addition to the amount payable under Clause 3. This sum of ₹5,00,000/- was to be added to the last instalment payable to the ‘Vendors’ by the ‘Purchaser’. In addition, they claimed that a sum of ₹1,71,971/- was paid by them to the Gujarat Finance Corporation to settle the respondents’ liability and, earlier, they had paid ₹19,50,000/- to Kantilal, which the respondents had agreed to reimburse to them. They asserted that they had sent cheques for ₹34,75,000/- to the respondents in October, 2007, but they were not encashed. They claimed that they were ready and willing to pay the balance sale consideration to the respondents, after deducting the amounts paid, for transfer of their 2/9th share in the property, possession of which was already delivered to them. In the alternative, they raised a claim for damages of ₹41,46,49,688/-. 31. Contesting these claims before the arbitral tribunal, the respondents raised the issue of limitation. They also asserted that the claimants had committed breach of various clauses of the agreement dated 06.08.2003. They denied that the claimants were in possession of their 2/9th share in the property. They denied that the claimants had paid ₹19,50,000/- to Kantilal and that they had agreed to reimburse the same. They also denied payment by the claimants of ₹1,71,971/- to Gujarat Finance Corporation and of ₹3,00,000/- to Jariwala. Their main contention was that the subject agreement was merely for development and was not an agreement to sell their share to the claimants. The respondents raised a counter-claim for compensation of ₹110,70,00,000/- along with costs of ₹20,00,000/-. 32. The majority award noted that the nomenclature of a document was not conclusive proof of the nature of transaction therein and undertook detailed analysis of the terms and conditions in the agreement dated 06.08.2003. It was noted that there was no separate defined property, whereupon the development was to be carried out and it was observed that, had the transaction been only for development, the agreement would have described the developments to be carried out by the claimants. It was further noted that the respondents had agreed to give their 2/9th share to the claimants and the consideration therefor was to be calculated on the basis of the FSI. Referring to Clause 3 of the agreement, it was observed that, if the transaction was only for development and the claimants were only developers, then the benefit or prejudice on account of a change in the FSI Rules ought not to go to the claimants as it would ordinarily be given to the owners. It was opined that Clause 3 indicated that the transaction was for sale and not for development. It was observed that, had it been only a development agreement, then the question of satisfying the claimants about marketable title to the property would not arise and indemnifying the claimants in that regard would also not arise. 33. It was further noted that, if the property was given to the claimants only for development, the agreement would have provided for possession thereof to be returned after completion of the development but there was no such clause. Further, as Clause 22 of the agreement envisaged sale of the flats, shops and other units in the proposed project to be built by the claimants without any portion of the sale consideration being paid to the respondents or even their approval or consent being required for such sale or transfer, it was opined that this clause demonstrated that it was an agreement to sell. Similarly, the liability for payment of taxes, rates, cesses etc. being with the claimants after the cut-off date, 31.03.2002, was held to be a further indication in this regard. It was noted that, as per Clauses 36 and 37, the deed of conveyance was to be executed by the respondents in favour of the purchasers or their nominees, including the co-operative society to be formed at the cost of the claimants, and no stipulation was made as to any additional amount being paid to the respondents therefor, apart from the consideration of ₹2,28,75,000/- that was agreed upon. Reference was made to Clause 50, which provided that the claimants would continue all pending cases/litigation/proceedings, in relation to the property agreed to be developed, at their own risk and cost, and it was observed that this clause would not have been agreed to by the respondents had they intended to remain the owners. It was observed that this clause showed that the claimants, being the purchasers, were to take on the responsibility of fighting pending litigation relating to the property which they would not have taken on, if they were not the owners. 34. Reference was then made to the stand of Upendra and Pankaj in Civil Suit No. 445 of 2008, wherein they had candidly asserted, by way of their written statement, that they had agreed to sell the property to defendant No.7 therein, viz., Shreeram Builders, claimant No.1. It was opined that this admission would be binding upon them and reference was made to the decision of this Court in Union of India vs. Moksh Builders and Financiers Ltd. and others3, wherein it was observed that an admission by a party would be substantive evidence of the fact admitted, irrespective of whether the said party appeared in the witness box and whether such party was confronted with that statement. The respondents’ contention that the admission must necessarily be made in the same proceedings was rejected. It was further held that, as the sons of Upendra and Pankaj, respondent Nos. 3 to 5 herein, were out of India, the stand taken by Upendra and Pankaj would be binding on them also. 35. It was noted that, though the respondents denied the payments made by the claimants, Upendra admitted in his cross-examination that the claimants had paid ₹80,000/- and ₹2,00,000/- towards municipal taxes. He also admitted that nearly ₹19,00,000/- was paid by the claimants to meet their needs. Adverting to the contention of the respondents that time was the essence of the contract, in terms of the payments being made within the stipulated timeframes, it was observed that, as the respondents accepted belated payment of six instalments at the very outset, without raising any objection and without issuing a notice under Clause 7, they could not rely on the time stipulation. It was noted that the respondents had not offered to return the monies received by them from the claimants under the agreement even after the purported termination thereof. 36. Dealing with the breaches of the agreement alleged by the claimants, it was noted that out of the consideration of ₹2,28,75,000/-, the respondents admitted receipt of ₹55,00,000/- by way of earnest money and as regards the payment of balance amount of ₹1,73,75,000/-, it was noted that the claimants had paid the initial six instalments and the respondents received the same. It was noted that payment of those six instalments was with delay, but the respondents neither raised a protest nor received the instalments without prejudice to their rights arising out of the agreement. The contention of the respondents that the delayed payment of these instalments amounted to a breach was rejected, as Clause 7 of the agreement specifically provided for a notice being given to the claimants in the event of a breach, calling upon them to remedy the same within 15 days. It was further noted that only the cheques given for the 7th to 9th instalments had been returned but even at that stage, no notice was given in writing under Clause 7. Considering this majority award in the impugned judgment dated 05.03.2024 passed in the appeals filed by both sides, the learned Judge of the High Court held that, as per the agreement, there were three conditions precedent to execution of the deed of conveyance in favour of the claimants, viz., (i) the deed of conveyance would be executed along with conveyance deeds in relation to the properties of the other owners, i.e., Vyomesh and Madhukant’s heirs; (ii) execution of the conveyance deed would be after the project was fully constructed, and (iii) all instalments and amounts payable under the agreement were duly paid. The learned Judge held that none of these conditions stood fulfilled and, therefore, the obligation to execute the conveyance deed had not arisen. 41. It was also noted that determination of the sale consideration depended on additional amounts being added after the construction and the consideration, therefore, remained variable. Without determination of the full consideration payable after completion of the project, the learned Judge opined that the majority award could not have directed execution of the deed of conveyance in favour of the claimants. Noting that the validity of the award had to be tested under the unamended Section 34 of the Act of 1996, the learned Judge opined that the award was contrary to Section 28(3) of the Act of 1996 as it was in violation of the terms of the contract. Reference was made to the decision of the Bombay High Court in Board of Control for Cricket in India vs. Deccan Chronicle Holdings Ltd. 4, wherein it was held that the arbitral tribunal could not rewrite the contractual terms. The learned Judge opined that the majority award had not interpreted the contract in a reasonable manner and ignored the material terms thereof, whereby a jurisdictional error had been committed. Another instance cited in this regard was the consideration of the sum of ₹19,50,000/- said to have been paid by the claimants to Kantilal and others under the earlier MoU. The learned Judge noted that, as per Clause 51, the agreement dated 06.08.2003 was in supersession of all prior agreements and, therefore, the amount paid, if any, under the earlier MoU could not have been taken into account under the present agreement. It was further noted that the majority award, despite granting the relief of specific performance, had awarded the alternate relief of damages in the event the primary relief granted was set aside and the learned Judge held that this was not in keeping with the procedure and amounted to violation of the fundamental policy of Indian law. 42. The learned Judge further held that the subject agreement was not an agreement to sell but was an agreement for development only. In that regard, he opined that the responsibility to make out a marketable title was only in the context of satisfying the claimants that the respondents had the right to transfer the development right in their favour. The learned Judge opined that the agreement had to be read as a whole and, by doing so, no inference could be drawn that it was not a development agreement. As regards the admission made by Upendra and Pankaj in the earlier suit, the learned Judge opined that the legal position, on the strength of the decision in Ramabai Shriniwas Nadgir vs. Government of Bombay5, was that an admission of a party could be used against the maker only in the same proceedings in which it is made. Pausing here for a moment, we may note that reliance placed on this decision by the learned Judge was misplaced, as the said decision was commented upon by the Bombay High Court itself in Dattatraya Shripati Mohite vs. Shankar Ishwara Mohite and another 6 and it was held that Ramabai (supra) is not an authority in support of the view that the Court is not entitled to consider admissions made by a party in the course of proceedings in other suits relating to the same subject matter. This aspect was also noted by this Court in Basant Singh vs. Janki Singh and others 7. 43. Further, the learned Judge was also of the opinion that acceptance of the belated payment of six instalments did not estop the Thanawalas from raising the contention that default in that regard constituted breach of the agreement. Reference in this regard was made to Clauses 52 and 53 of the agreement. The learned Judge opined that there was clear lack of readiness and willingness on the part of the claimants in performing their part of the agreement but the same had been overlooked in the majority award. The learned Judge, accordingly, concluded that the majority award was patently illegal as it struck at the root of the agreement and shocked the conscience of the Court. The appeals were disposed of in the light of these findings. 44. This being the factual framework vis-à-vis the majority award and its consideration under Sections 34 and 37 of the Act of 1996, we may note that the edict of the Constitution Bench in Gayatri Balasamy vs. ISG Novasoft Technologies Limited8 puts it beyond the pale of doubt that appellate jurisdiction under Section 37 of the Act of 1996 is coterminous with, and as broad as, the jurisdiction of the Court deciding objections under Section 34 thereof. Therefore, the High Court, in exercise of such appellate jurisdiction, was empowered to again test the award on the anvil of Section 34, notwithstanding its confirmation under Section 34 by the learned Principal District Judge, Thane. However, we may also note that Section 34 was subjected to changes by the Arbitration and Conciliation (Amendment) Act, 2015 (Act 3 of 2016), with retrospective effect from 23.10.2015 and in that context, in Ssangyong Engineering and Construction Company Limited vs. National Highways Authority of India (NHAI) 9, this Court affirmed the view taken earlier in Board of Control for Cricket in India vs. Kochi Cricket Private Limited and others 10 that the amended Section 34 of the Act of 1996 would apply only to applications filed thereunder on or after 23.10.2015. In effect, Section 34 of the Act of 1996, as amended by the Act 3 of 2016, has no application in the present case as both sides filed their applications under Section 34 of the Act of 1996 long prior to 23.10.2015. The unamended Section 34 of the Act of 1996 would, therefore, be applicable. Section 34 of the Act of 1996, as it then stood, reads as under: ‘34. Application for setting aside arbitral award.—(1) Recourse to a court against an arbitral award may be made only by an application for setting aside such award in accordance with sub-section (2) and sub-section (3). (2) An arbitral award may be set aside by the Court only if— (a) the party making the application furnishes proof that— (i) a party was under some incapacity, or (ii) the arbitration agreement is not valid under the law to which the parties have subjected it or, failing any indication thereon, under the law for the time being in force; or (iii) the party making the application was not given proper notice of the appointment of an arbitrator or of the arbitral proceedings or was otherwise unable to present his case; or (iv) the arbitral award deals with a dispute not contemplated by or not falling within the terms of the submission to arbitration, or it contains decisions on matters beyond the scope of the submission to arbitration: Provided that, if the decisions on matters submitted to arbitration can be separated from those not so submitted, only that part of the arbitral award which contains decisions on matters not submitted to arbitration may be set aside; or (v) the composition of the arbitral tribunal or the arbitral procedure was not in accordance with the agreement of the parties, unless such agreement was in conflict with a provision of this Part from which the parties cannot derogate, or, failing such agreement, was not in accordance with this Part; or (b) the Court finds that— (i) the subject-matter of the dispute is not capable of settlement by arbitration under the law for the time being in force, or (ii) the arbitral award is in conflict with the public policy of India. Explanation.—Without prejudice to the generality of sub-clause (ii) it is hereby declared, for the avoidance of any doubt, that an award is in conflict with the public policy of India if the making of the award was induced or affected by fraud or corruption or was in violation of section 75 or section 81. (3) An application for setting aside may not be made after three months have elapsed from the date on which the party making that application had received the arbitral award or, if a request had been made under section 33, from the date on which that request had been disposed of by the arbitral tribunal: Provided that if the Court is satisfied that the applicant was prevented by sufficient cause from making the application within the said period of three months it may entertain the application within a further period of thirty days, but not thereafter. (4) On receipt of an application under sub-section (1), the Court may, where it is appropriate and it is so requested by a party, adjourn the proceedings for a period of time determined by it in order to give the arbitral tribunal an opportunity to resume the arbitral proceedings or to take such other action as in the opinion of arbitral tribunal will eliminate the grounds for setting aside the arbitral award.’ 45. In Renusagar Power Co. Ltd. vs. General Electric Co. 11, dealing with the scope of ‘public policy’ in the context of unamended Section 34 in the Act of 1996, this Court held that an award which is contrary to (i) the fundamental policy of Indian law or (ii) the interest of India or (iii) justice or morality, would be against ‘public policy’. Thereafter, in Oil & Natural Gas Corporation Ltd. vs. Saw Pipes Ltd. 12, this Court held that the phrase ‘public policy of India’ used in Section 34 of the Act of 1996 is required to be given a wider meaning and that the concept of ‘public policy’ connotes some matter which concerns public good and public interest. It was noted that what is for public good or in public interest or what would be harmful to public good or public interest has varied from time to time. Therefore, in addition to the narrower meaning given to ‘public policy’ in Renusagar (supra), this Court held that an award could be set aside if it was patently illegal. In the result, this Court held that an award could be set aside if it was contrary to: (a) fundamental policy of Indian law; or (b) the interest of India; or (c) justice or morality; or (d) if it is patently illegal. It was observed that such illegality must go to the root of the matter and should not be of a trivial nature. It was held that an award could also be set aside if it is so unfair and unreasonable that it shocks the conscience of the Court, as it would be opposed to ‘public policy’ and require to be adjudged void. 46. In Centrotrade Minerals & Metals Inc. vs. Hindustan Copper Ltd. 13, elaborating on the scope of ‘patent illegality’, this Court observed that such patent illegality must go to the root of the matter and should be so unfair and unreasonable as to shock the conscience of the Court and where an arbitrator had gone contrary to or beyond the expressed law of the contract or granted relief on a matter not in dispute, it would come within the purview of Section 34 of the Act of 1996. 47. In Delhi Development Authority vs. R.S. Sharma and Company, New Delhi14, this Court summarized the law thus: – ‘21. From the above decisions, the following principles emerge: (a) An award, which is (i) contrary to substantive provisions of law; or (ii) the provisions of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996; or (iii) against the terms of the respective contract; or (iv) patently illegal; or (v) prejudicial to the rights of the parties; is open to interference by the court under Section 34(2) of the Act. (b) The award could be set aside if it is contrary to: (a) fundamental policy of Indian law; or (b) the interest of India; or (c) justice or morality. (c) The award could also be set aside if it is so unfair and unreasonable that it shocks the conscience of the court. (d) It is open to the court to consider whether the award is against the specific terms of contract and if so, interfere with it on the ground that it is patently illegal and opposed to the public policy of India.’ 48. In Oil and Natural Gas Corporation Limited vs. Western Geco International Limited15, this Court considered what would constitute ‘fundamental policy of Indian law’. Without exhaustively elaborating on the concept, three distinct juristic principles were held to be part and parcel of ‘fundamental policy of Indian law’. The first and foremost principle was that in every determination, whether by a Court or other authority, that affects the rights of a citizen or leads to any civil consequences, the Court or authority concerned is bound to adopt what is, in legal parlance, called a ‘judicial approach’ in the matter. The second principle was that a Court and so also a quasi-judicial authority, while determining the rights and obligations of a party before it, must do so in accordance with the principles of natural justice. It was noted that, besides the ‘audi alteram partem’ rule, one of the facets of the principles of natural justice is that the Court or authority deciding the matter must apply its mind to the attendant facts and circumstances while taking a view one way or the other. It was observed that non-application of mind is a defect that is fatal to any adjudication. The requirement that an adjudicatory authority must apply its mind was held to be so deeply embedded in our jurisprudence that it could be described to be a ‘fundamental policy of Indian law’. The third principle recognized as a salutary juristic fundamental in administrative law is that a decision which is so perverse or irrational that no reasonable person could have arrived at the same cannot be sustained. Perversity or irrationality of decisions was to be tested on the touchstone of the Wednesbury principle of reasonableness. 49. In Associate Builders vs. Delhi Development Authority16, considering Section 28(3) of the Act of 1996, this Court observed that an arbitral tribunal must decide in accordance with the terms of the contract but, if an arbitrator construes a term of the contract in a reasonable manner, it would not mean that the award can be set aside on that ground. It was observed that construction of the terms of the contract is primarily for an arbitrator to decide, unless the arbitrator construes the contract in such a way that it could be said to be something that no fair-minded or reasonable person could do. It was further observed that when a Court is applying the ‘public policy’ test to an arbitral award, it does not act as a Court of appeal and a plausible view of facts by the arbitrator necessarily has to be accepted, as the arbitrator is the ultimate master of facts. Similarly, in UHL Power Company Limited vs. State of Himachal Pradesh 17, a 3-Judge Bench of this Court confirmed that, if a plausible interpretation of the contract is available, no fault can be found with the arbitrator if he accepted one such interpretation as against another. 50. In OPG Power Generation Private Limited vs. Enexio Power Cooling Solutions India Private Limited and another 18, a 3-Judge Bench of this Court held that an arbitral tribunal must decide in accordance with the terms of the contract and in case it does so against the terms of the contract, the award would be patently illegal. On the same lines, in South East Asia Marine Engineering and Constructions Limited (SEAMEC LTD.) vs. Oil India Limited19, this Court had held that, if on a full reading of the contract, the view of the arbitral tribunal on the terms of the contract is not a plausible view, the award would be considered perverse and, as such, amenable to interference. In Ssangyong Engineering (supra), this Court found that the award created a new contract by applying a circular that was not even placed before the arbitral tribunal. It was, therefore, held that a fundamental principle of justice was breached, i.e., that a unilateral addition or alteration of a contract could not be foisted upon an unwilling party nor can a party to the agreement be made liable to perform a bargain not entered into by it. It was held that such a course of conduct would be contrary to the fundamental principles of justice as followed in this country and shocked the Court’s conscience. It was, however, cautioned that this ground would be available only in exceptional circumstances and, under no circumstances, would the Court interfere with an award on the ground that justice, in its opinion, was not done. 51. Before applying these settled legal principles to the present case, we may note that the subject agreement dated 06.08.2003, though titled ‘Agreement for Development’, does not conform to the typical prototype of a development agreement, whereunder a landowner would expect returns, usually in the shape of a share in the built-up area. The normal bargain in a development agreement is that the landowner would provide the land on which the development is to be made while the developer would bring in the funds and the wherewithal to undertake such development and upon completion of the project, both would share the built-up area in the agreed upon ratio. However, the subject agreement was not a run-of-the-mill development agreement but was a complex document that contemplated development being undertaken but, in effect, also providing for conveyance of title over the respondents’ share in the land to the appellant or its nominee. In effect, the respondents literally washed their hands off the whole project and did not expect any returns from the development itself. They were not benefited in any manner by the actual development, either by appropriating to themselves a share in the built-up area or a part of the sale proceeds thereof. They were only to be paid the consideration on the strength of the FSI and the additional amounts payable in the context of the extra FSI, if any, and basements, if any. Further, the price for computing the consideration payable to the respondents was based on the projected FSI of 25,000 square feet and remained flexible only till municipal sanction of the plan. Thereafter, the consequences of changes in the FSI Rules, be it beneficial or otherwise, were to be borne by the appellant alone. Ordinarily, such benefit would go to the landowner also and not just to the developer. 52. In any event, irrespective of the slotting of the subject agreement, in terms of whether it was only for development or it was also an agreement to sell, the performance of obligations envisioned thereunder was clearly set out. Once the agreement made it clear that the parties thereto were to perform their respective obligations in a particular order, the same was binding upon them and the agreement had to be given effect to as envisaged therein. Viewed in that context, it was not essential to identify and label the character of the agreement as it was the terms thereof, stipulating sequential performance of obligations, that assumed importance. While so, the majority award proceeded on the premise that the subject agreement was not for development but was an agreement to sell and, treating it as such, it went on to analyse the terms thereof. In that regard, we may note that Section 28(3) of the Act of 1996 mandates that, in all cases, the arbitral tribunal shall decide and make the award by taking into account the terms of the contract and the trade usages applicable to the transaction. Trite to state, when the terms of the contract are clear and explicit, it would not be necessary to import trade usages, as that step would usually be necessary when the contract is silent on such aspects. It is in the light of this statutory provision that we have to examine the majority award, as the High Court interfered with it on the ground that there was violation of Section 28(3) of the Act of 1996. 53. The fundamental error writ large in the majority award is that it dealt with the matter as if it was a regular suit for specific performance of an agreement to sell and not as an arbitration arising out of a contract that visited obligations upon the parties thereto with a binding sequence as to how they were to perform their respective parts. That erroneous approach continued even before this Court, as arguments were advanced as if we were dealing with a regular appeal arising out of a decree for specific performance of an agreement to sell. On those lines, it has been stated on behalf of the appellant that it paid about 82% of the total consideration of ₹2,28,75,000/-, i.e., ₹1,87,63,226/-, and that the balance consideration of ₹41,11,744/- was deposited with the District Judge’s Court at Thane on 15.07.2017. The appellant expressed willingness to conclude the sale transaction under the agreement, by agreeing to enhancement of the consideration to ₹13,72,75,000/-, as per the direction in the majority award. It was stated that, though the appellant filed an appeal against the direction to pay six times the agreed rate of ₹915/- per square foot, the appellant would not pursue the same and was willing to abide by the direction in that regard. However, our jurisdiction presently is different from that of an appellate Court dealing with a regular specific performance decree, as we are only required to test the validity of the majority award on the anvil of Section 34 of the Act of 1996. 54. As stated earlier, the agreement terms specified the sequence in which they were to be acted upon. However, it is clear that the said sequence did not come to pass in reality. For starters, the violation of the payment schedule, in terms of the agreement, is apparent. We may note that, under Clause 3 of the agreement, the consideration payable to the respondents, as set out therein, was to be remitted within the time frame stipulated under Clause 6 thereof. It was made clear that such payments were to be timebound and it was affirmed more than once that time was of the essence. Clause 6 provided for three alternative modes for paying the balance consideration in ten instalments of ₹17,37,500/- each, stipulating that the mode which would be earlier in point of time had to be resorted to. The first mode was that it would be paid within 30 months from the date of issuance of the commencement certificate. The second mode was that it would be paid within 30 months from the date of sanction of the municipal plan. Neither of these events took place, as the admitted position is that the appellant failed to secure a sanctioned plan for the development that was to be carried out. Therefore, the third mode was the only mode that could be adopted for payment of the consideration. This mode stated that the first instalment would be paid on 30.04.2005; the second instalment on 01.08.2005; the third instalment on 01.06.2006 and successive instalments, thereafter, on the first day of each fourth successive month. The amount was to be paid in full by 01.03.2008. 55. However, the first instalment due on 30.04.2005 was remitted on 02.05.2005 with 02 days delay. Further, out of the sum of ₹17,37,500/- due towards this instalment, ₹1,95,000/- was paid only on 06.07.2005. The second instalment, due on 01.08.2005, was paid on 20.09.2005 with 50 days delay. The third instalment that was due on 01.06.2006 was paid on 27.06.2006 with 26 days delay. The fourth instalment, due on 01.09.2006, was paid on 06.10.2006 with 35 days delay. The fifth instalment, due on 01.12.2006, was paid on 04.01.2007 with 34 days delay. The sixth instalment, due on 01.03.2007, was paid on 17.04.2007 with a delay of 47 days. The seventh, eighth and ninth instalments were due on 01.06.2007, 01.09.2007 and 01.12.2007 respectively. Ten cheques dated 10.10.2007, for ₹34,75,000/-, and five cheques dated 27.03.2008, for ₹17,37,500/-, were offered by the appellant towards these instalments. However, the cheques were not encashed and were returned by the respondents on 16.04.2008. The delay in offering payment of these instalments, reckoned from the due dates, works out to 144 days, 52 days and 117 days respectively. The tenth instalment of ₹17,35,500/- that was due on 01.03.2008 was never tendered, but the appellant communicated readiness to make the payments due, under its letter dated 27.03.2008. However, as this letter was not accepted, letter dated 09.04.2008 was again sent along with fresh cheques. These cheques were also returned by the respondents on 16.04.2008, under cover of a letter. Thus, the admitted position is that the payment of the balance consideration was not completed by 01.03.2008, in terms of Clause 6 of the agreement. 56. No doubt, the first six instalments were paid belatedly by the appellant and were accepted by the respondents without protest. The failure on their part to raise an objection was held against them in the majority award. This is due to the fact that Clause 7 of the agreement provided that, in the event of the appellant and its partners committing default in payment of any instalments, the respondents were to serve upon them a notice in writing giving the details of the default and calling upon them to remedy the same within 15 days. We may, however, note that the clause, having stated so, goes on to record that the due payments were to be treated as the essence of the presents under the agreement and that time shall be the essence. Further, all that this clause stated was that, if there was a delay in any payment, the respondents were to issue a notice and the payment due had to be cleared within 15 days of the receipt thereof. The failure on the part of the respondents to issue a notice under Clause 7, therefore, had the effect of curing the delay in the payment of those six instalments which were accepted by the respondents without protest. However, it did not mean that either the time stipulations or the oft-repeated insistence in the agreement that time shall be the essence thereof stood wiped out. To hold so would mean that, irrespective of when the amounts due under the agreement were remitted, the respondents were bound to accept the same. That was never the intention of the parties, as is clear from the agreement itself. 57. More importantly, the total consideration payable by the appellant to the respondents was not fully determined as per the agreement terms and remained fluid as on the date of the award. The consideration payable to the respondents, though reckoned on the projected FSI area of 25,000 square feet in Clause 3, was left open to modification as any change in the FSI Rules prior to sanctioning of the municipal plan was to enure to the benefit of the respondents and was to be taken into account while computing the total consideration. As the appellant did not secure a sanctioned plan, the total consideration amount remained open to change and could not be restricted to the amount worked out on the basis of the estimated FSI area of 25,000 square feet. Similarly, Clause 20 of the agreement provided for further addition being made to the FSI area if the land, allegedly in the adverse possession of the respondents, was gotten regularized by the appellant. This additional amount was to be paid when the adverse possession was regularized by the competent authority. Further, the consideration was to increase if the appellant constructed basements in the proposed project. Clause 4 stated that a sum of ₹5,00,000/- was to be paid in this regard and the said sum was to be added to the last instalment. The clause made it clear that it was only if basements were constructed that it would be payable. Therefore, the consideration amount was amenable to alteration after completion of the project. These agreement clauses made it clear that the consideration payable to the respondents did not stand frozen and remained flexible, linked with the sanctioning of the building plan and the total FSI area available at that time; the additional FSI area that would be available upon regularization of the respondents’ adverse possession; and construction of basements, if any, in the project. 58. Surprisingly, the majority award completely overlooked and ignored the obligations in the agreement and the sequence in which they were to be performed. Even if the award, on the analysis undertaken, proceeded on the premise that the subject agreement was an agreement to sell and was not just for development, it was not for the two learned arbitrators to rewrite its terms. As per the agreement, the total consideration payable to the respondents hinged upon later events that did not materialize by the date of the award. Further, as per the agreement, even the estimated total consideration of ₹2,28,75,000/- was to be paid in full by 01.03.2008, but it was not paid. Significantly, it was only on payment of all the amounts due under the agreement that a conveyance deed was to be executed by the respondents in favour of the appellant or its nominee. Thus, the execution of the conveyance deed was linked to the full amount being paid to the respondents. Admittedly, this event had not come to pass by the time the majority award was passed, be it in terms of the estimated consideration or the actual consideration, which still remained indeterminate. To compound matters further, the majority award not only directed execution of a conveyance deed but also went on to include a part of the excluded property therein, contrary to the agreement terms. The agreement made it quite clear that the bungalow and the appurtenant gardens along with Room No. 1 in Chawl No. 1, in their entirety, were to be left out from the scope of the agreement. However, the majority award merely excluded the 2/9th share of the respondents in the excluded property, thereby including the rest of such excluded property within the proposed deed of conveyance. Notably, the owners of the remaining 7/9th share in the excluded property were not before the arbitral tribunal, whereby their share therein could have been dealt with to their detriment. 59. The majority award also fell in error in ignoring the clear mandate of Clause 51 that all prior agreements stood superseded by the subject agreement, including the MoU dated 15.03.2002. The sum of ₹19,50,000/- allegedly paid to Kantilal was under the said MoU. As all the rights under that MoU stood forfeited under Clause 51, the payment made under the said MoU, if any, could not be considered under the subject agreement. However, the majority award took into account the alleged payment made under the said MoU and adjusted a sum of ₹13,18,334/- from the amount payable, treating it as the sum reimbursable by Upendra and Pankaj. Similarly, the sum of ₹26,222/- which was adjusted against the amount payable to the respondents, being the liability of Upendra and Pankaj to the Gujarat Financial Corporation, was also beyond the scope of the agreement. Last but not the least, when the agreement stipulated the rate as ₹915/- per square foot for the FSI area that would be available at the time of sanctioning of the plan and the rate of ₹450/- per square foot for the extra FSI area, if the respondents’ adverse possession was regularized, it was not for the two learned arbitrators to come up with a new rate at which the consideration should be paid. Strangely, no computation exercise was undertaken in this regard and no reasoning was offered as to why the rate at six times the stipulated rate of ₹915/- per square foot was adopted, bringing the net rate to ₹5490/- per square foot. This direction in the majority award was, therefore, completely beyond the scope of the agreement and defied logic. 60. Further, the agreement did not provide for any claim being made by either side towards damages, liquidated or unliquidated. Though both sides raised claims in this regard before the arbitral tribunal, be it termed as damages or as compensation, it ought to have been noted that the agreement did not speak of any such relief being granted to either of them. The award of damages to the appellant in the majority award was, therefore, without foundation. Further, the grant of such relief, in the alternative, if the relief of specific performance was set aside, is contrary to the established tenets applicable to an arbitral award and was, therefore, opposed to public policy, as rightly noted by the High Court. 61. Thus, the two learned arbitrators not only construed the express terms of the agreement contrarily but completely supplanted them with new terms, as per their own fancies. Their approach was clearly at variance with the edicts of this Court as to interpretation of the underlying contract. Their award, therefore, ought not to have granted the relief of specific performance to the appellant and its partners, by holding them entitled to execution of a deed of conveyance for the 2/9th share of the respondents, in total derogation of the agreement terms. These were the aberrations in the majority award that weighed with the High Court while exercising appellate jurisdiction under Section 37 of the Act of 1996. Though the High Court also went into the issue as to whether the subject agreement was an agreement to sell or was merely for development, that exercise was not necessary, in the light of the correct finding by the High Court that Section 28(3) of the Act of 1996 stood violated by the rewriting of the contract in the majority award. In essence, the judgment dated 05.03.2024 passed by the High Court gave effect to the settled principle of law that it is not open to an arbitral tribunal to do so. 62. However, we may note one other crucial fact at this stage. Though the respondents attributed breach of the agreement to the appellant to justify its termination by way of their legal notice dated 09.04.2008, there is no denying the fact that they themselves failed to abide by Clause 54 of the agreement. Clause 53 thereof recorded that, in the event of there being any default in payment of amounts within time, the agreement would come to an end and the rights given to the appellant would automatically stand terminated. In the event of this clause having application, Clause 54 stated to the effect that if no construction was made by then on the 2/9th share of the respondents, they would be liable to pay back the amount paid towards consideration only, without interest. The respondents, therefore, had to return the consideration paid by the appellant, as no construction was made on their 2/9th share. It was not open to them to retain the said amount when they wished to retract from the agreement. Therefore, though we find no grounds to uphold the majority award for the reasons aforestated, we deem it appropriate to exercise jurisdiction under Article 142 of the Constitution, so as to do complete justice instead of relegating the parties to a fresh round of litigation on this issue. Further, we may note that the ‘no-interest’ condition would have applied had the respondents returned the monies on 09.04.2008, when they terminated the agreement, but they failed to so and retained the same till date. It would only be fair that they do so now with reasonable interest. We, accordingly, direct the respondents to return the amounts paid to them under the agreement with simple interest thereon @ 9% per annum from the date of each payment made by the appellant till the date of actual refund. Further, the appellant’s amounts lying in deposit with the Courts may also be returned, along with the interest accrued thereon, if any. The appeals are disposed of in the above terms. Parties shall bear their own costs.

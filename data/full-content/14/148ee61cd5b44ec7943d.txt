The principal question was whether protection against arrest granted by a competent court must necessarily be restricted to a fixed period, compelling the accused to surrender and seek regular bail, or whether such protection could continue during investigation and throughout the trial. A related question concerned whether anticipatory bail automatically ceases when the accused is summoned by the criminal court. These issues arose because different Benches of the Supreme Court had adopted conflicting approaches. Some decisions recognised the continuing nature of anticipatory bail, whereas others required its duration to be restricted until a particular procedural stage. The Constitution Bench examined the legislative history of Section 438, the purpose of protecting personal liberty, the powers of criminal courts, and the competing interests of the accused and investigating authorities. It ultimately held that anticipatory bail should not ordinarily be limited by time and that protection may continue until the conclusion of trial, depending upon the facts and conditions imposed by the court. The reference arose principally from conflicting interpretations of the earlier Constitution Bench decision in Shri Gurbaksh Singh Sibbia and Others v. State of Punjab, (1980) 2 SCC 565. In that decision, the Supreme Court had recognised that Section 438 confers wide discretionary powers upon the High Court and Court of Session to protect individuals who reasonably apprehend arrest for non-bailable offences. Subsequent decisions, including Siddharam Satlingappa Mhetre v. State of Maharashtra, (2011) 1 SCC 694, and Bhadresh Bipinbhai Sheth v. State of Gujarat, (2016) 1 SCC 152, supported the view that anticipatory bail could ordinarily continue without an automatic expiry date. In contrast, decisions such as Salauddin Abdulsamad Shaikh v. State of Maharashtra, (1996) 1 SCC 667, K.L. Verma v. State, (1998) 9 SCC 348, and HDFC Bank Limited v. J.J. Mannan, (2010) 1 SCC 679, reflected an approach requiring protection to be restricted to a particular period or procedural stage. The Court therefore had to reconcile these authorities and determine whether the statutory language authorised any universal rule compelling an accused to seek regular bail after receiving anticipatory bail. The controversy was significant because an automatic expiry requirement could expose a person to arrest despite an earlier judicial finding that pre-arrest protection was justified. The Court examined the legislative background of Section 438, including the recommendations contained in the 41st Report of the Law Commission of India. The provision was introduced to address situations in which persons might be falsely implicated in criminal proceedings because of personal hostility, political rivalry or other improper motives, and to prevent unnecessary deprivation of liberty where arrest was not justified. The Court explained that anticipatory bail is a direction issued before arrest requiring the release of the applicant on bail if arrested in connection with the specified accusation. It differs from ordinary bail under Sections 437 and 439 primarily in the stage at which judicial protection is granted. While ordinary bail follows arrest or detention, anticipatory bail protects a person who reasonably apprehends arrest. The Court emphasised that Section 438 contains no express requirement that an anticipatory bail order must expire after a fixed number of days, upon filing of the chargesheet, or when the trial court issues summons. Reading such restrictions into the provision as mandatory conditions would unnecessarily curtail the broad judicial discretion conferred by Parliament and could undermine the constitutional importance of personal liberty and the presumption of innocence. The Constitution Bench also considered the submissions advanced by the learned amici curiae and the representatives of the prosecution. One approach emphasised that the courts must retain sufficient discretion to restrict the duration of anticipatory bail in appropriate circumstances, particularly where the investigation requires cooperation, recovery of incriminating material or custodial interrogation. Another approach stressed that an anticipatory bail order should generally operate like an ordinary bail order and should not terminate merely because the investigation progresses to a later stage. The Court recognised that the interests of the investigating agency and the liberty of the accused must be balanced through judicial scrutiny rather than inflexible procedural rules. It clarified that anticipatory bail does not prevent the police from continuing an investigation, questioning the accused, collecting evidence or seeking appropriate judicial directions where circumstances justify intervention. Equally, the grant of anticipatory bail does not create immunity from investigation or prosecution. Courts may impose conditions requiring the applicant to cooperate with investigators, refrain from threatening or influencing witnesses, avoid tampering with evidence, and comply with other obligations reasonably connected with the case. Such conditions must be tailored to the circumstances rather than imposed mechanically in every matter. On the first question referred for consideration, the Supreme Court held that protection granted under Section 438 should not ordinarily be limited to a fixed period merely to enable the accused to surrender before the trial court and apply for regular bail. The Court rejected the proposition that every anticipatory bail order must automatically expire after a predetermined period or at the completion of investigation. It observed that where a competent court has exercised its discretion to grant pre-arrest protection, the order can continue without an automatic temporal restriction unless the court considers special or restrictive conditions necessary in the particular circumstances. However, the Court did not eliminate judicial discretion to impose a time limitation in appropriate cases. A court granting anticipatory bail may prescribe restrictions where justified by the facts, the seriousness of the accusation, the role attributed to the applicant, the evidence available, or other relevant considerations. The governing principle is therefore that an expiry date is not compulsory, rather than that anticipatory bail must invariably remain unlimited. The decision preserves the authority of the High Court and Court of Session to determine suitable conditions while rejecting a general rule that forces every protected accused to seek regular bail after a fixed interval. On the second question, the Supreme Court held that anticipatory bail does not automatically terminate when the investigating agency files a chargesheet or when the accused is summoned by the court. Protection may continue through subsequent stages of criminal proceedings, including trial, depending upon the terms of the original order and the conduct of the accused. The Court explained that the progression of a criminal case from investigation to prosecution does not, by itself, justify extinguishing an existing order of pre-arrest bail. At the same time, such protection is not a blanket authorisation covering future offences or unrelated incidents. An anticipatory bail order must relate to the particular accusation or incident for which the applicant apprehended arrest. The investigating agency remains entitled to seek appropriate relief if the accused absconds, refuses to cooperate, threatens witnesses, tampers with evidence or otherwise violates the conditions imposed by the court. The competent court may direct arrest under Section 439(2) where legally justified, and an appellate or superior court may examine the correctness of the original bail order if material facts or crucial circumstances were overlooked. Thus, the continuing operation of anticipatory bail remains subject to judicial control and does not deprive investigators of lawful remedies. In its final determination, the Constitution Bench resolved the conflict between the earlier lines of authority by reaffirming the broad principles laid down in Gurbaksh Singh Sibbia. It rejected the mandatory time-bound approach reflected in Salauddin Abdulsamad Shaikh and subsequent decisions following that approach. It also disapproved the contrary proposition, attributed to observations in Siddharam Satlingappa Mhetre and similar judgments, that courts cannot impose restrictive conditions while granting anticipatory bail. The Court clarified that the duration and conditions of anticipatory bail must be governed by the facts of each case and the proper exercise of judicial discretion. It further recognised that the investigative requirements concerning discovery of evidence, including those associated with Section 27 of the Indian Evidence Act, could be addressed consistently with the principles concerning limited or deemed custody discussed in Gurbaksh Singh Sibbia. The resulting legal position is that anticipatory bail may ordinarily continue until the end of trial, need not expire upon filing of the chargesheet or issuance of summons, and remains subject to lawful conditions, modification, cancellation and appellate scrutiny. The judgment establishes a significant safeguard for personal liberty while preserving the ability of criminal courts and investigating authorities to respond to misconduct or changed circumstances. List of Cases Discussed: 1. Shri Gurbaksh Singh Sibbia and Others v. State of Punjab – Supreme Court, (1980) 2 SCC 565 – Earlier Constitution Bench decision establishing the broad discretionary nature of anticipatory bail under Section 438 Cr.P.C. and the need to avoid unnecessary restrictions not imposed by the legislature. Its principles were reaffirmed. 2. Siddharam Satlingappa Mhetre v. State of Maharashtra – Supreme Court, (2011) 1 SCC 694 – Considered regarding the continuing duration of anticipatory bail. Observations suggesting that restrictive conditions could not be imposed were overruled. 3. Bhadresh Bipinbhai Sheth v. State of Gujarat – Supreme Court, (2016) 1 SCC 152 – Considered in relation to the principles governing anticipatory bail, personal liberty and the exercise of judicial discretion. 4. Salauddin Abdulsamad Shaikh v. State of Maharashtra – Supreme Court, (1996) 1 SCC 667 – The approach requiring anticipatory bail to be limited to a fixed period was overruled. 5. K.L. Verma v. State and Another – Supreme Court, (1998) 9 SCC 348 – Considered among the decisions following the time-bound approach to anticipatory bail, which was rejected. 6. Sunita Devi v. State of Bihar and Another – Supreme Court, (2005) 1 SCC 608 – Considered among the authorities imposing restrictions on the duration of anticipatory bail. 7. Nirmal Jeet Kaur v. State of M.P. and Another – Supreme Court, (2004) 7 SCC 558 – Considered concerning the duration of pre-arrest protection and the requirement of approaching the competent court for regular bail. 8. HDFC Bank Limited v. J.J. Mannan – Supreme Court, (2010) 1 SCC 679 – Considered regarding the effect of filing a chargesheet and subsequent criminal proceedings on anticipatory bail protection. 9. Satpal Singh v. State of Punjab – Supreme Court, (2018) 4 SCC 303 – Considered among the decisions reflecting the restrictive approach to anticipatory bail. 10. Balchand Jain v. State of M.P. – Supreme Court, (1976) 4 SCC 572 – Referred to for explaining the meaning and legal operation of anticipatory bail as bail in anticipation of arrest. 11. Joginder Kumar v. State of U.P. – Supreme Court, (1994) 4 SCC 260 – Considered in the submissions concerning the principle that the existence of a power to arrest does not make arrest necessary in every case. 12. Lalita Kumari v. Government of Uttar Pradesh – Supreme Court, (2014) 2 SCC 1 – Referred to in the discussion concerning registration of criminal cases and the statutory framework governing police action. 13. Arnesh Kumar v. State of Bihar – Supreme Court, (2014) 8 SCC 273 – Considered regarding restrictions on unnecessary arrests and the safeguards governing the exercise of police powers. 14. M.C. Abraham v. State of Maharashtra – Supreme Court, (2003) 2 SCC 649 – Referred to for the principle that rejection of anticipatory bail does not automatically require the police to arrest the accused. 15. State of U.P. v. Deoman Upadhyaya – Supreme Court, AIR 1960 SC 1125 – Considered regarding custody and discovery of evidence under Section 27 of the Indian Evidence Act, including the concept of deemed custody. 16. Uday Mohanlal Acharya v. State of Maharashtra – Supreme Court, (2001) 5 SCC 453 – Referred to in the submissions concerning bail and the powers of the criminal court during subsequent procedural stages. 17. Prakash Kadam v. Ramprasad Vishwanath Gupta – Supreme Court, (2011) 6 SCC 189 – Considered regarding appellate scrutiny of bail orders where relevant material or crucial circumstances were not properly considered. 18. State through CBI v. Amarmani Tripathi – Supreme Court, (2005) 8 SCC 21 – Considered concerning the legal grounds for interfering with a bail order and the relevant considerations governing bail.

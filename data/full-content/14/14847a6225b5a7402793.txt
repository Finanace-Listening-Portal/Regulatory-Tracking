Summary: Deemed export supplies under GST involve specified domestic transactions that qualify for refund benefits even though the goods do not leave India. The refund framework is governed by Sections 54 and 147 of the CGST Act, Rule 89 of the CGST Rules, Notification No. 49/2017-Central Tax and relevant CBIC circulars. Either the supplier or the recipient may claim the refund, subject to safeguards preventing duplicate claims and ensuring proper treatment of input tax credit. For supplies to Export Oriented Units and other eligible recipients, documentary compliance begins before dispatch through prior intimation in Form A. The recipient must subsequently endorse the tax invoices acknowledging receipt of goods and maintain prescribed Form B records. Where the supplier claims the refund, recipient declarations concerning non-availment of ITC and non-filing of a separate refund claim are particularly important. The article explains the two-year limitation period under Section 54, the significance of the relevant GST return filing date, and the facility to consolidate successive tax periods, including periods spanning financial years. It also covers filing through FORM GST RFD-01, departmental scrutiny, deficiency management, refund orders and statutory interest on delayed refunds. The principal practical recommendation is to establish a transaction-level compliance system before making deemed export supplies, rather than assembling evidence only when the refund application is filed. Unlike conventional exports, the goods do not leave the territory of India. Nevertheless, Parliament has chosen to confer export-like tax benefits on specified transactions in recognition of their contribution to export-oriented industries and manufacturing ecosystems. One of the most significant benefits available in respect of deemed exports is the refund of GST paid on such supplies. While the statutory entitlement appears straightforward, the refund process often proves challenging in practice due to the extensive documentary requirements prescribed by law. For suppliers dealing with Export Oriented Units (EOUs) and other notified recipients, understanding the legal framework before initiating supplies is critical. Several refund disputes originate not at the stage of filing the refund application but much earlier, when mandatory pre-supply compliances are overlooked. Statutory Framework Governing Deemed Export Refunds Refunds relating to deemed export supplies are governed principally by: Section 54 of the CGST Act, 2017; Section 147 of the CGST Act, 2017; Rule 89 of the CGST Rules, 2017; Notification No. 49/2017-Central Tax; Circular No. 14/14/2017-GST; and Circular No. 135/05/2020-GST. These provisions collectively determine who may claim the refund, the documentation that must accompany the claim, the limitation period applicable to such refund, and the procedural safeguards intended to prevent duplication of benefits. The First Question: Who Should Claim the Refund? One of the most important decisions in any deemed export transaction concerns the identity of the claimant. The second proviso to Rule 89(1) permits either the supplier or the recipient to seek refund of the tax paid on deemed export supplies. However, the law clearly contemplates a single refund claimant. Both parties cannot pursue a refund in respect of the same transaction. The recipient may file the refund claim in its own right. Alternatively, the supplier may seek the refund, provided two essential conditions are satisfied. First, the recipient must not avail input tax credit in respect of the deemed export supplies. Secondly, the recipient must furnish a declaration confirming that it has not availed such credit, will not avail such credit in future, and shall not independently seek refund in respect of the same supplies. From a practical standpoint, suppliers should obtain these declarations contemporaneously rather than waiting until the refund application is prepared. In departmental scrutiny proceedings, declarations obtained long after the transaction often attract additional questions from refund authorities. Why Documentation Matters More Than Taxability Experience shows that refund officers rarely dispute whether supplies made to a duly recognised EOU qualify as deemed exports. The focus of scrutiny tends to be on documentary compliance. The procedural safeguards prescribed by Circular No. 14/14/2017-GST are therefore not mere formalities. They constitute the evidentiary foundation of the refund claim. Failure to maintain prescribed records can create avoidable litigation even where substantive eligibility is beyond doubt. For this reason, the compliance process should begin before the supply itself takes place. Form A: The Compliance Requirement That Is Frequently Overlooked Before any supply is made, the recipient unit is required to furnish a prior intimation in Form A. This intimation must be provided to: the supplier; the supplier’s jurisdictional GST officer; and the recipient’s jurisdictional GST officer. Many taxpayers concentrate on collecting invoices and transport documents while overlooking this preliminary requirement. However, Form A serves an important evidentiary purpose. It establishes at the outset that the recipient is a qualifying unit and that the proposed supplies are intended to be treated as deemed exports. As a matter of good practice, businesses should preserve email acknowledgements, delivery confirmations, or any other evidence demonstrating that Form A was furnished before the supplies were effected. Endorsed Invoices: The Critical Evidence of Receipt Once the goods are supplied, the supplier issues the tax invoice and discharges the GST liability through regular GST returns. Upon receipt of the goods, the recipient is required to endorse the tax invoice acknowledging receipt of the deemed export supplies. The endorsed invoice must then be shared with the supplier as well as the jurisdictional officers concerned. This requirement assumes particular importance because Circular No. 14/14/2017-GST recognises the endorsed invoice as proof of deemed export supplies. In practice, endorsed invoices frequently become the primary document examined by refund officers during verification proceedings. Suppliers should therefore ensure that endorsements are clear, complete and capable of demonstrating actual receipt of goods by the recipient unit. Maintenance of Form B Records EOUs and other eligible recipient units are also required to maintain digital records of deemed export supplies in Form B. A copy of Form B pertaining to the relevant month must be furnished to the recipient’s jurisdictional GST officer on or before the 10th day of the succeeding month. While Form B documentation may not receive the same attention as refund filings, deficiencies in underlying records can become significant during departmental verification. Maintaining accurate and contemporaneous records is therefore essential. Refund Claims by Suppliers: Importance of Recipient Declarations Where the supplier proposes to claim the refund, the recipient’s declarations assume considerable significance. The supplier should obtain: a declaration confirming non-availment of input tax credit; an undertaking that the recipient shall not claim refund in respect of the supplies; and invoice-specific confirmation identifying the transactions covered by the declarations. Obtaining invoice-wise declarations substantially reduces the risk of future disputes regarding duplication of benefits. From a risk management perspective, generic declarations lacking invoice references are more vulnerable to departmental objections, particularly in cases involving recurring supplies over an extended period. Understanding the Two-Year Limitation Period Limitation remains one of the most misunderstood aspects of deemed export refund claims. Section 54(1) permits filing of a refund application within two years from the relevant date. For deemed exports, the relevant date is specifically defined under Explanation (2)(b) to Section 54. The statute provides that the relevant date is the date on which the return relating to such deemed export supplies is furnished electronically. This distinction is important. Taxpayers sometimes proceed on the assumption that limitation should be computed from the date of invoice or the period selected in the refund application. Neither approach is supported by the statutory framework. The limitation period must be examined with reference to the return filing date relevant to each supply. Consequently, refund eligibility should be assessed supply-wise rather than merely period-wise. This becomes particularly important where refund applications cover multiple months or several financial years. Filing of Refund Application in FORM GST RFD-01 Once eligibility, documentation and limitation have been verified, the claimant may proceed with filing FORM GST RFD-01 on the GST portal. The application is generally filed under the category relating to refund of tax paid on deemed export supplies by the supplier or recipient, as applicable. A significant relaxation is available regarding consolidation of claims. Circular No. 135/05/2020-GST clarifies that taxpayers may bunch successive tax periods and there is no prohibition on combining claims spanning multiple financial years. However, businesses should remember that bunching of periods does not alter the invoice-wise examination of limitation. Each supply must independently satisfy the statutory time limit. Documents That Should Accompany the Refund Claim A robust refund application should ordinarily be supported by: a statement containing invoice numbers and invoice dates; copies of tax invoices duly endorsed by the recipient; evidence of prior intimation through Form A; declaration regarding non-availment of ITC by the recipient; and undertaking from the recipient confirming that no separate refund claim shall be filed and authorising the supplier to seek refund. Most refund deficiencies arise because supporting documentation is assembled retrospectively. Taxpayers who maintain these documents contemporaneously generally encounter fewer procedural objections. Departmental Scrutiny and Refund Processing After filing, the refund application is examined by the proper officer. Where discrepancies are identified, a notice may be issued in FORM GST RFD-08 seeking clarification or additional evidence. At this stage, the quality of documentation assembled throughout the transaction lifecycle becomes critical. Taxpayers who have preserved Form A records, endorsed invoices and recipient undertakings are usually in a stronger position to address departmental queries efficiently. Following examination, the refund authority may sanction the claim or pass an order in FORM GST RFD-06 where any portion of the claim is proposed to be rejected. Interest on Delayed Refunds The GST framework also seeks to protect taxpayers from administrative delays. Where a refund is not sanctioned within sixty days from receipt of the application, interest becomes payable under Section 56 of the CGST Act. While interest provides a statutory remedy, businesses should preferably focus on minimising delays by ensuring that refund applications are complete at the time of filing rather than relying upon subsequent correspondence to rectify deficiencies. Concluding Remarks In the context of deemed exports, eligibility is often the easiest aspect of the refund process. The greater challenge lies in establishing documentary compliance with the procedural safeguards prescribed under the GST law. Businesses supplying to EOUs and similar units should view refund preparedness as a process that begins before the first invoice is issued. Form A intimations, endorsed invoices, Form B records, recipient declarations and limitation analysis should all form part of a structured compliance framework rather than an exercise undertaken immediately before filing FORM GST RFD-01. Experience consistently shows that well-documented claims stand a significantly better chance of timely sanction, whereas missing procedural records often lead to prolonged correspondence, avoidable disputes and delayed realization of legitimate refunds.

Abdul Samad Vs State of Telangana & Anr., held that a divorced Muslim woman is entitled to seek maintenance under Section 125 of the Code of Criminal Procedure, 1973, notwithstanding the availability of remedies under the Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Divorce) Act, 1986. The Court clarified that the 1986 Act does not extinguish or supersede the secular statutory remedy available under Section 125 CrPC. Justices B.V. Nagarathna and Augustine George Masih delivered separate but concurring judgments, affirming the applicability of both statutory frameworks and dismissing the husband’s criminal appeal. The dispute arose from matrimonial proceedings between the appellant and his former wife, who married on 15 November 2012. Following matrimonial differences, the wife left the matrimonial home on 9 April 2016 and subsequently initiated criminal proceedings. The husband pronounced triple talaq on 25 September 2017 and obtained an ex parte divorce certificate dated 28 September 2017. He claimed to have offered ₹15,000 towards maintenance for the iddat period, which the wife allegedly refused. The wife thereafter filed an application under Section 125(1) CrPC before the Family Court. By order dated 9 June 2023, the Family Court directed interim maintenance of ₹20,000 per month. The Telangana High Court subsequently reduced the amount to ₹10,000 per month by order dated 13 December 2023, which the husband challenged before the Supreme Court. The principal contention of the appellant was that the Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Divorce) Act, 1986 constituted a special statutory framework governing the maintenance rights of divorced Muslim women and therefore prevailed over Section 125 CrPC. He argued that the non-obstante provisions of Sections 3 and 4 of the 1986 Act excluded recourse to the general maintenance provision. Alternatively, he contended that a divorced Muslim woman could invoke Section 125 CrPC only through the procedure contemplated under Section 5 of the 1986 Act. The amicus curiae opposed this interpretation, submitting that the two enactments operated in distinct fields and that the 1986 Act contained no express or necessarily implied exclusion of the secular remedy. The Supreme Court examined the legislative history and social-welfare purpose of Section 125 CrPC, emphasising that the provision was enacted to prevent destitution and provide maintenance irrespective of religion or personal law. Referring to the constitutional principles of equality, dignity and social justice, the Court considered the decisions in Mohd. Ahmed Khan v. Shah Bano Begum, Danial Latifi v. Union of India, Shabana Bano v. Imran Khan, Khatoon Nisa v. State of Uttar Pradesh and other precedents. It observed that the expression “wife” under Section 125 includes a divorced woman who has not remarried and that the statutory remedy remains available even where personal-law provisions independently regulate rights arising upon divorce. The Court distinguished the scope and conditions of the two enactments. Section 125 CrPC provides a secular maintenance remedy based on the claimant’s inability to maintain herself and the husband’s sufficient means and neglect or refusal to maintain her. In contrast, Section 3 of the 1986 Act provides a divorced Muslim woman with entitlements arising from divorce, including reasonable and fair provision and maintenance, mahr or dower and specified properties. The Court held that these rights are not identical and can operate concurrently. The existence of a special remedy under the 1986 Act therefore does not automatically displace the general remedy under Section 125 CrPC, and the absence of an election under Section 5 of the 1986 Act does not render a Section 125 application incompetent. Addressing the possibility of overlapping maintenance obligations, the Supreme Court explained that Section 127(3)(b) CrPC provides a mechanism to account for amounts already received under personal or customary law. Where a former husband has made reasonable and fair provision sufficient to meet the divorced woman’s future needs, the competent court may consider such provision while determining or modifying maintenance under Section 125 CrPC. The Court clarified that simultaneous availability of remedies does not confer an automatic entitlement to duplicate maintenance. Justice Nagarathna also addressed the additional statutory remedies under the Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Marriage) Act, 2019, including the position of women affected by an illegal pronouncement of triple talaq. Ultimately, the Supreme Court affirmed the Telangana High Court’s order dated 13 December 2023 and dismissed Criminal Appeal No. 2842 of 2024. The Court concluded that divorced Muslim women may invoke Section 125 CrPC as well as the 1986 Act, either independently or concurrently, subject to appropriate consideration of payments already made. The decision establishes that the protection against destitution provided by the secular maintenance law is not withdrawn merely because the claimant belongs to the Muslim community or has rights under a separate personal-law enactment. The ruling preserves the concurrent operation of the two statutory remedies while preventing unjustified double recovery. List of Cases Discussed / Relied Upon 1. Mohd. Ahmed Khan Vs Shah Bano Begum & Others (Supreme Court), (1985) 2 SCC 556: The Constitution Bench recognised the independent right of a divorced Muslim woman to claim maintenance under Section 125 CrPC, notwithstanding the personal law applicable to the parties. The judgment formed an important foundation for the Court’s interpretation of the secular maintenance remedy. 2. Danial Latifi & Another Vs Union of India (Supreme Court), (2001) 7 SCC 740: The Constitution Bench upheld the constitutional validity of the Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Divorce) Act, 1986 by interpreting its provisions to require reasonable and fair provision for a divorced Muslim woman’s future needs. The Supreme Court relied extensively on this decision while examining the relationship between the 1986 Act and Section 125 CrPC. 3. Shabana Bano Vs Imran Khan (Supreme Court), (2010) 1 SCC 666: Relied upon for the proposition that a divorced Muslim woman’s application under Section 125 CrPC remains maintainable before the Family Court so long as she has not remarried, and maintenance cannot be restricted to the iddat period. 4. Khatoon Nisa Vs State of Uttar Pradesh & Others (Supreme Court), (2014) 12 SCC 646: Considered for the maintainability of proceedings under Section 125 CrPC even without an election under Section 5 of the 1986 Act. The Court referred to the substantially similar considerations governing maintenance under the two enactments. 5. Shamim Bano Vs Asraf Khan (Supreme Court), (2014) 12 SCC 636: Discussed concerning the availability of maintenance under Section 125 CrPC and the 1986 Act. The judgment supported the continued jurisdiction of the competent court to grant maintenance to a divorced Muslim woman. 6. Shamima Farooqui Vs Shahid Khan (Supreme Court), (2015) 5 SCC 705: Relied upon to affirm the applicability of Section 125 CrPC to divorced Muslim women before Family Courts and to emphasise the statutory protection against financial destitution. 7. Fuzlunbi Vs K. Khader Vali & Another (Supreme Court), (1980) 4 SCC 125: Discussed for the secular and social-welfare character of maintenance proceedings and the legislative objective of protecting women from destitution. 8. Bai Tahira Vs Ali Hussain Fidaalli Chothia (Supreme Court), (1979) 2 SCC 316: Considered in the discussion of maintenance payments made upon divorce and the operation of Section 127(3)(b) CrPC. The precedent was relevant to preventing deprivation of maintenance merely because a divorced woman had received an amount under personal law. 9. Shri Bhagwan Dutt Vs Smt. Kamla Devi & Another (Supreme Court), (1975) 2 SCC 386: Referred to for the preventive and independent nature of maintenance jurisdiction under the Code of Criminal Procedure, distinct from rights arising under personal law. 10. Inderjit Kaur Vs Union of India & Others (Supreme Court), (1990) 1 SCC 344: Discussed regarding the conditions governing maintenance, including the wife’s inability to maintain herself and the husband’s sufficient means and neglect or refusal to maintain her. 11. Iqbal Bano Vs State of Uttar Pradesh & Another (Supreme Court), (2007) 6 SCC 785: Cited in the submissions concerning the interpretation of the 1986 Act and its relationship with maintenance proceedings under the Code of Criminal Procedure. 12. Jain Ink Manufacturing Company Vs Life Insurance Corporation of India & Another (Supreme Court), (1980) 4 SCC 435: Relied upon by the appellant for the principle that special legislation may prevail over general legislation and that potentially conflicting statutory provisions require harmonious interpretation. 13. Chennupati Kranthi Kumar Vs State of Andhra Pradesh & Others (Supreme Court), (2023) 8 SCC 251: Cited by the appellant in support of the interpretative principles governing special and general statutory enactments. 14. Olga Tellis Vs Bombay Municipal Corporation (Supreme Court), (1985) 3 SCC 545: Referred to through the discussion in Danial Latifi concerning the constitutional protection of life and dignity under Article 21 and its relevance to the rights of economically vulnerable persons. 15. Maneka Gandhi Vs Union of India (Supreme Court), (1978) 1 SCC 248: Discussed through Danial Latifi in the constitutional context of personal liberty, dignity and the requirement of just, fair and reasonable legal protection. 16. Shahid Jamal Ansari Vs State of Uttar Pradesh (Allahabad High Court), 2008 SCC OnLine All 1077: Examined as an example of the judicial view that a divorced Muslim woman’s maintenance remedy lay exclusively under the 1986 Act. The Supreme Court did not accept that restrictive interpretation. 17. Mumtazben Jusabbhai Sipahi Vs Mahebubkhan Usmankhan Pathan (Gujarat High Court), 1998 SCC OnLine Guj 279: Referred to as an authority supporting the continued availability of Section 125 CrPC despite the enactment of the 1986 Act. 18. Kunhimohammed Vs Ayishakutty (Kerala High Court), 2010 SCC OnLine Ker 567: Discussed extensively for its harmonious interpretation of the two enactments. The decision recognised that Section 125 CrPC and Section 3 of the 1986 Act operate in distinct statutory domains. 19. Mrs. Humera Khatoon & Others Vs Mohd. Yaqoob (Allahabad High Court), 2010 SCC OnLine All 202: Cited among the High Court decisions recognising the availability of maintenance under Section 125 CrPC to divorced Muslim women. 20. Sazid Vs State of Uttar Pradesh & Others (Allahabad High Court), 2011 SCC OnLine All 1059: Referred to as part of the judicial approach favouring the continued operation of Section 125 CrPC alongside the 1986 Act. 21. Jubair Ahmad Vs Ishrat Bano (Allahabad High Court), 2019 SCC OnLine All 4065: Discussed among the High Court authorities adopting a beneficial interpretation of the statutory maintenance framework. 22. Shakila Khatun Vs State of Uttar Pradesh & Another (Allahabad High Court), 2023 SCC OnLine All 75: Cited as an authority recognising that the 1986 Act does not necessarily extinguish the secular maintenance remedy under Section 125 CrPC. 23. Khalil Abbas Fakir Vs Tabbasum Khalil Fakir & Another (Bombay High Court), 2024 SCC OnLine Bom 23: Referred to among the decisions supporting the availability of Section 125 CrPC notwithstanding the existence of the special statutory framework governing divorced Muslim women. 24. Other Authorities Discussed: The separate concurring judgment of Justice B.V. Nagarathna additionally examined judicial authorities concerning maintenance, divorce-related financial provision, the relationship between personal law and secular remedies, and the prevention of overlapping recovery. These authorities informed the Court’s conclusion that the two statutory remedies coexist, subject to adjustment of benefits already received.

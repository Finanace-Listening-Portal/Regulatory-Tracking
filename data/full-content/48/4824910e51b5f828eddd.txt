Summary: The Rajasthan High Court, in Jaipur Development Authority v. Additional Commissioner, CGST Audit, Jaipur & Others, held that mere short payment or non-payment of GST does not, by itself, establish fraud, wilful misstatement or suppression of facts with intent to evade tax so as to justify proceedings under Section 74 of the CGST Act, 2017. The Court examined the show cause notice dated 30 September 2025, issued following an audit under Section 65 for FY 2019–20 to FY 2023–24, concerning road-cutting charges, additional floor area ratio (FAR) charges, betterment levy and receipts from the sale of Master Plan booklets and application forms. It observed that the disputed receipts were recorded in the Jaipur Development Authority’s books and that the controversy primarily concerned their taxability. The Court clarified that the expression “to evade tax” qualifies fraud, wilful misstatement and suppression of facts under Section 74, and that the notice must disclose specific circumstances indicating an intention to evade tax. Mere detection of an alleged liability during an audit cannot automatically establish such intention. Although misstatement and suppression may coexist, their invocation must be supported by relevant facts. The Court further distinguished proceedings under Section 73, applicable to cases without fraud or deliberate evasion, from those under Section 74, which attract extended limitation and more stringent penalties. Holding that the impugned notice failed to establish the jurisdictional foundation necessary for invoking Section 74, the High Court quashed the show cause notice and all consequential proceedings, without deciding whether the disputed receipts were taxable under GST. The judgment reinforces that an allegation of tax evasion requires more than an alleged short payment, and that the Department must substantiate the statutory conditions for invoking Section 74. The final audit report dated 29 September 2025 led to six show cause notices that day. On the following day, the Additional Commissioner issued the separate Section 74 notice challenged before the High Court. In its narration of facts, the Court described the disputed items as road-cutting charges, additional floor area ratio (FAR) charges and betterment levy, and receipts from the sale of Master Plan booklets and application forms. The department found the receipts in JDA’s own books during the audit. (Paras 5–8.) JDA maintained that the charges arose from or were incidental to its statutory functions. It said that road-cutting charges recovered the cost of restoring public roads, that additional FAR charges and betterment levy were regulatory levies, and that the amounts collected for booklets and forms were nominal printing recoveries. The department, on the other hand, relied on the alleged failure to disclose and pay the liability as evidence of evasion. The Court treated JDA’s position on taxability as arguable, not as finally established. (Paras 10, 11.9 and 39–42.) What the Court held 1. The notice must explain why the alleged short payment amounts to evasion The Court read the words “to evade tax” as applying to each of the three grounds in Section 74: fraud, wilful misstatement and suppression of facts. In other words, the notice must disclose not only the conduct complained of but also facts from which an intent to evade can reasonably be inferred. Explanation 2 defines what amounts to suppression; it does not remove the separate requirement of intent. Even if a required disclosure was omitted, the question remains: what shows that the omission was aimed at evading tax? (Paras 30–35.) The distinction has a direct bearing on limitation and penalty. For the periods in question, the Court contrasted the five-year time limit for passing an order under Section 74 with the three-year limit under Section 73, reckoned from the due date of the annual return in a short-payment case. It also contrasted the tax-equivalent penalty under Section 74 with the penalty under Section 73(9), which is 10% of the tax or ₹10,000, whichever is higher. If non-payment alone were enough to establish intent, there would be little room left for Section 73 to operate. (Paras 22, 31–35 and 39.) 2. An audit finding is not, by itself, proof of evasion The department’s case was that the liability would have remained undetected but for the audit. The Court looked instead at what the audit had actually revealed. The receipts were recorded in JDA’s books, the dispute concerned whether those receipts were consideration for a taxable supply, and an advance ruling in a similar matter supported the view JDA had taken. In these circumstances, the Court found no basis in the notice to turn a disagreement over taxability into an allegation of deliberate evasion. (Paras 39–43.) The judgment should not be read as saying that an entry in the books answers every allegation of suppression. A transaction may be recorded in the ledger while information required in a GST return remains undeclared. The two questions must be kept separate: Was there a disclosure obligation, and was it complied with? If not, what facts indicate that the omission was intended to evade tax? (Paras 27–28 and 34–35.) 3. Misstatement and suppression can be alleged together The Court rejected JDA’s argument that the two allegations were mutually exclusive. A false declaration can both state something incorrect and conceal the true position. For instance, a taxpayer who falsely denies having supplied a service has made a misstatement and, by the same answer, withheld the true fact. The defect in JDA’s notice was not that it used both expressions. It was that the notice did not disclose the facts necessary to support the allegation of intent. (Paras 25–28 and 43–47.) 4. A writ can lie where the defect goes to the officer’s power to issue the notice Ordinarily, a taxpayer must reply to a show cause notice, allow the adjudicating authority to decide the matter and pursue the statutory appeal if necessary. The High Court did not dilute that rule. It distinguished a dispute on the merits from a defect apparent on the face of the notice that goes to the officer’s power to invoke the provision. It entertained JDA’s writ on the latter ground without deciding whether GST was payable on the disputed receipts. (Paras 15 and 17–24.) What taxpayers should take from the judgment A reply to a Section 74 notice should deal with taxability and the allegation of evasion separately. A strong reply on classification or exemption does not, by itself, answer the department’s allegation about the taxpayer’s conduct. Equally, an inadequate allegation of intent does not establish that the underlying transaction is free from GST. The following points are worth examining when preparing the reply: (Paras 21–24 and 34–43.) Identify the precise allegation Identify the precise allegation. What fact does the notice say was misstated or withheld? In which return, statement or document was it required to be disclosed? Does the notice explain how the alleged conduct led to the short payment and why it indicates evasion? (Paras 21 and 26–35.) Put the disclosure trail on record Put the disclosure trail on record. Reconcile the entries in the books with the relevant returns, annual returns and audit submissions. State exactly what was disclosed and where. If a receipt was in the books but not separately shown in a return, explain the return treatment rather than claiming full disclosure in general terms. (Paras 27, 35 and 39–42.) Explain the basis for the tax position Explain the basis for the tax position. If the dispute concerns whether a receipt is consideration for a supply, identify the applicable provision and explain why the taxpayer adopted that treatment. Any contemporaneous correspondence, working paper or ruling relied upon is more useful than a bare assertion that the position was bona fide. (Paras 40–42.) Examine limitation financial year by financial year Examine limitation financial year by financial year. Keep the objection to invoking Section 74 distinct from the computation of limitation and from the reply on merits. The Court noted that Sections 73 and 74 govern periods up to FY 2023–24, while Section 74A applies to later periods. The judgment’s reasoning on intent is useful, but its comparison of time limits and penalties under Sections 73 and 74 should not be carried over mechanically to a Section 74A notice. (Paras 31–35.) JDA’s status as a statutory authority was relevant to the Court’s assessment of intent, but it was not a blanket immunity. The Court expressly left open cases involving deliberate falsification or a collateral purpose. Nor did it decide JDA’s other objections about combining five financial years, the officers involved in issuing the notice and its summary, or the absence of a pre-notice intimation in Form GST DRC-01A. Those submissions should not be cited as findings of the Court. (Paras 11.5–11.8, 14, 36–38 and 46–48.) The judgment does not reconcile those passages. The operative order identifies the quashed notice by its date, issuing respondent and statutory provision. (Paras 7, 16 and 48(ii).) Conclusion The useful point in Jaipur Development Authority is not that an audit-based demand can never be raised under Section 74. It is that an audit may reveal an alleged short payment without revealing any intent to evade tax. The department must show what takes the case from the first to the second. For the taxpayer, the practical course is to answer both questions on the record: why the tax treatment was adopted, and why the facts relied upon in the notice do not support the allegation of evasion. The High Court quashed the notice because the second question had no adequate answer on its face; it left the first open. (Paras 23, 39–43 and 46–48.)

On November 4, the Indian Cyber Crime Coordination Centre (I4C) and the Indian Computer Emergency Response Team (CERT-In) warned financial institutions about an escalation in financially motivated cyberattacks involving artificial intelligence (AI) agents. The warning follows a data leak involving nearly 3,900 State Bank of India customers and a $2 million fraud involving two Mumbai-based NBFCs. The incidents have highlighted vulnerabilities in payment and loan disbursement systems that connect banks with fintechs and other financial service providers. Advt What has triggered the latest concerns over cybersecurity in the financial sector?The immediate trigger is a series of incidents involving unauthorised access to financial institutions' systems and fraudulent fund transfers.State Bank of India disclosed on October 21 that 3,897 customer records containing personally identifiable information had been exposed on the dark web. The records included names, mobile numbers, dates of birth, email addresses, residential addresses and loan-related information. SBI said customer balances were safe and that the incident had no material impact on its business operations. It engaged an independent cybersecurity agency to investigate the breach.In another incident, two Mumbai-based NBFCs lost around $2 million through more than 700 unauthorised instant transfers on October 8 and 9. According to a police complaint reviewed by Reuters, hackers compromised the application programming interface (API) used to send loan disbursement instructions to ICICI Bank.These incidents prompted RBI to intensify its scrutiny of banks' cybersecurity preparedness, particularly vulnerabilities in APIs connecting banks with fintech companies and non-bank lenders.On November 4, I4C and CERT-In issued a fresh warning about financially motivated attacks using AI agents, citing cases of multi-crore unauthorised transactions through fund disbursement systems and payment APIs. Advt Why are banks and fintech companies becoming more vulnerable to cyberattacks?The rapid digitalisation of financial services has increased the number of technology systems connected to banks' core infrastructure. Banks increasingly work with fintech companies, NBFCs, payment aggregators, technology vendors and cloud service providers to offer lending, payments and other services.APIs allow these systems to communicate and exchange information. For instance, a digital lending platform may use an API to send loan disbursement instructions to a partner bank, which then transfers money to the borrower's account. Advt While these integrations make financial services faster and more efficient, they also create additional points of vulnerability. A weakness in a fintech company's application, an exposed API or compromised credentials belonging to a third-party vendor can potentially provide attackers access to systems connected to a bank.Smaller lenders and third-party technology vendors can be easier targets because they may have fewer resources to implement sophisticated cybersecurity controls. RBI has also expressed concern that implementation of existing cybersecurity requirements has not kept pace with the expansion of digital financial infrastructure. Advt How are hackers exploiting these vulnerabilities to steal money?The October 9 CERT-In advisory identified multiple attack campaigns targeting NBFCs, digital lending platforms, fintech companies, payment service providers and wallet operators.Attackers exploit vulnerabilities in internet-facing applications, exposed APIs and privileged system access to enter financial institutions' networks. They then steal credentials belonging to payment aggregators, payment gateways or partner banks.Once inside, hackers can invoke payment and disbursement APIs directly from within the institution's own authorised infrastructure. This allows them to bypass controls that would ordinarily apply when a loan is created, approved, subjected to KYC checks and sanctioned for disbursement.The CERT-In advisory said these attacks could bypass application-level controls relating to loan creation, KYC verification, loan sanction, beneficiary verification, balance sufficiency and per-transaction limits.Attackers have also exploited dormant or outdated APIs, unsecured configuration files and credentials stored in source code. In some cases, they establish backdoors to maintain access to compromised systems.Funds are subsequently transferred to mule accounts across different banks and quickly moved or withdrawn through payment systems such as IMPS and NEFT, according to the November 4 I4C warning. Attackers may also delete transaction records to conceal their activities and complicate investigations.How is artificial intelligence changing the nature of cyberattacks?Artificial intelligence is making cyberattacks faster, more automated and easier to scale. Earlier, hackers often needed considerable time and technical expertise to identify vulnerabilities, develop exploits and execute attacks. AI-assisted tools can automate several of these activities.CERT-In's November 4 advisory designated the threat actor responsible for the latest campaign as CRAFTY FALCON. The agency said the attackers were using publicly available AI-driven offensive security tools, including ARTEX, PentAGI, Cairn and CyberStrikeAI, to conduct reconnaissance, generate customised attack scripts and execute multiple stages of attacks at machine speed.The agency found that attackers were targeting cloud infrastructure, exploiting exposed testing environments and using shared credentials to move from non-production systems into live production environments.The campaign also involved testing unauthorised payment routes through small-value transactions, sometimes as little as Rs 1, before attempting larger transfers. Attackers were found to be timing operations to coincide with weekends, holidays and other periods when security monitoring may be weaker.CERT-In has separately reported incidents in which AI agents used for research and evaluation exceeded their authorised tasks and accessed live systems and confidential information belonging to Indian organisations. These incidents are distinct from deliberate attacks by financially motivated cybercriminals using AI-assisted tools, although both highlight the risks arising from inadequate controls over AI access to sensitive systems.Why are API-based attacks particularly difficult to detect?APIs are designed to allow different systems to exchange information and execute instructions automatically. When a bank receives a legitimate API request from an authorised partner, its systems may process the instruction without requiring the same checks that would apply to a transaction initiated manually by a customer.Hackers exploit this trust by stealing API credentials or compromising the systems that generate these requests. Since fraudulent instructions may originate from infrastructure that the bank already recognises as authorised, conventional security controls may not immediately flag them.The October 9 CERT-In advisory identified the use of long-lived API tokens hard-coded into source code, plaintext credentials in configuration files and databases, deprecated APIs and applications without multi-factor authentication as some of the vulnerabilities exploited by attackers.In such cases, the bank may receive what appears to be a legitimate instruction from an authorised partner, even though the underlying request has been generated by a compromised system.What steps are RBI and cybersecurity agencies taking to address the threat?RBI has stepped up scrutiny of banks' cybersecurity preparedness following the recent incidents. It has asked lenders to demonstrate compliance with existing cybersecurity requirements and assess vulnerabilities in their API connections with fintech companies and NBFCs.In an October 19 advisory, RBI directed regulated entities to treat their technology environments as potentially compromised until forensically cleared following credential exposure or confirmed unauthorised access.The measures include removing administrative interfaces from public internet access, enforcing multi-factor authentication for administrative access, restricting cloud and backend access on a least-privilege basis, and auditing source code and configuration files for exposed credentials. Banks and payment companies have also been asked to maintain inventories of their APIs and shut down dormant, deprecated and testing interfaces.The November 4 I4C advisory asked financial institutions to urgently implement measures prescribed in CERT-In's critical-severity advisories. It also recommended daily and per-transaction payment limits, beneficiary-name verification and monitoring of unusually rapid transactions.Financial institutions have been asked to report incidents to CERT-In within six hours of detection. Banks and NBFCs are also required to report incidents to RBI through its DAKSH portal within the same timeframe, according to the November 4 advisory.CERT-In has additionally recommended round-the-clock monitoring, particularly during weekends and holidays, isolation of testing environments from production systems, stronger authentication of third-party service providers and transaction monitoring to detect suspicious fund transfers.What should banks and fintechs do differently to prevent such attacks?Financial institutions need to move beyond perimeter-based security, which focuses primarily on protecting the outer boundaries of their networks. They must ensure that every system, application and user accessing their infrastructure is independently authenticated and authorised.This requires regular audits of APIs, immediate closure of unused interfaces, stronger controls over credentials and access privileges, and separation of testing environments from live banking systems. Institutions must also ensure that third-party vendors follow equivalent security standards.Transaction monitoring needs to become more sophisticated. Banks should identify unusual transaction patterns, including small-value test transfers to new beneficiaries followed by large disbursements, repeated transfers to new accounts and sudden spikes in automated payment requests.CERT-In has also recommended real-time monitoring of cloud infrastructure, forensic verification after an incident and periodic threat hunting to identify attackers who may have retained access even after an initial security breach has been addressed.Institutions have also been advised to use server-side transaction validation, cryptographically signed API requests, short-lived access tokens and stronger controls over cloud management systems. Payment and merchant settlement instructions should be subject to independent approval and reconciliation.How serious is the financial sector's exposure to cyber fraud?The scale of digital financial activity makes the sector an attractive target for cybercriminals. According to data cited by I4C from the National Cybercrime Reporting Portal and the Citizen Financial Cyber Fraud Reporting and Management System, more than 53.87 lakh cyber fraud complaints were registered between FY24 and FY26. The total reported amount exceeded Rs 56,087 crore, of which more than Rs 9,079 crore was marked as lien and over Rs 206 crore was refunded.These figures cover reported cyber fraud across financial and other categories and should not be interpreted as losses arising exclusively from attacks on banks or fintech infrastructure.The recent incidents, however, highlight a different dimension of the threat: attackers are increasingly targeting the systems through which financial institutions themselves move money. As banking, lending and payments become more interconnected, a security failure at one institution or technology provider can potentially affect several participants in the financial ecosystem.For banks and fintechs, cybersecurity is therefore no longer just an IT or compliance function. It is becoming a core operational and financial risk that requires oversight from senior management and boards.

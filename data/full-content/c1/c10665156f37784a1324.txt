(Supreme Court of India) Summary: The Supreme Court of India, in Vijay Karia & Ors. Vs Prysmian Cavi E Sistemi SRL & Ors. (Civil Appeal Nos. 1544–1545 of 2020, decided on 13 February 2020), examined the limited grounds available for resisting enforcement of foreign arbitral awards under Section 48 of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996. The appeals arose from the Bombay High Court’s judgment dated 7 January 2019, which held four awards rendered by a sole arbitrator in London under the London Court of International Arbitration (LCIA) Rules enforceable in India. The Supreme Court emphasised that proceedings for enforcement of foreign awards cannot be converted into appeals on facts or law. Judicial intervention must remain confined to the narrowly defined statutory exceptions, consistently with India’s obligations under the New York Convention. The dispute originated from a Joint Venture Agreement dated 19 January 2010 between Vijay Karia and other individual shareholders of Ravin Cables Limited and the Italian company Prysmian Cavi E Sistemi SRL. Under the agreement, Prysmian acquired a 51% majority shareholding in Ravin. A separate Control Premium Agreement provided for payment of €5 million to the existing shareholders. The joint venture arrangements regulated management responsibilities, appointment of the Chief Executive Officer, restrictions on competing cable businesses, valuation of shares and consequences of material contractual breaches. The agreement provided for arbitration seated in London under the LCIA Rules, governed by English law, and expressly excluded the application of Part I of the Indian Arbitration and Conciliation Act. Disputes subsequently arose over management control of Ravin, the powers of its Chief Executive Officer, appointments of senior personnel, access to company records and alleged interference with business operations. Prysmian alleged that Vijay Karia and the other shareholders had obstructed implementation of the joint venture arrangements and committed material breaches of the agreement. The shareholders, in turn, alleged breaches by Prysmian, including issues concerning its group’s acquisition of another Indian cable business through the Draka group and direct sales or competing activities in India. Both sides invoked contractual provisions relating to events of default and share purchase rights, including valuation at a premium or discount. The disputes were referred to a sole arbitrator appointed through the LCIA. The arbitrator delivered four awards addressing contractual interpretation, jurisdiction, allegations of material breach, counterclaims and consequential relief. The arbitral findings substantially favoured Prysmian. The arbitrator found that the existing shareholders had committed material breaches concerning the management and operation of Ravin and had failed to rectify those breaches within the contractual period. Several counterclaims advanced by the shareholders were rejected after examination of the contractual provisions and evidence. The resulting awards were subsequently presented for enforcement in India, where the Bombay High Court rejected the objections raised by the shareholders. Before the Supreme Court, the appellants challenged enforcement on multiple grounds, including alleged procedural unfairness, inability to present their case, non-consideration of material issues, contractual interpretation, valuation methodology and alleged bias of the arbitrator. The central question was whether these objections established grounds for refusal of enforcement under Section 48 or merely sought reconsideration of factual and legal conclusions already reached in arbitration. The Court examined the structure of Section 48 and the principles governing enforcement of foreign awards under the New York Convention. The Supreme Court reaffirmed that the public-policy exception under Section 48(2)(b) must be interpreted narrowly. Relying on the principles established in Renusagar Power Co. Ltd. v. General Electric Co. and Shri Lal Mahal Ltd. v. Progetto Grano SPA, the Court distinguished the enforcement of foreign awards from proceedings for setting aside domestic arbitral awards. An alleged error in applying law, interpreting contractual provisions or evaluating evidence does not ordinarily justify refusal of enforcement. The Court reiterated that Section 48 does not permit a second examination of the merits of the underlying dispute. On the objection that the appellants were unable to present their case, the Supreme Court explained that Section 48(1)(b) protects the essential requirement of a fair hearing. The provision is attracted where circumstances beyond a party’s control prevent meaningful participation in the proceedings, such as denial of an opportunity to address a decisive argument or rebut material evidence introduced without notice. However, an unfavourable finding, rejection of evidence or alleged failure to deal adequately with a submission does not automatically establish procedural unfairness. The Court stressed that foreign awards should be interpreted supportively, with an inclination towards enforcement rather than invalidation. The Court also considered the distinction between an arbitral tribunal’s failure to determine a material claim and its rejection of that claim through reasoning considered inadequate by the losing party. A complete failure to decide a fundamental claim or counterclaim may, in exceptional circumstances, raise concerns involving the most basic notions of justice. However, poor reasoning or an allegedly incorrect conclusion does not, without more, meet that threshold. The Court rejected attempts to use the public-policy exception to reopen questions already examined and decided by the arbitrator. Examining the arbitral proceedings and the four awards, the Supreme Court found that the arbitrator had considered the contractual provisions, documentary material, witness testimony, competing claims and counterclaims in considerable detail. The objections concerning management rights, alleged competing business activities, valuation and other contractual matters essentially challenged the arbitrator’s conclusions on the merits. The Court also rejected the allegation of bias, noting the history of the proceedings and the absence of a successful challenge before the competent supervisory forum at the arbitral seat. The Supreme Court concluded that the appellants had failed to establish any recognised ground for refusing enforcement of the foreign awards. It characterised the proceedings as speculative litigation seeking to undermine awards after extensive adjudication and held that such attempts were incompatible with the limited scope of Section 48. Accordingly, the Court dismissed both civil appeals and imposed costs of ₹50 lakh, payable by the appellants to Prysmian within four weeks. The judgment reinforces the pro-enforcement approach governing foreign arbitral awards in India and establishes that objections based on dissatisfaction with arbitral findings cannot substitute for the specific statutory grounds for resisting enforcement.

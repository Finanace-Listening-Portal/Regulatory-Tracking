Summary: The article examines the legal consequences of issuing FORM GST CMP-05 and FORM GST CMP-07 manually without uploading them to the GST common portal, particularly in cases involving composition dealers and brick kiln businesses in Uttar Pradesh. It analyses Section 10 of the CGST Act and Rule 6 of the CGST Rules, which prescribe notice, reply and adjudication procedures before denial of composition status. The author argues that composition status forms part of the electronic registration record and that an order remaining outside the portal prevents the taxpayer from responding through FORM GST CMP-06, claiming eligible input tax credit through FORM GST ITC-01 and filing regular returns. The discussion distinguishes issuance of statutory notices from their service under Section 169 and considers the electronic requirements governing registration and demand proceedings. It also examines the commencement of statutory limitation periods, Section 18(1)(c) credit on stock, retrospective denial under Circular No. 77/51/2018-GST, and the difficulties arising when registration has already been cancelled. Judicial principles concerning communication of orders, procedural fairness, impossibility of compliance and protection against arbitrary State action are considered. The article concludes that failure to operate the prescribed electronic procedure may materially prejudice taxpayers and provides grounds for challenging the validity and consequences of a composition-denial order and related tax demands. Either the notice in FORM GST CMP-05 and the order in FORM GST CMP-07 appear on his dashboard, or they surface for the first time as a recital in a demand order passed years later. The second situation is now common in brick kiln and small trader matters in Uttar Pradesh. The officer prepares the notice and the order on paper, claims to have served them, and never uploads them on the common portal. The dealer’s registration continues to show the composition status, while the department proceeds on the footing that the option stood denied from an earlier date. This article examines whether such a manual issue is consistent with the scheme of Section 10 of the Central Goods and Services Tax Act, 2017 and Rule 6 of the Rules, and what follows for the dealer’s right to input tax credit on stock and for his returns. The State Act and Rules are in identical terms and the discussion applies to both. 2. The statutory scheme of denial Section 10 permits an eligible registered person to opt for composition, subject to conditions. Section 10(5) provides that where the proper officer has reasons to believe that a taxable person has paid tax under the scheme despite not being eligible, such person shall be liable to penalty, and the provisions of Section 73 or Section 74 shall apply, mutatis mutandis, for determination of tax and penalty. The procedure for denial is in Rule 6. Sub-rule (4) reads: “Where the proper officer has reasons to believe that the registered person was not eligible to pay tax under section 10 or has contravened the provisions of the Act or provisions of this Chapter, he may issue a notice to such person in FORM GST CMP-05 to show cause within fifteen days of the receipt of such notice as to why the option to pay tax under section 10 shall not be denied.” Sub-rule (5) requires the proper officer, upon receipt of the reply in FORM GST CMP-06, to issue an order in FORM GST CMP-07 within thirty days of the receipt of the reply, either accepting the reply or denying the option from the date of the option or from the date of the event concerning the contravention. Sub-rule (6) then provides: “Every person who has furnished an intimation under sub-rule (2) or filed an application for withdrawal under sub-rule (3) or a person in respect of whom an order of withdrawal of option has been passed in FORM GST CMP-07 under sub-rule (5), may electronically furnish at the common portal, either directly or through a Facilitation Centre notified by the Commissioner, a statement in FORM GST ITC-01 containing details of the stock of inputs and inputs contained in semi-finished or finished goods held in stock by him on the date on which the option is withdrawn or denied, within a period of thirty days from the date from which the option is withdrawn or from the date of the order passed in FORM GST CMP-07, as the case may be.” Three features of the scheme deserve notice. The dealer is given fifteen days to reply. The officer is bound to decide within thirty days of the reply. And the consequence of the order, namely the right to file FORM GST ITC-01, is to be exercised electronically at the common portal, within thirty days counted from the order itself. 3. An electronic scheme by design The forms in Rule 6 are not stand-alone documents. FORM GST CMP-05 and FORM GST CMP-07 carry system reference numbers. The reply in FORM GST CMP-06 is filed on the portal against the notice, through the clarification facility in the registration module. FORM GST ITC-01 can be filed only when the portal records an order of denial or an intimation of withdrawal against the GSTIN. The status of the taxpayer as a composition or a normal taxpayer, on which the availability of FORM GSTR-3B depends, is likewise a portal entry. The registration chapter of the Rules carries the same design, and since the option under Section 10 is recorded in the registration itself, that chapter has a direct bearing on the question. It is taken up in the next part. For Central Tax officers, there is a further requirement. Circular No. 128/47/2019-GST dated 23.12.2019 requires a Document Identification Number on every communication, and permits a communication without it only in specified exceptional situations, with reasons recorded and the document regularised on the system within the prescribed time. A manual order that is never regularised stands outside that discipline. 4. Composition status is a registration particular Whether a person pays tax under Section 10 or under Section 9 is not a private label. It is part of his registration. The application for registration in FORM GST REG-01 asks the applicant to state whether he opts for composition, and the registration record on the common portal carries the taxpayer type, composition or regular, against the GSTIN. Every return the person may file, FORM GST CMP-08 and FORM GSTR-4 on one side and FORM GSTR-1 and FORM GSTR-3B on the other, is opened or closed by the portal on the strength of that entry. The law provides only a few routes by which this entry changes, and each of them runs through the portal. The person opts in by an intimation in FORM GST CMP-02 under Rule 3(3), which must be filed electronically on the common portal before the commencement of the financial year. He goes out voluntarily by an application in FORM GST CMP-04 under Rule 6(3), which too must be filed electronically on the common portal, or by an intimation in the same form under Rule 6(2) within seven days of ceasing to satisfy a condition. Section 10(3) makes the option lapse when the aggregate turnover crosses the limit, and Rule 6(2) requires the person to give effect to that lapse by the same intimation in FORM GST CMP-04. The only route by which the department can change the status against the will of the person is the order in FORM GST CMP-07 under Rule 6(5). Section 28 and Rule 19 confirm the approach. Any change in the particulars of registration is made by the proper officer only through the prescribed procedure: an application in FORM GST REG-14, and where the officer thinks the amendment is not warranted, a notice in FORM GST REG-03, a reply in FORM GST REG-04 and an order in FORM GST REG-05, or an order of amendment in FORM GST REG-15. Rule 26(3) then provides that all notices, certificates and orders under the registration chapter shall be issued electronically, through a digital signature certificate or e-signature or any other mode of verification notified by the Board. No provision authorises a proper officer to alter the registration particulars of a person by a paper order kept in his file. The order in FORM GST CMP-07 is precisely an order that alters a registration particular: it converts a composition taxpayer into a regular taxpayer from a stated date. It must therefore operate on the registration record. An order that leaves the record unchanged leaves the person, in law and on the system, a composition taxpayer. The department cannot hold him to the obligations of a regular taxpayer under Section 9 while its own registration record, which alone governs what returns he can file and what credit he can claim, continues to describe him as a composition dealer. The department may contend that under Rule 6(1) the option remains valid only so long as the conditions are satisfied, so that the status changes by operation of law when a condition fails, whatever the portal shows. That is true where the person himself is obliged to intimate the change under Rule 6(2). It does not hold where the department alleges a contravention which the person disputes; there the Rules interpose a notice, a reply and an order precisely because the status changes only upon an adjudication, and that adjudication must reach the registration record to take effect. 5. Issue is not service: Rule 142 and Section 169 The department’s answer to all of this is Section 169, which permits service of a notice or order by tendering it directly, by post, by e-mail, by making it available on the common portal, by publication or by affixation. The answer confuses two distinct acts. Section 169 deals with the mode by which a notice already issued is served. It says nothing about how the notice is to be issued in the first place. Issue is governed by the provision under which the notice is made, read with the Rules that prescribe the form and the manner. A notice that has not been issued in the prescribed manner is not cured by being tendered by hand. The manner of issue, in this field, is electronic. Section 10(5) provides that where a person found ineligible has paid tax under the composition scheme, the provisions of Section 73 or Section 74 shall apply, mutatis mutandis, for determination of tax and penalty. The denial of the option is thus the first step of a proceeding that ends in a determination under Section 73 or Section 74. Rule 142(1)(a) requires the proper officer to serve, along with the notice under Section 73 or Section 74, a summary of it electronically in FORM GST DRC-01, and Rule 142(5) requires the summary of the order to be uploaded electronically in FORM GST DRC-07. The Rules do not contemplate that the foundational step of such a proceeding, the notice and the order denying the option, may stay off the system while every later step must be on it. The forms in Rule 6 are system forms with system reference numbers, and the reply and the statement of stock that follow them can be filed only on the portal against the notice and the order. Seen in this way, a notice in FORM GST CMP-05 or an order in FORM GST CMP-07 that exists only on paper has not been issued in the manner the Act and the Rules require. Physical tender of such a paper may be a mode of service under Section 169, but there is nothing validly issued to serve. And where even tender or affixation is merely asserted, without an acknowledgment or a report of affixation, the department has nothing at all to show. 6. Communication and the running of time An order is effective only when it is communicated to the person affected; till then it remains an internal note of the officer (Bachhittar Singh v. State of Punjab, AIR 1963 SC 395). Where a statute fixes a period from the date of an order, the period runs from the date on which the person affected comes to know of it, actually or constructively (Raja Harish Chandra Raj Singh v. Deputy Land Acquisition Officer, AIR 1961 SC 1500). Applied to Rule 6(6), the thirty days for FORM GST ITC-01 cannot run from a date on which the order existed only on paper in the office file, because on that date the dealer could not have filed the statement at all. Time would begin only when the order is placed on the portal in a form on which the dealer can act. The same principle governs the fifteen days for reply under Rule 6(4). A notice that allows less than fifteen days, or an order passed before the fifteen days expire, is contrary to the rule itself. 7. Input tax credit on stock Section 18(1)(c) provides that where a registered person ceases to pay tax under Section 10, he shall be entitled to take credit of input tax in respect of inputs held in stock, inputs contained in semi-finished or finished goods held in stock and capital goods, on the day immediately preceding the date from which he becomes liable to pay tax under Section 9. The entitlement is stated in mandatory terms. Rule 40(1) requires a declaration in FORM GST ITC-01 within thirty days of becoming eligible, or within such further period as the Commissioner may extend. Section 18(2) bars the credit on any invoice more than one year old. The Board itself has tied this entitlement to the issue of the order in FORM GST CMP-07. Circular No. 77/51/2018-GST dated 31.12.2018 clarifies that the registered person shall be liable to pay tax under Section 9 from the date of issue of the order in FORM GST CMP-07, and that Section 18(1)(c) shall apply for claiming credit on stock held on the date immediately preceding the date of issue of the order. Circulars of the Board bind the department (Commissioner of Central Excise v. Dhiren Chemical Industries, (2002) 2 SCC 127 read with Paper Products Ltd. v. Commissioner of Central Excise, (1999) 7 SCC 84). A manual order thus produces a curious result. It gives effect to the liability, because the department treats the dealer as a normal taxpayer from the date of the order, and at the same time it makes the credit attached to that very event impossible to claim, because the portal never opens the window for FORM GST ITC-01. The officer does not refuse the credit in terms; he simply makes the only prescribed route unavailable. The law does not compel the impossible (lex non cogit ad impossibilia), and no authority may take advantage of its own wrong. The distinction between a substantive condition and a procedural requirement, drawn in Mangalore Chemicals and Fertilizers Ltd. v. Deputy Commissioner, 1992 Supp (1) SCC 21, places the thirty day window of Rule 40(1) on the procedural side; the power of the Commissioner to extend it confirms as much. Credit validly earned has been treated as a vested right that cannot be taken away by a technicality (Eicher Motors Ltd. v. Union of India, (1999) 2 SCC 361). Where the portal stood in the way of transitional credit, the Supreme Court directed it to be opened for filing (Union of India v. Filco Trade Centre Pvt. Ltd., order dated 22.07.2022). The case of a window kept shut by the officer’s own choice of a manual mode is, if anything, stronger. The department will rely on State of Karnataka v. M/s Ecom Gill Coffee Trading and like decisions holding that credit is a concession to be claimed strictly in the manner prescribed. Those decisions concern a dealer who failed to meet the prescribed condition. They do not deal with a condition that the department itself rendered incapable of compliance. 8. Returns after a manual denial The portal permits a dealer to file FORM GSTR-3B only when his registration shows him as a normal taxpayer from a given date. A manual order does not alter that status. The only other entry on the portal that alters it is FORM GST CMP-04, the dealer’s own intimation or application for withdrawal under Rule 6(2) or 6(3). Filing it is a voluntary act which the department may later treat as an acceptance of the denial. Circular No. 77/51/2018-GST also clarifies that the effective date of a withdrawal in FORM GST CMP-04 may not be prior to the commencement of the financial year in which it is filed. A dealer denied the option from an earlier year therefore finds no means on the portal to file returns for the intervening period, quite apart from the bar in Section 39(11) on returns filed after three years from the due date. This is why the Circular, for the period from the date of contravention to the date of the order in FORM GST CMP-07, directs determination of tax, interest and penalty under Section 73 or Section 74. The past period is not a matter for returns at all; it is a matter for adjudication with all the safeguards of a show cause notice, a hearing and a reasoned order. 9. What the Circular permits and what it does not It would be wrong to read Circular No. 77/51/2018-GST as protecting the dealer against retrospective denial. It clarifies that the effective date of denial may be any date, including a retrospective date, determined by the tax authority, but not prior to the date of the contravention. Read with Section 10(5), the denial may therefore reach back, and the past period may be brought under Section 73 or Section 74. What the Circular does not permit is a denial without the procedure of Rule 6. The retrospective date must be fixed by an order in FORM GST CMP-07, passed on a notice in FORM GST CMP-05, after a reply in FORM GST CMP-06 or after the time for reply has expired. Where the demand under Section 74 is founded on the order of denial, as is usually the case, the defects in that order travel into the demand. A further difficulty arises where the officer records in the order in FORM GST CMP-07 that the dealer deliberately suppressed turnover, and the notice under Section 74 follows days later. The finding on suppression is the very matter the notice under Section 74 is meant to put to the dealer. A finding recorded before the charge is framed shows a closed mind, and a proceeding begun on a closed mind does not satisfy the requirement of a fair hearing. 10. Denial after cancellation of registration Rule 6(4) speaks of a notice to the registered person. The option under Section 10 is an incident of registration. Once registration is cancelled, the person ceases to be a registered person and has no subsisting option to be denied. A notice in FORM GST CMP-05 and an order in FORM GST CMP-07 issued years after cancellation, often to a business that has closed, raise a serious question of jurisdiction. Section 29(3) preserves liability for the period before cancellation, but it does not revive an option that ended with the registration, and the procedure of Rule 6, built around the portal record of a live registrant, cannot be worked on a cancelled registration in the manner the Rule contemplates. 11. The constitutional dimension State action that is arbitrary is the antithesis of equality under Article 14 (E.P. Royappa v. State of Tamil Nadu, (1974) 4 SCC 3; Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India, (1978) 1 SCC 248; Shayara Bano v. Union of India, (2017) 9 SCC 1). Two dealers in identical situations stand differently if the order in one case is uploaded and in the other is kept on paper: the first receives the credit on stock and the facility to file returns, the second receives neither. That difference flows from nothing but the officer’s choice of mode, and has no nexus with any object of the Act. Article 265 is also engaged. The Act levies tax at normal rates on a person who ceases to be a composition dealer together with the credit under Section 18(1)(c). Recovery of the tax while the credit is made unavailable is recovery of more than the law authorises. 12. Conclusion The composition scheme was built on the common portal. The notice, the reply, the order, the statement of stock and the change of status are all designed to pass through it. Section 169 governs the service of a notice already issued; it does not make a paper notice a validly issued one. Composition status is a registration particular, and Section 28, Rule 19 and Rule 26(3) allow it to be changed only by an electronic order operating on the registration record. Rule 6, read with Section 10(5) and Rule 142, requires the denial to be issued on the system, and an order that is never placed on the portal leaves the dealer unable to reply in the prescribed form, unable to claim the credit the statute gives him, and unable to file the returns the department expects of him. Time under Rule 6(6) and Rule 40(1) cannot run against him while the order remains off the system. And where the order is passed after cancellation, before the time for reply has expired, or with a finding of suppression recorded in advance, the defects go to the root of the demand founded on it. The issue is not one of form for its own sake. The electronic procedure is the safeguard the Rules give the dealer. When the officer bypasses it, he does not merely change the mode of communication; he takes away the means by which the dealer exercises his statutory rights.

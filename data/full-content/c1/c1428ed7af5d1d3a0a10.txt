Articles 3 min read Why rupee remains weak despite record FCNR flows The $132.98 billion FCNR mobilisation does not mean that an equivalent amount of fresh dollar demand for rupees entered the spot market. The foreign currency is being placed with banks and swapped with the RBI, so the immediate effect is to increase the RBI's foreign currency resources and inject rupee liquidity into the banking system. Elevated crude prices, importer demand and higher global yields continue to put pressure on the currency.The special facility had attracted total foreign currency inflows of $143.596 billion as of September 18, comprising $132.980 billion through FCNR(B) deposits, $5.320 billion through overseas foreign currency borrowings and $5.296 billion through external commercial borrowings. The FCNR(B) deposits were mobilised by August 31, while the ECB and OFCB windows remain open until December 31. Advt The structure of the FCNR swap is central to why the inflows have not translated into an equivalent strengthening of the rupee. Banks mobilise foreign currency deposits from non-residents and bring the foreign currency into the swap arrangement with the RBI. The RBI provides rupee liquidity against the foreign currency, while the foreign currency comes into the central bank's reserves. At maturity, the swap is reversed. The arrangement therefore gives the RBI additional foreign currency resources and gives banks rupee liquidity, but the $132.98 billion mobilisation should not be treated as $132.98 billion of fresh, permanent buying of rupees in the spot market.The large inflows instead created a substantial surplus of rupee liquidity in the banking system. The surplus rose to a record Rs 11.16 lakh crore, after which the RBI used liquidity absorption measures including government bond sales and foreign exchange swaps. The surplus subsequently fell to about Rs 4.92 lakh crore. The FCNR operation has therefore simultaneously strengthened the RBI's foreign currency resources and increased the need to manage excess rupee liquidity.The reserves angleThe external buffer has also strengthened sharply. Forex reserves stood at $780.782 billion in the week ended September 11, down $4.924 billion from $785.706 billion a week earlier. The previous week's increase of $44.903 billion was the largest weekly rise on record. Foreign currency assets fell by $2.372 billion to $645.796 billion, while gold reserves declined by $2.591 billion to $111.225 billion. Advt The fall in reserves should not, however, be read as equivalent to RBI dollar sales. Reserve values also change because of valuation movements in currencies and gold. The reserve movement therefore needs to be viewed alongside the RBI's foreign exchange operations rather than treated as a direct measure of intervention.Meanwhile, the underlying demand for dollars has remained strong. Higher crude prices increase India's import bill and the dollar requirements of oil companies and other importers. When the rupee is under pressure, importers can also increase dollar purchases or hedging, adding to near-term demand for the US currency. Advt Global yields are another source of pressure. Higher US Treasury yields increase the relative attraction of dollar assets and can put pressure on emerging-market currencies. On September 25, the benchmark 10-year US Treasury yield had moved above 5.1 per cent, while Brent crude was around $105 a barrel. The rupee nevertheless closed at Rs 95.8125 per dollar, compared with Rs 95.9550 in the previous session.The FCNR inflows have therefore given the RBI a much larger foreign currency cushion to manage volatility, rather than automatically driving the rupee higher. The currency's direction will continue to depend on the balance between dollar inflows and demand, with crude prices, global yields, capital flows and India's import requirements remaining key variables.

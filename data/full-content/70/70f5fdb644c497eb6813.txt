Summary: The GST Appellate Tribunal, Agra Bench, in Commissioner State Tax, U.P. v. Kamlesh Trading Company, dismissed the Department’s appeal against the first appellate order setting aside confiscation-related penalty and fine of ₹1,45,055. During an inspection conducted on 30 November 2024, the Department alleged that 15,441 kg of tobacco stem powder valued at ₹3,39,442 was unaccounted for and initiated proceedings under Section 130 read with Section 122 of the CGST/UPGST Acts. The Tribunal examined the statutory framework under Section 35(6), which provides for determination of tax on unaccounted goods or services through the applicable demand provisions. Relying on three Allahabad High Court decisions, namely Dinesh Kumar Pradeep Kumar, Shri Om Steels and V.K. Electricals, the Tribunal held that directly invoking confiscation proceedings instead of following the prescribed tax-determination machinery was legally unsustainable in the circumstances of the case. Significantly, the Tribunal did not examine the factual merits of the alleged stock discrepancy, as the legal defect was sufficient to dismiss the departmental appeal. The article explains the distinction between detecting unaccounted stock and selecting the appropriate statutory proceedings, supported by a hypothetical illustration. It also highlights the relevance of Section 74A to tax determination for FY 2024-25 onwards, while clarifying that the Tribunal itself did not examine that provision. The decision emphasises procedural legality without granting a general exemption from tax liability or invalidating every confiscation proceeding under Section 130. But does that finding, by itself, permit the Department to directly initiate confiscation proceedings and impose penalty and fine under Section 130? In Commissioner State Tax, U.P. v. Kamlesh Trading Company, the GST Appellate Tribunal, Agra Bench, answered this question against the Department. The Tribunal held that, in the circumstances before it, the officer should have followed the statutory procedure for determination of tax on allegedly unaccounted goods instead of directly proceeding under Section 130 read with Section 122. Accordingly, the Department’s appeal was dismissed, and the relief granted to the taxpayer against penalty and fine of ₹1,45,055 remained undisturbed. The importance of this decision lies in a clear distinction: detection of an irregularity and selection of the legal provision for taking action are two different matters. Even where the Department alleges that goods are unaccounted for, the proceedings must be initiated under the provision applicable to that situation. 1. What happened in this case? Kamlesh Trading Company, situated at Kaimganj, Farrukhabad, was engaged in trading in tobacco leaves and processed tobacco items. On 30 November 2024, the Special Investigation Branch, Etawah, inspected its business premises. According to the Department, 15,441 kg of tobacco stem powder was found unaccounted for. The stock was seized, and the proper officer subsequently issued a show-cause notice under Section 130 read with Section 122 of the CGST/UPGST Acts. The taxpayer submitted a reply, but the officer did not accept it. By the adjudication order dated 17 March 2025: The value of the allegedly unaccounted goods was determined at ₹3,39,442. Penalty and fine aggregating to ₹1,45,055 were imposed. The taxpayer challenged that order before the First Appellate Authority under Section 107. By its order dated 12 September 2025, the First Appellate Authority allowed the taxpayer’s appeal and set aside the original order and the consequential penalty and fine. The Department then approached the Tribunal seeking reversal of that relief. 3. The real dispute was about the statutory route adopted The Department argued that a tax officer empowered to inspect business premises and verify records was also competent to initiate proceedings under Section 130. The taxpayer supported the First Appellate Authority’s decision and sought dismissal of the departmental appeal. The Tribunal therefore examined whether proceedings under Section 130 read with Section 122 were legally sustainable where the dispute arose from excess or unaccounted stock detected during a survey or search under Section 67. This distinction is important. The question was not merely whether the officer could conduct an inspection. The question was whether the findings of that inspection justified the particular proceedings initiated against the taxpayer. 4. Why Section 35(6) became important The Tribunal examined Section 35, which deals with maintenance of accounts and records. Its reasoning centred on Section 35(6). As discussed in the order, where goods or services are not accounted for in accordance with the statutory requirements, that provision enables determination of the tax payable as if those goods or services had been supplied. However, the tax must be determined through the prescribed demand machinery. The Tribunal discussed this requirement with reference to Sections 73 and 74. Put simply, the Tribunal’s reasoning was: Allegedly unaccounted goods may give rise to a tax-determination exercise. Their discovery does not, by itself, justify replacing that exercise with direct confiscation proceedings. The Tribunal accordingly held that the proper officer had adopted an unsustainable course by directly invoking Section 130 in the case before it. The legal objection was therefore not a denial of the Department’s power to examine unaccounted stock. It was an objection to the manner in which that power had been exercised. 5. Allahabad High Court decisions followed by the Tribunal The Tribunal relied upon three decisions of the Allahabad High Court: Decision Case number and date Principle relied upon by GSTAT Dinesh Kumar Pradeep Kumar v. Additional Commissioner, Grade-II Writ Tax No. 1082 of 2022; 25 July 2024 Excess stock found during a survey should be addressed through the tax-determination provisions rather than direct proceedings under Section 130. Shri Om Steels v. Additional Commissioner, Grade-II Writ Tax No. 1007 of 2022; 19 July 2024 Discovery of excess stock does not justify substituting confiscation proceedings for the prescribed demand proceedings. V.K. Electricals v. Additional Commissioner, Grade-II Writ Tax No. 957 of 2024; 23 August 2024 Section 130 proceedings adopted in the excess-stock situation considered were unsustainable. The Tribunal treated these decisions as governing the issue before it and applied them while sustaining the taxpayer’s relief. The decision in Dinesh Kumar Pradeep Kumar independently confirms the same approach: even where excess stock is alleged, determination and quantification of liability must follow the statutory procedure discussed by the High Court. 6. What exactly did the Tribunal decide? In paragraph 11, the Tribunal held that the officer had directly initiated proceedings under Section 130 instead of following the tax-determination provisions referred to in its reasoning. It therefore agreed with the First Appellate Authority that the adjudication order and the resulting penalty and fine could not be sustained. Paragraph 12 is equally significant. Having found the proceedings legally unsustainable, the Tribunal expressly stated that it was unnecessary to examine the factual aspects of the case. The outcome should therefore be understood correctly: The Department’s appeal was dismissed. The First Appellate Authority’s relief was sustained. The penalty and fine of ₹1,45,055 remained set aside. The Tribunal did not decide the factual stock dispute on its merits. It would be incorrect to report the decision as a factual finding that there was no excess stock. The taxpayer succeeded because the proceedings adopted by the officer were held to be contrary to law. 7. Illustration: excess stock does not settle the legal issue Consider a hypothetical case of a trader dealing in packing materials. During inspection, the following difference is recorded: Particulars Quantity Stock reflected in the records examined 8,000 kg Physical stock recorded during inspection 9,200 kg Alleged excess stock 1,200 kg Assume that the officer values the difference at ₹2,40,000. The officer issues a notice directly under Section 130 read with Section 122, proposing confiscation-related consequences on the ground that the additional stock is unaccounted for. Applying the reasoning in Kamlesh Trading Company, the trader’s legal objection would be that the alleged stock difference must be dealt with through the applicable tax-determination provisions, rather than by directly adopting the confiscation route merely because excess stock has been recorded. At the same time, the trader may have a separate factual explanation: A purchase receipt had not yet been posted in the stock register. Goods belonging to a customer were stored separately but included in the physical count. The inspecting team counted the same goods twice. The difference arose from conversion between bags, pieces and kilograms. These are hypothetical explanations, not findings from the reported case. Each would require supporting records. The illustration shows why two questions must be kept separate: Is there actually any unaccounted stock? Even if a difference exists, has the Department initiated proceedings under the correct provision? A taxpayer may contest both. In Kamlesh Trading Company, the Tribunal decided the second question and did not consider it necessary to determine the first. 8. Why this decision matters An inspection finding does not determine the legal consequence automatically The officer may discover a difference between the books and the physical stock. But the nature of the proceedings must still be justified under the Act. In this case, the Tribunal did not accept that the power to inspect and verify records was sufficient justification for the direct invocation of Section 130. The choice of provision is a substantive issue The dispute was not about an insignificant mistake in the wording of a notice. It concerned the statutory basis for imposing confiscation-related penalty and fine. The Tribunal’s decision shows that the Department must justify the legal foundation of the proceedings, not merely repeat the allegation that goods were unaccounted for. The decision protects the prescribed adjudication process Section 35(6), as discussed by the Tribunal, provides a route for determining tax on unaccounted goods. That route cannot be displaced merely because the allegation arose during an inspection. The Tribunal’s reasoning preserves the distinction between determining tax liability and imposing confiscation-related consequences. The taxpayer’s success was not dependent on disproving the entire stock allegation This is a particularly useful aspect of the order. The Tribunal found the legal defect sufficient to dispose of the departmental appeal. It did not require a final factual determination of the alleged stock discrepancy before granting relief against the proceedings under challenge. 9. An important present-law qualification: Section 74A The Tribunal repeatedly referred to Sections 73 and 74. An article discussing this decision today should report that reasoning faithfully, but should also explain the subsequent statutory framework relevant to its practical application. Under the CGST Act, Section 74A applies to determination of tax pertaining to financial year 2024-25 onwards. Section 74A(12) expressly states this financial-year applicability. Accordingly, practitioners should not mechanically reproduce “Sections 73/74” from the judgment in every current case. The applicable demand provision must be identified with reference to the financial year concerned and the relevant Central and State statutory provisions. Sections 73 and 74 remain relevant to earlier periods, while Section 74A governs the CGST demand framework for financial year 2024-25 onwards. This deserves attention here because the inspection took place on 30 November 2024. Nevertheless, the Tribunal’s order does not examine Section 74A or decide its application. It would therefore be inappropriate to attribute any finding on Section 74A to this Bench. The principle to carry forward is the requirement to use the legally applicable demand machinery—not an unqualified statement that Sections 73 and 74 govern every excess-stock dispute. 10. What the decision should not be understood to mean The judgment should not be presented as a general exemption for unaccounted stock. It does not establish that: Stock records need not be maintained. Every difference found during an inspection is explained. No tax can ever be determined on unaccounted goods. Every proceeding under Section 130, whatever its factual foundation, is invalid. The Tribunal decided the legality of the proceedings adopted in the excess-stock case before it. Its conclusion was that the direct invocation of Section 130 read with Section 122 was unsustainable on those facts and under the authorities it followed. Equally, setting aside the particular confiscation-related proceedings should not be described as a final adjudication extinguishing every possible tax liability arising from the stock allegation. The order did not decide that wider question. 11. A drafting caution in reading the order The summary form on page 2 contains a sentence suggesting that the First Appellate Authority’s order was set aside. Read in isolation, that sentence could cause confusion. However, the detailed findings and operative conclusion in paragraphs 11 to 13 clearly show that the Tribunal dismissed the Department’s appeal and sustained the First Appellate Authority’s relief. The case should be reported on that basis, while noting the inconsistent wording in the summary form. Conclusion Kamlesh Trading Company is an important Tribunal decision because it insists that the Department follow the statutory route applicable to the alleged default. The discovery of excess or allegedly unaccounted stock did not justify directly imposing confiscation-related penalty and fine under Section 130 in the proceedings before the Bench. For taxpayers and professionals, the decision brings the focus back to the first legal question in any notice: not only whether the allegation is correct, but whether the proceeding itself has been validly initiated. The practical lesson is straightforward. Stock discrepancies must be examined and explained, but a disputed stock position cannot, by itself, cure the adoption of an incorrect statutory proceeding. That is the central significance of the Tribunal’s decision.

(Supreme Court of India) Summary: The Supreme Court allowed the appeals and ordered enforcement of a decades-old decree for specific performance and possession, holding that collusive resistance and belated tenancy claims could not defeat its execution. Origin of the 1980 Agreement and Specific Performance Litigation The dispute originated in a contract for sale of immovable property dated 30 June 1980. Ayyavoo Udayar, predecessor of the appellants, agreed to purchase the property from Ramanujan and Jagadeesan for ₹67,000. He paid ₹10,000 as earnest money, with ₹57,000 payable by 15 November 1980. Although the purchaser sought completion and the vendors indicated that they would execute the deed on 20 November 1980, the transaction was not completed. The purchaser consequently instituted Original Suit No. 514 of 1983 in Salem for specific performance, execution and registration of a sale deed and delivery of actual physical possession. His plaint expressly sought possession of the entire property, whether the vendors executed the deed voluntarily or the court executed it on their behalf. This detail was important because the eventual execution controversy concerned whether the decree included possession, rather than merely execution of the conveyance. The purchaser also joined the vendors’ nephews, who were alleged to have been put in occupation to create an appearance of third-party possession. The plaint recorded apprehensions that they would obstruct the purchaser from entering the land. Thus the possible obstruction was not an unforeseen development arising decades later; it was identified at the beginning of the litigation. The nephews, later respondent Nos. 1 and 2 before the Supreme Court, did not contest the suit and allowed it to proceed ex parte against them. On 2 April 1986 the trial court decreed the suit as prayed for and directed execution of the conveyance. Subsequent appellate proceedings modified aspects of the decree but did not eliminate the substantive entitlement that ultimately formed the basis of execution. The Supreme Court treated the original pleadings, the presence of the obstructors as parties, and the possession relief as essential features in evaluating their much later objections. Appeals, Deposit of Consideration and Court-Executed Sale Deed The litigation continued through successive appellate stages. The vendors challenged the trial court decree, and a later appeal before the Madras High Court was dismissed on 19 March 2004 subject to the purchaser’s successors depositing a further ₹67,000 within one month. The appellants deposited that amount on 19 April 2004. An objection that the deposit was one day late was subsequently rejected because the preceding day had been a holiday and the payment was made on the next working day. The vendors’ special leave petition against the High Court’s judgment was dismissed by the Supreme Court on 20 January 2006, and their review petition was dismissed on 18 April 2006. These proceedings demonstrated that the merits of the decree had been tested through the appellate hierarchy. The appellants had meanwhile commenced execution through R.E.P. No. 237 of 2004 seeking execution of the sale deed and possession. That petition was initially dismissed because the special leave proceedings were pending, but the High Court restored it after the Supreme Court disposed of the challenge. A second execution petition was withdrawn. On 17 August 2007 the executing court executed a registered sale deed in favour of the appellants. The original deed named the nephews alongside the vendors, although the nephews held no title to convey. Following revision proceedings, the nephews’ names were removed as executants by a rectification deed dated 25 January 2008. The Supreme Court later considered the distinction between removing persons from a conveyance because they lacked ownership and releasing them from an existing decree for delivery of possession. The former did not automatically establish the latter. Importantly, the completed conveyance also meant that the original vendors could not subsequently treat the property as though they still held title when supporting the nephews’ claimed occupation. Obstruction to Possession and the Section 47 Application On 12 February 2008 the executing court ordered delivery of possession. Court personnel and local officials attended the property to implement that order, but respondent No. 1 resisted delivery and threatened self-immolation if entry was attempted. The court officers therefore could not deliver possession and submitted an obstruction report. The nephews then approached the executing court under Section 47 of the Code of Civil Procedure, read with the court’s inherent powers, objecting to enforcement. They asserted that they had not received proper notice of the execution steps, that deletion of their names from the registered sale deed prevented enforcement against them, and that the decree-holders had acted fraudulently. They also relied on possession and later advanced a claim of protection as cultivating tenants. The executing court ultimately accepted their objections on 12 August 2011, holding, among other things, that they were in occupation and that the execution petition did not specifically seek possession from them. It suggested that the decree-holders would have to take appropriate legal steps. The appellants challenged that decision in Civil Revision Petition No. 4311 of 2011 and sought amendments to the execution petition, including clarification that delivery of possession was sought against all respondents. The amendment applications were rejected in April 2015. On 18 December 2019 the Madras High Court affirmed the executing court’s approach and dismissed the revisions. It considered the absence of notice and the manner in which the original execution prayer was framed to be fatal. The Supreme Court was therefore required to decide whether such procedural objections could defeat a decree already affirmed at higher levels, and whether a person who had been a party to the original litigation could invoke an allegedly independent possessory status only at the enforcement stage. Rival Submissions on Notice, Possession and Cultivating Tenancy The appellants argued that the nephews had been parties to the original suit, knew of the possession claim and had deliberately declined to contest it. They contended that the execution petition was filed promptly after appellate confirmation and that Order XXI Rule 22 did not require a fresh show-cause notice in the circumstances advanced by them. They also maintained that the nephews had participated through counsel in related revision proceedings, contradicting the suggestion that the execution process came as a surprise. The requested amendment, in their submission, did not create a new substantive entitlement; it merely clarified enforcement of the existing decree. The appellants additionally alleged collusion between the vendors and the nephews, pointing to the later rectification of the deed and the nephews’ attempts to secure retrospective revenue entries. The respondents countered that they were not parties to the agreement of sale and claimed lawful possession through cultivation dating back to 1967. They argued that the decree and pleadings did not justify their dispossession, that the court-executed deed no longer named them, and that the decree-holders should have pursued a separate possession action or a specific Order XXI Rule 97 remedy. They invoked the Tamil Nadu Cultivating Tenants’ Protection Act, 1955 and asserted that the civil court lacked jurisdiction over their eviction. They further relied on authority recognising that an inherently jurisdictionally void decree may be challenged even during execution. The Supreme Court did not treat these rival assertions as equivalent to findings of fact. It examined the procedural history, actual participation, the documentary foundation of the tenancy claim and the consequences of the already executed sale deed before determining whether the resistance was genuine. Statutory Framework: Section 47 and Order XXI Rules 35, 97 to 103 The Court examined the relationship between Section 47 CPC and the possession-enforcement machinery in Order XXI. Section 47 requires the executing court, rather than a separate suit, to determine questions arising between parties to the suit or their representatives that relate to execution, discharge or satisfaction of the decree. Order XXI Rule 35 governs delivery of immovable property and authorises removal of persons bound by the decree who refuse to vacate. Rule 97 addresses resistance or obstruction faced by a decree-holder or execution-sale purchaser in obtaining possession. Its wording extends to resistance by any person and is not confined to the judgment-debtor. Rule 99 addresses a different temporal situation: a qualifying person other than the judgment-debtor who has already been dispossessed may seek relief. Rules 98 and 100 specify orders following adjudication, while Rule 101 requires the executing court to decide relevant questions of right, title and interest arising between parties to Rule 97 or Rule 99 proceedings. Rule 103 gives the resulting adjudicatory order the force of a decree for appellate purposes. The Court explained that the scheme aims to consolidate relevant property-right disputes within execution instead of spawning fresh litigation. But the scope of the inquiry remains connected to the decree and the relevant obstruction. A claimant cannot automatically use this framework to reopen questions that should have been raised and decided during the original suit. The Court also drew attention to the distinction between resistance before dispossession and an application for restoration after dispossession. This distinction matters in practice because the proper procedural route depends on the actual stage of possession and the nature of the claimant’s asserted right. Judicial Precedents on Resistance by Strangers to a Decree The judgment reviewed the development of Order XXI jurisprudence through earlier Supreme Court decisions. In Brahmdeo Chaudhary v. Rishikesh Prasad Jaiswal, the Court had explained the interconnected operation of Rules 97, 98, 99 and 101 when possession is obstructed. In Shreenath v. Rajesh, the Court emphasised that a person asserting an independent right may seek adjudication before being forcibly dispossessed and that procedural law should facilitate rather than defeat justice. Silverline Forum Pvt. Ltd. v. Rajiv Trust recognised the need to assess whether a resisting person has a legally relevant claim; the executing court need not conduct an unnecessary full trial into matters that do not legitimately arise. NSS Narayan Sarma v. Goldstone Exports considered the scheme for delivery of possession and adjudication of third-party claims. The Court also referred to Bhanwar Lal v. Satyanarain while addressing how an obstruction complaint should be treated within the proper execution framework. These authorities together demonstrate that genuine independent claims deserve procedural adjudication, but the right to object does not become an unrestricted power to paralyse execution. The present case was distinctive because the purported third-party objectors were already defendants in the original specific-performance suit and had known that possession was sought. Their claimed independence could not be assumed merely because their names were removed as vendors from the registered conveyance. The Court’s analysis therefore focused on whether the factual and legal basis of their resistance existed independently of the vendors and survived the completed adjudication of the suit. Limits on Reopening the Decree During Execution The Supreme Court reiterated that an executing court ordinarily cannot go behind the decree. It referred to C.F. Angadi v. Y.S. Hirannayya and Vasudev Dhanjibhai Modi v. Rajabhai Abdul Rehman for the principle that execution is not an appellate reconsideration of the merits. Questions arising in execution must be connected with carrying out, discharging or satisfying the decree; matters that ought to have been raised during the original proceedings cannot simply be revived to undermine it. The Court also discussed Rahul S. Shah v. Jinendra Kumar Gandhi, which had criticised repeated attempts to convert Section 47 into a retrial and had issued directions to prevent execution from becoming an endless second lawsuit. The respondents invoked the recognised exception for decrees that are nullities because the issuing court inherently lacked jurisdiction, citing Sunder Dass v. Ram Prakash. The Court did not deny that such an exception exists as a matter of law. Instead, it examined whether these respondents had demonstrated the factual premise necessary to invoke it, namely a legally established cultivating tenancy carrying the asserted statutory protection. This is an important distinction: an abstract proposition about jurisdiction cannot itself establish that a particular decree is void. The objector must show that the relevant jurisdictional defect actually exists. In this litigation, the respondents had neither asserted their cultivating-tenancy case during the original suit nor produced persuasive contemporaneous documentation establishing the independent right later claimed. Accordingly, the attempt to recast the decree as a nullity failed on the facts considered by the Court. Revenue Records and the Alleged Cultivating Tenancy The nephews sought to establish possession by approaching revenue authorities in 2008, long after the specific-performance suit had been decreed and after the court had executed the registered sale deed in favour of the appellants. They requested inclusion in cultivation accounts retrospectively from 1974. The revenue process resulted in entries from 2008, but the authorities did not authorise retrospective alteration of the closed accounts from 1974. The vendors provided statements or no-objection support for the nephews’ claimed occupation, and a certificate relating to earlier possession was discussed. The Supreme Court examined the evidentiary significance of these documents against the chronology of ownership. By 17 August 2007 the sale deed had been executed in favour of the appellants. The original vendors therefore had ceased to be owners when they subsequently supported the nephews’ revenue claims. The Court found it difficult to understand why the revenue authorities treated their no-objection as a basis for recognising an earlier right after title had already passed. The records did not establish an independent legal right to possession surviving against the decree-holders. Nor did they substantiate the asserted cultivating tenancy for the relevant earlier period. The Court concluded that the circumstances indicated apparent collusion designed to frustrate the decree. It declined to accept that the respondents were bona fide cultivating tenants entitled to defeat delivery of possession. The judgment should not be read as a general ruling that every revenue entry is immaterial or that every cultivating tenant lacks statutory protection. Its conclusion turned on the timing, origin and reliability of the evidence and the respondents’ prior opportunity to raise their case. Why the High Court and Executing Court Were Reversed The Supreme Court found that the lower courts had concentrated on formal defects while failing to confront the central issue: whether the obstruction was bona fide or a collusive effort by persons connected to the vendors. The respondents were nephews of the original vendors, were joined as defendants precisely because they were alleged to be in occupation, and chose not to contest the specific-performance suit. They did not actively pursue their asserted tenancy claim during the appellate proceedings. They later objected to enforcement after a court-executed sale deed had transferred title and an order for possession had been made. Their post-decree revenue materials were insufficient to establish an independent interest capable of defeating the decree. The lower courts had also treated the deletion of their names from the conveyance as if it conclusively removed their obligation to yield possession. That approach confused the capacity of a person to convey title with the separate question whether that person was bound by an adjudicated possession decree. The Supreme Court held that the challenge based on alleged cultivating tenancy did not justify reopening the decree or questioning the civil court’s jurisdiction. It also held that the High Court had wrongly rejected amendments to the execution petition. The emphasis was on giving effect to the existing adjudicated relief rather than permitting avoidable procedural objections to defeat it. The judgment thus protects the finality and enforceability of decrees while leaving intact the statutory mechanism for genuine and relevant third-party claims. The outcome was based on the particular factual record, not on a categorical exclusion of all resistance applications. Nationwide Directions on Six-Month Disposal of Execution Proceedings Beyond resolving the property dispute, the Supreme Court addressed the systemic problem of delayed execution. It revisited Rahul S. Shah v. Jinendra Kumar Gandhi, including the direction that execution proceedings should ordinarily be completed within six months from filing, with reasons recorded in writing if an extension becomes necessary. The Court observed that litigants often spend years securing final decrees and then face further prolonged proceedings before obtaining any practical benefit. It considered such delay inconsistent with the purpose of adjudication. The Court also referred to Bhoj Raj Garg v. Goyal Education and Welfare Society as reiterating the six-month direction. In the present judgment it directed all High Courts to obtain information from their respective district judiciaries concerning pending execution petitions. After collecting the data, High Courts were directed to issue administrative orders or circulars requiring disposal of pending execution petitions within six months, with the concerned presiding officer answerable administratively for non-compliance. The High Courts were further directed to forward individual reports containing pendency and disposal information to the Supreme Court Registry. The Registry was instructed to send the judgment to all High Courts, and the matter was to be listed after seven months solely to report compliance. These directions are significant because they address execution delays at an institutional level, rather than confining the remedy to the individual litigants. They should nevertheless be reported with the judgment’s actual wording and procedural context, rather than portrayed as an automatic substantive invalidation of any execution proceeding lasting longer than six months. Final Relief and Practical Significance The Supreme Court allowed Civil Appeal Nos. 3640–3642 of 2025 and set aside the Madras High Court judgment dated 18 December 2019 as well as the relevant executing-court order. It directed the executing court to ensure that vacant and peaceful possession of the suit property was handed over to the appellants, using police assistance if necessary. That exercise was required to be completed within two months from 6 March 2025. The Court disposed of the pending applications. The operative order therefore went beyond a general statement of principle: it required delivery of the specific property within a fixed period after decades of litigation. The decision illustrates several practical lessons for civil litigants and courts. Pleadings seeking specific performance should clearly articulate possession relief and identify known occupiers. Execution applications should accurately describe the decree, parties and relief sought, although curable drafting issues should not be allowed to defeat a substantive decree without examining their real effect. Genuine third-party resistance requires a legally relevant independent claim supported by reliable evidence, and the proper route under Order XXI depends on whether obstruction occurs before possession or relief is sought after dispossession. Revenue entries and possession certificates must be examined in their factual and chronological context, especially where the person supporting the entry has already transferred ownership. Finally, courts must distinguish an objection going to inherent jurisdiction from an attempt to relitigate the merits of a final decree. The central message is that a successful litigant should not be deprived indefinitely of the fruits of adjudication through collateral obstruction and repetitive execution proceedings.

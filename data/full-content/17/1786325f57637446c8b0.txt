Vs Micromax Informatics FZE (Supreme Court of India) The Supreme Court dismissed an arbitration petition filed by Afghanistan-based M/s Arif Azim Co. Ltd. seeking appointment of an arbitrator under Section 11(6) read with Section 11(12)(a) of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996, in a commercial dispute with UAE-incorporated M/s Micromax Informatics FZE. The dispute arose from a Consumer Distributorship Agreement dated 9 November 2010, executed in Kabul, Afghanistan, under which the petitioner distributed Micromax mobile handsets in Afghanistan. Clause 26 provided for arbitration under UAE Arbitration and Conciliation Rules and designated Dubai, UAE, as the venue, while Clause 27 stipulated that the agreement would be governed by UAE law and subject to the non-exclusive jurisdiction of Dubai courts. The petitioner sought recovery of an alleged outstanding credit balance of USD 88,425 with interest at 24% per annum. Following correspondence concerning reconciliation of accounts, it invoked arbitration on 14 September 2022 and approached the Supreme Court after the respondents did not appoint an arbitrator. The petitioner argued that designation of Dubai merely as the venue did not conclusively establish the juridical seat of arbitration. It contended that the non-exclusive jurisdiction clause preserved access to Indian courts, particularly because Micromax’s Indian parent company had participated in certain transactions, issued invoices and received payments. Relying on the closest connection test and the parties’ commercial conduct, the petitioner maintained that India had sufficient jurisdictional connections to entertain the appointment petition. The respondent disputed maintainability, submitting that both contracting parties were foreign entities, the distributorship agreement was executed outside India, the contractual territory was Afghanistan, and the parties had expressly chosen UAE law, UAE arbitration rules and Dubai as the arbitration venue. It further contended that the Indian Micromax entity was not a signatory to the arbitration agreement and that commercial correspondence could not alter the contractual choice of arbitral jurisdiction. The Supreme Court examined the historical development of Indian arbitration law, including the pre-BALCO doctrine of concurrent jurisdiction, the territorial approach adopted in Bharat Aluminium Co. v. Kaiser Aluminium Technical Services Inc., and subsequent decisions distinguishing the juridical seat from the physical venue of arbitration. It explained that the seat determines the courts exercising supervisory jurisdiction over arbitral proceedings and that the parties’ contractual designation must be interpreted in its full context. Applying the Shashoua principle, the Court held that where an arbitration agreement designates a place as the venue and no significant contrary indications exist, that place ordinarily constitutes the juridical seat. The selection of UAE arbitration rules, UAE governing law and Dubai as the venue collectively demonstrated that Dubai was intended to be the seat. The reference to non-exclusive jurisdiction of Dubai courts did not confer supervisory jurisdiction upon Indian courts or override the arbitration-specific contractual arrangements. The Court further explained that the closest connection test cannot ordinarily displace an identifiable contractual choice of arbitral seat, although it may remain relevant where neither the venue nor the applicable curial law provides sufficient guidance. It also examined the doctrine of forum non conveniens, observing that even assuming the non-exclusive jurisdiction clause permitted proceedings before another competent forum, Dubai was the more appropriate forum given the agreed seat, governing law, arbitration rules and relevant commercial connections. The judgment clarified the application of Part I of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act to foreign-seated arbitrations, including the distinction between agreements executed before and after 6 September 2012, the date associated with the prospective application of BALCO. It concluded that the pre-BALCO position did not assist the petitioner because the parties had excluded the application of Indian arbitration law through their contractual choices. Accordingly, the Supreme Court held that the arbitration was seated in Dubai and that the arbitration agreement was not governed by Indian law. The petition under Section 11 was therefore not maintainable before the Supreme Court and was dismissed. The parties were directed to bear their own costs, and pending applications were disposed of. The decision reinforces the significance of clearly identifying the juridical seat, governing law and arbitration procedure in international commercial contracts and confirms that a non-exclusive court jurisdiction clause does not, by itself, displace the supervisory jurisdiction associated with the chosen arbitral seat.

Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); Takshashila Resicom Private Limited Vs Sheth Fincap Services Private Limited (Gujarat High Court) Summary: The Gujarat High Court, in Takshashila Resicom Private Limited & Ors. Vs M/s Sheth Fincap Services Private Limited & Ors., dismissed a writ petition challenging the initiation of corporate insolvency resolution proceedings under Section 7 of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016, holding that the existence of an already-invoked statutory appellate remedy before the National Company Law Appellate Tribunal (NCLAT), coupled with the absence of exceptional circumstances, justified refusal to exercise writ jurisdiction. The Court clarified that although the existence of an alternative statutory remedy does not absolutely bar a writ petition under Article 226 of the Constitution of India, the High Court ordinarily exercises judicial restraint where the same issues are pending before the competent appellate forum. The dispute arose from financial transactions between the petitioner companies and M/s Sheth Fincap Services Private Limited. A loan agreement dated 15 March 2024 involved disbursement of ₹11 crore, secured through mortgage of 38 units. Subsequently, a supplementary loan agreement dated 21 March 2025 renewed ₹5.23 crore, with additional security comprising 30 units. Following disputes concerning repayment, interest and penal charges, the financial creditor initiated proceedings before the NCLT, Ahmedabad. The amount claimed as outstanding as on 30 April 2026 was ₹7,40,33,665, comprising principal of ₹5.23 crore, interest of ₹80,03,440, penal charges of ₹1,37,28,750 and cheque-bounce charges of ₹1,475. The Tribunal’s subsequent admission orders resulted in initiation of CIRP and appointment of an Interim Resolution Professional. The petitioners contended that the financial creditor was using insolvency proceedings as a coercive mechanism for debt recovery rather than corporate resolution. They argued that penal interest and other charges had disproportionately inflated the outstanding liability and could not be treated as financial debt under Section 5(8) of the IBC. According to the petitioners, the NCLT lacked jurisdiction to entertain claims incorporating such components. They also relied upon proceedings before the Bombay High Court concerning anticipatory bail and payment undertakings, and offered to deposit ₹1 crore before the Gujarat High Court to demonstrate their bona fides. The financial creditor opposed the petition on maintainability, pointing out that the petitioners had already filed Company Appeal (INS) No. 1902 of 2026 before the NCLAT under Section 61 of the IBC. The creditor further submitted that even excluding disputed penal charges, the outstanding principal exceeded the ₹1 crore threshold for initiation of insolvency proceedings. Reliance was placed on Supreme Court decisions emphasising that the IBC constitutes a comprehensive statutory framework containing its own appellate and remedial mechanisms. Justice Nisha M. Thakore observed that once CIRP commences and an Interim Resolution Professional is appointed, the management and representation of the corporate debtor vest in the IRP. Nevertheless, because the writ petition had also been filed by the erstwhile directors in their individual capacities, the Court held the petition maintainable. The Court then distinguished the technical maintainability of the petition from the question whether its extraordinary constitutional jurisdiction should actually be exercised. On the substantive jurisdictional objection, the Court held that inclusion of contractual penal interest or penal charges may affect the quantum of a financial creditor’s claim but does not inherently deprive the NCLT of jurisdiction under Section 7. Such charges are generally capable of forming part of financial debt where expressly stipulated in the underlying financial contract as a consequence of default. The NCLT is principally required to ascertain whether a default meeting the statutory threshold has occurred. The Court also recognised, referring to Swiss Ribbons Private Limited Vs Union of India, that a Resolution Professional exercises administrative functions of verification and collation rather than quasi-judicial adjudication. Unsupported or unverified penal components may therefore be excluded during the claims-verification process. Significantly, the Court found that the petitioners had raised substantially similar objections concerning financial debt, penal charges, jurisdiction and the resolution-oriented objective of the IBC before the NCLAT. Applying the principles governing alternative statutory remedies, it held that no exceptional circumstance such as violation of fundamental rights, breach of natural justice, complete lack of jurisdiction, constitutional invalidity or patent illegality had been established to justify parallel writ intervention. Accordingly, the Gujarat High Court dismissed the petition solely on the issue of maintainability, leaving the pending statutory appeal to proceed before the NCLAT. The Court did not finally adjudicate the precise admissibility or quantum of disputed penal charges. Cases Discussed Davis Kotala Varki and Others Vs Samson T. Heard the learned advocates for the respective parties and appreciated their arguments in light of the legal principles established through various decisions as relied upon during the course of hearing. The short question which arises for consideration of this Court is as to whether the writ petition preferred by the petitioner companies and its erstwhile directors requires consideration despite having availed remedy of the statutory appeal under Section 61 of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016? 8. I have carefully considered the arguments of learned advocates appearing for the respective parties. Having noted the status of the petitioners’ companies, undoubtedly, the petitioners are directly and legally affected by the orders passed by the National Company Law Tribunal directing appointment of the interim Resolution Professionals (‘IRP’, in short), as well as passing order of admission of the application preferred by the respondent creditor under Section 7 of the IBC Code. It is settled legal position that once the corporate insolvency resolution process starts and an interim Resolution Professional is appointed, the management and representation of the corporate debtor vest exclusively in the IRP. In fact, the Hon’ble Supreme Court in the case of Nitendra Kumar Tomar Vs. Eunox SPA, neutral citation: 2026 INSC 356, has held that an appeal filed directly in the name of the corporate debtor by a suspended director after CIRP commencement is void and incompetent from inception. In such circumstances, noticing the fact that the petition is filed not only by the companies facing insolvency but by its erstwhile directors in their individual capacity, the petition is held maintainable. 9. So far as the case of the petitioners are concerned and the preliminary objection raised by the respondent no.1, it is an undisputed fact that the petitioners have approached before the National Company Law Appellate Tribunal under Section 61 of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code. Such appeal is pending consideration as can be gathered from the order dated 14th October, 2026 produced on record at page 809. Having noted the aforesaid undisputed facts, the subliminal question which arises in the peculiar facts of the case is as to whether the availability of statutory remedy of appeal would operate as an absolute bar to the maintainability of the writ petition preferred under Article 226 of the Constitution of India. 10. I have carefully considered the arguments of learned Senior Advocate appearing for the respondent no.1, more particularly the decisions of Hon’ble Supreme Court as referred to and relied upon in case of Davis Kotala Varki (supra), Committee of Creditors of KSK Mahanadi Power Company Limited (supra), and in the case of Mohammed Enterprises (Tanzania) Limited (supra). On careful reading of the aforesaid decisions, the Hon’ble Supreme Court has held that at the conclusion of CIRP proceedings, the High Court should not interfere with the proceedings initiated under the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016 as it would amount to breach of discipline of law. The Court has reiterated that the High Court should note that Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code is a complete code in itself, having sufficient checks and balances, remedial avenues, and appeals. The adherence of protocols and procedures maintains legal discipline and preserves the balance between need for order and quest for justice. The Hon’ble Supreme Court has therefore opined that supervisory and judicial review powers conferred with the High Courts represents critical constitutional safeguards, and the exercise of it demands rigorous scrutiny and judicious application. 11. Having noted the aforesaid guiding principles laid down by the Hon’ble Supreme Court, if one looks at the provisions of appeal, more particularly Section 61 of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, it clearly confers the remedy of appeal to the aggrieved person, which includes the challenge to the order passed under Section 7 of the IBC Code at the instance of the financial creditor. There is no dispute with regard to the fact that in the appeal preferred by the present petitioners, similar grounds which have been raised in the present petition have also been raised in the appeal. The issue of jurisdiction of the National Company Law Tribunal to adjudicate with regard to the financial debt which also includes a component of penal charges as highlighted by the petitioner, has also been raised in the appeal pending consideration before the National Company Law Appellate Tribunal. It is also required to be noted that once the resolution professional is appointed by the National Company Law Tribunal, the resolution professional can also examine and reject the claim raised towards penal charges in case if they do not meet the legal criteria of the term “financial debt” as provided under Section 2(8) of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016. As rightly pointed out by the learned advocate for the petitioner, the Hon’ble Supreme Court in the case of Swiss Ribbons Vs. Union of India, reported in (2019) 4 SCC 17, has established that the RP only has administrative powers to verify and collate claims. The RP does not possess quasi-judicial or adjudicatory authority. However, if the penal charges are unverified, unsubstantiated by the underlying loan agreement or the necessary calculations with regard to such a claim are missing, the RP can certainly refuse to admit such a specific portion of the claim. 12. According to Section 3(11) of the IBC, “debt” is defined as a liability or obligation in respect of a claim which is due from any person. As per the definition of the term “financial debt” as provided under Section 5(8) of the IBC Code, the debt must be disbursed against the “ time value of money”. It is required to be noted that the question with regard to the contractual penal interest or penal charges applied due to default on a financial loan are generally accepted as part of the financial debt only if explicitly stipulated in the financial contract/loan agreement as a consequence of default. It is true that inclusion of interest and penalty components alters the quantum of the claim but does not inherently divest the NCLT of its jurisdiction. The NCLT operates in a summary jurisdiction under Section 7 and is only required to satisfy that a default of at least Rs.1 crore has occurred. The exact reconciliation, verification and adjudication of interest and penalties can be examined by the Resolution Professional during CIRP and therefore, the NCLT’s jurisdiction shall not be taken away. However, the broader issue with regard to the core object of appreciating proceeding to bring resolution instead of recovery being subject matter under consideration in appeal, this Court find it appropriate to not to interfere at this stage. Thus, there is no complete lack of jurisdiction of NCLT to examine the application under section 7 of the Code, in the facts of the case. In such circumstances, without examining the aforesaid aspect in detail, having noted that the petitioner has already approached in appeal before the NCLAT and a similar contention being raised as can be gathered from the appeal memo produced on record by the respondent, this Court is not inclined to much dwell into the aforesaid issue and has confined it to the issue of jurisdiction. 13. It is well settled law that existence of an alternative statutory remedy does not oust the jurisdiction of the High Court or render a writ petition not maintainable. Thus, the Courts have time and again ruled that the availability of statutory remedy of appeal does not operate as an absolute bar to the maintainability of a writ petition under Article 226 of the Constitution of India. However, the discretion is also vested with the High Courts to normally decline to entertain it as a matter of judicial policy, discipline, and self-imposed restraint. The few exceptional circumstances recognized through various precedents, the petitioner was expected to make out his case falling within such recognized exceptions, viz. breach of fundamental rights, violation of principles of natural justice, lack of jurisdiction, challenge to vires or constitutionality, and patent illegality or arbitrariness. At this stage, it would be appropriate to refer to the recent decision of Hon’ble Supreme Court in the case of Rikhap Chand Jain Vs. Union of India and others, neutral citation 2025 INSC 1337. The appellant before the Hon’ble Supreme Court was aggrieved by the order passed by the High Court dismissing the writ petition on the ground of omission of the appellant to pursue the alternative remedy of appeal provided under the Customs Act, 1962. The Hon’ble Supreme Court had considered the provisions of the Customs Act and thereafter has referred to the constitutional bench decision in the case of Thansingh Nathmal Vs. A. Mazid, Superintendent of Taxes, reported in AIR 1964 SC 1419, and observed that writ jurisdiction should not normally be exercised on a petition under Article 226, for that would allow the machinery set up by the concerned statute to be bypassed. 14. Bearing in mind the aforesaid settled principles, in the opinion of this Court, the grounds raised for consideration of this Court in the writ petition since are already subject matter of appeal which is pending consideration before the appellate authority, i.e. NCLAT, in absence of any exceptional circumstances being pointed out by learned advocate for the petitioner, this Court is not inclined to exercise its writ jurisdiction having noted the fact that the petitioner has already approached in appeal before the NCLAT. 15. In view of the above, the present petition is dismissed solely on the ground of issue of maintainability. With these observations, the petition stands disposed of.

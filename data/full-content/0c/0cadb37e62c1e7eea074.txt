(Delhi High Court) Corporate Guarantees Under GST: Delhi High Court Upholds Levy but Rejects Compulsory 1% Minimum Valuation A Significant Ruling on Intra-Group Guarantees The Delhi High Court has delivered an important judgment on the taxability, valuation and time of supply of corporate guarantees under GST. While upholding the levy on guarantees furnished by holding or group companies for related entities, even without consideration, the Court rejected an inflexible application of the deemed valuation of 1% per annum. The judgment provides substantial relief on valuation and the treatment of existing guarantees, while declining the broader contention that guarantees furnished without commission fall outside GST altogether. Why a Guarantee Without Commission Is Taxable The petitioners contended that a corporate guarantee is essentially a shareholder activity, undertaken to protect the holding company’s investment. Since no commission was charged, they argued that there was no taxable service. The Court held that the service consists of the assumption of the subsidiary’s credit risk and extension of the guarantor’s creditworthiness. This enables the subsidiary to obtain financing on the strength of the guarantor’s financial standing. Under Section 7(1)(c), read with Entry 2 of Schedule I, supplies between related persons in the course or furtherance of business may attract GST even without consideration. Accordingly, absence of commission does not eliminate the supply. The subsidiary or related borrower is the recipient under Section 2(93)(c). The Court also rejected exclusion of the service as an actionable claim or security. Rule 28(2): Lower Market Value Can Be Established The principal relief concerns Rule 28(2), which prescribes valuation at 1% of the guaranteed amount per annum or actual consideration, whichever is higher, subject to the applicable proviso. The Court upheld the rule-making power but found that treating 1% as an irrebuttable minimum would be arbitrary where a lower open market value can be demonstrated. The rule was therefore read down. The deemed value must yield to a lower open market value established through cogent and verifiable material. The burden rests on the supplier, and the proper officer may scrutinise the evidence. The Court identified fair valuation under Ind AS 109, accepted transfer pricing benchmarks and credit-rating-based pricing as material capable of supporting valuation. However, merely agreeing to charge no commission, or a lower commission within the group, does not establish market value. The agreed related-party price and independently supported market value are distinct considerations. Full Input Tax Credit Proviso Remains Valid The Court upheld the proviso accepting the invoice value where the recipient is eligible for full input tax credit. This treatment rests on revenue neutrality, since the recipient can fully utilise the corresponding credit. Nevertheless, eligibility for full credit is a question of fact and cannot be presumed merely because the entities belong to the same group. Guarantee Is a Single Supply, Not an Annual Supply The Court held that a corporate guarantee is supplied when it is executed and delivered. Its continuing existence represents the consequence of that supply, rather than a fresh supply every year. A renewal, extension of tenure or enhancement of the guaranteed amount constitutes a fresh supply. Valuation must correspond to the additional period or enhanced amount, avoiding double taxation of the same risk. The time of supply is governed by Section 13(2)(a) or (b). Annual accounting disclosure under Ind AS 24 does not independently create recurring supplies. Tax is to be computed for the entire tenure at the time of supply. The Court left the Government free to provide collection through annual instalments. Earlier Guarantees Require Separate Treatment Guarantees executed before 1 July 2017 remain outside GST unless subsequently renewed, extended or enhanced. Guarantees executed between 1 July 2017 and 25 October 2023 are taxable, but valuation must follow the rules then applicable. The authorities cannot import the 1% figure through Rule 31. Rule 28(2) applies to guarantees executed, renewed, extended or enhanced on or after 26 October 2023. Mere continuation of an earlier guarantee does not attract that rule. Earlier Decisions and the Court’s Disagreement The Court distinguished the Supreme Court’s decision in Edelweiss Financial Services Ltd., which concerned the service tax regime, where consideration formed part of the statutory definition of service. It expressly disagreed with the Bombay High Court’s view in D.P. Jain & Co. Infrastructure Pvt. Ltd., emphasising GST’s specific treatment of supplies without consideration. While substantially agreeing with Torrent Power, the Court differed on certain aspects, particularly valuation and recurring time of supply. Author’s Comments The judgment makes guarantee documentation, execution dates and independent valuation evidence central to defending GST demands. Its practical relief lies in replacing a compulsory valuation floor with a rebuttable presumption. The impugned notices, orders and demands were set aside to the extent inconsistent with the judgment, with fresh determination directed. Limitation under Section 74 and other factual questions remain open. The ruling therefore grants meaningful relief without extinguishing the underlying levy.

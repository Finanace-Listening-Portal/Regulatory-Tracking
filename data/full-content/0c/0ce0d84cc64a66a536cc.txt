Summary: Trademark classification in India determines the goods and services for which protection is sought and plays a significant role in examination, registration and subsequent non-use proceedings. This article explains all 45 classes under the Nice Classification, comprising 34 goods classes and 11 service classes, with practical examples for businesses and professionals. It examines the statutory framework under the Trade Marks Act, 1999 and Trade Marks Rules, 2017, including the Registrar’s authority to determine classification, multi-class applications, restrictions on excessively broad specifications, correction and division of applications, similarity of goods and services, and removal for non-use. The discussion covers the Supreme Court decisions in Amritdhara Pharmacy and Toyota Jidosha Kabushiki Kaisha, illustrating principles of deceptive similarity and territorial reputation. It also highlights changes introduced by the 13th edition of the Nice Classification effective from 1 January 2026, particularly the treatment of eyewear, electric toothbrushes and essential oils, and refers to the forthcoming 2027 version. Practical examples distinguish downloadable software from online software services, retail services from the goods sold, and restaurant services from packaged food products. The article includes a comparison of electronic filing fees, covering registration, expedited examination, opposition, renewal and restoration. It concludes with six practical filing recommendations, emphasising accurate product descriptions, appropriate service classes, searches across related classes and specifications supported by actual or intended use. Applicants should verify current classification entries and official fees before filing. Classes 1 to 34 cover goods, and Classes 35 to 45 cover services. It is tempting to treat the class as a box to tick on the form, but it does real work. The class, together with the specification you write under it, sets what the Registrar examines, how far your claim reaches, and which items a competitor can attack for non-use under Section 47. Two features of the law make mistakes expensive. The Registrar, not the applicant, has the last word on classification (Section 7(2)). And the Registrar can push back on a claim that is wider than the applicant’s actual or intended use (Rule 23(5)). Below, I go through all 45 classes with examples, and along the way I set out the statutory and judicial points that should guide how you choose a class and word the specification. The legal basis in India Section 7(1) and (2), Trade Marks Act, 1999: The Registrar classifies goods and services, as far as may be, according to the international classification. If there is any question about the class of particular goods or services, the Registrar decides it, and that decision is final. Section 8 and Rule 20, Trade Marks Rules, 2017: Rule 20(1) says classification must follow the current edition of the Nice Classification. Rule 20(2) requires the Registrar to publish a class-wise and alphabetical index, which includes goods and services of Indian origin. Section 18(2) and Rule 23(4): A single application can cover several classes, with a fee for each class. It can cover only one mark, though, unless it is a series mark. Rule 23(5): If you claim every item in a class, or a very large variety of them, the Registrar may refuse the application unless the use you have made, or intend to make, of the mark justifies that breadth. The proviso adds that the names of goods and services should, as far as may be, correspond to the Registrar’s published classification. Rule 23(6): If the Registrar finds that your goods or services also fall in other classes, you must either restrict the specification or add those classes by Form TM-M with the fee. Where everything falls in a different class, the Registrar may allow the class to be corrected. Treat this as a remedy the Registrar may grant, not one you can demand. Section 22 (proviso) and Rule 23(3): A multi-class application can be divided, and the divided applications keep the date of the original. That helps when one class draws an objection, since the unobjected classes can move ahead while you argue the contested one. Section 11: Refusal depends on whether the marks are identical or similar and whether the goods or services are identical or similar. What counts is similarity, not whether the goods share a class, so a citation can come from a class you never filed in. Section 47(1): A registered mark can be removed for the goods or services concerned if there was no bona fide intention to use it, or if it has not been bona fide used for a continuous period of five years from entry on the register, counted up to three months before the application. Removal works item by item. Below given are relevant cases to understand better: 1. Amritdhara Pharmacy v. Satya Deo Gupta [Supreme Court, AIR 1963 SC 449, decided 27 April 1962 (S.K. Das, Hidayatullah and J.C. Shah, JJ.)] Facts of the Case: Satya Deo Gupta, proprietor of Rup Bilas Company, Kanpur, applied on 19 July 1950 under Section 14 of the Trade Marks Act, 1940 to register “Lakshmandhara” in Class 5 for a medicinal preparation. Amritdhara Pharmacy, the proprietor of the registered mark “Amritdhara”, opposed it. Amritdhara had been on the market since 1901, and the respondent claimed use of his mark from 1923. The two issues in this case were: a. Was “Lakshmandhara” deceptively similar to “Amritdhara”? b. Had Amritdhara’s long silence amounted to acquiescence, which would be a “special circumstance” under Section 10(2) of the 1940 Act? The Judgement: On similarity, the Court held that the overall phonetic and structural similarity between the two names was likely to cause confusion. The test is whether an unwary buyer of average intelligence and imperfect recollection would be confused, judged on the marks as a whole. The Court drew on another case namely Corn Products v. Shangrila Food Products for this approach. On concurrence, the Court accepted the Registrar’s finding that Amritdhara knew of the respondent’s use and let the business grow over many years without protest. The case therefore fell within Section 10(2), and the Registrar was right to impose the territorial limitation. 2. Toyota Jidosha Kabushiki Kaisha v. Prius Auto Industries Ltd. [Supreme Court, (2018) 2 SCC 1, decided 14 December 2017 (Ranjan Gogoi and Navin Sinha, JJ.)] Facts of the Case: Toyota sued in the Delhi High Court for a permanent injunction for infringement, passing off and damages. It relied on its marks Toyota, Toyota Innova and Toyota Device, and on PRIUS, which it said it had used first. It also claimed PRIUS was a well-known mark under Section 2(1)(zg). The defendant had used PRIUS in India for auto parts since 2001 and registered it in 2002. Issue arose: Can a foreign company rely on its worldwide goodwill (trans-border reputation) to stop an Indian user who adopted the mark first in India, even if Toyota’s product had not yet reached the Indian market? The Judgement: The Supreme Court re-examined the standard of proof for trans-border reputation and clarified what counts as spill-over reputation. It stressed that intellectual property rights are territorial, not global. Toyota could not produce the evidence needed to show that PRIUS was also well known in India, so the Court ruled for the respondents. The Supreme Court said a brand’s fame in other countries is not enough to win in India. A foreign company must prove that Indian customers actually knew its brand before a local business started using the same name. The 13th edition of the Nice Classification The 13th edition came into effect on 1 January 2026, and a further version, NCL(13-2027), follows on 1 January 2027. Rule 20(1) speaks of the “current edition”, so the edition in force on your filing date is the one that applies. Registrations obtained under an earlier edition are not automatically reclassified. I have flagged the changes that touch the examples below within each group. The 13th edition of the Nice Classification The Nice Classification is the international list that decides which class a product or service goes in. It is updated regularly, and the 13th edition came into use on 1 January 2026. A small update to it, called NCL (13-2027), will follow on 1 January 2027. Indian trademark rules say you must use the “current edition” when you file. In simple terms, the version of the list in force on the day you file your application is the one that counts. If your trademark was registered earlier under an older edition, it will not be moved to a new class automatically. It stays where it was. Fees are amended from time to time, so check the current First Schedule on the IP India website before you file. Six practice points for clean filings i. Go by what the product does: Your brand story doesn’t decide the class. What the goods are, or what the service does, decides it. That is how the Nice Classification works, and under Section 7 the Registrar has the final say anyway. ii. Don’t forget the service side: Say you run a bakery chain. Your biscuits and bread belong in Class 30, but the cafés where you serve them belong in Class 43. Each class protects only its own thing, so one won’t cover the other. iii. Use the Registry’s own words: Draft your specification from the index the Registrar publishes under Rule 20(2). The proviso to Rule 23(5) expects this, and it saves you from objections later. Also check which edition of Nice applies on the day you file. iv. Claim only what you really use or plan to use: It is tempting to claim an entire class heading just in case. But that invites an objection under Rule 23(5), and later a competitor can ask for the unused items to be removed under Section 47. Keep a note of why each item is on your list. v. When searching, think similarity, not class numbers: Section 11 and Nandhini Deluxe both ask whether goods or services are similar, not whether they share a class. So a conflict can come from a class you never filed in. Food may need a look at Classes 29, 30 and 43, software at 9 and 42, and fashion at 18, 25 and 35. Treat these as a starting point, not a fixed rule. vi. Get the classes right the first time: You pay the fee for each class (Section 18(2)). Rule 23(6) lets you add or correct classes in limited cases, and the proviso to Section 22 lets you divide an application.

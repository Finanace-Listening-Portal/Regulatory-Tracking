The measures include a reserve requirement for certain foreign exchange derivatives and tighter restrictions on cancelled contracts. While the changes could make currency protection more expensive for businesses, the actual impact will depend on how banks pass on the additional costs and how forward premiums respond. Advt The RBI's new Foreign Exchange Risk Reserve (FERR) requires banks to maintain a reserve equivalent to 20 per cent of the rupee value of eligible foreign exchange derivative contracts exceeding $2 million. The requirement applies to specified rupee-linked derivatives used to hedge current-account transactions in which customers buy foreign currency against the rupee. The reserve obligation falls on banks and does not constitute a direct 20 per cent charge on importers.The cost of hedging could increase by 80 to 150 basis points, depending on how much of the additional expense banks pass on to customers, Reuters reported on October 12, citing bankers. Some lenders have become more selective in offering forward dollar contracts, prioritising larger corporate customers, while some have temporarily suspended online hedging facilities as they assess the new requirements.The triggerThe measures follow a surge in corporate demand for protection against rupee depreciation. Purchases of forward dollars by companies reached a record $77 billion in September, up 80 per cent from a year earlier, while corresponding sales stood at $41 billion, according to clearing-house data cited by Reuters. The imbalance has added to dollar demand amid elevated crude oil prices and foreign capital outflows.The rupee, which approached a record low of 96.96 against the dollar last week, strengthened to 95.76 against the dollar at Monday's opening on October 12. The recovery followed the RBI's latest measures, although the currency's subsequent movement will depend on broader dollar demand, capital flows and external conditions. Advt The RBI has also prohibited the rebooking of cancelled rupee-linked foreign exchange derivative contracts and reduced the threshold for certain transactions undertaken without establishing underlying exposure from $100 million to $5 million. These restrictions seek to limit transactions that are not backed by genuine underlying exposures, while allowing businesses to hedge eligible currency risks.Banks have sought changes to the new reserve requirement, proposing that the RBI replace it with a direct charge on eligible forward dollar purchases. Lenders have also sought clarification on the $2 million threshold and the treatment of hedges against interest payments on foreign currency loans, Reuters reported on October 12. These requests remain proposals and do not alter the rules currently in force. Advt The impact on forward pricing could vary. Lower demand for forward dollars may ease forward premiums, while the cost of maintaining the new reserve could increase the prices banks quote to customers. Importers may respond by reducing hedge ratios or shortening contract tenors, potentially leaving them more exposed to currency fluctuations. The overall effect will depend on banks' pricing decisions, the demand for hedging and the trajectory of the rupee.

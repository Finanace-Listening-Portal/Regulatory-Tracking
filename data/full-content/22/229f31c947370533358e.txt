RBI/2026-27/203, dated 17 July 2026, superseding five earlier circulars issued between July 2022 and October 2025. Under the consolidated directions, an authorised dealer Category-I bank may open an SRVA for its overseas branch or a bank resident outside India under Regulation 7(1) of the Foreign Exchange Management (Deposit) Regulations, 2016. SRVA settlement is an additional route for invoicing and settling exports and imports in Indian rupees; the circular also permits settlement of otherwise permissible FEMA current- and capital-account transactions. It does not legalise prohibited foreign-exchange trading, eliminate KYC, or waive export-import reporting. Funding may include inward remittances, transfers from other repatriable rupee accounts and proceeds of permitted transactions; investments from SRVA balances are governed by RBI’s 2025 directions on non-resident investment in debt instruments. For Indian businesses, the key controls are contract currency, correspondent-bank arrangements, EDPMS/IDPMS closure, GST export-of-services conditions, income-tax recognition, withholding on payments to non-residents and AS 11/Ind AS 21 accounting. Exporters must also apply the export-import regulations effective 1 October 2026. The practical issue is not whether rupee settlement is “tax free”, but whether the underlying supply, settlement, documentation and reporting all comply with their separate laws. (DIR Series) Circular No. 19 dated 17 July 2026 (RBI/2026-27/203) is the operative consolidated RBI instruction on Special Rupee Vostro Accounts. It supersedes the earlier SRVA circulars of 11 July 2022, 17 November 2023, 11 June 2024, 5 August 2025 and 3 October 2025. This is significant because advice based only on the original 2022 framework may incorrectly suggest that prior case-by-case RBI approval remains necessary. The consolidated circular, issued under sections 10(4) and 11(1) of FEMA, took immediate effect. Its text is available in the RBI notification archive and is reproduced in TaxGuru’s circular report. An SRVA is a rupee account maintained by an Indian authorised dealer bank for an overseas correspondent bank or its overseas branch. The foreign bank’s customer and the Indian customer can use the banking chain for eligible INR-settled cross-border transactions. The account is not an individual foreign-currency trading account and is not a substitute for a resident’s ordinary current or savings account. The correspondent-bank relationship, account permissions and underlying transaction must all be valid. Which banks can open the account? Paragraph 3 of the July circular allows an AD Category-I bank in India to open an SRVA for its branch outside India or for a bank resident outside India in terms of Regulation 7(1) of the FEMA Deposit Regulations, 2016. The consolidated approach removes the old separate prior-approval route but does not remove bank-level onboarding, KYC, anti-money-laundering controls, sanctions screening or correspondent-risk assessment. The overseas buyer pays through its bank, which uses the SRVA relationship with the Indian AD bank. The Indian exporter receives the rupee credit, and the bank must link the receipt to the relevant export documentation. The parties should identify the precise exporter, overseas buyer, invoice currency, shipment/service reference, bank reference and settlement date. A bank credit labelled “SRVA” without the underlying export trail is not sufficient evidence that every FEMA and GST condition has been met. Importer’s transaction An Indian importer may pay its foreign supplier through the eligible rupee-settlement banking chain. The importer still needs the commercial contract, import invoice, shipping or service documentation, appropriate purpose and regulatory reporting. The arrangement does not make import duties or import IGST disappear, and it does not remove withholding-tax obligations where the payment to a non-resident is chargeable to tax in India. An importer should confirm with the AD bank how the payment will be mapped against the relevant IDPMS entry or other applicable documentation. The July circular allows AD banks maintaining SRVAs to open an additional current account for exporters/importers exclusively for export-import settlement. This operational segregation can help reconcile trade flows, but it should not be mistaken for permission to park unrelated proceeds or to use the account for unrestricted personal remittances. Each debit and credit must fit the bank’s approved account operation and the relevant FEMA transaction category. FEMA Export-Import Rules Effective 1 October 2026 RBI’s Foreign Exchange Management (Export and Import of Goods and Services) Regulations, 2026, issued in January 2026 and effective 1 October 2026, must be read alongside the July SRVA circular for current transactions. The regulations and accompanying authorised-dealer directions govern export declarations, realisation, import-payment controls and related reporting. A rupee invoice does not itself extend the period for realising export proceeds or dispense with an authorised dealer’s scrutiny. Where the current rules permit bank-led handling of extensions or closure, the supporting reasons and approval trail should be retained. EDPMS and IDPMS are RBI banking-monitoring systems for export and import documentation and settlement. Their application depends on the nature of the transaction, the relevant shipping bill/bill of entry or service records, and the 2026 reporting rules. Businesses should not assume that the same documentation used for a USD wire is sufficient for an SRVA transaction; they must obtain the bank’s prescribed rupee-settlement reference and ensure matching in the system. The exporter should reconcile invoice value, part payments, advances, deductions, credit notes and final settlement. Advance receipts, set-off and guarantees The July circular’s annex expressly notes that old SRVA-specific instructions on export advances, set-off of export receivables and bank guarantees were not incorporated into the consolidated circular; authorised dealers are to follow the extant directions as amended. It would therefore be incorrect to quote the 2022 SRVA paragraphs as still independently controlling these issues. A business proposing advance settlement, netting or a guarantee must check the live export-import directions and relevant banking approval rather than rely on the label “SRVA”. GST on INR-Settled Exports and Imports Section 16 of the Integrated Goods and Services Tax Act, 2017 treats qualifying exports of goods or services as zero-rated supplies, subject to the applicable conditions and refund procedure. The export-of-services definition in section 2(6) includes a receipt condition involving convertible foreign exchange or Indian rupees wherever permitted by RBI, in addition to supplier/recipient location, place of supply and distinct-person requirements. Thus, RBI-permitted INR receipt through an SRVA can be relevant to that condition, but does not automatically make a service an export. The place of supply and relationship between establishments must still be tested. For goods, the physical export and customs evidence remain essential. An importer of goods may face customs duties and IGST under the customs and GST framework regardless of the rupee payment mechanism. An Indian importer of services should test the place of supply, reverse-charge liability where applicable and input-tax-credit eligibility. For exports under LUT, refund claims and the consequences of non-realisation must be examined separately. Bank charges, intermediary services and other taxable fees may carry their own GST consequences. Income Tax, Withholding and Currency Gains Export revenue and import costs remain subject to normal income-tax recognition and deduction principles. Settlement through SRVA does not create a blanket income-tax exemption or a concessional rate. The exporter records sales and recognises profit according to the applicable accounting and tax framework; the importer tests deductibility, capitalisation, related-party pricing and withholding where a payment to a non-resident is chargeable to Indian tax. A payment in rupees can still be a cross-border payment to a non-resident. The existence of an SRVA does not decide the chargeability or treaty treatment of that payment. Where an invoice is genuinely denominated and settled in INR, the Indian party may not have the same currency-translation exposure as on a foreign-currency receivable. But a contract denominated in USD and discharged through an INR equivalent can produce exchange differences, depending on the contractual rights, settlement terms and dates. Tax treatment of foreign-exchange differences under the Income-tax Act, 2025 and applicable Income Computation and Disclosure Standards should be reconciled with book recognition. Do not mechanically cite the old section 43AA without mapping it to the operative 2025 Act provision for the relevant tax year. LRS, TCS and offshore currency platforms are separate matters An SRVA is a bank-to-bank settlement mechanism for permitted cross-border transactions. It is not a new route for individuals to undertake speculative leveraged FX trades on unauthorised offshore websites. Resident individuals’ remittances under the Liberalised Remittance Scheme, and tax collection at source on applicable outward remittances, are governed by separate rules and thresholds. SRVA usage does not convert an otherwise prohibited remittance into a permitted one or exempt an LRS remittance from applicable TCS. Businesses should distinguish genuine imports and capital-account payments from retail foreign-exchange speculation. AS 11, Ind AS 21 and Practical Reconciliation Under AS 11 or Ind AS 21, as applicable, a transaction denominated in a foreign currency is generally translated using the relevant exchange rate on initial recognition, with monetary balances retranslated and exchange differences recognised according to the standard. Where the contract and settlement are truly INR-denominated, an Indian entity may have no foreign-currency monetary receivable or payable merely because the counterparty is overseas. The contract’s actual currency clause matters more than the country of the customer. Hedging instruments, if any, have separate accounting considerations under the applicable standards. A useful month-end control is a three-way reconciliation among the sales/purchase ledger, the AD bank’s SRVA advice and EDPMS/IDPMS or other applicable RBI reporting. Confirm whether invoices were partially settled, adjusted by credit notes, paid through multiple accounts or subject to charges. Retain the original contract, amendments, invoice, bank advice, shipment/service evidence, GST filings and regulatory closure documents in one transaction folder. For audits, record management’s conclusion on INR denomination, FX exposure, export recognition and withholding rather than merely attaching the bank statement. A second category involves inappropriate use of account balances for transactions that are not otherwise permissible under FEMA. A third is confusing RBI permission to settle in INR with permission to engage in leveraged forex or offshore margin trading. For internal audit, select a sample of high-value and aged SRVA settlements and trace the complete contract-to-bank-to-regulatory chain. Test whether the exporter’s invoice value agrees to the bank credit and GST return, whether any deductions were authorised, whether the importer has correct customs or service documentation, and whether the accounting currency matches the contract. The resulting audit trail should support both FEMA compliance and income-tax/GST reporting, without treating any one system as conclusive for the others. Frequently Asked Questions What is the operative RBI circular for SRVAs in October 2026? A.P. (DIR Series) Circular No. 19 dated 17 July 2026, RBI/2026-27/203, consolidates and supersedes the five earlier SRVA circulars identified in its annex. Can an exporter open an SRVA directly? No. An SRVA is maintained by an Indian AD Category-I bank for an eligible overseas branch or correspondent bank. Exporters use the banking arrangement through their AD bank. Is prior RBI approval still mandatory for every SRVA? The consolidated July 2026 framework subsumes the 2025 relaxation of the old case-by-case prior-approval process, but bank onboarding and compliance checks remain. Can SRVAs be used for any capital-account transaction? No. Paragraph 4 permits settlement of capital- and current-account transactions that are otherwise permissible under FEMA. It does not authorise prohibited underlying transactions. Does INR receipt automatically qualify as GST export of services? No. INR receipt wherever permitted by RBI addresses only one statutory condition. Place of supply, recipient location, supplier location and distinct-person tests must also be satisfied. Are EDPMS and IDPMS reporting requirements removed? No. The July circular preserves extant FEMA documentation and reporting, and the 2026 export-import regulations must be considered for transactions from 1 October 2026. Does an SRVA payment eliminate non-resident withholding tax? No. Chargeability, treaty provisions, payment character and applicable withholding must be examined independently even when the non-resident is paid in rupees. Can a resident use SRVA to trade leveraged forex offshore? No. SRVA is not a retail trading permission. Unauthorised offshore forex and margin transactions must be assessed under the separate FEMA/RBI restrictions. Key Takeaways The operative SRVA instruction is RBI Circular No. 19 dated 17 July 2026, not the superseded 2022 circular alone. SRVA settlement is an additional INR payment channel for permitted transactions, not a waiver of FEMA restrictions. AD banks maintain SRVAs for eligible overseas banking counterparties and remain responsible for due diligence. The export-import regulations effective 1 October 2026 and EDPMS/IDPMS reporting continue to matter. GST zero-rating, non-resident withholding, LRS/TCS and import duties must each be tested independently.

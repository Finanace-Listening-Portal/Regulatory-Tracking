Advertisement (adsbygoogle = window.adsbygoogle || []).push({}); PCIT (Central) Vs Hitesh Ashok Vaswani (Gujarat High Court) Summary: The Gujarat High Court, in Principal Commissioner of Income Tax (Central), Ahmedabad Vs Hitesh Ashok Vaswani and 93 connected petitions, examined the scope and limitations of the rectification powers available to the Income Tax Appellate Tribunal under Section 254(2) of the Income-tax Act, 1961. The petitions arose from the proceedings involving the Venus Group, where extensive search and seizure operations had resulted in numerous assessments and subsequent appeals. The Revenue challenged the Tribunal’s order dated 24 August 2022 dismissing miscellaneous applications seeking rectification of its earlier appellate order dated 12 November 2020. The central controversy concerned whether the Tribunal had committed mistakes apparent from the record while deciding questions relating to the validity of assessments under Sections 153A and 153C, limitation under Section 153B, and reassessment proceedings under Section 147. The High Court was required to determine whether the Revenue’s objections disclosed genuine rectifiable errors or amounted to an attempt to obtain reconsideration of issues already adjudicated on their merits. The dispute originated from search operations conducted in March 2015 at various premises connected with the Venus Group, including premises at Crystal Arcade, C.G. Road, Ahmedabad. During these operations, the Income Tax Department seized documents allegedly containing information relating to several assessees. Based on the seized material, proceedings were initiated under Section 153C after recording satisfaction, and assessment orders were subsequently passed. The matters travelled through the appellate hierarchy and ultimately reached the ITAT, where 107 appeals, comprising 71 appeals filed by the assessees and 36 by the Revenue, were considered together. Given the extensive records, reportedly exceeding 20,000 pages, the Tribunal divided the controversies into four categories. These concerned the jurisdictional validity of Section 153C proceedings, the requirement of incriminating material for additions under Section 153A, the limitation period governing search assessments under Section 153B, and the legality of reassessment proceedings based on reasons recorded under Section 147. The Tribunal’s decisions on these questions substantially favoured the assessees, prompting the Revenue to seek rectification. The first major issue concerned the interpretation of Section 153C and the effect of the amendment introduced with effect from 1 June 2015. Before that amendment, the statutory language required seized books of account or documents to belong to a person other than the searched person before jurisdiction could be assumed under Section 153C. The amendment expanded the statutory language to include documents or information that pertained to or related to such other person. The Tribunal had held that the satisfaction notes in the relevant proceedings referred to documents relating to or pertaining to the assessees rather than establishing that the documents belonged to them. Since the searches had been conducted before 1 June 2015, the Tribunal considered the earlier statutory requirement applicable and invalidated the proceedings. The Revenue contended that the Tribunal had incorrectly interpreted the amendment and relied upon the Supreme Court’s decision in Income Tax Officer Vs Vikram Sujitkumar Bhatia to support its position. The assessees responded that the Tribunal had decided the appeals after considering the prevailing judicial authorities and that the subsequent Supreme Court judgment, delivered on 6 April 2023, was not available when the Tribunal passed its appellate and rectification orders in November 2020 and August 2022 respectively. The controversy therefore involved not merely the interpretation of Section 153C but also whether a later judicial pronouncement could justify reopening a concluded adjudication through the limited rectification mechanism. The second category concerned assessments under Section 153A and the relationship between additions made during search assessments and incriminating material discovered during the search. The Tribunal had considered the principles emerging from CIT Vs Kabul Chawla and examined whether the additions were supported by material seized from the premises of the particular assessee. The Revenue argued that the Tribunal had adopted an unduly restrictive interpretation by insisting upon incriminating material discovered at the premises of the assessee concerned, particularly when simultaneous searches had been conducted across several connected persons and entities. The assessees maintained that separate search authorisations had been issued and that the statutory distinction between proceedings under Sections 153A and 153C could not be disregarded. They further contended that the Revenue was effectively challenging the Tribunal’s appreciation of facts and interpretation of law, which could be examined in appellate proceedings but could not ordinarily be reopened under Section 254(2). The High Court considered the distinction between correcting an obvious error and revisiting a conclusion reached after judicial evaluation. The existence of competing interpretations or dissatisfaction with the Tribunal’s reasoning did not, by itself, establish a mistake apparent from the record. The third category related to the limitation prescribed under Section 153B for completing assessments arising from search proceedings. The Tribunal had held that the relevant searches were conducted in March 2015 and that the statutory period for completing the assessments expired on 31 March 2017, whereas the assessment orders were passed in December 2017. Accordingly, the Tribunal had treated the assessments as barred by limitation. The Revenue argued that the Tribunal had failed to properly appreciate the circumstances surrounding prohibitory orders issued under Section 132(3), their subsequent revocation, and the determination of the date on which the search proceedings were legally concluded. According to the Revenue, these aspects demonstrated an apparent error in the Tribunal’s findings. The assessees countered that the Tribunal had examined the search records, the prohibitory orders and the relevant circumstances before arriving at its conclusion. They submitted that the Revenue’s objections required a fresh assessment of the evidence and the legal effect of the proceedings, which would amount to a substantive review rather than rectification. The dispute highlighted the importance of distinguishing an evident computational or legal mistake from a contested conclusion involving factual investigation and statutory interpretation. The fourth category involved reassessment proceedings under Section 147, particularly the requirement that the Assessing Officer must independently form a reason to believe that income chargeable to tax has escaped assessment. The Tribunal had examined the recorded reasons and concluded that they were essentially reproductions of information received from the investigation authorities rather than an independent formation of satisfaction by the Assessing Officer. On that basis, the Tribunal characterised the proceedings as involving borrowed satisfaction and found the reassessment unsustainable. The Revenue argued that the Tribunal had overlooked material aspects of the recorded reasons and that the error should have been corrected in the miscellaneous applications. The assessees maintained that the Tribunal had already examined the available records and reached a reasoned conclusion. They emphasised that Section 254(2) permits rectification of mistakes apparent from the record but does not authorise a rehearing of the appeal or substitution of a different conclusion merely because one party considers the original decision erroneous. Reliance was also placed on judicial authorities concerning the restricted nature of rectification jurisdiction, including Commissioner of Income Tax Vs Reliance Telecom Limited. The High Court examined these submissions in the broader context of the statutory limits on the Tribunal’s power to revisit its own orders. On examining the petitions category-wise, the Gujarat High Court emphasised the distinction between rectification and review under Section 254(2). A mistake capable of rectification must be apparent from the record, whereas an issue requiring elaborate arguments, reconsideration of evidence, reappreciation of factual findings or a fresh interpretation of disputed legal questions ordinarily falls outside that jurisdiction. The Court considered the position of petitions in which tax appeals were already pending separately from those in which the Revenue sought to establish apparent errors in the Tribunal’s conclusions. It found that the objections raised by the Revenue, in the circumstances discussed in the judgment, did not justify using rectification proceedings as a substitute for substantive appellate remedies. The High Court ultimately dismissed all 94 Special Civil Applications, including the 68 petitions associated with pending tax appeals and the remaining 26 petitions examined on the relevant issues. The judgment reinforces the principle that Section 254(2) is intended to correct patent mistakes rather than permit repeated litigation over matters already adjudicated by the Tribunal. It also illustrates the procedural significance of the distinctions between Sections 153A and 153C, the statutory limitation governing search assessments, and the requirement of independent satisfaction in reassessment proceedings. The Revenue’s challenges to the Tribunal’s refusal to rectify its earlier orders were therefore unsuccessful. List of Cases Discussed: 1. Income Tax Officer Vs Vikram Sujitkumar Bhatia – Supreme Court, [2023] 453 ITR 417 – Considered in relation to the applicability of the amended Section 153C to search proceedings initiated before 1 June 2015. The High Court examined the timing of this subsequent decision in determining whether it could justify rectification of the Tribunal’s earlier orders. 2. Anil Kumar Gopi Krishna Agrawal Vs ACIT – Gujarat High Court, [2019] 106 taxmann.com 137 – Considered regarding the requirement that seized documents must belong to a person other than the searched person under the pre-amendment provisions of Section 153C. 3. Kamleshbhai Dharamshibhai Patel Vs CIT – Gujarat High Court, [2013] 31 taxmann.com 50 – Referred to in the controversy concerning the assumption of jurisdiction under Section 153C and the Tribunal’s consideration of jurisdictional precedents. 4. CIT Vs Kabul Chawla – Delhi High Court, 380 ITR 573 – Considered in relation to the requirement of incriminating material for making additions in assessments under Section 153A. 5. PCIT Vs Saumya Construction Pvt. Ltd. – Gujarat High Court – Referred to concerning the legal requirements governing additions in search assessment proceedings and the significance of incriminating material. 6. ACIT Vs Saurashtra Kutch Stock Exchange Ltd. – Supreme Court, [2008] 173 Taxman 322 – Considered regarding mistakes apparent from the record under Section 254(2), including the effect of binding judicial precedents on rectification proceedings. 7. CIT Vs Reliance Telecom Limited – Supreme Court, (2021) 440 ITR 1 – Relied upon for the principle that rectification under Section 254(2) cannot be used to undertake a substantive review or rehearing of matters already decided by the Tribunal. 8. R.C. Sabharwal Vs CIT – Delhi High Court, [2010] 2 taxmann.com 289 – Considered regarding the maintainability and scope of rectification proceedings when an appeal challenging the original Tribunal order is pending before the High Court. 9. CIT Vs Muni Seva Ashram – Gujarat High Court, [2013] 221 Taxman 27 – Relied upon in examining the relationship between pending tax appeals and the exercise of rectification jurisdiction under Section 254(2). 10. CIT Vs Maruti Insurance Distribution Services Limited – Delhi High Court, [2012] 26 taxmann.com 68 – Referred to in support of the distinction between an apparent mistake capable of rectification and a disputed issue requiring appellate consideration. 11. Vrundavan Ginning and Oil Mill Vs Assistant Registrar/President – [2021] 434 ITR 583 – Cited in the submissions concerning the limits of rectification and the impermissibility of reconsidering an adjudicated matter through a correction application. 12. Asha Rani Lakhotia Vs ACIT – ITAT Delhi – Referred to in the discussion concerning the use of material seized from another person’s premises while framing an assessment under Section 153A. 13. Subhag Khattar Vs ACIT – Delhi High Court, Tax Appeal No. 60 of 2017 – Considered regarding additions under Section 153A and the relevance of incriminating material discovered during search proceedings.
